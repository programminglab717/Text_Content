# The Arithmetic of a Desert Crossing

A litre of water weighs a kilogram. That sentence contains most of what there
is to say about crossing a desert on foot, and everything else is commentary.

A person walking through desert heat with a load can lose somewhere between
eight and twelve litres of water a day through sweat and breath. Call it ten,
to keep the numbers round. Ten kilograms a day, every day, is more than most
people can carry in addition to food, shelter and whatever else the journey
requires. Three days of water is thirty kilograms and a serious load. Six days
of water is sixty kilograms and not a load at all — it is an impossibility.
The walker's range is therefore not set by fitness or willpower. It is set by
the distance between places where water can be got, and if that distance
exceeds two or three days, an unassisted person on foot simply does not cross.

Every technique in the long history of desert travel is a way of pushing
against that constraint: reducing the daily loss, increasing the carrying
capacity, or arranging in advance for water to be waiting.

## Reducing the Loss

The body's cooling system is evaporative, which means it spends water to shed
heat, and in dry air it works extremely well — so well that it will keep
working until there is nothing left to spend. Heat stroke in a desert usually
arrives after dehydration has begun to shut sweating down, which is why the
skin of a person in real trouble often goes dry and hot rather than clammy.

The traditional countermeasures are all about limiting the heat load rather
than enduring it. Loose, layered robes over most of the body sound
counterintuitive to anyone raised on the idea that you strip off in hot
weather, but they cut direct solar radiation on the skin, trap a layer of
humid air that slows evaporation from the body itself, and keep sweat on the
skin where it can do its work instead of dripping into the sand. Covering the
head and the back of the neck matters more than almost anything else. So does
not moving in the middle of the day. Caravans and nomadic groups across the
Sahara, Arabia and Central Asia have generally travelled in the early morning
and the evening, and sometimes at night, and spent the hours around noon under
whatever shade could be built or found. A person lying still in shade at
forty degrees loses a fraction of what the same person loses walking in the
sun.

Salt is the other half of it. Sweat carries sodium as well as water, and
someone who drinks large volumes of plain water while sweating heavily over
days can dilute their blood sodium to the point of confusion and collapse.
Desert diets are salty for a reason, and so are the rehydration mixtures used
now.

## Why the Camel Won

The dromedary is the most effective piece of desert technology ever assembled,
and its advantages compound. It can drink an enormous volume in a single
session — well over a hundred litres — and rehydrate faster than its blood
chemistry ought to allow, because its red blood cells are oval and elastic
enough to survive the osmotic shock. It tolerates losing a quarter or more of
its body water, a degree of dehydration that would kill most mammals. It lets
its body temperature swing through several degrees over the course of a day,
absorbing heat in the afternoon and radiating it at night rather than sweating
it away. Its kidneys concentrate urine heavily. The hump is fat, not water,
but fat stored in a single mass on the back rather than distributed under the
skin, which leaves the rest of the body free to lose heat.

On top of all that, it carries a serious load, walks on soft sand on broad
splayed feet, browses thorn scrub that no other domestic animal will eat, and
can in extremity be eaten itself. The camel converts a two-day water radius
into something closer to two weeks, and that single change is what made
trans-Saharan trade possible. Before camels arrived in North Africa, the
desert was a boundary; afterwards it was a road network.

The great salt caravans ran on exactly this arithmetic. Routes north from the
Niger bend to the salt pans at Taoudenni, and east from Agadez to Bilma,
crossed hundreds of kilometres of ground with only a handful of wells along
the way, and the whole enterprise depended on hitting each of them. Caravans
travelled in the cool season, moved long hours, and were led by men whose
knowledge of the route was a professional inheritance. A stretch of the western
Sahara is called the Tanezrouft, which is usually translated as something like
the land of thirst: a flat gravel plain, several hundred kilometres across,
with no wells, no vegetation and no landmarks at all.

## Reading Ground That Has No Landmarks

Navigation in featureless desert is a genuinely difficult skill and it was
solved long before instruments. Caravan guides worked from the stars at night,
from the sun and the direction of prevailing winds by day, and from the shape
and orientation of dunes, which in a stable wind regime line up with
remarkable consistency and can be used like a grain in wood. They read sand
texture and colour, the particular scrub that grows where groundwater is
shallow, and the accumulated evidence of animal tracks converging.

Above all they read distance travelled. In the absence of anything to sight
on, dead reckoning is all you have, and a guide who can hold a bearing and
estimate a day's progress accurately enough to arrive within sight of a well
after four days is doing something very hard. The stakes are absolute: a well
missed by ten kilometres in country where you cannot see ten kilometres is a
well that does not exist.

Where the ground permitted it, societies simply moved the water instead.
Qanats and karez — gently sloping tunnels that tap an alluvial aquifer at the
foot of mountains and run underground for kilometres to deliver water at the
surface without evaporation loss — irrigated the oasis towns of Iran, Central
Asia and the western Chinese deserts, and it is those oases, strung out at
intervals a loaded caravan could manage, that the Silk Road actually consisted
of. The road was the wells.

## The Crossings That Failed

European exploration of deserts has a poor record, and the failures are
instructive because they usually come from ignoring one of the constraints
above.

Sven Hedin nearly died in the Taklamakan in the 1890s because his caravan set
out across the dunes with far less water than the crossing required, having
misjudged the distance. Most of his men and animals died; he survived by
crawling to a river channel. Charles Sturt dragged a boat into central
Australia in expectation of an inland sea and found instead a stony desert and
a summer so severe that his party was pinned at a single waterhole for months,
their supplies rotting and their hair and nails reportedly ceasing to grow.

The Burke and Wills expedition of 1860 is the canonical disaster. It was
lavishly funded, badly organised, led by a man with no relevant experience,
and committed to speed over caution. A forward party reached the northern
tidal country and turned back exhausted; the survivors returned to the depot
at Cooper Creek to find that the party waiting for them had departed that same
day. Burke and Wills died there, in a place with water and in a landscape that
supported a substantial Aboriginal population year-round. The people who lived
there offered food, including a flour made from nardoo fern spores, and the
Europeans ate it without the leaching and preparation that removes an enzyme
in the plant destroying thiamine. It is a cruel detail and it captures the
larger point: they starved surrounded by food they did not know how to
process.

The contrast is the traveller who defers to local knowledge entirely. Wilfred
Thesiger crossed the Rub' al Khali of southern Arabia twice in the late
1940s, on camels, with Bedu companions who chose the route, the timing and the
rationing. He was carried by their competence and said so plainly in the book
he wrote afterwards. His crossings worked because they were essentially Bedu
journeys with an Englishman attached.

## What Changed, and What Did Not

Vehicles altered the equation without abolishing it. A four-wheel-drive truck
carries a great deal of water and covers in a day what a caravan covers in a
week, which shifts the binding constraint from water to fuel and to mechanical
failure. That is progress of a kind, but it is a narrower margin than it
looks: a truck that breaks down two hundred kilometres from anything leaves
its occupants exactly where an unassisted walker would be, with the difference
that they are no longer on a route where anyone else is likely to pass.
Satellite navigation and emergency beacons have removed the navigation problem
and much of the rescue problem, which is why modern desert deaths cluster
among people who had neither.

What has not changed is the ratio. Ten kilograms a day, and the distance to
the next water.
