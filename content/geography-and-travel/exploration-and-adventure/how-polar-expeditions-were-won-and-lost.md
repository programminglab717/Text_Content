# How Polar Expeditions Were Won and Lost

A sledge loaded for a southern journey on the Ross Ice Shelf carried something
close to two hundred pounds for every man in the harness. On a hard,
wind-packed surface four men could move it at roughly walking pace for nine
hours a day. On soft snow after a fall, or on the dry crystalline grit that
forms when the temperature drops far enough, they could not. The runners stop
gliding and begin ploughing, and a load that felt manageable at breakfast
becomes something that has to be argued forward a few yards at a time.

Almost nothing about the heroic-age polar expeditions matters more than this,
and almost nothing is less visible in the retelling. The stories get told as
questions of courage, priority and luck. The outcomes were mostly questions of
mass.

## The Tyranny of the Sledge

Consider a party that must carry everything it eats and burns. Suppose four
men can haul forty days of supplies. If they have to come back the same way,
their range is twenty days out — and less than that, because they are hauling
the heaviest load at the start and their daily distance rises as the sledge
lightens. To go further, you have only two real options, and both are
expensive.

The first is depot-laying. You make the outward journey in advance, or in
several advances, dumping caches of food and fuel at known points and marking
them so they can be found again in a whiteout. This works, but every pound
placed a hundred miles south had to be hauled a hundred miles south by
someone, and that someone ate while doing it. Depots multiply the total work
enormously in exchange for extending the final push.

The second is supporting parties. Extra men haul for the polar party, feed it,
and turn back in stages. This is the strategy Robert Falcon Scott used, and
the one Robert Peary refined on the Arctic sea ice into a cascade of
divisions, each breaking trail and then retreating. The exchange rate is poor
because supporters consume rations on the way out as well as the way back, so
each additional day of range for the summit party costs several man-days of
food somewhere behind it. The mathematics is unforgiving and it explains a
great deal: why Ernest Shackleton turned around in 1909 within roughly a
hundred miles of the South Pole, why the final parties were always small, why
the last stretch was always the one with the thinnest margin.

Amundsen's answer was different in kind. He used dogs, and he planned from the
beginning to shoot them progressively as the loads lightened, feeding the
carcasses to the surviving dogs and to the men. An engine that can be eaten
when it is no longer needed breaks the arithmetic open. It was not a trick or
a flourish; it was the single most consequential decision of the race, and it
came out of years spent among Arctic peoples who had solved the same problem
long before Europeans showed up to solve it again.

## What Eats What

The British expeditions used ponies, motor sledges and men. Each choice looks
odd now and each had a defensible logic at the time. Ponies can haul more than
dogs per animal and were available; the difficulty is that they sink in soft
snow where dogs float, they cannot eat seal or each other's meat, and their
fodder therefore has to be carried in addition to everything else. Motor
sledges were genuinely ahead of their time — tracked vehicles, the ancestors of
everything that later crossed Antarctica on tracks — and they broke down
almost immediately in conditions that no engine of that period could tolerate.

That left man-hauling, which the Royal Navy had inherited from Arctic searches
for Franklin and had come, in some quarters, to regard as morally superior.
That framing has been overstated by later writers, but the practical
consequence was real: a man in harness is a very poor prime mover. He converts
food into work at a mediocre efficiency, he cannot be fed on seal blubber
alone without preparation, and unlike a dog he cannot be slaughtered at the
turning point.

Fridtjof Nansen had already demonstrated a third approach and largely been
ignored. Rather than fight the drifting Arctic pack, he built the *Fram* with a
rounded hull designed to be squeezed upward out of the ice instead of being
crushed by it, drove her into the pack north of Siberia, and let the current
carry the ship for three years. When the drift proved unlikely to pass over
the pole, he and Hjalmar Johansen left the ship with dogs and kayaks, pushed
north, turned back, and wintered in a stone hut on Franz Josef Land living on
walrus and bear before an astonished English expedition found them. The
lesson — that the environment can be enlisted rather than opposed — is the
through-line of every successful polar journey since.

## Fuel, Scurvy and the Slow Arithmetic

Food and fuel failures killed more polar travellers than crevasses and
blizzards combined. Two failures deserve particular attention because both
were solvable and neither was understood in time.

The first is fuel. Snow has to be melted before it can be drunk, and melting
it is energetically expensive; a polar party burns paraffin at a rate that
makes the fuel tins one of the heaviest single items on the sledge. Scott's
returning party found several depots short of the paraffin they should have
contained. The leather washers sealing the tin caps shrank and hardened in
extreme cold, and the volatile fuel escaped or evaporated across the months
between laying the depot and returning to it. Losing fuel means losing water,
and losing water on a ration of dry biscuit and pemmican means dehydration
compounding exhaustion.

The second is scurvy. Fresh meat, particularly seal, prevents it; the
Norwegians and the Arctic-experienced ate seal without theorising about why
it helped. British provisioning had drifted into an unlucky error decades
earlier, when the Navy's antiscorbutic supply shifted from Mediterranean
lemon juice to West Indian lime juice, which is markedly weaker, and the juice
was often boiled and stored in copper in ways that destroyed what potency
remained. By the early twentieth century the Navy had half-lost a piece of
practical knowledge it had once possessed, and the working theory in some
quarters blamed tainted tinned food rather than any missing nutrient. Vitamin
C was not isolated until the 1930s.

Underneath both sits a simpler problem: calories. A man hauling a heavy sledge
at altitude in extreme cold burns far more than the ration allowances of the
period provided. The plateau around the South Pole sits near three kilometres
above sea level, the air is thin and desiccating, and the body spends energy
warming every breath and every mouthful of melted snow. Scott's party was
losing weight steadily from the start of the journey. There is no heroism that
compensates for a sustained daily deficit of a thousand calories or more; the
body simply consumes itself, and then the judgement goes.

## The Ground Itself

Antarctica offers three distinct hauling surfaces and each demands something
different. The great shelves are flat but often soft. The glaciers that break
through the Transantarctic Mountains — the Beardmore, the Axel Heiberg — are
steep, crevassed, and swept by wind that scours them into blue ice. The
plateau beyond is high, cold, and carved into sastrugi: ridges of
wind-hardened snow, sometimes knee-high, arranged across your line of travel
with what feels like deliberate malice. Sledges tip on them, runners crack,
skis catch.

The Arctic Ocean is worse in a different way. It is not land but a shifting
raft of sea ice, broken by leads of open water that must be swum, ferried or
detoured around, and heaped into pressure ridges where floes collide. Worst of
all, it moves. A party can sledge north for twelve hours and end the day
further south than it started, because the ice beneath it drifted faster than
the men walked. This is the reason claims of reaching the North Pole on foot
in the early twentieth century have been so persistently disputed: without
celestial navigation of a precision that the conditions rarely allowed, and
without any fixed ground to measure against, the relationship between effort
and position becomes genuinely uncertain.

Whiteout removes the last certainty. When overcast light is scattered evenly
by cloud above and snow below, shadow disappears completely. There is no
horizon, no slope, no texture. Travellers describe stepping off drops they
could not see and stumbling over ridges a foot high, and the standard remedy
is to throw something dark ahead — a glove, a food bag — simply to give the eye
an object to steer toward.

## The Open Boat

Shackleton's *Endurance* expedition is remembered for the boat journey, and the
boat journey deserves it, but the more instructive part is what preceded it:
eighteen months of decisions made in the knowledge that no one was coming.
When the ship was crushed in the Weddell Sea the party camped on drifting
floes, hauled boats across pressure ice until the effort proved futile,
drifted north for months on ice they could not steer, and finally sailed to
Elephant Island — a place with no shelter, no food store and no shipping.

From there the *James Caird* carried six men roughly eight hundred nautical miles to
South Georgia across the worst water in the world. Frank Worsley navigated it
with a sextant, a chronometer and perhaps four sun sights in sixteen days,
taken while a companion braced him and the horizon swung through thirty
degrees. The error in an unlucky sight would have carried them past the island
into the South Atlantic with no landfall ahead. They found it. Then three of
them crossed the unmapped interior mountains in thirty-six hours with fifty
feet of rope and screws driven into their boot soles, because the whaling
stations were on the far side.

None of that was luck, exactly. It was the residue of a very long series of
decisions about weight, about what to carry and what to abandon, made by
people who had already learned that the ice does not negotiate.
