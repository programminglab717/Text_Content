# The Craft of Cave Exploration

Somewhere within the first few hundred metres of any cave, usually at a bend,
the last of the daylight goes. Not dims — goes. After that the only light in
the system is the one you brought, and the quality of your day depends
entirely on decisions you made in a car park about batteries, spare bulbs and
how much food is in the bottom of your bag. Cavers talk about this moment
less than you would expect, because after the first dozen trips it stops
registering. What replaces it is an acute attention to logistics.

Cave exploration is unlike almost every other kind of exploration in one
respect: the ground is genuinely unknown, and it is unknown in a way that
satellites cannot fix. There is no aerial survey of a cave. Nobody has mapped
it from orbit. The only way to establish what is down there is for a person to
physically go and look, and then to come back and write it down accurately
enough that the next person can find the same place.

## Getting In and Getting Down

Entrances are unglamorous. Many are holes in a field with a steel lid and a
padlock, some are a slot behind a hedge, some are the mouth of a stream that
disappears into a hillside. The classification that matters underground is not
how a cave looks from outside but whether it is horizontal or vertical —
whether the passage wanders roughly level through a limestone bedding plane, or
whether it drops in a series of shafts.

Horizontal caving is mostly walking, stooping, crawling and the occasional
climb, and its difficulties are duration and constriction. Vertical caving
requires rope. For most of the twentieth century that meant flexible ladders
made of wire and aluminium rungs, lifelined from above — heavy, slow, and
requiring a team at the top of every pitch. The change came from American
cavers in the mid-century who worked out how to descend and ascend a single
fixed rope with mechanical devices, and Single Rope Technique is now close to
universal.

The mechanics are simple enough to explain and take a season to make fluent. A
descender — a rack of bars or a simple bobbin — threads the rope and converts
the descent into controlled friction. For going up, two toothed cams grip the
rope: one on a chest harness, one on a handle attached by a foot loop. You
stand up in the foot loop, the chest cam slides and then bites, you sit down on
it, and you have gained forty centimetres. Repeat several hundred times for a
long shaft. It is efficient, monotonous work, and it is the part of caving
that most resembles a gym.

## The Rope Is the Route

Rigging a pitch well is the central skill of vertical caving, and the reason
is friction. A rope running over a limestone edge under a loaded caver will
saw through itself faster than most people's intuition allows. So the rigger's
job is to keep the rope hanging free, in air, from top to bottom.

This is done with anchors placed down the shaft wall rather than only at the
top. A rebelay is a mid-shaft anchor where the rope is tied off and a new
length continues below, so a fall is limited to the short section you are on
and the rope below hangs clear of the wall. A deviation is lighter: a sling
that pulls the rope sideways a metre or so to clear a projection without
taking the caver's weight. Passing either one while hanging on the rope in the
dark, with water going down your neck, is a manoeuvre that gets practised on
trees at home for good reason — you must transfer your weight from one system
to another without ever being unattached.

Where fixed anchors do not exist, riggers use natural features: threads,
flakes, boulders that have plainly been where they are for ten thousand years.
The judgement involved in deciding whether a particular block qualifies is
learned largely by apprenticeship, which is why caving remains a club sport in
a way that hill walking never has been.

## Surveying, Because Nobody Else Will

A cave that has been visited but not surveyed is barely known. Passage
underground doubles back, loops, climbs and drops in ways that defeat memory,
and a verbal description of a junction is next to useless a decade later. So
every serious exploration project is also a survey project.

The traditional method is straightforward and tedious. Two people work a
series of stations, sighting a compass and a clinometer from one marked point
to the next and measuring the distance with a tape, while a third sketches
cross-sections and passage detail on waterproof paper. The data are reduced
later into a plan and an elevation. Modern teams use a laser rangefinder with
a built-in compass and inclinometer that records a shot in seconds, which has
roughly tripled the rate at which new passage can be properly recorded, but
the sketching still has to be done by hand by someone standing in the passage
looking at it.

Accuracy matters for reasons beyond tidiness. Surveys are what let cavers work
out that two separate systems are likely to connect — that a passage heading
north-east in one cave ends forty metres from a passage heading south-west in
another, and that a dig at the right spot might join them. Most of the great
long systems were assembled this way, by connection rather than single
discovery. The Mammoth Cave system in Kentucky, by a wide margin the longest
known cave on earth at well over six hundred kilometres of mapped passage,
grew largely through a sequence of connections between caves that had been
explored independently for generations.

## Water

Water made the cave and water still runs it. Limestone dissolves slowly in
weakly acidic rainwater, which is why nearly all large caves are in
carbonate rock, and the active streamway at the bottom of a system is usually
the place where that process is still happening.

Two consequences dominate practical caving. The first is flooding. A
streamway that is ankle-deep in dry weather can fill its passage completely
within an hour of heavy rain on the hill above, and the passage ceiling is the
ceiling. Cavers check forecasts obsessively, avoid certain systems entirely in
wet conditions, and identify in advance which chambers sit high enough to sit
out a flood pulse. Groups have waited a day and a half for water to drop.
Groups have also drowned.

The second is the sump: a section where the passage roof goes below the water
surface and the way on is entirely submerged. A sump ends exploration for
everyone who is not a cave diver, and cave diving is its own discipline with
its own hard-won rules. A continuous guideline is laid from open air and never
let go of. Gas is planned on a rule of thirds, a third in, a third out, a
third in reserve for the failure you have not had yet. Visibility, typically
good on the way in, can be reduced to nothing by silt stirred up by fins, in
which case the line is the only navigation you have. The flooded systems of
the Yucatán, where rainwater has hollowed out hundreds of kilometres of
passage beneath the limestone shelf, are explored almost entirely this way.

The 2018 rescue of a junior football team from a flooded cave in northern
Thailand made the constraints briefly legible to everyone. The boys were
several kilometres in, past multiple sumps, and the eventual solution — diving
each of them out sedated, on a guideline, in near-zero visibility — was
extraordinary precisely because every conventional option was unavailable.

## Cold, Time and the Difficulty of Rescue

A cave holds close to the mean annual temperature of the surface above it,
which in temperate latitudes means somewhere in the range of five to ten
degrees. That is not cold in air. It becomes very cold when you are wet,
immobile and in contact with rock, because water and stone strip heat from a
body far faster than air does. Hypothermia, not falling, is the most common
serious problem on a long trip, and the standard defences are a wetsuit or an
oversuit over fleece, eating regularly, and above all continuing to move.

Time behaves strangely underground. Without daylight cues most people badly
underestimate elapsed hours, and long exploratory trips in deep systems run to
twenty, thirty, occasionally more than forty hours. Deep expedition caves in
the Western Caucasus, where the deepest known systems descend more than two
kilometres below their entrances, are worked from underground camps: sleeping
bags, stoves and food hauled down and left in place, so that a team can spend
a week below without the cost of re-descending the entrance series every day.

The reason cavers are so conservative is that rescue is close to impossible in
any conventional sense. A helicopter cannot reach you. A stretcher cannot be
carried through a crawl that takes an unencumbered person twenty minutes on
their stomach. Getting an injured caver out of a deep system takes dozens of
volunteers, specialist hauling rigs on every pitch, and frequently more than a
day. This is why the culture puts such weight on self-reliance and on not
being the person who needs collecting — and why, in a sport with a reputation
for recklessness, the actual practice is unusually careful.
