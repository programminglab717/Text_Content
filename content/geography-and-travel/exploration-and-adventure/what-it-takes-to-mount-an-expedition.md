# What It Takes to Mount an Expedition

Before any expedition leaves, there is a floor. Usually a garage, sometimes a
rented lock-up near a container port, occasionally a university sports hall
borrowed for a fortnight in exchange for a lecture. Laid out across it is
everything the trip will consume, grouped by category and labelled in marker
pen, while somebody walks the aisles with a luggage scale and a laptop, weighing
each bag twice and writing the figure on the outside large enough that it will
still be readable after three weeks of rain. This is the least romantic part of
exploration and the part that most reliably decides whether the rest of it
happens at all.

## The Arithmetic Comes First

Every expedition begins as a multiplication problem. People times days gives
person-days, and person-days times a daily ration gives the size of the food
pile. Dried and packed with some discipline, a day's food for a working adult
lands somewhere near a kilogram — less if you are prepared to eat joylessly,
more if the work is cold, because a body hauling a sledge in serious cold burns
calories faster than almost any diet can replace. Polar travellers have long
pushed daily rations up towards five or six thousand calories and still come
home noticeably thinner than they left.

Fuel obeys the same logic and forgives less. Where the only available water is
snow, fuel is water, and melting snow is expensive in heat; a stove that runs
out on day forty of a fifty-day crossing has not inconvenienced anyone, it has
ended the trip. So the fuel figure gets calculated per person per day, then
padded, then rounded up, then checked against somebody else's numbers from a
comparable route. The same goes for batteries, water-treatment cartridges,
sunscreen, and the small unglamorous consumables that nobody thinks to count
until they are gone.

What makes this harder than simple multiplication is that the supplies have to
be carried, and the carrying consumes supplies. Go deep enough and you reach a
point where an extra day of travel requires more than a day's worth of food
just to move the food. Nineteenth and early twentieth-century polar parties
solved this by laying depots: a first push out from the base dropping caches at
intervals, a second push using those caches to reach further, and so on, so that
the final small party travelling at the sharp end is supported by an invisible
pyramid of earlier work. Himalayan siege-style climbing used the same structure
vertically, with camps instead of depots and porters instead of sledges. Alpine
style is the deliberate refusal of that pyramid — carry everything, move fast,
accept a much thinner margin — and the choice between the two is less about
ethics than about how long you intend to be committed.

## Paper Before Boots

Almost nowhere on Earth is legally unclaimed, and the permitting layer is
usually the longest lead item on the plan. A peak may require a climbing permit
from a ministry, a park entry fee, and a liaison officer whose salary, travel
and equipment you are expected to cover. Scientific work adds research permits,
which often require a local institutional partner named on the application, and
if anything biological is coming home there are export permits at one end and
import permits at the other, with the Convention on International Trade in
Endangered Species governing a wider range of material than most first-time
applicants expect. Some of these take months. Some are issued only by an office
that is open three mornings a week in a capital city several days from the
trailhead.

Getting equipment across a border is a parallel problem. Kit that is entering a
country temporarily and leaving again is not an import in spirit, but customs
officials deal in paperwork rather than spirit, and an expedition arriving with
sixty cases of unfamiliar hardware looks a great deal like a commercial
shipment. Carnets and temporary admission documents exist for exactly this, and
an agent who knows the port is worth more than any amount of correspondence
from abroad. Certain items — lithium batteries above a given capacity, stove
fuel, flares, ammunition, compressed gas, anything with a laser in it — attract
dangerous-goods rules that can make air freight impossible and sea freight slow.
Sea freight is cheap and takes weeks, and the standard mistake is to book the
flights first and then discover that the cargo cannot arrive before the team
does.

Local hiring is both a logistical necessity and the part of the plan with the
most ethical weight. In most of the world's mountain ranges and forests, the
people who make an expedition possible are porters, drivers, cooks, boatmen and
guides recruited locally, and the difference between a well-run trip and a
shabby one shows up in their terms of employment: load limits actually enforced,
boots and sleeping bags issued rather than assumed, insurance that pays out to a
family rather than existing on paper, wages agreed in advance and in writing.
The history of exploration is thick with expeditions that were celebrated at
home and remembered locally as something closer to an extraction, and reputations
now travel fast enough between valleys that a team which treated a porter badly
in one season will find recruitment harder in the next.

Money sits underneath all of it. A small expedition might run on savings and a
couple of grants from geographical societies or mountaineering foundations, most
of which award modest sums and expect a written report afterwards. Larger ones
depend on sponsorship, which means selling something — images, data, a film,
the appearance of a logo on a tent. The unwelcome truth about sponsorship is
that it converts a trip into a set of deliverables, and deliverables have
deadlines that do not care about weather. A team that has promised live
broadcast content from a summit has quietly given itself a reason to go up on a
day it should have stayed down.

## Choosing People, and Then Rehearsing

The selection question that matters is not who is strongest. It is who you can
still stand on week six, in a small tent, when the weather has been bad for
nine days and somebody has lost a glove. Long-duration teams tend to fail
socially before they fail physically, and experienced leaders screen for
temperament as hard as for skill: tolerance of boredom, willingness to be wrong
in front of others, the ability to do a tedious chore without commentary.

Skills need redundancy. One person with a broken wrist should not take the
expedition's only competent navigator, radio operator, or driver out of service.
Medical cover is a particular case — many teams carry drugs and kit well beyond
what an untrained person could use, backed by a remote physician reachable by
satellite who can authorise and talk somebody through what needs doing. That
relationship is arranged in advance, along with evacuation insurance, which is a
different product from ordinary travel cover and is priced on the assumption
that a helicopter and a hospital bed in a neighbouring country may both be
required. Knowing the actual ceiling of a policy — altitude limits, exclusions
for unroped glacier travel, whether it covers search as well as rescue — is part
of the planning, not part of the emergency.

Communications deserve a decision rather than a default. A satellite messenger
that sends short preset texts is cheap, light and adequate for a team whose
worst case is a helicopter pickup from open ground; a handheld satellite phone
or a small terminal that can carry a voice call and a weather file is a
different order of capability and cost, and has a different appetite for power.
Whatever is chosen has to be paired with a plan on the other end: a named person
at home who knows the itinerary, understands what silence means and what it does
not, and has the authority and the phone numbers to start a rescue without
waiting for a family committee. Schedule a check-in for every second evening and
a missed call becomes information. Schedule nothing, and a missed call becomes
an argument.

Then comes the shakedown. Almost every well-run expedition does a compressed
dress rehearsal somewhere accessible: a weekend on a local hill in bad weather
with the real loads, the real stoves, the real radios and the real people. The
purpose is to find the failures that only appear under use. Zips that seize
with grit, a fuel bottle whose thread does not match the pump, a satellite
messenger that cannot get a fix from inside a tent, two team members whose
systems for packing a pulk are quietly incompatible. Every one of these is
trivial at home and expensive three hundred kilometres from a road.

## Margins, Windows and the Decision to Go

Schedules for remote travel are built out of two different kinds of time. There
is the time the route takes when everything works, and there is float: spare
days deliberately inserted to absorb storms, illness, a closed border, a ferry
that runs weekly and has just left. Teams that plan without float find
themselves making decisions under a clock, which is the condition under which
most serious accidents are made. Teams with generous float usually come home
having used most of it and having enjoyed the trip more.

The weather window is the polar and high-altitude version of this. Forecasts for
remote regions are coarser than the ones people are used to at home, and the
real skill is not reading a forecast but knowing how much of it to believe and
how quickly you can retreat if it is wrong. Somebody should own the go or
no-go call explicitly, and the team should have agreed in advance — in a warm
room, weeks earlier — what conditions constitute a turnaround. Deciding that in
the moment, with the summit visible and the weather merely unpleasant, has
historically gone badly.

The last thing to organise is the ending. Expeditions have a strong tendency to
plan meticulously up to the objective and vaguely afterwards, as though the
return were an administrative formality. It is not. Exhausted people descending
in deteriorating weather with the pressure off account for a large share of
mountaineering fatalities, and the same slackening shows up in vehicle
accidents on the drive out and in the paperwork failures that strand a shipment
in a bonded warehouse for six months. The floor in the lock-up eventually has to
be filled again, in reverse, with everything that came back and a written
account of what did not.
