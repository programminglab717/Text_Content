# How Mountaineering Became a Sport

In August 1786 a Chamonix crystal hunter named Jacques Balmat and a local
doctor named Michel-Gabriel Paccard reached the summit of Mont Blanc. They
were not there for the view. A Genevan natural philosopher, Horace-Bénédict de
Saussure, had offered a reward for a route to the top years earlier, and his
interest was scientific — he wanted barometric readings from the highest point
in the Alps, and he wanted to know what the air was like up there. He made the
climb himself the following summer, carrying instruments, accompanied by
something close to a small procession of guides and porters.

That is the respectable origin story, and it is worth noticing how quickly it
stopped being true. Within two generations people were going up mountains with
no instruments at all, and the justification had shifted from measurement to
something nobody could quite articulate and everybody recognised.

## Peaks as Problems

For most of the preceding centuries, high mountains were obstacles, hazards or
the dwelling places of unpleasant things. Alpine villagers crossed passes
because they had to; they had little reason to climb summits and generally
regarded people who did as unwell. What changed in the late eighteenth and
early nineteenth centuries was partly Romanticism — the sublime became
fashionable, and the Alps became a destination for the kind of traveller who
wanted to be overwhelmed — and partly the arrival of a British leisured class
with time, money and a competitive instinct looking for somewhere to put it.

The conventional bracket for the Golden Age of alpinism runs from 1854, when
Alfred Wills climbed the Wetterhorn and wrote about it well, to 1865. In
that decade most of the major Alpine summits fell, very often to British
clients led by Swiss, French and Italian guides who did the actual work of
route-finding, step-cutting and rescue while receiving a fraction of the
credit. The Alpine Club was founded in London in 1857, the first organisation
of its kind anywhere, and it established a pattern that would repeat for a
century: the sport's institutions sat in the cities, while its competence sat
in the valleys.

The guides deserve better than they usually get. Men like Michel Croz, Melchior
Anderegg and Christian Almer were professionals with an empirical understanding
of snow, ice and rockfall built from a lifetime in the same few valleys. Cutting
a staircase up a steep ice slope with a long-handled axe, hundreds of steps at a
time, is exhausting, technical work, and it was theirs. The client carried less,
cut nothing, and wrote the book.

## The Matterhorn, and the Cost of Winning

The Golden Age ends where it does because of one day in July 1865. Edward
Whymper had failed on the Matterhorn repeatedly from the Italian side,
usually with the Valtournenche guide Jean-Antoine Carrel, who wanted the
mountain for Italy. Whymper crossed to Zermatt, assembled a party at short
notice, and went up the Hörnli ridge — which turned out to be far easier than
its profile suggested. Seven men reached the summit. On the descent the
youngest and least experienced slipped, pulled three others off with him, and
the rope between the upper and lower halves of the party broke. Four men died,
including Croz and Lord Francis Douglas.

The reaction was ferocious. Newspapers asked what the point of any of it was,
a question climbers have been answering unconvincingly ever since. But the
accident also marked a real transition. The obvious summits were gone. What
remained was harder routes to summits already climbed — ridges instead of
snow slopes, faces instead of ridges — and that reorientation is what turned
mountaineering into a sport with internal standards rather than a form of
exploration with a finite supply of objectives.

## Guideless, and Then Steeper

The so-called Silver Age belongs to climbers who began deliberately choosing
difficulty. A. F. Mummery is the emblematic figure: he climbed hard new lines,
argued for going without guides as a matter of principle, and observed with
some accuracy that every mountain passes through three stages in the public
mind — an inaccessible peak, the hardest climb in the Alps, and an easy day
for a lady. He disappeared on Nanga Parbat in 1895, the first serious attempt
on an eight-thousand-metre peak, decades before anyone had the equipment or
the physiological knowledge to survive one.

The Eastern Alps developed a distinct culture in the same period, centred on
steep limestone and on rock climbing as its own discipline rather than a means
of reaching a snowy top. That is where the first arguments about equipment
ethics started. Pitons — steel spikes hammered into cracks — made hard routes
survivable and also made them less hard, and climbers like Paul Preuss argued
that using them to make upward progress was a kind of cheating. The debate has
never really ended. Every generation since has fought some version of it about
pitons, bolts, fixed ropes, supplementary oxygen and helicopters.

Equipment, meanwhile, changed more in fifty years than in the previous
thousand. Nailed boots gave way to rubber lug soles in the 1930s. Crampons had
been redesigned earlier by Oscar Eckenstein into a ten-point frame that made
step-cutting largely unnecessary on moderate ice; the addition of forward-
pointing spikes in the 1930s meant a climber could walk up ice that had
previously required hours of chopping. The ice axe shortened. After the war,
nylon replaced hemp rope, which mattered enormously, because a hemp rope holding
a leader fall was a coin toss and everybody knew it.

Those changes produced the great north faces. The Matterhorn's north face fell
in 1931, the Grandes Jorasses over the following years, and the Eiger's
Nordwand — loose, cold, raked by stonefall, with a reputation earned by a
series of grim failures — in 1938 to an Austrian-German party. The politics
around those climbs in the 1930s were ugly and the nationalism was explicit,
which is part of why some of that history is told carefully now.

## The Big Mountains

The Himalaya and Karakoram absorbed the next phase, and they demanded a
different approach entirely. Above roughly eight thousand metres the body
cannot acclimatise; it degrades continuously, and the only question is how
fast. The response, through the 1950s, was siege tactics borrowed from
military logistics: large teams, fixed ropes, chains of stocked camps, and
supplementary oxygen, all to place two people on a summit for twenty minutes.
Annapurna fell in 1950 to a French party, the first eight-thousander climbed.
Everest went in 1953 to a large British expedition, with Edmund Hillary and
Tenzing Norgay on the summit and oxygen sets on their backs. K2 followed in
1954.

The interesting reversal came twenty years later. Reinhold Messner and Peter
Habeler climbed Everest without supplementary oxygen in 1978, which a good
deal of medical opinion had held to be impossible or fatal. Messner returned
in 1980 and did it alone, from the north, in the monsoon period, with almost
nothing. The argument he made — that a mountain climbed by siege has been
overwhelmed rather than climbed — carried the sport back toward the lightweight
alpine style that had been standard in the Alps for a century. Small teams,
no fixed ropes, everything carried, no line of retreat. It is far more
dangerous and it is now the prestige form.

## Back to the Rock

While all that was happening in the great ranges, the technical centre of
gravity had quietly migrated to a valley in California. Yosemite's granite
walls are a thousand metres of steep, featureless-looking stone, and climbing
them required inventing both hardware and a style of multi-day living on
vertical rock. El Capitan's Nose was first climbed in 1958 over an extended
siege; within a few years the same wall was going in days.

The Yosemite climbers then did something unusual: they noticed that
hammering pitons into the same cracks season after season was destroying the
routes, and they changed the rules. Aluminium wedges and cams that could be
slotted and removed by hand replaced steel spikes, a shift usually called the
clean climbing revolution, and it pushed the whole sport toward leaving no
trace. That ethic and the gear that came with it made free climbing — using
protection only to catch a fall, never to pull on — the standard measure of
difficulty. Sport climbing, bolted and gymnastic, followed in the 1980s, and
competition climbing grew out of that and eventually reached the Olympics,
about as far from Saussure's barometer as it is possible to get.

The other modern development is commercial. Guided ascents of Everest began in
earnest in the early 1990s and turned the highest mountain on earth into a
product with a price. Whether that is a corruption or simply the logical end
of a lineage that began with a wealthy Englishman paying a Chamonix guide to
cut his steps is a question the sport keeps declining to settle.
