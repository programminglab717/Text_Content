# The Long Practice of Overland Travel

At a border post in the Sahel the important object is not the vehicle. It is the
document wallet: a plastic folder holding a vehicle registration, an
international driving permit, a carnet with its counterfoils, photocopies of
everything in triplicate because the office has no photocopier, passport
photographs in various sizes, a pen that works, and small-denomination notes in
two currencies. The driver spends four hours moving between three windows in an
order nobody has written down. The truck sits in the sun. Somewhere around the
third hour it becomes clear that overland travel is not principally about
driving.

Going by land is the oldest way to cross a continent and the most awkward one to
arrange, which is exactly why people keep doing it. Flying resolves a journey
into two airports. Driving or riding or taking trains resolves it into every
kilometre in between, including the parts that are boring, and the boring parts
turn out to be where the sense of distance actually lives.

## The Trail That Closed

For roughly two decades after the early 1960s there was a functioning overland
corridor from Western Europe to South Asia that a young person with very little
money could travel. It ran through Istanbul, across Anatolia to Tehran, east
through Mashhad into Afghanistan by way of Herat, Kandahar and Kabul, over the
Khyber into Pakistan, and on through Lahore to Delhi and Kathmandu. Some people
drove it in ageing vans. Others rode local buses in stages. British companies
ran converted lorries with bench seats and a roof rack of jerry cans, taking
paying passengers overland in convoy and camping along the way.

What made the route work was a specific alignment of conditions: cheap fuel,
visas that could be obtained at borders or with little fuss, governments that
were indifferent to a trickle of shabby foreigners, and a string of towns where
a bed cost almost nothing. The corridor also carried a good deal of ordinary
commerce, so a broken van could be welded at a roadside workshop by people who
fixed trucks every day.

It closed quickly and for reasons that had nothing to do with travellers. The
Iranian revolution in 1979 and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in the same
year removed two consecutive countries from the map of the possible, and the
through-route has never reopened in the same form. That ending is the recurring
lesson of overland travel: routes are contingent. They depend on political
arrangements that can dissolve in a week, and the window in which any particular
crossing is straightforward tends to be shorter than anyone travelling it
assumes.

## Paper, Rubber and Diesel

The carnet de passages en douane is the single document that defines serious
overland motoring. It is a customs guarantee — an undertaking, backed by a bond
or bank security lodged with a motoring organisation at home, that the vehicle
will not be sold in a country it merely passes through. Enter a country, get a
counterfoil stamped; leave, get the matching half stamped. Fail to get the exit
stamp and the guarantee can be claimed against, which is why experienced drivers
are pedantic to the point of rudeness about watching the stamp go into the book.
The required security is calculated from the vehicle's value and the tariffs of
the strictest country on the itinerary, which is one reason people take older,
cheaper vehicles than they could afford.

Insurance is bought and rebought at frontiers, since domestic policies rarely
travel; regional schemes exist in some places to cover a group of neighbouring
states with one certificate. Visas dictate routing more than roads do, because
many are valid only for a limited period from issue and can only be obtained
from an embassy in a particular city, so the itinerary becomes a chain of
applications, each collected at the right moment in the right capital. A single
country refusing transit can force a detour of thousands of kilometres or a sea
crossing.

Vehicle choice is a long-running argument with two defensible positions. One
camp wants simplicity: an older diesel with mechanical injection, no engine
management to speak of, and a model line so widely used that a part can be found
in any market town. Fuel quality is the practical driver here — sulphur content
and general filth in diesel across much of Africa and Central Asia will punish a
modern high-pressure common-rail system in ways that a 1990s engine shrugs off.
The other camp points out that modern vehicles simply break less often, and that
an unbreakable vehicle needs no local mechanic. Both are right, and the
compromise most long-distance travellers reach is an older, ubiquitous
four-wheel-drive, carrying two spare wheels, spare belts, filters, hoses, fluids
and a serviceable jack, driven slowly.

Self-sufficiency is measured in range rather than in gear. The two numbers that
shape a route are how far the vehicle will go between fuel stops and how long
its occupants can go between water sources, and long-range tanks and jerry cans
exist to stretch the first while a filter and a decent tank stretch the second.
Remote stretches of Africa, Central Asia and Australia have fuel gaps measured
in hundreds of kilometres, sometimes with a pump that turns out to be dry, and
prudent drivers buy fuel when it is available rather than when it is needed.
Where you sleep follows from the same logic: a rooftop tent gets you off the
ground and out of the mud in ten minutes but makes a night move awkward, while a
ground tent is cheaper and quieter and requires somewhere flat. The universal
etiquette of wild camping is to ask. In most rural places all land belongs to
somebody, permission is given readily when sought, and the difference between a
guest and a trespasser is a conversation lasting two minutes.

Because that is the other half of it. Overlanders talk about daily distance the
way sailors talk about noon-to-noon runs, and the honest figure on rough roads
is modest — a few hundred kilometres is a full day, and a border crossing
consumes most of one. Budget a maintenance day every week or so, expect the
schedule to be set by the worst road rather than the best, and the whole rhythm
of the trip changes from covering ground to living on the move.

## The Gaps

No overland route is continuous, and the interruptions are informative.

The Pan-American Highway runs, in principle, from northern Alaska to southern
Chile and Argentina, and it has exactly one break: the Darién Gap, a stretch of
swamp, river and rainforest on the Panama–Colombia frontier where no road has
ever been built. The reasons are a mixture of terrain, cost, environmental
objection, concern about the northward spread of livestock disease, and the
security situation in the region. Vehicles heading south are therefore loaded
into shipping containers or onto roll-on ferries at a Panamanian port and
collected in Colombia, which is expensive, slow and a rite of passage. The
Darién is a reminder that a road is a political decision as much as an
engineering one.

Rail has its own discontinuities. The Trans-Siberian and its branches make it
possible to cross Eurasia by train, but Russian and Chinese railways use
different track gauges, so at the frontier the carriages are lifted and their
bogies swapped in a shed in the middle of the night — passengers sometimes still
aboard, listening to their coach being jacked into the air. Africa's Cairo to
Cape Town line has historically depended on ferries and on whichever borders
happened to be open in a given decade, and the practical route between Egypt and
Sudan has shifted with politics more than with roads.

Sea crossings sit inside almost every long overland itinerary, which produces a
small definitional argument that overlanders enjoy having. Most settle on a rule
that water is permitted where the land genuinely ends or where no legal crossing
exists, and that flying over a country you could have driven through is the only
real failure.

Money on the road behaves differently from money at home. Large parts of a long
overland route run through cash economies where a card is useless and the
nearest working cash machine is a day away, so travellers carry a reserve of
hard currency — clean, unmarked notes, since worn or defaced ones are refused
outright in some countries — hidden in more than one place. Official and street
exchange rates diverge in states with currency controls, which creates both an
opportunity and a straightforward way to be robbed or arrested. Border fixers,
unofficial fees and the slow negotiation over what is and is not a real charge
are a permanent feature, and the accumulated advice from people who travel these
roads is consistent: stay polite, stay patient, ask for a receipt, and treat
every hour spent in an office as part of the journey rather than an interruption
to it.

## Why Keep Doing It

The reasons people give are usually practical and usually not the real ones.
Cost is offered, though a year of vehicle maintenance, fuel, visas and shipping
is not cheap. Carbon is offered, which holds up better for buses and trains than
for a heavy diesel truck carrying two people.

What is actually on offer is gradient. Landscape changes continuously rather
than in cuts, so the shift from oak to olive to date palm, or from steppe to
desert, happens at a speed a person can register. Language does the same thing,
sliding through dialects rather than jumping between them. So does architecture,
bread, the shape of roofs, the way people greet a stranger. You acquire the
geography of a continent physically, as a sequence, and it stays acquired in a
way that arrival by air does not manage.

The other thing on offer is dependence. A traveller who breaks down eighty
kilometres from the nearest town has to ask for help, and asking for help is the
fastest route into somebody's actual life that exists. Most overlanders'
strongest memories are of a mechanic in a shed with a hand-cranked drill, a
family who fed them while the welding cooled, a police officer who waved the
paperwork away. Those encounters are not incidental to the journey. They are
produced by the journey's inefficiency, which is the thing that flying is
designed to eliminate.

Trips end for unglamorous reasons — a visa refused, money running out, a war
closing a frontier, a gearbox that finally goes in a place with no gearboxes.
Very few end at the destination written on the map at the start, and the people
who have done it longest tend to say that this hardly matters, since the point
was never the far end of the line.
