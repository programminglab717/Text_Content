# The Real Priorities of Wilderness Survival

The moment almost always arrives quietly. You are on a trail you have walked
before, or a variant of it, and the light is going, and you come to a junction
that you do not remember. Neither branch looks wrong. You take one, walk for
fifteen minutes, and the ground begins sloping in a direction that makes no
sense. There is no drama in this. Nobody has fallen off anything. But the
situation you are now in is the situation that rescue teams spend most of their
callouts resolving, and how the next hour goes depends far more on what you do
with your head than on anything in your pack.

Survival instruction tends to be sold backwards, with fire-by-friction and
snare-building at the front and the dull essentials buried behind them. The
ordering that actually matters is closer to the old rule of threes: roughly
three minutes without breathable air, three hours without shelter in hostile
conditions, three days without water, three weeks without food. The numbers are
crude, and the boundaries move with temperature, exertion and individual
physiology. As a ranking of what will kill you first, though, it is close enough
to be useful, and it says something uncomfortable — that the skills people most
want to learn address the threat that is least likely to matter.

## Cold Is the Thing

In temperate country, exposure kills more lost walkers than anything else, and
it does so at temperatures people find unthreatening. Hypothermia is not an
arctic phenomenon. It is a wet, windy, ten-degree afternoon combined with a
cotton shirt and a person who has stopped moving. Water conducts heat away from
skin many times faster than still air does, and wind strips the thin insulating
layer that clothing holds against the body, so the combination of damp and
breeze on a mild day removes heat at a rate the body cannot match with
shivering alone.

The dangerous part is what it does to judgement. Core temperature drops a couple
of degrees and fine motor control goes first — the fumbles, in the instructors'
shorthand, along with the stumbles, mumbles and grumbles. Fingers stop being
able to work a zip, a lighter, a knot. Then comes slurred speech, apathy,
irritability, and a strange inability to see the obvious. People die with a
bivvy bag in the rucksack they were too cold to open. The practical conclusion
is that you must act on cold while you are still competent, which means putting
on the extra layer before you feel you need it, eating before you feel hungry,
and stopping to build shelter while there is still daylight and dexterity to do
it with.

The inverse problem behaves differently and is often handled worse. In desert
and hot country the threat is not a slow drain but a runaway: sweating cools the
body only while there is water to sweat, and once output exceeds intake the core
temperature climbs. Heat exhaustion announces itself with nausea, headache,
clammy skin and a thumping pulse and is recoverable with shade, rest and fluids.
Heat stroke is a genuine emergency, and the sign people wait for — the classic
teaching that the skin goes hot and dry — is unreliable; confusion, aggression
or collapse in somebody who has been working in heat should be treated as heat
stroke whether or not they are still sweating. The counterintuitive discipline
in hot deserts is to stop moving. Walking at midday converts water into distance
at a terrible exchange rate, and the survival literature is full of people found
a short distance from a vehicle they should never have left. Shade, even
improvised from a space blanket propped on sticks with an air gap beneath it,
and travel confined to the cool hours, stretch a fixed quantity of water much
further than willpower does.

Shelter, in the emergency sense, is not a structure. It is the elimination of
three heat losses: wind, wet, and conduction into the ground. The last of these
is the one beginners neglect. Lying directly on cold earth drains warmth
astonishingly fast, and a thick mattress of dry leaves, bracken or conifer
boughs underneath you is worth more than an elaborate roof above. A debris
shelter built properly is essentially a sleeping bag made of forest floor:
a low ridgepole, ribs leant against it, and then armfuls of dead leaves piled on
until the thickness is measured in forearms rather than fingers, with the
interior stuffed full so that there is no air space to heat. It takes a couple
of hours of steady work, which is another reason to start early. A plastic
survival bag or a tarp does most of the same job in three minutes, which is why
one lives permanently in the bottom of a sensible daypack.

## Fire, Water, and the Order of Operations

Fire is the skill most romanticised and most often misunderstood. The difficult
part is almost never ignition; in wet conditions it is fuel preparation. A
reliable fire is built as a graded sequence — something that catches from a
spark, then material the thickness of a pencil lead, then pencils, then thumbs,
then wrists — and each stage must be fully assembled and within arm's reach
before the first one is lit. Rain complicates this less than people assume,
because the inside of dead standing wood stays dry when the forest floor is
sodden. Snap dead twigs off the underside of conifer branches rather than
picking them up off the ground. Split a wrist-thick piece of standing deadwood
and shave feather sticks from its dry core. Birch bark lights wet; so does resin
from a wounded pine. Carrying a small quantity of artificial tinder — cotton
wool worked with petroleum jelly, a waxed firelighter, a strip of inner tube —
removes almost all of the difficulty, and there is no virtue in refusing it.

Water is the next tier down, and the calculation around it is more nuanced than
the usual advice suggests. Untreated surface water in most of the world carries
some risk of giardia, cryptosporidium, bacteria or viruses, and the standard
responses are boiling, filtration, or chemical treatment with chlorine dioxide
or iodine. Boiling is the most robust: bringing water to a rolling boil is
sufficient at any altitude a walker is likely to reach, and the extra minutes
often recommended add little. Filters handle protozoa and bacteria but not
viruses unless they are very fine. Chemicals work slowly in cold water and
struggle against cryptosporidium. The nuance is this: waterborne illness takes
days to develop and is usually survivable, while dehydration degrades your
decision-making within hours. Somebody who is genuinely running dry, a day from
help, with no means of treatment, is generally better off drinking from the
clearest running source available and accepting the consequences later.

Food comes last, and its ranking is frequently ignored because hunger is loud.
A healthy adult can go a remarkably long time without eating while remaining
capable of walking out. Foraging and trapping, meanwhile, are poor economics in
a short emergency: a snare line takes hours to set and may catch nothing for
days, and identifying edible plants under stress, out of season, in unfamiliar
country, is how people poison themselves. In an incident measured in days rather
than weeks, calories are a comfort problem, not a survival one.

## Staying Found, and Being Seen

Most of this is avoidable, and the avoidance is boring. Leave a written plan
with somebody who will act on it, including where you parked and when to raise
the alarm. Set a turnaround time and obey it. Carry the small unglamorous
items — map and compass with the competence to use them, headtorch, spare
layer, survival bag, whistle, lighter, a little food — which together weigh
less than a litre of water and change the character of a bad night entirely.

When you are already lost, the first move is to stop. The standard mnemonic is
STOP: stop, think, observe, plan. Its value is that it interrupts the specific
failure mode that gets people into trouble, which is walking fast while
reasoning badly. There is a well-documented tendency to bend the map — to
reinterpret the landscape so that it agrees with where you believe you are,
explaining away a river on the wrong side or a ridge that should not be there.
Once that begins, every additional minute of walking makes the problem worse,
because you are moving away from your last known position and enlarging the
search area for the people who will eventually come looking.

There is also a strong pull downhill, into valleys and drainages, on the
reasonable-sounding theory that water leads to civilisation. In gentle country
it sometimes does. In steep, forested or canyon terrain it leads into thick
vegetation, cliff bands and cold air, out of sight of any aircraft and off every
path anyone would search. Searchers generally have more success on ridgelines
and in open ground, which is also where you can see and be seen.

Being found is an active skill. Three of anything — three whistle blasts, three
fires, three flashes — reads as distress almost everywhere. A whistle carries
far further than a voice and does not wear out the lungs. A signal mirror aimed
at an aircraft is visible at extraordinary distances on a sunny day, and a
mobile phone will sometimes connect to emergency services on another network
even where it shows no bars of its own; switching it off between attempts to
save the battery is usually smarter than leaving it hunting for signal. On the
ground, the trick is contrast and geometry: nature produces very few straight
lines, so a large X or a straight line of dark branches on a pale surface, or
brightly coloured kit spread out in an open clearing, registers to a spotter in
a way that a person crouched under a tree never will.

The last piece is temperament, and it is the one that cannot be bought. Accounts
of survival incidents return over and over to the same pattern: the people who
came through were not necessarily the fittest or the best equipped, but the ones
who accepted the situation quickly, gave themselves small achievable tasks, and
kept working. Panic burns energy, produces sweat that will chill you later, and
generates decisions that make sense for about four minutes. Sitting down,
admitting out loud that you are lost, and then spending the remaining daylight
gathering leaves is an unheroic response that works.
