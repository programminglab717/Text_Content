# The Making of a Long-Distance Trail

The white blaze on the Appalachian Trail is a painted rectangle about the size
of a playing card, applied to a tree trunk or a rock face at eye height, spaced
so that from one you can usually see the next. Somebody walked to that tree
carrying a can of paint and a brush. Somebody else will walk back in a few years
when the bark has grown and the paint has faded. Multiply that by a corridor
running from Georgia to Maine and you have the basic fact about long trails
that maps obscure: they are not geographic features. They are maintained
objects, kept in existence by continuous small labor, and they disappear within
a decade of being left alone.

## An Idea Before a Path

The proposal that became the Appalachian Trail was published in 1921 by Benton
MacKaye, a forester with regional-planning instincts, and it was not really a
proposal for a hiking route. MacKaye imagined a chain of work camps, farms, and
small communities strung along the Appalachian crest, a refuge from industrial
cities where people might do something more useful with themselves. The
footpath was the connective tissue of the scheme. What survived was the
connective tissue and nothing else.

Turning it into a walkable line took a different temperament, and it arrived in
the person of Myron Avery, a lawyer who pushed a measuring wheel over most of
the route and organized the volunteer clubs that cut it. The trail was declared
continuous in the late 1930s, though "continuous" at the time meant a great deal
of road walking and a route that wandered wherever a landowner was willing. The
Pacific Crest Trail had a parallel history a few years behind, sketched in the
1930s out of existing Sierra and Cascade routes. Federal recognition for both
came only in 1968, with legislation that created a national system of scenic
trails and, more importantly, a mechanism for buying the ground under them.

Europe's long trails descend from different ancestors. The French network of
grandes randonnées, marked with a red stripe over a white one, was assembled
after the Second World War largely out of old agricultural and pilgrimage paths,
which is why it feels less like wilderness and more like a way of getting from
one village to the next on foot. Britain's Pennine Way, opened in 1965, came out
of a decades-long fight over access to private moorland, including a mass
trespass on Kinder Scout in the 1930s that has taken on more significance in
retrospect than it had at the time. The Camino de Santiago is a medieval
pilgrimage that emptied out and then refilled, revived in the late twentieth
century with a scheme of yellow arrows painted by a parish priest and his
successors. New Zealand's Te Araroa, stitched together from existing tracks,
beaches, and road sections, was only declared complete in 2011.

## The Line on the Ground

A trail that runs straight down a slope is a ditch waiting to happen. Water
follows the tread because the tread is the lowest, most compacted, least
vegetated line available, and once a channel forms it deepens every storm until
the trail is a knee-deep trench of exposed roots and the walkers have moved
sideways onto fresh ground, widening the scar. Most of the badly eroded trails
people encounter are old routes that were never designed at all — herd paths,
logging skids, or straight-line surveyor's routes adopted because they were
already there.

Designed trail avoids this with a handful of rules that sound trivial and are
not. The most important is the relationship between the grade of the trail and
the grade of the hillside it crosses: keep the trail's climb well under half the
sideslope, roughly speaking, and water crossing the tread has somewhere to go
besides down it. Average grades are kept in the neighborhood of ten percent,
with steeper pitches short. The tread is cut into the hillside as a bench rather
than built up on fill, and it is tilted slightly outward so sheet flow runs
across and off rather than pooling. Every so often the trail dips and rises
again — a grade reversal — forcing any water that has gotten onto the tread to
leave at the low point rather than accumulating for the next two hundred meters.

Where the ground is wet, the route is turnpiked: parallel retaining logs or
stones filled with imported mineral soil to lift the tread above the water
table. Where the slope is too steep for anything else, crews build rock steps,
which is the most expensive kind of trail work there is, hauling and setting
stones that weigh as much as the people setting them. Switchbacks get the trail
up a face that would otherwise be a fall-line scramble, and they only survive if
the corners are built on ground that discourages cutting — a steep landing, a
rock outcrop, deliberate brush. A cut switchback establishes a fall-line
drainage across every turn below it, which is why maintainers take it
personally.

## Land, Not Terrain

The hardest part of building a thousand-mile trail is usually not the digging.
It is assembling the right to be there. For decades the Appalachian Trail ran
across private land by handshake, and a route that existed by permission could
be withdrawn by permission; the trail lost sections to development, logging, and
irritated owners, and was patched onto road shoulders. The corridor was
eventually secured through a long acquisition program of purchase and easement
that took the better part of thirty years, parcel by parcel, and it is that
legal ribbon, rather than the paint on the trees, that made the route permanent.
The Pacific Crest Trail has its own version of this problem in the checkerboard
of alternating public and private sections left over from nineteenth-century
railroad land grants.

Countries with strong access traditions need less of this scaffolding. Where a
right to roam exists over open country, as in much of Scandinavia and in
Scotland, a long route is more a recommendation than a legal creation. England
and Wales sit between the two systems, with a dense inherited network of public
rights of way — footpaths that cross fields and farmyards because people have
walked them for centuries — and a mapping process that turned custom into
recorded right, sometimes in the face of stiles that mysteriously fell into
disrepair.

## Walking It

The people who walk an entire long trail in one season develop a strange,
regular life. Distance settles somewhere in the range of twenty to twenty-five
miles a day once the body adapts, which takes a few weeks and costs most people
their first pair of shoes. Food runs four to six days between towns, resupplied
by shopping at whatever store the trail town has or by mailing boxes ahead to
post offices. Gear gets ruthlessly reduced; a pack that seemed reasonable at the
southern terminus is noticeably lighter by the third state, having shed a camp
chair, a second knife, and most of a first-aid kit.

Start dates cluster, because the weather at both ends of a long trail sets a
window: leave too early and you hit deep snow in the high country, leave too
late and winter closes the far end before you arrive. The result is a moving
bubble of hikers who leave within a few weeks of each other and travel in loose
company for months, splitting and recombining at towns. Trail names get handed
out by other hikers, usually for something embarrassing. Strangers who leave
coolers of drinks at road crossings acquire the title of trail angels. Most
people who set out to walk the whole thing do not finish, and the reasons are
rarely dramatic — a knee, a family emergency, money, or simply the recognition
somewhere in the long middle section that the novelty has worn off and the
remaining thousand miles are a job.

## Wear

Popularity is the current threat to almost every famous long trail. Books,
films, and social media have loaded the well-known routes with far more feet
than the tread was designed for, concentrated into the same start windows.
Campsites spread outward into the vegetation, privies overflow, water sources
get fouled, and the most photographed viewpoints turn into bare rock and dust.
Managing agencies respond with permit quotas and staggered start dates, which
work but feel to many walkers like a contradiction of the thing they came for.

Meanwhile the maintenance never stops. Every spring, volunteers walk their
assigned sections with saws, clearing blowdowns from winter storms, brushing
back growth, cleaning the drainage structures that silt up, rebuilding steps
frost has heaved, and repainting blazes. A section of trail left unwalked and
uncut for five years is legible only to someone who knows what to look for; at
ten it is gone. The trail is less a thing that exists than an activity that
keeps being performed, and the painted rectangle on the tree is the receipt.
