# Ocean Rowing and the Small-Boat Voyage

In the summer of 1896 two Norwegian-American clam fishermen, George Harbo and
Frank Samuelsen, pushed off from New York in an open boat about eighteen feet
long and rowed until they reached the Isles of Scilly. It took them around two
months. The boat had no cabin, no shelter and no means of righting itself;
they were swamped and rolled, and got back in. They did it for prize money
that mostly did not materialise, and for more than seventy years nobody
repeated the crossing.

That voyage is the origin point of a strange, small discipline. Ocean rowing
is not a survival exercise, not usually a rescue, and not a shortcut to
anywhere. It is a deliberate choice to cross a thousand miles of water at
roughly two and a half knots using the least efficient prime mover available,
and it has produced some of the most carefully engineered small craft afloat.

## The Ancestor Cases

The technique of getting a very small boat a very long way was first developed
under duress. The most extreme demonstration is William Bligh's, in 1789.
Turned out of the *Bounty* into a twenty-three-foot open launch with eighteen
men and a few days of provisions, Bligh navigated roughly three and a half
thousand nautical miles to Timor in about seven weeks, by dead reckoning,
without a chart of most of it, rationing food by the ounce and keeping a
running survey of the Great Barrier Reef as he went. Whatever else is true of
Bligh, that passage is among the finest pieces of practical seamanship on
record, and its lessons — freeboard is everything, water discipline is
everything, a captain who lets the rationing slip is finished — were absorbed
by everyone who came after.

Shackleton's boat journey from Elephant Island in 1916 is the other canonical
case, and the interesting part from a boat's point of view is the
modification. The ship's carpenter raised the gunwales of the
twenty-two-foot whaler, built a rough deck over most of her out of sledge
runners, packing-case lids and canvas, and sealed it with oil paint and seal
blood. Something like a ton of shingle went in as ballast. The result was
effectively a decked, self-draining boat rather than an open one, which is
the difference between a craft that can take a Southern Ocean sea across the
deck and one that fills and goes down.

## What an Ocean Rowing Boat Actually Is

A modern ocean rowing boat is a direct descendant of that improvisation, with
the improvisation taken out. It is typically six to eight metres long, built
in composite, and shaped around a single design goal: to come back upright
after being rolled.

That is achieved less by cleverness than by geometry. Most of the hull volume
is sealed into watertight cabins — a sleeping cabin at one end, a smaller
compartment at the other for gear and batteries — while the rowing position in
the middle is an open, self-draining well. Ballast sits low, often as water
tanks in the bilge. Roll the boat and the enclosed air at the extremities
wants to be on top while the ballast wants to be at the bottom, so the boat
completes the roll and pops up, usually within a few seconds. Builders
demonstrate this by inverting a finished hull in a harbour and letting go.

The condition attached to all of it is that the hatch must be shut. A cabin
hatch left open during a capsize floods the compartment that makes the
self-righting work, and a flooded ocean rowing boat does not recover. This is
why crews close the hatch behind them every single time, in fair weather, at
three in the morning, on the four hundredth occasion, and why that habit is
drilled harder than any rowing technique.

The rest of the fit-out follows from the fact that nothing can be resupplied.
Solar panels on the cabin roofs charge a battery bank that runs navigation
lights, an autopilot, satellite communications and — the item that changed the
sport more than any other — an electric reverse-osmosis watermaker. Before
watermakers, a crew had to carry every litre it would drink, which meant
carrying a large part of its own displacement in ballast that got lighter as
the food ran out. Now a few litres a day are pressed out of the sea, with a
manual pump aboard as the backup for when the electrics fail, which they do.

There is also a sea anchor, and it does more work than it gets credit for.
When the wind turns against the boat, rowing becomes pointless — two knots of
boat speed cannot fight a contrary wind on a hull with that much windage — and
the choice is to lose miles or stop. A parachute anchor streamed on a long
line from the bow holds the boat head to the weather and nearly stationary,
while the crew shuts the hatches and waits. Crews have spent a week on the
anchor. It is the single most demoralising piece of equipment on board and the
one nobody leaves ashore.

## Two Hours On, Two Hours Off

Most crossings are rowed continuously, around the clock, with a shift pattern
of roughly two hours rowing and two hours resting. A pair rows alternately; a
four rows in shifts of two. Nobody sleeps for more than about ninety minutes
at a stretch from the day they leave to the day they land, which on a trade
wind Atlantic crossing means a month to seven weeks of fragmented sleep, and
considerably longer on the Pacific.

The classic Atlantic route runs from the Canaries south-west into the trades
and across to the Caribbean, which is the same logic Columbus used and the
same reason the route is rowed in that direction rather than the other. A row
against the prevailing wind and current is a different order of problem, which
is why east-to-west Atlantic crossings are now almost routine in a racing
sense while the Pacific and the Indian Ocean remain properly serious.

Solo rowers face the sleep question in its sharpest form. There is nobody to
keep watch, so a solo boat crossing a shipping lane is relying on an AIS
transponder to announce itself to bridge watchkeepers, plus a radar reflector
and a very great deal of hope. A rowing boat presents almost no radar target;
it sits low, it is made of materials that reflect poorly, and it disappears
entirely between swells.

## The Body as the Engine

Rowing ten or more hours a day for weeks burns several thousand calories more
than an ordinary diet supplies, and no realistic quantity of freeze-dried food
closes the gap. Crews load rations well above what looks sensible and still
finish lighter than they started, sometimes dramatically so. The practical
consequence is that the cooking — boiling water on a small gas stove in a
rolling well — has to be done religiously, because a skipped meal is a deficit
that cannot be repaid later.

The injuries are unglamorous and predictable. Salt water plus constant
pressure produces sores on the parts of the body in contact with the seat,
which is why rowers work in loose clothing or nothing at all and treat the
seat like a medical surface. Hands blister, then crack, then stop healing in
the permanently damp environment. Tendons in the wrists and forearms inflame.
Backs go. And sleep deprivation, layered over all of it for weeks, degrades
judgement in ways the sufferer is by definition poorly placed to notice — the
reason experienced crews write down their decision rules before departure and
then follow them rather than reasoning afresh at four in the morning.

## The Same Problem, Under Sail

Rowing is the pure case, but the small-boat voyage has a much wider tradition,
and the physics that keeps a rowing boat alive keeps the others alive too. A
light boat is not fighting the sea; it rises to it. What kills small craft is
not wave height but wave steepness — a breaking crest that arrives faster than
the hull can lift, rolls the boat, and takes the rig or the crew with it.
Nearly every heavy-weather tactic in small-boat seamanship, from streaming
warps astern to lying to a drogue to actively steering across the face of a
sea, is an attempt to avoid presenting the boat beam-on to something that is
breaking.

Joshua Slocum demonstrated the possibilities at the end of the nineteenth
century by rebuilding a derelict oyster sloop and sailing her alone around the
world, balancing the rig so carefully that she would hold a course for days
while he read. H. W. Tilman, after a mountaineering career, bought a series of
elderly Bristol Channel pilot cutters — working boats, built heavy for the
job — and used them to reach Greenland and Patagonia well into his seventies.
Frank Dye sailed an open sixteen-foot dinghy from Scotland to Iceland and
Norway, capsized repeatedly in a North Sea gale, and righted and carried on
each time.

And the whole idea is far older than any of them. The Polynesian settlement of
the Pacific was accomplished in double-hulled sailing canoes over open water
without instruments of any kind, using a memorised star compass, the
interference patterns of ocean swells felt through the hull, and the flight
lines of land-finding birds. When a replica canoe sailed from Hawaii to Tahiti
in the 1970s navigated that way, by a navigator from the Caroline Islands who
had inherited the tradition intact, it settled an argument that European
scholarship had kept open for two centuries.
