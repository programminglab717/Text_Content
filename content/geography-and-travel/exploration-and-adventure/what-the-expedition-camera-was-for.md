# What the Expedition Camera Was For

On the ice of the Weddell Sea, with the Endurance crushed and the party facing
a march hauling boats, Frank Hurley went down into the sinking ship's flooded
darkroom and came back with his sealed tins of exposed glass plates. He and
Shackleton then sat on the floe and went through them, choosing a few dozen to
carry and smashing the rest on the spot so that Hurley could not be tempted to
go back for them. It is the most honest image in the history of expedition
photography: a man destroying two-thirds of his own work because the pictures
weighed too much, and understanding that the surviving fraction would have to do
the job of all of it.

The job was never simply to record. Photographs made in the field were
evidence, publicity, fundraising material, and scientific data, usually all at
once, and the technical history of the expedition camera is inseparable from
what its owners needed the pictures to accomplish when they got home.

## A Darkroom You Have to Carry

For the first three decades of photography, taking a camera into the field
meant taking a chemistry set. The wet collodion process, dominant from the
1850s through the 1870s, required that a glass plate be coated with a syrupy
collodion mixture, sensitized in a silver nitrate bath, exposed while still
damp, and developed within roughly ten minutes — because once the collodion
dried, it stopped working. Every element of that sequence had to travel with the
photographer and be functional wherever the picture was to be taken.

Roger Fenton solved it in the Crimea with a converted wine merchant's van,
which doubled as a darkroom and made him conspicuous enough to be shot at. The
American survey photographers of the 1860s and 1870s hauled the same
arrangement through the desert. Timothy O'Sullivan worked out of a mule-drawn
darkroom wagon in Nevada, coating plates in heat that dried the collodion before
he could get it to the camera, and in dust that settled into the emulsion. A
photographer in that period who wanted a picture from the top of a ridge had to
get the entire laboratory to the top of the ridge.

The most consequential example is William Henry Jackson, who accompanied the
Hayden survey into the Yellowstone country in 1871 with a pack train and plates
large enough that the negatives themselves were the finished picture — contact
printed, since enlargement was impractical. The prints he brought back, shown in
Washington alongside Thomas Moran's watercolors, gave legislators something no
written report could: proof that the geysers and terraces described by earlier
travelers were not tall tales. Yellowstone was set aside as a national park the
following year. The photograph as an argument about land policy arrived almost
as early as the photograph itself.

## Dry Plates and the Portable Sublime

The gelatin dry plate, commercially practical from the late 1870s, severed the
photographer from the darkroom. Plates could be bought ready-made, carried for
months, exposed, and developed on return. They were also far more sensitive,
which shortened exposures from many seconds to a fraction of one and made
handheld photography possible. Roll film and simple box cameras followed within
a decade, and photography became something an ordinary member of an expedition
could do rather than a specialist trade requiring a wagon.

The immediate beneficiaries were mountaineers. Vittorio Sella carried
large-format equipment onto glaciers in the Caucasus, the Karakoram, and the
Alaskan ranges in the 1880s and 1890s, at a time when the outfit was still
heavy enough to require porters and enough discipline to expose one sheet
properly rather than a dozen hopefully. His panoramas are still consulted,
partly for their extraordinary detail and partly because he recorded his
viewpoints precisely enough that the same ice can be found and rephotographed a
century later.

Getting those pictures in front of an audience was a separate problem, and for
most of the nineteenth century the audience never saw a photograph at all. Until
halftone printing matured in the 1880s and 1890s, a photograph could not be
printed on the same press as type. It had to be copied by hand onto a wood block
by an engraver, who made decisions about contrast, detail, and occasionally
content along the way. The caption "from a photograph" in an illustrated weekly
signals a chain of custody, not a direct reproduction, and comparing surviving
negatives against the published engravings shows figures added for scale,
weather improved, and horizons tidied. The alternative was the magic lantern:
glass slides projected to a paying hall, sometimes hand-colored, with the
explorer narrating beside the screen. Lecture tours of that kind were the
financial engine of a great many expeditions, and they rewarded the dramatic
image over the informative one, which shaped what photographers on the next
expedition went looking for.


## Cold, Static, and the Physics of Failure

Polar photography is a catalogue of ways that equipment stops working. Film
becomes brittle at low temperature and cracks across the sprocket holes; wound
too quickly in very dry cold, it generates static discharges that expose the
emulsion in branching, lightning-shaped marks. Shutter lubricants congeal, so
cameras destined for the ice were often stripped of grease entirely and run dry.
Metal eyepieces take skin off a bare eye or a bare hand. Bringing a cold camera
into a warm hut coats every surface, inside and out, with condensation that then
freezes when it goes back out, so gear either stays outside or goes into a
sealed bag before it comes in. Snow defeats exposure meters, which are built to
render an average scene as mid-grey and will therefore turn a snowfield into
mud unless the photographer deliberately overexposes.

Herbert Ponting handled all of this on the Terra Nova expedition, working with
plate cameras and a hand-cranked cinematograph in conditions that broke most of
what he owned. His footage and stills became the principal surviving record of
an expedition whose polar party died, and they were toured for years afterward
to pay its debts. Hurley's plates from the Endurance did the same work: the
lecture circuit and the film he assembled from the voyage helped settle the
expedition's accounts, which is part of why the decision on the floe about which
plates to keep was not a sentimental one.

## The Frame as an Argument

Expedition pictures were made for audiences with expectations, and they were
composed accordingly. Summit photographs exist to prove a claim; flags, ice
axes, and recognizable background ridges are in the frame because a skeptic will
want them there. Sponsors' crates were turned label-outward. Sledges were
photographed being hauled across ground the party had already crossed, posed
again because the camera had not been set up the first time. Skies were printed
in from other negatives when the original came out blank and white, a routine
practice that nobody at the time considered deceptive.

The exclusions matter more. The porters, guides, dog drivers, and local hunters
who made most expeditions possible appear in the frame far less often than they
appear in the diaries, and when they do it is frequently at the edge, unnamed in
the caption. A photograph of a European standing alone on a glacier is a
composition, not a situation. Reading the whole plate — including the corners
that the published crop removed — has become one of the more productive ways of
recovering who was actually present.

The gap between what a photograph proves and what it is asked to prove keeps
reappearing. When George Mallory's body was found on Everest in 1999, one of the
first questions was whether the small folding camera he had carried might be on
him, since a frame exposed above the final obstacles would settle a question
that has been argued for a century. The camera has never been found, and the
argument continues in the absence of the only witness anyone would accept.

## Pictures That Became Measurements

The strangest afterlife of expedition photography is its conversion into data.
Repeat photography — returning to the exact spot where a historical image was
made and exposing a new frame along the same sightlines — turns a
nineteenth-century souvenir into the first term in a time series. The method is
finicky: the original camera position must be recovered from the geometry of
ridgelines and boulders in the background, the focal length estimated, the
season matched so that snow cover is comparable. Done carefully, it yields an
unambiguous record of how far a glacier terminus has retreated, how a treeline
has moved, how a channel has migrated, over a baseline no instrument network can
match because no instrument network existed then.

Alaska's tidewater glaciers, the Alpine and Himalayan ice, and the arid
rangelands of the American West have all been documented this way, using
survey and expedition plates made by people who were trying to impress
subscribers rather than to establish a baseline. Aerial photographic surveys
flown in the middle of the twentieth century for mapping purposes are now run
through photogrammetric software to produce elevation models of ice surfaces
that no longer exist.

Hurley's smashed plates were a calculation about weight against value, made with
the information he had. What he could not have known is which of the survivors
would matter, or that the value of a photograph made in a place hard to reach
tends to rise long after everyone who argued about it has stopped caring.
