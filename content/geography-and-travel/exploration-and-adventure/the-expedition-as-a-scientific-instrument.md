# The Expedition as a Scientific Instrument

In June 1769, on a headland in Tahiti that the Endeavour's people called Point
Venus, a small party set up a clock, a quadrant, and a pair of telescopes and
waited for a black dot to touch the edge of the sun. The whole voyage — the
ship, the crew, the two years of sailing, the naturalists and their
collections — existed because of the few seconds when that contact would occur,
and because the same contact was being watched from Hudson Bay, from the North
Cape, from Baja California, from a scattering of other places chosen for their
distance from one another rather than for any interest they held.

That is the peculiar logic of a scientific expedition. It is not simply travel
with a notebook. It is a way of putting an instrument somewhere the phenomenon
can be measured and nowhere else, at a moment that will not be repeated, and
accepting an enormous ratio of logistics to data in order to do it.

## Measuring the Solar System from a Beach

The transits of Venus in 1761 and 1769 offered a method, proposed decades
earlier by Halley, for doing something astronomers badly wanted: fixing the
absolute scale of the solar system. Relative distances between the planets were
well known from Kepler's laws. The missing quantity was the length of the
astronomical unit itself, and a transit gave a way to get it. Observers far
apart on the Earth would see Venus cross a slightly different chord of the solar
disk, and the difference in the duration of the crossing yields the parallax,
and from the parallax, the distance.

Dozens of parties went out, sponsored by academies and navies across Europe, in
what amounted to the first internationally coordinated scientific campaign. The
results were disappointing relative to the effort. The sharp timing the method
required turned out to be unobtainable, because as Venus's disk approaches the
solar limb it appears to stretch toward it and cling, a smearing of the contact
moment produced by atmospheric turbulence and the optics of the telescopes. Two
competent observers side by side could disagree by many seconds. The campaign
still narrowed the solar distance considerably, but its more lasting effect was
organizational: it established that a question could be assigned to a fleet of
travelers, and that the measurement lived in the aggregate rather than in any
one observer's notebook.

The cost of being one node in such a network was borne unevenly. Guillaume Le
Gentil sailed for India to observe the 1761 transit, was diverted by war, and
found himself at sea at the critical hour, where no useful timing was possible.
He decided to stay in the Indian Ocean for eight years and wait for the 1769
event. The morning came and a cloud covered the sun. He went home to discover
that he had been declared dead and his estate distributed.

## The Shape of the Earth

Three decades before Tahiti, the French Academy of Sciences sent two parties to
settle an argument about whether the Earth bulged at the equator or at the
poles. Newtonian theory predicted an oblate spheroid, flattened at the poles.
French measurements suggested the opposite. The test was to measure the length
of one degree of latitude at two widely separated places: if a degree is longer
near the pole, the Earth is flatter there.

One party went north to Lapland and returned within a couple of years with a
clean result. The other went to the Andes, to the high country of what is now
Ecuador, and stayed for nearly a decade. They measured a baseline on a plain,
carried triangulation over volcanic peaks where signal stations had to be
occupied for weeks in freezing fog, and were slowed by altitude, illness,
bureaucratic suspicion, the murder of one of their party, and the exhaustion of
their funds. By the time they published, the northern result had already
settled the question. What the southern mission produced instead was a body of
incidental science that outlived the original point: careful work on the local
deflection of the vertical near mountains, early attempts to weigh a mountain
and through it the Earth, the first serious European account of rubber and
cinchona bark, and a map of the Amazon descended by one of them on the way home.

Long expeditions have a habit of doing this. The framing question gets answered
elsewhere or turns out to be malformed, and the durable value lies in the
material collected along the way by people with time on their hands and good
training.

## The Ship as a Laboratory

The Challenger expedition of the 1870s is the cleanest example of the
expedition designed as an instrument rather than a journey. A Royal Navy
corvette had most of its guns removed and the space converted into laboratories,
storage for specimens, and platforms for sounding and dredging gear. The ship
then spent three and a half years zigzagging across the Atlantic, the Southern
Ocean, and the Pacific, stopping at hundreds of stations to do the same set of
things: sound the depth with weighted line, take water temperatures at a series
of depths, sample the water, and drag a dredge across the bottom.

The findings dismantled a comfortable assumption. It had been argued that below
a few hundred fathoms the ocean was lifeless, too cold and too dark and too
pressurized for anything to live. The dredges came up full of animals from every
depth sampled, thousands of them new to science. They also came up with
manganese nodules lying loose on the abyssal plain, with the fine clays and
oozes that revealed how deep-sea sediment accumulates, and with a set of
temperature and salinity profiles that gave the first picture of deep water
circulating as distinct masses.

The voyage produced the data; the twenty years afterward produced the science.
Specimens were parceled out to specialists across Europe and North America, and
the reports ran to fifty volumes. That division of labor — a mobile platform
that collects, and a distributed community that analyzes for decades — is
essentially the model still in use.

## From the Traverse to the Network

The twentieth century shifted the emphasis from the single heroic line to the
synchronized network. The first International Polar Year in the 1880s set up
stations around the Arctic to make simultaneous magnetic and meteorological
observations, on the reasoning that the phenomena in question were planetary and
could not be captured by one party on one journey. A second followed fifty years
later, and the International Geophysical Year of the late 1950s extended the
idea to the whole Earth: dozens of countries, thousands of stations, coordinated
programs in geomagnetism, glaciology, oceanography, and the upper atmosphere.
Antarctic bases built for it became permanent, and the political settlement that
followed set the continent aside for science. The continuous record of
atmospheric carbon dioxide begun on Mauna Loa during that year is still running.

The expedition did not disappear; it became the delivery mechanism for
instruments that stay behind. A cruise now drops moorings and profiling floats
that report for years, or it comes back with a physical archive rather than
observations — the most extreme case being ice cores, where the point of
spending a season on an ice sheet is to bring home cylinders of ancient
atmosphere that will be measured by laboratories for the next thirty years.
Ocean drilling works the same way, with a ship holding position over a hole for
weeks to extract sediment that encodes millions of years of climate.

## What a Journey Cannot Tell You

The structural weakness of expedition science is that a traverse is a line
through a volume. You learn the season you visited, along the track you chose,
with the gear you brought. Ships go to the high latitudes in summer because
winter ice makes it impractical, which means the polar oceans were long
described by their least representative months. A dredge catches what a dredge
catches, and the abyssal fauna was reconstructed for a century from animals
robust enough to survive being scraped into a net and hauled up through several
kilometers of warming water.

There is also the matter of who supplied the competence. Expeditions in
unfamiliar country ran on local knowledge — guides who knew the passes, hunters
who knew the ice, interpreters, porters, and the people who made the clothing
and handled the dogs that kept polar parties alive. Their contribution shows up
constantly in the narratives and almost never in the authorship, and a fair
amount of modern scholarship consists of reading the old accounts to recover the
names.

The autonomous float and the satellite have absorbed most of what expeditions
used to do for sampling, which leaves the expedition to the tasks a machine
cannot yet manage: putting a drill through two kilometers of ice, recovering a
sample that must be handled by someone who understands what it is, or looking
at a piece of seafloor with enough context to notice that what is there was not
what anyone predicted.
