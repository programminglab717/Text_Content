# The Sahara Between Its Green Phases

On a sandstone plateau in southeastern Algeria, at Tassili n'Ajjer, there are painted walls where long-horned cattle walk in file, hunters carry bows, and figures sit in the shade of what look like mat shelters. The country around that rock today would kill a cow in a week. Far to the east, in the Gilf Kebir near the Egyptian-Libyan border, a rock shelter holds small human figures with their arms and legs arranged in a posture that every modern viewer reads instantly and correctly as swimming. Nobody painted those scenes from hearsay about a distant country. They painted what was outside the shelter.

The Sahara is not a permanent feature of the planet. It is a phase. The region has swung between desert and savanna repeatedly, on a rhythm set by slow changes in the shape and orientation of Earth's orbit, and the painters at Tassili were working in the last centuries of the most recent green interval, watching the lakes shrink around them.

## A Quiet Basement Under a Restless Skin

Underneath the sand lies some of the oldest and least disturbed rock on any continent. The African craton has not been through a major mountain-building episode in an extremely long time, and its Precambrian basement surfaces in the volcanic massifs of the Hoggar in Algeria, the Aïr in Niger, and the Tibesti in Chad, where Emi Koussi rises as the highest point in the whole desert. Elsewhere the basement is buried beneath thick stacks of sandstone and limestone deposited by shallow seas and long-vanished river systems. Fossil shells and shark teeth turn up in the middle of the driest ground in the world, along with the bones of enormous crocodilians and the dinosaur beds of Niger and Morocco, all of it laid down when the region sat at a different latitude under a different climate.

The desert's surface is a thin and mobile skin over all of that. Sand seas, the ergs that supply most photographs of the Sahara, cover only a minority of it, perhaps a quarter. Far more ground is reg, a gravel plain swept clean of fines, or hamada, bare rock pavement polished by grit. Wind sorts the debris: the finest particles leave entirely as dust, the coarsest stay put as lag gravel, and the well-graded quartz grains in between pile into dune fields that migrate downwind, crescent-shaped barchans on open plains and long parallel ridges where the wind reverses seasonally. When radar flown on the space shuttle in the early 1980s looked through the sand sheet of the eastern desert, it found river valleys underneath, a full drainage network carved by water and then buried, invisible from the air but perfectly legible to a beam that penetrates a few metres of dry sand.

## The Machinery of Dryness

The Sahara sits where it does for a reason that has nothing to do with local geography. Air rising over the equatorial belt sheds its moisture as tropical rain, travels poleward at altitude, and descends around thirty degrees of latitude in each hemisphere, warming as it sinks and drying further as it warms. That descending branch of the Hadley circulation lies across North Africa, and the result is a lid: air near the ground can be humid, but it cannot rise far enough to rain. The same belt of sinking air produces the Arabian, Thar, Australian, and Kalahari deserts at matching latitudes, which is why a band of dry country rings the globe rather than being scattered at random.

Once established, a desert of this size helps keep itself dry. The meteorologist Jule Charney pointed out decades ago that a bright, bare surface reflects much of the sunlight that reaches it, so the air column above loses more heat by radiation than it gains, cools, and sinks — reinforcing exactly the subsidence that prevents rain. Vegetation would darken the surface and put water back into the air; without it, the feedback runs the other way.

That feedback is also what makes the Sahara capable of flipping. Precession slowly changes the season at which Earth passes closest to the Sun. When northern summers receive the extra sunlight, the continent heats harder, the rain belt that brings the West African monsoon pushes much further north than it does now, and the feedback reverses: rain supports plants, plants darken the ground and recycle moisture, and the margin advances. Sediment cores from lake beds in the middle of the desert record deep freshwater lakes, fish, hippopotamus, and dense pollen from grasses and trees. The change back is thought to have been rapid in places rather than gradual, a threshold crossed instead of a slope descended — a few centuries in which permanent water became seasonal water and then nothing.

Present-day conditions are harsh in more ways than heat. Dry air and cloudless skies mean nothing holds the day's warmth after sunset, so ground that was punishing in the afternoon can approach freezing before dawn, and frost is ordinary on winter mornings in the Hoggar. In winter the harmattan blows out of the northeast, hauling dust across the Sahel and out over the Atlantic in plumes visible from orbit.

## Waiting, as a Survival Strategy

Almost everything that lives in the Sahara is organized around intermittence. Annual plants persist as seed, sometimes for years, germinating only after a rain heavy enough to wet the soil to depth, then flowering and setting seed in a matter of weeks. Acacias and tamarisks hold ground along wadis by sending roots deep into moisture left over from floods that may have run a decade ago. Addax and dorcas gazelle get most of their water from the plants they eat, carry pale coats that reflect rather than absorb, and let their body temperature drift upward through the day rather than spending water on cooling. The fennec fox dumps heat through outsized ears and hunts at night. The Saharan silver ant does the opposite of what one would expect and forages in the hottest part of the day, when the lizards that eat it have retreated, protected by reflective hairs and long legs that hold it above the scorching surface for the brief window it can survive out there.

Scattered through the massifs are relicts, populations left behind by the retreat of the wetter world. A tiny stand of Saharan cypress clings to the Tassili plateau, a species reduced to a few hundred aged trees with almost no recruitment. In the gueltas of the Tibesti and Ennedi, permanent rock pools fed by seepage, small populations of West African crocodile survived in isolation for thousands of years after the rivers that once connected them dried. These are not curiosities so much as evidence: pieces of a wetter Sahara still hanging on wherever the geology hides enough water.

## Water Nobody Can See

The largest reserve of fresh water in North Africa is underground and mostly ancient. The Nubian Sandstone aquifer beneath Egypt, Libya, Sudan, and Chad holds water that fell during past humid phases and has been sitting in porous rock ever since. Where that water reaches the surface — at a fault, in a wind-scoured depression, along the edge of a basin — an oasis exists, and around it a style of agriculture that has changed very little. Date palms form an upper canopy that shades pomegranates, figs, and olives, which in turn shade vegetables and fodder at ground level, each storey cutting evaporation for the one below.

Getting water to gardens without pumps produced one of the more elegant pieces of engineering anywhere. A foggara — the same idea as the Persian qanat — is a gently sloping tunnel driven into a water-bearing layer, dug and maintained through a line of vertical shafts whose spoil rings are still visible from the air as strings of craters marching across the gravel. Gravity does the rest. At the outlet, the flow is divided among shareholders by a notched stone weir, each notch a specified width, so that entitlements are physically embodied rather than metered, and any dispute can be settled by looking at the stone.

Modern extraction runs on different assumptions. Deep boreholes and centre-pivot irrigation have printed green circles across parts of Libya, Egypt, and Saudi Arabia's share of the same dry belt, and Libya's Great Man-Made River carries aquifer water hundreds of kilometres north to the coastal cities. The water being lifted is not being replaced on any human timescale. It is a mineral deposit that happens to be liquid.

## Moving, Staying, and the Space Between

Saharan societies have long been structured around the difference between people who move and people who stay, and around the trade that difference makes possible. Tuareg, Toubou, Moorish, and Arab pastoralist groups worked the interior with camels, sheep, and goats; settled communities in the oases and along the Nile and the Niger grew dates and grain and metalworked. Neither could do without the other. Camels arrived comparatively late — the rock art of the earlier periods shows cattle, then horses and light chariots, before the camel appears at all — and their spread reorganized the desert into something crossable on a predictable schedule.

Salt remained the freight that justified the hardest journeys. Slabs cut at Taoudenni in Mali and Bilma in Niger moved south by camel to be exchanged for millet and cloth, and a much-reduced version of the Bilma caravan still runs each year, its economics propped up as much by custom as by arithmetic. Trucks took most of that trade in the twentieth century, along with the mines: gas and oil in Algeria and Libya, phosphate in Western Sahara, iron ore hauled to the Mauritanian coast on one of the longest trains in the world.

The last century has been hard on mobility. Colonial and then national borders were drawn across grazing circuits by people who had never used them. Severe Sahelian droughts in the 1970s and 1980s destroyed herds and pushed a generation of herding families into Nouakchott, Niamey, and Bamako. Conflict in Mali and Libya has closed routes that had been open for centuries, while the same desert knowledge that once guided salt caravans now guides vehicles carrying migrants north, at considerable risk, along tracks with no water on them.

Meanwhile the Sahara keeps exporting itself. Dust lifted from the Bodélé Depression, a dry lakebed floor in Chad, crosses the Atlantic on the trade winds and settles over the Amazon, where its phosphorus fertilizes a rainforest growing on leached soils. The desert's next green phase is already scheduled by the geometry of the orbit. It is simply not scheduled for anyone alive.
