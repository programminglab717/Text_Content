# The Mediterranean Basin: Sea of Many Shores

Stand on a limestone hillside above the Aegean in late August and the
landscape looks exhausted. The grass went brown in May. Thyme and savory
crackle underfoot and release their oils into air that smells faintly of
resin and dust. The only green is the dull grey-green of olive leaves and
the near-black of cypress, both of them holding their color through the
drought by refusing to spend water. Come back in November, after the first
serious rain, and the same hillside is fluorescent with new growth,
cyclamen pushing up through leaf litter, the soil dark and workable. The
Mediterranean runs its year backwards from most of temperate Europe. Winter
is when things grow. Summer is the season to be survived.

That inversion is the single fact from which most of the basin's character
follows — its plants, its farms, its fire, its diet, and a good deal of its
history. But the sea itself came first, and it arrived by a strange route.

## A Sea That Nearly Vanished

The Mediterranean is the shrunken remnant of a much larger ocean. Tethys
once separated the northern continents from Gondwana and wrapped most of
the way around the planet's midsection. Africa and Arabia have been
grinding northward into Eurasia for tens of millions of years, and the
Mediterranean is what is left of the water between them: a closing basin,
squeezed and folded at the edges. The mountains that ring it are the
wreckage of that collision. The Atlas, the Betic ranges, the Apennines, the
Alps, the Dinarides, the Hellenides, the Taurus — all of them are the same
event expressed in different rock.

The collision is not finished, which is why the region shakes. Slabs of old
oceanic floor are still sinking beneath Greece and beneath Calabria, and
the volcanoes strung above those subduction zones — Etna, Stromboli,
Vesuvius, Santorini, Nisyros — are the visible plumbing. Anatolia is being
extruded westward like a watermelon seed squeezed between Arabia and
Eurasia, sliding along the North Anatolian Fault, a strike-slip system that
has produced a sequence of destructive earthquakes marching westward toward
the Sea of Marmara. The Aegean behind it is stretching and thinning, which
is why it is a drowned landscape of islands rather than a coherent coast.

The most spectacular episode came late. Roughly five to six million years
ago the connection to the Atlantic closed, and the sea, which loses far
more water to evaporation than its rivers and rainfall can replace, began
to dry. It did not disappear once; it appears to have drawn down and
partially refilled repeatedly, leaving kilometer-thick beds of salt and
gypsum beneath the modern seafloor and cutting river canyons far below
present sea level — the Nile and the Rhône both incised gorges that are now
buried under sediment. The refilling, when the Atlantic finally broke back
through at Gibraltar, may have been abrupt: a flood through a narrowing
sill on a scale with no modern analogue. Whatever the timing, the basin
reset, and the sea we have now is young enough that its deep-water fauna is
essentially an Atlantic import.

## Winter Rain, Summer Drought

The climate has a simple engine. In summer the subtropical high-pressure
belt migrates north and parks over the basin, bringing sinking, stable,
rain-killing air. In winter the belt slides south and lets the mid-latitude
westerlies and their storm tracks in. The result is a reliable seasonal
switch: rain concentrated in the cool half of the year, drought
concentrated in the warm half, when plants would most like to be drinking.
It is an unusual arrangement. Only four other places on Earth manage it —
coastal California, central Chile, the Cape region of South Africa, and two
corners of southern Australia — all of them on west-facing subtropical
coasts, and all of them, tellingly, covered in shrubs that look remarkably
like each other despite sharing almost no ancestry.

Local relief chops that pattern into fragments. Mountains stand close to
the water almost everywhere, so rainfall can differ by a factor of several
over a day's walk, and the basin is less a region than a mosaic of small
ones. Winds carry local names because they behave as local events: the
mistral funneling cold down the Rhône valley, the bora falling off the
Dinaric plateau onto Trieste and the Kvarner, the sirocco dragging Saharan
dust north until cars in Naples wear a film of orange, the meltemi that
makes Aegean summer sailing both reliable and punishing.

The sea itself works like a slow still. Evaporation exceeds everything
flowing in, so surface Atlantic water pours eastward through Gibraltar
while saltier, denser water slides back out underneath — a two-layer
exchange that medieval and early modern sailors partly understood and
exploited. Tides are small enough to ignore in most harbors. The water is
warm, salty, and famously clear, and it is clear for an unglamorous reason:
it is nutrient-poor. Little upwelling, few great rivers since the Nile was
dammed, and the plankton that would cloud it never gets going. That poverty
is why the Mediterranean has never supported fisheries on the scale of the
North Atlantic, and why its most valuable fish, the bluefin tuna, is a
migratory visitor rather than a resident crop.

## Plants That Expect to Burn

Mediterranean vegetation is built around two constraints: no water in
summer, and fire on a recurring schedule. The signature adaptation is the
sclerophyll leaf — small, thick, stiff, waxy, often rolled or hairy
underneath, designed to keep photosynthesizing at low cost rather than to
grow fast. Holm oak, kermes oak, carob, mastic, olive, strawberry tree,
Aleppo pine, juniper. Where these shrubs grow dense and tall you get
maquis; where the soil is thinner and the cover lower and more aromatic,
garrigue in France, phrygana in Greece, matorral in Spain. The same
architecture appears as chaparral in California and fynbos at the Cape,
convergent solutions to the same problem.

Fire is not an interruption of this system but part of its maintenance.
Many pines hold their seed in cones sealed with resin that only heat
opens. Many shrubs carry a swollen woody lignotuber at the base, a
below-ground savings account that resprouts within weeks of a burn. And the
basin's extraordinary floral richness — it holds one of the densest
concentrations of endemic plant species anywhere outside the tropics, with
Crete, Cyprus, the Aegean islands, the Sierra Nevada of Spain, and the
Taurus each guarding their own lists — depends heavily on the geophytes:
bulbs, corms, and tubers that sit out the drought underground and flower in
autumn or early spring. Orchids, crocuses, cyclamen, fritillaries, tulips,
all keeping their assets below the surface.

The animals are the thinner part of the story. Island dwarf elephants and
pygmy hippos survived into the late Pleistocene on Cyprus, Crete, Sicily,
and Malta, and then did not. The monk seal, once common enough to be
casually hunted, now clings to a few caves. Large mammals in general were
squeezed out early by a landscape people have been reshaping for
millennia — Plato, writing in the fourth century BC, already described the
hills of Attica as stripped to the bone, the soil washed away, leaving what
he compared to the skeleton of a wasted body.

## Terraces, Olives, and the Habit of Spreading Risk

The agricultural answer to a dry summer is a plant that stores water and
carbohydrate for itself. Olives, figs, grapes, almonds, carob — all deep-
rooted, all drought-tolerant, all producing something that keeps without
refrigeration. Add winter-sown wheat and barley, which grow in the rainy
season and ripen just as the drought closes in, and you have the classic
triad that fed the ancient basin. Add sheep and goats, which convert the
scrub no one can farm into milk and meat and manure, and the system closes.

Terracing follows from the terrain: thin soils on steep limestone will
leave for the sea in one November storm unless someone builds a wall. Dry-
stone terraces climb the hills of Liguria, the Cinque Terre, Mallorca,
Lebanon, and the Cyclades, and they represent an investment of labor so
enormous that their widespread abandonment in the twentieth century counts
as one of the great quiet landscape changes of modern Europe.

What historians of the region tend to emphasize is not any single crop but
the strategy underneath: a landscape of small, sharply different
microregions, in which the way to survive a bad year is to hold scattered
holdings, plant several things that fail under different conditions, move
animals seasonally between valley and mountain pasture, and stay connected
by boat to places whose harvests are not correlated with yours. Fernand
Braudel built his history of the sixteenth-century Mediterranean on that
slow, structural layer — mountains, transhumance, shipping routes — rather
than on kings. The sea was not a barrier between the basin's shores. For
most of the last several thousand years it was the cheapest road available,
which is why a cargo of amphorae could move from Africa to Britain more
easily than a cart of grain could cross a hundred miles of Anatolia.

## The Modern Shore

Most of the basin's population now lives within sight of the water, and the
pressure is concentrated there: coastal sprawl, roads, marinas, and a
tourist season that peaks precisely when water is scarcest. Groundwater
pumped hard near the coast draws seawater inland, and once an aquifer goes
saline it does not readily come back. Inland, the opposite problem —
abandonment. Terraces collapse, grazing stops, brush and young pine fill
the gaps, and fuel accumulates in continuous sheets where it used to be
broken by fields and flocks. The fires that follow are larger and hotter
than the ones the vegetation evolved with.

Climate models agree on the direction with unusual unanimity: the basin
dries and warms faster than the global average, the summer high sits longer
and pushes further north, and the winter rains that everything depends on
grow less dependable. The vegetation has drought written into it. The
question is whether the margin it evolved with is wide enough for what is
coming, and in the southern and eastern arc of the sea — where the
population is growing fastest and the water was already thinnest — the
margin was never wide.
