# The Australian Outback: Living With the Dry

The red of the outback is rust. Iron in the soil, oxidized over a span of
time that is difficult to hold in the head, staining sand and dust and the
faces of cliffs the color of dried blood. It gets into clothes and never
quite comes out. Drive the Oodnadatta Track for a day and the film on the
windscreen will be the same shade as the ground, because it is the ground,
lifted and set down again by a wind that has been doing this for a very
long time.

Australia is the flattest and — leaving Antarctica out of it — the driest
inhabited continent, and the reasons are geological and atmospheric in
roughly equal measure. Most of the continent has not been through a
mountain-building event in hundreds of millions of years. No recent
glaciation scraped it clean and left fresh rock and young soil. It has
simply sat there, weathering, its high ground worn down and its low ground
filled in, until what remains is a vast low plateau of ancient crust
covered in the chemical residue of its own decay.

## Old Rock, Deep Weathering

The cores of the continent are cratons — Precambrian blocks that have been
stable since before there was free oxygen in the air. In the Jack Hills of
Western Australia, grains of zircon weathered out of those rocks have been
dated to more than four billion years, older than any intact rock yet
found. The Hamersley Range in the Pilbara preserves banded iron formations
laid down when early photosynthesizers first began oxidizing the ocean, and
those beds are now among the world's great iron ore deposits — the outback
sells its own antiquity by the shipload.

Because the surface is so old, the weathering runs deep. Silica and iron
have been dissolved, moved, and reprecipitated as hard crusts: laterite,
silcrete, ferricrete, sheets of case-hardened material that cap mesas and
form the gibber plains, those expanses of polished stone pavement where
nothing grows and nothing moves. Uluru and Kata Tjuta are the stubs of an
eroded mountain range — arkose sandstone at one, conglomerate at the other
— with bedding tilted almost vertical, so that what you see is the edge of a
formation standing on end, most of it still underground.

Water, where it exists, tends to be old and buried. The Great Artesian
Basin underlies a fifth of the continent, a stack of permeable sandstones
sealed beneath impermeable rock, recharged mainly along the western slopes
of the Great Dividing Range and moving inland at a creep of a metre or so a
year. Some of the water coming out of a bore in western Queensland fell as
rain long before any European saw the place. Where it reaches the surface
naturally it builds mound springs — small carbonate cones ringed with reeds,
each one an island ecosystem with its own endemic fish and snails, and each
one a fixed point on the routes people used to cross the country.

The surface drainage, by contrast, mostly goes nowhere. The continent's
interior drains inward to Kati Thanda-Lake Eyre, a salt pan sitting below
sea level that fills completely only a few times a century. When it does —
when a cyclone crosses the Queensland coast and its remains dump rain into
the Channel Country — the flood takes months to arrive, spreading out
through braided anastomosing channels across the Diamantina and Cooper
floodplains. Pelicans appear on a lake that has been dry dust for a decade,
having flown in from the coast, and nobody is entirely sure how they know.

## A Climate That Works in Pulses

Three rainfall systems touch the interior and none of them touches it
reliably. The northern monsoon soaks the Top End and Kimberley between
about November and April and leaves them rainless the rest of the year. The
southern fringe gets winter frontal rain off the Southern Ocean. The middle
gets whatever leaks in from either direction, plus the occasional decayed
tropical cyclone, and the year-to-year variability of that middle zone is
among the highest anywhere on Earth. An average rainfall figure for
Alice Springs is a nearly meaningless number: the distribution is a long
string of dry years punctuated by an extraordinary wet one.

The El Niño-Southern Oscillation is the main dial. La Niña years push warm
water and convection toward Australia and can drown the interior; El Niño
years starve it, and the big droughts and the big fire seasons line up with
them. Everything alive in the outback is organized around this rhythm
rather than around a calendar.

## Boom, Bust, and Spinifex

The dominant plant across enormous stretches of the interior is spinifex —
hummock grasses of the genus Triodia, growing as rings of needle-sharp
leaves with a bare dead centre, resinous enough to burn ferociously and
tough enough that almost nothing eats it. Where soils are heavier there is
mulga, a fine-leaved acacia whose upward-angled branches funnel rain down
the trunk to its own roots. In the south there is mallee, multi-stemmed
eucalypt shrubland sprouting from underground lignotubers, and across the
saline flats, chenopod shrublands of saltbush and bluebush that stay
grey-green through drought and keep sheep alive on country that looks
dead.

Animal life runs on the same pulse. Budgerigars and zebra finches breed in
waves after rain and crash between times. Desert frogs bury themselves,
seal into a cocoon of shed skin, and wait years. Shield shrimp hatch in
claypans within days of a downpour, mature, breed, and die before the water
goes, leaving eggs that can sit dry for decades. Red kangaroos can pause a
pregnancy until conditions improve. Nothing here is built to hold steady;
everything is built to exploit a window and then endure.

Fire is a normal part of the system, but the pattern of fire has changed.
For tens of thousands of years Aboriginal people burned country in small,
deliberate patches — clearing travel routes, flushing game, promoting the
regrowth that attracts it, and, as a side effect, breaking the landscape
into a mosaic of different ages that stopped any one fire from running
unchecked. Rhys Jones called it firestick farming. Where that burning
stopped, spinifex grew into continuous multi-decade fuel, and single
lightning strikes now take out areas the size of small countries. Many
ecologists link the collapse of Australia's desert mammal fauna — one of
the worst extinction records of the modern era — to the combination of that
changed fire regime with foxes, feral cats, and rabbits that stripped the
vegetation and undercut the burrowing species.

## How People Make It Work

Aboriginal occupation of the arid interior goes back tens of thousands of
years, and the knowledge that made it possible was geographic before it was
anything else: the locations of rockholes, soaks, and claypans, the order in
which to visit them, the seasons at which each was reliable. Songlines
encode routes as sequence — verse after verse naming features in the order a
traveller meets them, a navigational system carried in memory across
country with no landmarks a stranger would recognize. That knowledge is not
historical. Remote communities and ranger groups still use it, and native
title determinations now cover a substantial share of the interior.

European use came later and settled on two industries. Pastoralism took
leases measured in thousands of square kilometres, running cattle in the
north and sheep in the south, and it was made possible not by rain but by
the drill rig: sinking bores into the artesian basin let stock spread
across country that had previously been useless because it had no surface
water. That solved one problem and created several, including the steady
depressurization of the aquifer and the arrival of grazing pressure in
places that had never known a hard-hoofed animal.

Mining supplied the other economy, and the settlements it built are the
strangest ones. Kalgoorlie grew on gold and is still chewing a pit into the
ground beside the town. Coober Pedy mines opal, and roughly half its
residents live underground in dugouts because a metre of rock is the best
air conditioning available. The Pilbara iron operations run on a fly-in
fly-out workforce and haul ore on some of the heaviest trains in the world
to ports built for the purpose.

The connective tissue is ingenious and thin. Road trains — a prime mover
with three or four trailers — move cattle and freight on roads that go to
gravel at the first rain. The Royal Flying Doctor Service, started in the
1920s, turned the aeroplane and later the radio into a rural health system
across distances no ambulance could serve. School of the Air taught
children by radio for decades, and does it by satellite now. The Dingo
Fence, stretching thousands of kilometres, separates sheep country from
dingo country and is patrolled as infrastructure.

What defeats people out here is almost always a misjudgement of scale.
Burke and Wills crossed the continent in 1860 and died on the return within
reach of supplies, partly through bad luck and partly because they treated
the country as something to be pushed through rather than read. The lesson
has not aged much. Outback travel advice still reduces to the same few
rules — carry more water than you can imagine needing, tell someone your
route, and if the vehicle stops, stay with it. The vehicle is visible from
the air. You are not.
