# How Southeast Asia's Great Deltas Work

At the end of the dry season, the water in a canal in Bến Tre province tastes
faintly of the sea. Farmers there have learned to check it the way a cook checks
a sauce, because the difference between water that will grow rice and water that
will kill it is a matter of a few parts per thousand of salt, and because the
line where fresh becomes brackish now moves further inland each dry season than
it used to. That line is invisible, it shifts with the tide and the moon, and it
is one of the clearest windows onto how a delta actually works.

Four of the world's great deltas sit on the Southeast Asian mainland: the Red
River in northern Vietnam, the Mekong in Cambodia and southern Vietnam, the Chao
Phraya in Thailand, and the Ayeyarwady in Myanmar. Between them they carry a
substantial share of the region's people, most of its rice, and nearly all of its
freshwater fish. They are also, in geological terms, brand new and inherently
temporary, which is a strange thing to say about landscapes that support tens of
millions of lives.

## Deltas Are Mountains, Relocated

A delta is a pile of ground-up mountain range deposited where a river loses the
power to carry it. The Mekong begins on the Tibetan Plateau and falls through
the gorges of Yunnan, tearing at bedrock the whole way. The Ayeyarwady drains the
eastern Himalaya and the ranges of northern Myanmar. Every monsoon, these rivers
pick up silt, sand and clay and run it downhill. Where the channel meets standing
water, the current stalls, the coarse fraction drops first and the fine fraction
travels further out to settle in still water, and the river builds a platform of
its own making out into the sea.

Whether that platform ends up looking like a fan, a bird's foot, or a funnel
depends on the fight between the river and the ocean. Where the river wins, the
delta pushes out in long fingers. Where waves are strong, they smear the sediment
sideways into smooth arcs of beach ridge — the Red River delta shows this
clearly, and so does much of the Mekong's seaward edge, where old shorelines
survive inland as low sandy rises that people build roads and villages on because
they stay dry. Where tides dominate, as in the Ayeyarwady, the delta is cut by
broad channels that flare seaward and carry salt water far upstream twice a day.

None of these landforms is old. During the last glacial maximum, sea level was
more than a hundred metres lower, the Gulf of Thailand and the Java Sea were dry
land, and the rivers of Sundaland ran across an exposed shelf to a coastline far
out beyond today's. When the ice melted, the sea came back in, drowned all of it,
and only slowed around six or seven thousand years ago. Everything that is now
delta has been built since then, filling drowned valleys and then prograding
across shallow shelf. Some of the Mekong Delta is younger than the pyramids.

## Why a Delta Has to Keep Being Fed

Freshly deposited delta mud is mostly water. Under its own weight it compacts,
the grains reorganise, and the surface sinks. Organic layers oxidise and shrink
when they are drained. This means that a delta is subsiding all the time, and it
stays above sea level only because the river keeps laying new material on top of
it. Interrupt the supply and the land starts to go down.

Three things interrupt the supply, and all three are now happening at once.

Dams trap sediment behind them. The Mekong's mainstem cascade in China and the
newer barrages in Laos hold back a large fraction of what used to reach the
lower river, and the water released below a dam is sediment-starved and hungry,
so it scours its own bed and banks to make up the deficit. Sand mining does the
same thing more directly: dredgers lift construction sand out of the riverbed for
the concrete going into Phnom Penh and Ho Chi Minh City, the channel deepens,
banks lose their footing, and houses slide into the water. And embankments
prevent the river from spreading its load across the floodplain at all. High
dikes built to allow a third rice crop in the Vietnamese delta keep the flood off
the fields, which also keeps the silt off the fields — the silt goes out to sea
instead, and the soil beneath the rice slowly loses the annual top-up of
nutrients that made it fertile in the first place.

Groundwater pumping accelerates the sinking from below. Draw water out of
aquifer sands and the pressure that helps hold the overlying clays apart drops;
the clays consolidate and the surface subsides. Bangkok has been dealing with
this for decades. Jakarta's version of the problem, on the alluvial plain of
several small rivers, has become severe enough that the Indonesian government
began building a new capital on higher ground in Kalimantan. Across much of the
Mekong Delta, subsidence from pumping now outpaces global sea-level rise by a
comfortable margin, which means that for people living there the sea is rising
several times faster than the global average figure implies.

## The Flood Pulse

Upstream of the Mekong Delta proper, the system does something that has no real
equivalent anywhere else. During the wet season the mainstem rises so high at
Phnom Penh that it pushes water backwards up the Tonlé Sap river into the lake of
the same name. The lake expands enormously, drowning the surrounding forest and
floodplain, and then drains back out as the river falls.

That annual inundation is the engine of one of the largest inland fisheries on
earth. Fish move out of the channel into flooded forest to spawn and feed on the
sudden abundance of terrestrial organic matter, then wash back into the river as
the water drops. Cambodian households have organised their year and much of their
diet around the pulse for centuries. Weaken the flood — by holding back wet-season
water in reservoirs upstream, or by a run of dry monsoons — and the fishery
weakens with it, in a way that no amount of pond aquaculture replaces, because
what is lost is a source of cheap protein available to people with no capital.

## Rice, Shrimp, and the Trouble Underneath

The soils of these deltas are not uniformly good. Large areas of the Mekong
Delta sit on old mangrove and marsh deposits full of iron sulphide. Leave them
waterlogged and they are inert. Drain them and the sulphide oxidises to sulphuric
acid, the soil pH crashes, aluminium and iron become soluble at concentrations
that damage roots, and the ground turns hostile to rice. These acid sulphate
soils are why the Plain of Reeds and the U Minh region were among the last parts
of the delta to be brought into cultivation, and why they still carry stands of
melaleuca rather than paddy in places.

The productive parts, though, are extraordinarily productive. Canal networks dug
under French administration and greatly extended since, combined with
short-duration rice varieties, pumping and dikes, turned the Mekong Delta into
one of the largest rice-exporting regions in the world. The Red River delta is
older and more densely settled — its dike system has been maintained and rebuilt
for something close to a thousand years, to the point where in places the river
runs above the level of the surrounding fields, held in by earth banks, because
it has been depositing inside its own embankments for centuries while the plain
outside got nothing.

Not all of the change has run toward rice. Along the coastal belt, farmers have
converted paddies to brackish shrimp ponds, which are more profitable and which
tolerate the salt that is arriving anyway. Inland, pangasius catfish are raised
in cages and ponds along the mainstem in numbers large enough to supply
supermarket freezers on other continents. Both industries cost mangrove: the
seaward fringe of these deltas has been cut for ponds, for charcoal and for
timber, and in Vietnam a large stretch of the Cà Mau mangroves was additionally
defoliated during the war. Where the mangroves went, the coastline lost the thing
that was holding it together, and erosion rates jumped.

## Living With It

The engineering instinct — higher dikes, more pumps, sea gates — has an obvious
appeal and a well-documented trap. Every structure that keeps water off land
accelerates the subsidence of that land, so the structure has to get higher, and
the consequences of its failure grow. Cyclone Nargis, which came ashore across
the Ayeyarwady delta in 2008 and killed well over a hundred thousand people,
demonstrated the other half of the problem: a low, flat, densely populated
surface with its protective vegetation removed has essentially no defence against
a storm surge, and no high ground to run to.

The alternative approach, which Vietnamese policy has at least formally adopted
for the Mekong Delta, is to stop treating salt and flood as enemies. That means
accepting seasonal inundation in designated areas so that silt can land and
groundwater can recharge, shifting the coastal belt to rice-shrimp rotations that
work with the salinity cycle rather than against it, replanting mangrove as a
first line of defence, and — hardest of all — reducing groundwater extraction in
a region where clean surface water is scarce. Meanwhile young people leave for
factory work in the cities at a rate that has made out-migration one of the
delta's defining statistics, which is its own kind of adaptation, and a signal
about how the people who live on the sediment read their own ground.
