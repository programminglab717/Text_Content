# The Great Plains: A Grassland Under the Plow

The sod houses gave the settlers their nickname. There was no timber on the
open plains and no stone worth quarrying in most places, so the people who
filed homestead claims in Nebraska and Kansas in the 1870s cut their walls
out of the ground itself — strips of prairie turf, roots and all, stacked
like brick. Sod held together because prairie grass roots are dense enough
and deep enough to make the soil behave as a fabric. Cool in summer, warm
in winter, leaky in rain, and full of insects. It was the first thing the
newcomers learned about the place: the grass was the only building material,
and it was extraordinarily strong.

The second thing they learned took longer and cost more.

## Debris From a Mountain Range

The Great Plains are, in the most literal sense, the Rockies' waste pile.
When the Laramide orogeny pushed the ranges up starting around seventy
million years ago, rivers began stripping them and dumping the debris
eastward across a broad apron. The Ogallala Formation — sand, gravel, and
silt washed off the mountain front through the Miocene and Pliocene — is
that apron, and it is the reason the High Plains surface is so
disconcertingly flat. The Llano Estacado of west Texas and eastern New
Mexico is a preserved remnant of it, a tableland so featureless that
Spanish expeditions are said to have driven stakes to find their way back,
and it ends abruptly at the Caprock Escarpment where erosion has begun
eating into its edge.

Later processes added their own layers. Wind piled the Nebraska Sand Hills,
the largest dune field in the Western Hemisphere, now held in place by a
thin skin of grass that will blow again if it is broken. Farther east, glacial
outwash supplied silt that wind lifted and dropped as loess, in places tens
of metres thick, producing some of the most fertile and most erodible soil
on the continent. In the northern plains, the ice sheets themselves came
through, leaving till, rerouting the Missouri River to roughly its present
course along their former margin, and pockmarking the Dakotas and the
Canadian prairies with prairie potholes — shallow depressions left by
buried ice blocks, which together form the most productive duck breeding
ground in North America.

## Rain That Runs Out Going West

The Rockies do to the plains what any big range does to its lee side. Air
moving in off the Pacific is stripped of moisture on the way over, so the
western plains get little from that direction. What rain they do get comes
mostly from the other way, humid air pulled north off the Gulf of Mexico,
and that supply weakens the farther west you go. The result is a steady
moisture gradient running east to west across the continent's midsection —
wetter near the Missouri, drier against the mountains.

John Wesley Powell, surveying the arid West in the 1870s, identified the
hundredth meridian as the rough line beyond which ordinary rain-fed farming
would fail, and argued that western land policy should be redrawn around
water rather than around the surveyor's grid. Congress ignored him, having
already committed to a homestead system that handed out identical
rectangular parcels regardless of whether the rectangle in question could
support a family. The line was never a wall in any case. It moves. Wet
decades push it west and dry ones pull it east, and the whole tragic
history of plains settlement is, in one reading, the story of people
arriving during a wet stretch and mistaking it for the climate.

Everything about plains weather is extreme because there is nothing in the
way. Arctic air comes south in winter with no barrier at all; Gulf air
comes north in spring; where they meet, over the central plains, they
produce the most violent convective weather on the planet, including the
highest tornado frequency anywhere. Chinook winds coming off the mountains
can raise temperatures tens of degrees in an hour. Blizzards can drop them
the same amount. And drought arrives on a cycle that is irregular but
utterly dependable in the long run — the 1890s, the 1930s, the 1950s, and
repeatedly since.

## Bison, Fire, and Grass

Before all of this was farmland it was one of the great grazing ecosystems
on Earth, running from tallgrass prairie in the humid east — big bluestem,
Indiangrass, switchgrass, tall enough to hide a horse in a good year —
through mixed-grass country to the shortgrass steppe of blue grama and
buffalograss in the rain shadow. The grasses kept most of their biomass
underground, which let them survive both fire and grazing and which built
the deep black soils that later made the region worth plowing.

Bison numbered in the tens of millions and moved in enormous herds, grazing
an area hard, fertilizing it, and leaving. That pulsed disturbance, combined
with fire set by lightning and by Indigenous peoples, maintained a shifting
patchwork of short and tall vegetation that a whole assemblage of animals
depended on. Prairie dogs were the other great engineers, clipping
vegetation around their towns and digging burrows that burrowing owls,
rattlesnakes, and the black-footed ferret all relied on. Pronghorn evolved
to outrun a predator that no longer exists, which is why they are
absurdly, unnecessarily fast. Greater prairie chickens gathered each spring
on the same patches of short grass to boom and display.

The human history was reshaped by an import. Horses, dispersed north from
Spanish New Mexico from the late seventeenth century onward, turned plains
life inside out within a few generations. Peoples who had been farming in
river-bottom villages or hunting on foot became mounted, mobile, and
militarily formidable — the Comanche, the Lakota, the Cheyenne — and built
economies around the bison herd. The village agriculturalists of the
Missouri, the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara, stayed put and traded, and were
then devastated by smallpox brought upriver.

The herds went in a single human lifetime. Commercial hide hunting, new
tanning methods that made bison leather industrially useful, and railroads
that could carry hides east combined in the 1870s and 1880s to reduce a
population of tens of millions to a few hundred wild animals. The collapse
was not incidental to the wars over the plains; military officers
understood and said plainly that destroying the herds would end Indigenous
independence, and it did. A handful of private herds and a remnant in
Yellowstone are the reason the species exists at all.

## Breaking the Sod, and the Consequences

What replaced the bison was wheat and cattle, and both required technology
before they could work. The steel plow could cut sod that broke wooden
ones. Barbed wire fenced land that had no timber for rails. Windmills
lifted groundwater for stock. Railroads carried grain out and lumber in.
By the early twentieth century, dry-farming techniques and a run of wet
years convinced hundreds of thousands of people that the semi-arid plains
could be farmed like Illinois.

Wartime wheat prices in the 1910s finished the argument. Tractors plowed up
enormous acreages of shortgrass that had never been broken, in country
where the grass had been the only thing holding the soil down. When the
drought of the 1930s arrived on schedule, the exposed ground went into the
air. The dust storms of that decade darkened skies as far east as
Washington, buried fences and machinery, and drove families off the land in
one of the largest internal migrations in American history. The federal
response — shelterbelts, contour plowing, terracing, the creation of a soil
conservation service — was the first serious national admission that
farming the plains required different rules than farming the Midwest.

The next solution came from below. After the Second World War, cheap
energy, deep turbine pumps, and center-pivot sprinklers unlocked the High
Plains Aquifer, the groundwater held in that old Ogallala gravel. Irrigation
made the dry plains reliably productive, supported enormous cattle feedlots
and the meatpacking towns that serve them, and turned a marginal region
into a dependable one. But the aquifer's recharge is minimal in the south,
where the water is essentially fossil, and decades of pumping have drawn it
down severely in parts of Texas, Oklahoma, and western Kansas. Some
districts have already gone back to dryland farming because there is no
longer enough water to run a pivot.

Meanwhile, the rural population has been draining away for a century.
Counties across the central plains hold fewer people now than they did
before the Dust Bowl, and some hold fewer than the frontier density
threshold the Census once used to declare an area settled. In the 1980s
Frank and Deborah Popper proposed, to considerable local fury, that much of
the region be allowed to revert to a "Buffalo Commons" — grassland, bison,
and tourism instead of subsidized row crops. Something not entirely unlike
it has begun happening without anybody planning it. Conservation programs
pay to keep marginal ground in grass, a large private reserve is assembling
contiguous prairie in Montana, and tribal nations across the plains have
been rebuilding bison herds on their own land for decades — restoring an
animal to country that still carries, under the wheat, the soil that animal
helped make.
