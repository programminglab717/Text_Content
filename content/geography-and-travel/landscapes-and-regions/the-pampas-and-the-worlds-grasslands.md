# The Pampas and the World's Grasslands

Drive west out of Buenos Aires and the first thing you notice is what is
missing. No rock. No fold in the ground. No natural tree line to break the
horizon, only the windbreaks of eucalyptus and poplar that estancia owners
planted around their houses in the nineteenth century, standing in the
distance like ships. The soil under the crop is black to a depth that would
make a Northern European farmer weep. There are no stones in it at all.

The Pampas is a flat, fine-grained, extraordinarily fertile plain, and each
of those three properties has the same cause. Wind and rivers have been
delivering sediment here for a very long time — silt ground off the Andes,
dust blown from Patagonia, ash from the volcanic arc — and depositing it in
a slowly subsiding basin without ever giving it anywhere to go. The result
is loess and loess-like material tens of metres thick, a landscape built by
accumulation rather than by erosion. What it lacks in drama it repays in
agriculture.

## Why Grass Instead of Forest

The interesting question about any grassland is not why grass grows there.
Grass grows almost everywhere. The question is why trees do not, and the
answer usually involves three factors working together rather than one
doing all the work.

The first is water, specifically its seasonality. Most grasslands sit in a
zone with enough annual rainfall to support plant growth but not enough
reliability to carry a tree through a bad stretch. A tree is a long-term
capital investment in wood, and wood has to be maintained through every
drought. A grass writes off its leaves each dry season and keeps its
investment underground.

The second is fire. Dry grass is the best fuel in nature — fine, continuous,
and quick to ignite — and fire moving through a grassland kills tree
seedlings while barely inconveniencing the grasses, whose growing tissue
sits at the base of the leaf, at or below ground level. Mow a lawn, or burn
it, and you remove the old leaf without touching the factory. That
intercalary meristem is the single most consequential piece of anatomy in
the plant kingdom from a human point of view, because it is also what makes
grass grazeable, and therefore what makes herding possible.

The third is grazing itself. Large herbivores in numbers trample and browse
woody seedlings, keep the sward short, and recycle nutrients fast. Grasses
answer back with silica — phytoliths, microscopic glass particles that wear
teeth down — which is why grazing mammals evolved tall, continuously erupting
molars over the same stretch of the Miocene in which C4 grasses spread
across the tropics and subtropics. Grassland and grazer built each other.

Underground, grasses invest the majority of their biomass in roots that die
and regrow annually, feeding organic matter into the soil at depth rather
than dropping it on top as leaf litter. Over thousands of years that
produces mollisols: dark, crumbly, deep, chemically rich soils, the best
arable land on the planet. Every major grain belt on Earth sits on a former
grassland. The Pampas, the North American prairie, the Ukrainian and Russian
chernozem belt, the Manchurian plain, the Canadian prairie provinces — the
world's bread and beef come from the places where trees could not get a
foothold.

The Pampas is a mild embarrassment to that tidy three-factor account, which
is part of what makes it worth looking at closely. Its humid eastern
portion receives enough rain to grow forest — comparable totals fall on
wooded country elsewhere in the world — and yet the vegetation that
Europeans found was grass almost to the edge of the gallery woodland along
the Paraná. Explanations have been argued over for more than a century: the
sheer flatness and poor drainage, which waterlogs roots; a hard calcium
carbonate layer called tosca sitting a metre or so down and stopping deep
rooting; the near-absence of a native tree flora suited to the conditions;
regular burning, both natural and set; periodic drought and salinity. Most
likely all of them contributed. The useful lesson is that biome boundaries
are rarely drawn by rainfall alone, which is why the introduced eucalypts
around the estancias grow perfectly well.

## The Argentine Case

The Pampas proper runs across Buenos Aires province and into Córdoba, Santa
Fe, La Pampa, Uruguay, and the campos of southern Brazil. It is not
uniform. The humid Pampa in the east gets reliable rain and grows maize,
soy, and wheat; the drier Pampa to the west shades into scrub and was
historically cattle country. Drainage is poor almost everywhere — the land
is so flat that water ponds rather than running off, and large tracts flood
in wet years and turn saline in dry ones.

Its weather comes from two directions and neither is gentle. The pampero
arrives from the southwest behind a cold front, dry and abrupt, stripping
the humidity out of the air in an afternoon; the storms that build ahead of
it are among the most violent anywhere, since the corridor between the
Andes and the Atlantic assembles hail and enormous convective systems on a
scale matched only by the North American plains. From the opposite quarter
the sudestada pushes Atlantic air up the Río de la Plata, piling water into
the estuary and flooding the low ground around Buenos Aires. Rainfall falls
away steadily from northeast to southwest, and the agricultural map follows
that gradient almost line for line.

Its native fauna is a reminder that South America's megafauna was mostly
wiped out at the end of the Pleistocene. Glyptodonts, giant ground sloths,
and the odd-looking native ungulates are gone. What was left when Europeans
arrived was modest: pampas deer, the greater rhea, viscachas colonizing the
ground in warrens, burrowing owls taking over the holes, a rich bird fauna
of seedeaters and tyrant flycatchers. The lone tree in the classic pampa
image, the ombú, is not really a tree at all but a giant herb with soft,
water-filled tissue that will not burn well and will not make a plank.

Into that half-empty grazing niche, in the sixteenth century, the Spanish
introduced cattle and horses, and both went feral on a scale that startled
everyone. Within a few generations the Pampas held wild herds — cimarrones —
that could be hunted for hides and tallow by anyone willing to ride after
them. The gaucho emerged from that economy: a mounted, largely landless
worker in a country where the only capital that mattered was an animal you
could catch. The early export trade was not really beef. It was leather,
tallow, and salted meat from the saladeros, because fresh beef could not
cross an ocean.

## Wire, Rail, and Refrigeration

Three technologies changed that, and with it the whole social order of the
plain. Barbed wire made it cheap to enclose land, which turned open range
into private property and put the gaucho out of business as a free agent.
Railways, built radially from Buenos Aires by British capital, made inland
production worth moving. And refrigerated shipping, from the last decades
of the nineteenth century, let Argentine beef reach European tables. Wheat
followed, immigrants followed from Italy and Spain in enormous numbers, and
by the early twentieth century Argentina was one of the richest countries
in the world on the strength of a grass-fed export economy.

The most recent transformation has been the soybean. Over the last few
decades a package of herbicide-tolerant varieties and no-till planting made
row-cropping viable on land that used to be pasture, and it spread — first
across the Pampas, then north into the Chaco and the Cerrado of Brazil.
Cattle were pushed onto poorer country or into feedlots. No-till has real
virtues, including far less erosion than the plough it replaced, but a
continuous soy rotation returns much less organic matter to the soil than
the grass it displaced, and the fertility that accumulated over millennia
is being spent faster than it is being made.

## The Same Story, Told in Other Languages

Set the world's grasslands side by side and the family resemblance is
obvious. The North American prairie graded from tallgrass in the humid
east through mixed grass to shortgrass against the Rockies, a moisture
gradient laid out across half a continent. The Eurasian steppe ran almost unbroken from
the Hungarian puszta to Mongolia and Manchuria, and its combination of
grass, horses, and mobility produced the mounted nomad confederations that
repeatedly reshaped Eurasian history. The African savannas kept their trees
and kept their megafauna, and are the one case where the full grass-fire-
grazer system still runs with its original cast. South Africa has the veld,
Australia the Mitchell grasslands of Queensland, and the high Andes and
Tibet have cold grasslands where altitude does the work that drought does
elsewhere.

Wherever they occur, grasslands share a set of problems. They are the most
converted biome on Earth and among the least protected, partly because a
flat plain of grass looks like nothing worth saving to people who reserve
their conservation instincts for forests and mountains. Their carbon is
stored underground rather than in visible wood, which means that ploughing
releases it and that nobody photographs the loss. Their fauna is adapted to
moving across long distances, so fencing hurts them in ways it does not hurt
forest animals — the saiga antelope of the Kazakh steppe and the pronghorn
of the American plains both run into infrastructure built for other
purposes. And the ones that are grazed by livestock sit at the centre of an
argument that keeps getting flattened into slogans: whether well-managed
grazing can maintain and even rebuild grassland soil carbon, or whether
that claim is mostly a way of feeling better about beef.

The honest answer is that it depends almost entirely on stocking rate,
rainfall, and how long the land is rested, and that the same practice can be
restorative in one place and ruinous a hundred kilometres away. What is not
in dispute is that the grass itself is durable. Burn a prairie, graze it,
freeze it, and it comes back from buds at ground level. It is the plough
that ends it, because the plough goes after the part of the plant that was
never meant to be exposed.
