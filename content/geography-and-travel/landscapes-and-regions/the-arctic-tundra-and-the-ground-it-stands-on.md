# The Arctic Tundra and the Ground It Stands On

Flying over the Arctic coastal plain in July, the first thing that registers is the geometry. The ground is tiled into polygons a few tens of metres across, each outlined by a shallow trough, like a drying mudflat scaled up by a factor of a thousand. Between the polygons lie thousands of shallow lakes, and the lakes are not randomly shaped. They are ovals, and nearly all of them are elongated in the same direction, as if someone had dragged a comb across the landscape. Nothing living made those patterns. They were made by water freezing and thawing in soil, which in the Arctic is the dominant landscape process, more consequential than rivers or wind or the plants growing on top.

The usual way to introduce the tundra is through its vegetation: no trees, low shrubs, lichens, a short brilliant summer. That is accurate but secondary. The ground comes first, and almost everything else here is a consequence of it.

## Frozen Ground Is a Material With Its Own Rules

Permafrost is simply ground that stays at or below freezing for at least two consecutive years, regardless of what it is made of. Across much of the Arctic it is hundreds of metres thick, laid down over tens of thousands of years, and in places it holds so much ice that the soil is more ice than mineral. Above it sits the active layer, a skin from a few centimetres to a couple of metres deep that thaws each summer and refreezes each autumn. Everything that lives on land lives in that skin.

Frozen ground behaves like rock. It bears loads, holds steep faces, and does not let water through. Thawed ice-rich ground behaves like soup. That difference, and the ease of crossing between the two states, generates the whole repertoire of Arctic landforms. In deep winter the ground contracts as it cools and cracks, the way a ceramic glaze crazes. Meltwater runs into the crack in spring and freezes there, leaving a thin vertical vein of ice. Repeat that for a few centuries and the veins grow into wedges metres wide at the top, and the network of wedges is what draws the polygons visible from the air. Where groundwater is trapped and freezes beneath a drained lakebed, the expanding ice can push a whole hill up from underneath, producing a pingo: a conical mound with an ice core, sometimes tens of metres high, standing alone on a flat plain.

When ice-rich ground thaws, the ice volume leaves as water and the surface drops. Geologists call the resulting collapse landscape thermokarst — sinkholes, slumped hillsides, ponds where there was flat ground, and hummocked terrain that is exhausting to walk across. Large areas of Siberia and interior Alaska are underlain by yedoma, a particularly ice-rich silt that accumulated during the last glacial period and is threaded with the bones of mammoth and steppe bison. When a yedoma bluff on a riverbank begins to fail, it can retreat many metres in a single summer, releasing organic material that has been out of circulation since the Pleistocene.

## A Desert That Is Also a Swamp

Much of the Arctic receives less precipitation than many hot deserts. Cold air holds little water vapour, and the polar atmosphere is not an energetic delivery system. And yet the tundra in summer is waterlogged: standing water everywhere, soggy moss, mosquitoes in numbers that have to be experienced to be credited. The reason is drainage, or its absence. Frozen ground beneath the active layer is effectively impermeable, so the modest amount of meltwater and rain that arrives cannot go anywhere but sideways or up. On flat terrain it simply sits.

Winter is defined less by snow depth than by wind. Snowfall is light, and what falls is redistributed constantly, scoured off ridges and packed into hard drifts in the lee of anything that interrupts the flow. Where snow does collect it is a blanket: under half a metre of it, the ground surface may sit tens of degrees warmer than the air above, which matters enormously to anything trying to survive the winter at ground level. Where the wind strips it away, the ground freezes harder and deeper, and plants exposed to blowing ice crystals are pruned back as though by a blade.

The northern limit of trees tracks summer warmth rather than winter cold, running roughly where the average temperature of the warmest month falls to about ten degrees Celsius, and it wanders across continents accordingly. North of it the growing season is not long enough to build and harden woody tissue above the snowline, and the growing season is what limits nearly everything.

## Life Working at Speed, Then Not at All

Arctic plants are organized around two constraints: a short season and no depth. Roots cannot go below the active layer, so they spread sideways. Almost everything grows low, where the boundary layer of still air is warmest and the winter snow will cover it. Dwarf willow and dwarf birch creep across the ground as woody mats, and a willow stem the thickness of a pencil may be many decades old. Cushion plants like moss campion grow into tight domes that trap heat and hold moisture, adding a few millimetres a year. Arctic poppies and mountain avens carry parabolic flowers that track the sun across the sky and focus warmth into the centre, where insects gather and seeds develop faster than the ambient temperature should allow.

Lichens do the slow work. Mats of reindeer lichen take decades to establish and are the mainstay of winter grazing for caribou, which dig through snow to reach them. Trampling or a summer fire can undo a century of growth, and recovery is measured in human generations.

Against this slowness, the summer is frantic. Insects emerge from meltwater pools in pulses dense enough to drive caribou onto windswept ridges and lingering snow patches for relief, and that harassment shapes the migration routes of whole herds as much as forage does. Shorebirds arrive from as far away as southern South America, nest on open ground, raise chicks on the insect flush, and leave within weeks. Snow geese and brant strip whole coastal flats of vegetation and depart.

Predator populations here are legible in a way they rarely are elsewhere, because the food webs are short. Lemmings build up and crash on a cycle of a few years, breeding under the snow in winter when conditions favour them. In a good lemming year, snowy owls lay large clutches, arctic foxes raise big litters, and jaegers nest successfully. In a crash year the owls may not breed at all and simply leave, which is why they turn up far to the south in some winters and not others. Musk oxen, which do not migrate, survive by minimizing movement, growing an inner layer of wool finer than cashmere beneath their guard hairs, and forming an outward-facing ring when threatened — a tactic that works well against wolves and disastrously against rifles.

Decomposition is the other half of the story. Cold, wet, acidic, oxygen-poor soil breaks organic matter down far more slowly than plants produce it, so dead material accumulates as peat and is eventually locked into permafrost. Over tens of thousands of years this has built a store of soil carbon considerably larger than what the atmosphere currently holds.

## Knowing the Ice

People have lived across the circumpolar north for thousands of years, and the ways of doing it differ sharply between regions. Inuit, Iñupiat, and Yupik societies across Alaska, Canada, and Greenland built their year around the sea and the sea ice: sealing at breathing holes and at the floe edge, whaling from the ice edge in skin or aluminium boats, fishing, and hunting caribou inland in season. Sea ice is not an obstacle in that system, it is infrastructure — the winter road network, the hunting platform, the surface on which a sled travels faster than a boat could. Knowing it means reading colour, texture, sound, and the history of the wind for the past several days, and the vocabulary for ice conditions in Inuit languages is a technical one, distinguishing states that matter for whether a given route will hold.

In northern Fennoscandia and across Siberia, the organizing relationship is with reindeer rather than the sea. Sámi herding families work with semi-domesticated herds that move between coastal summer pasture and inland winter lichen ground. Nenets on the Yamal peninsula run some of the longest land migrations still practised anywhere, moving herds and collapsible pole-and-hide tents hundreds of kilometres a year, crossing frozen rivers on schedule. Chukchi, Even, and Sakha herders maintain related systems further east.

Modern Arctic life layers new costs onto these. Snowmobiles and outboards replaced dogs and sail, which made travel faster and tied hunting to a fuel supply that must be barged or flown in. In fly-in communities, store food is expensive and often poor, so locally taken meat and fish are not a cultural nicety but the difference between eating well and not. Wage work, mining, and administration have pulled people into settlements built by outside governments, frequently in places chosen for an airstrip rather than for hunting.

## Building Where the Ground Might Leave

Every piece of northern infrastructure is a bet about frozen ground. A heated building set directly on permafrost thaws the soil beneath it and sinks unevenly, so structures are raised on piles driven into the frozen layer with an air gap beneath, or set on thick gravel pads that insulate what is below. Water and sewer lines cannot be buried in ground that heaves, so some towns run them above the surface in insulated conduits between buildings. The Trans-Alaska Pipeline crosses hundreds of kilometres on elevated supports fitted with heat pipes — passive devices that carry warmth out of the ground in winter and vent it to the air through finned radiators, keeping the soil around each support frozen.

These bets are becoming harder to win. Warming is faster in the Arctic than nearly anywhere, and the visible consequences are mostly failures of ground rather than of air temperature: buckled runways and roads, tilted apartment blocks in Russian northern cities, foundations requiring repair decades before their design life. Winter ice roads, which supply remote mines and communities by running heavy trucks over frozen lakes and muskeg, open later and close earlier, compressing the season in which a year's freight can move. Along low coasts where permafrost was holding the shoreline together, thaw plus less protective sea ice plus bigger autumn waves have combined to erode metres of land in a season, and several Alaskan villages have been working through the long, expensive process of relocating entirely.

There are subtler shifts too. Shrubs are expanding northward and upward, and taller shrubs catch snow, which insulates the ground in winter and can warm it further. Thawing ground has occasionally released old pathogens along with old carbon; an anthrax outbreak on the Yamal peninsula a decade ago was traced to infected reindeer carcasses exposed by an unusually warm summer. And as ancient organic matter comes out of the freezer, microbes get to work on material that has been unavailable to them since mammoths walked over it, returning carbon dioxide and methane to an atmosphere that is already the reason the ground gave way.
