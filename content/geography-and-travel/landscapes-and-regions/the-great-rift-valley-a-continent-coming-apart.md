# The Great Rift Valley: A Continent Coming Apart

Drive northwest out of Nairobi and after about an hour the road reaches a lip and the ground simply ends. The land drops away in a single step of several hundred metres, and below it a flat floor runs out to a far wall that is visible in clear weather, perhaps fifty kilometres away, hazed blue. Roadside stalls sell sheepskins and carved bowls at the viewpoint. What the viewpoint is actually showing is a piece of continent in the act of being pulled apart — two escarpments that were once joined, a floor that has dropped between them, and a line of volcanoes down the middle where the thinned crust has let magma through.

The geologist John Walter Gregory, working in East Africa near the end of the nineteenth century, recognized the valley for what it was and gave the whole system its popular name. The eastern branch still carries his: the Gregory Rift. Modern usage prefers the East African Rift System, partly because "valley" undersells a structure that runs from the Red Sea to Mozambique and is doing something no other landscape on a continent is currently doing at this scale.

## A Plume, a Flood of Lava, and Two Cracks

The sequence starts underneath. Around thirty million years ago an upwelling of unusually hot mantle arrived beneath what is now Ethiopia and Eritrea, and the crust above it was buried under flood basalts — successive sheets of lava that built a plateau kilometres thick. The Ethiopian highlands are the eroded remains of that event, which is why they are so high, so extensive, and so abruptly dissected by canyons, and why their soils are volcanic rather than the ancient, leached material that covers much of the continent.

Hot mantle beneath a continent does more than erupt. It domes the crust upward and weakens it, and the crust responds by pulling apart along three arms radiating from the Afar region. Two of those arms opened all the way and filled with seawater: the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden. The third runs south into the African interior and is the rift proper, still on land, still early in the same process.

South of Ethiopia the rift divides around an obstacle. The Tanzania craton is a very old, cold, thick block of continental lithosphere that resists deformation, so the rift splits and passes on either side of it. The eastern branch runs through Kenya into northern Tanzania, past Lake Turkana, Naivasha, Magadi, and Natron. The western branch swings through Uganda, along the Congo border, and down through Lakes Albert, Edward, Kivu, and Tanganyika to Malawi. Between the two arms, the ground has sagged gently rather than cracked, forming the shallow basin that holds Lake Victoria.

Rifting does not proceed as a neat trench with parallel walls. It works in segments, each one a half-graben — a block tilted down against a single master fault on one side, with a gentler ramp on the other — and successive segments commonly face opposite ways, linked by tangled accommodation zones where the polarity flips. That geometry sets where the deep water is, where rivers can enter, and where a road can get down the wall. The shoulders alongside the faults are lifted as the floor drops, which is why the rift is flanked in Kenya and Ethiopia by highlands that wring rain out of passing air and enjoy a mild climate directly on the equator.

## Lakes With Nothing in Common but a Name

The rift lakes are usually grouped together and are about as different from one another as lakes can be.

Tanganyika, in the western branch, sits in a deep, narrow, long-lived basin. Its floor lies far below sea level, it is among the deepest lakes anywhere, and it has held water more or less continuously for millions of years. Below a few hundred metres it is permanently anoxic — nothing mixes that deep — so its life is concentrated in a thin, crowded upper layer where hundreds of endemic cichlid species have partitioned the habitat down to individual rock crevices and sand patches.

Victoria could hardly be less alike. It is enormous in area and shallow, a sag pond rather than a fault trough, formed when the uplifting rift shoulders reversed the drainage of rivers that had been flowing west. Sediment cores indicate it has dried out repeatedly, most recently within the last several tens of thousands of years, which means its own celebrated flock of cichlid species radiated with remarkable speed after the basin refilled. In the twentieth century, Nile perch were introduced as a sport and commercial fish; the perch grew into a substantial export industry, and a large proportion of the endemic cichlids vanished or were pushed to the margins within a few decades.

In the closed basins of the eastern branch, water enters and leaves only by evaporation, and dissolved sodium carbonate leached from volcanic rock concentrates until the lakes turn strongly alkaline. Magadi and Natron are caustic enough to preserve carcasses and crust over with trona. They are also, improbably, the foundation of one of Africa's great wildlife spectacles: alkaline-tolerant cyanobacteria bloom in the warm shallows, lesser flamingos filter them in enormous numbers, and Natron's hostile flats provide nesting islands that almost nothing can reach.

Kivu, on the Rwanda-Congo border, has its own peculiarity. Volcanic gases seeping into a deep, stably stratified basin have loaded its bottom water with dissolved carbon dioxide and methane, held down by pressure. Rwanda has built plants to extract the methane for electricity, which generates power and reduces the reservoir of gas at the same time.

## Lava That Comes Out Cool

The rift's volcanoes include ordinary basaltic cones and vast collapsed calderas — Ngorongoro is one, its floor grazed by animals standing inside a volcano's ruins — but the strangest sits just south of Lake Natron. Ol Doinyo Lengai erupts natrocarbonatite, a sodium-carbonate lava that is the coolest known, dark and unusually runny when it emerges, sometimes flowing at night without glowing much. Within days of reaching the air it reacts with moisture and turns chalk white, so the cone looks snow-dusted in a place that has never seen snow. Nothing else on the planet currently does this.

At the far northern end, in the Danakil Depression, the rift floor lies well below sea level. Erta Ale has held an active lava lake for decades. At Dallol, brines rising through salt deposits precipitate yellow, orange, and green terraces of sulphur and potash salts in a landscape that gets as hot as any inhabited place on Earth. Afar people have been cutting slabs of salt out of the flats and carrying them to the highlands by camel for a very long time, and the trade has only recently begun to yield to trucks.

Volcanic ash is also the reason the Serengeti looks the way it does. Repeated ashfalls from Lengai and its predecessors laid down a calcium-rich layer near the surface of the southeastern plains, which cements into hardpan and prevents deep rooting. Trees cannot get established; short grasses can, and they are unusually rich in minerals. That is the nursery ground the wildebeest migration returns to each wet season to calve.

## Why the Fossils Are Here

It is often said that human origins lie in the Rift Valley. The more careful statement is that the rift is where the evidence survives and can be found, which is a consequence of its structure rather than a claim about where our ancestors preferred to live. Subsiding basins accumulate sediment instead of shedding it, so bones washed into a lakeshore get buried rather than weathered away. Volcanoes lay down ash falls between the sediment layers, and those layers can be dated by the decay of potassium in their crystals, which gives the whole sequence a calendar. Continuing faulting tilts the stack, and erosion in an arid landscape with little vegetation cuts down through it and lays the layers out on the surface where a person walking a gully can see them.

Olduvai Gorge, cut into an old lakebed on the edge of the Serengeti, is where Mary and Louis Leakey spent decades and established the sequence of stone tool industries that gave the earliest one its name. At Laetoli, a party of hominins walked across damp volcanic ash more than three million years ago and the ash set, preserving a trail of upright footprints with an arched foot and no grasping toe. In the Afar, the partial skeleton known as Lucy came out of sediments at Hadar in the 1970s, and on the west side of Lake Turkana, Kamoya Kimeu found a scrap of skull that led to the nearly complete skeleton of a young Homo erectus. None of this is because early hominins were confined to East Africa. It is because East Africa keeps good records.

## Making a Living on a Fault

The rift shoulders are, by a wide margin, the most densely populated parts of their countries. Elevation moderates equatorial heat, suppresses malaria, and forces rain out of passing air, and volcanic soils reward the effort of farming. The Ethiopian highlands support an agricultural system found nowhere else, built on teff, a tiny-grained cereal fermented into injera, and in the southwest on enset, a banana relative grown not for fruit but for the starchy pulp scraped from its stem and buried to ferment. The forests of that southwest are also where Arabica coffee grows wild, and where it was first taken into use.

On the rift floor, where rainfall is unreliable, the logic is mobility: Maasai, Samburu, Turkana, Afar, and Borana pastoralists move cattle, camels, sheep, and goats between grazing and water on schedules refined over centuries. That logic has been squeezed from several directions at once — by conservancies and parks, by irrigated farms taking the valley bottoms, by borders, and by droughts arriving too close together for herds to rebuild in between.

Some of the same geology that makes the valley dangerous has become an asset. At Olkaria, near Lake Naivasha, wells tap steam from shallow magma and generate a large share of Kenya's electricity, one of the few places where a country's power supply runs directly on the fact that its continent is splitting. Around the lake itself, greenhouses grow roses for European markets, flown out overnight.

The splitting continues on a schedule of its own. In the Afar, over a few weeks in 2005, magma forced its way into a fissure system near Dabbahu and the ground opened by several metres, with hundreds of earthquakes and a new gash across the desert. Episodes like that, repeated over a few million years, are how an ocean starts. The Red Sea is already on the other side of the sill.
