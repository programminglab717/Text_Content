# Patagonia: Wind, Ice, and the Long Steppe

On the eastern flank of the southern Andes, the trees tell you which way the
wind blows without your having to feel it. Lenga beeches on an exposed slope
carry branches on one side only, the windward face scoured bare, the whole crown
combed downwind into a shape that looks permanently mid-gust even on a still
morning. A few dozen kilometres further east the trees give up entirely and the
land opens into a gravel plain that runs to the Atlantic, and there the wind has
nothing left to sculpt but grass, dust, and the patience of whoever is standing
in it.

Patagonia is usually taken to mean everything south of roughly the Río Colorado
on the Argentine side and the Lake District on the Chilean, down through Tierra
del Fuego to Cape Horn. It is one of the emptiest inhabited regions outside the
poles, and it is emptier in the middle than at the edges. Nearly everything about
it — the wind, the ice, the wool economy that shaped its last century and a half
— follows from two facts of physical geography: a mountain range running
north-south, and a band of ocean winds running west-east with nothing in their
way.

## A Range Built on a Sinking Ocean Floor

The Andes here are the product of subduction, the Pacific seafloor sliding
eastward beneath the South American plate and melting at depth to feed a chain
of volcanoes. But Patagonia complicates the standard picture in an unusual way.
Off the coast near the Taitao Peninsula, an active spreading ridge — the boundary
where the Nazca and Antarctic plates are moving apart — is itself being carried
into the trench and consumed. Where a spreading centre goes down instead of a
cold slab, the subducting plate tears open beneath the continent. The result is a
gap in the volcanic chain, a stretch of several hundred kilometres with no active
arc volcanoes, and an anomalously hot, thin lithosphere that has left its mark on
the landscape in basalt flows and raised plateaus.

South of the gap, and north of it, the volcanoes resume. Hudson, in Aysén, put
enough ash over the Santa Cruz steppe in 1991 to kill livestock and drive
ranchers off the land. Chaitén reawakened in 2008 after a very long silence and
buried its namesake town in ash and lahar.

Much of what climbers come for is older and deeper. The granite of the Paine
massif and of Fitz Roy and Cerro Torre belongs to the Patagonian batholith,
magma that cooled slowly at depth and has since been stripped of its cover. The
Torres del Paine are a particularly legible piece of geology: the pale towers are
granite, and the dark caps on top of them are the much older sedimentary rock
that the granite intruded into. The intrusion is young, the roof is old, and
glaciers cut the whole assembly into vertical faces while everyone else in the
world was inventing agriculture.

Ice is the other great shaping force. During the Pleistocene glacial maxima an
ice sheet covered the southern Andes and spilled east onto the plains. What
remains are the Northern and Southern Patagonian Icefields, the largest bodies of
ice in the southern hemisphere outside Antarctica, and a chain of enormous
finger lakes — Argentino, Viedma, General Carrera — sitting in troughs the
glaciers gouged and dammed with moraine. On the Chilean side, the same troughs
drowned by the sea became the fjords and channels of the archipelago. On the
Argentine side, meltwater rivers spread out sheets of rounded cobbles, the
rodados patagónicos, that still armour the surface of the steppe and make it
ring underfoot.

## The Wind and the Shadow It Casts

Between about forty and fifty-five degrees south there is almost no land to
interrupt the westerlies. They blow across thousands of kilometres of Southern
Ocean and arrive at the Chilean coast saturated and cold. Forced up over the
Andes, the air cools and unloads: the outer islands and the western slopes are
among the wettest places on earth, with several metres of rain a year and a
persistent grey ceiling. Having wrung itself out, the same air descends the
eastern slope, warming and drying as it sinks, and within forty or fifty
kilometres the annual rainfall drops by an order of magnitude.

That is the Patagonian steppe: a cold desert in the lee of a wet one. Cool
summers, long winters that are less severe than the latitude suggests because the
ocean is never far, and wind that is not a weather event but a background
condition. Spring and early summer are the worst of it. Gusts strip topsoil,
flatten grass, and make the few roads a matter of steering into the crosswind.
Guidebooks that describe Patagonia as having four seasons in a day are describing
a real phenomenon: air masses move so quickly across an unobstructed plain that
the sky can go from clear to sleet and back in an hour.

## Two Ecologies, Forty Kilometres Apart

The forests of the Chilean side are relict temperate rainforest of a kind that
survives in only a few places. Their dominant trees are southern beeches,
Nothofagus — the evergreen coihue, the deciduous lenga that burns orange in
April, the stunted ñire on wind-blasted ground. Further north grows the alerce, a
conifer that can live for thousands of years and whose rot-resistant timber was
cut out of the valleys wholesale in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The
understory is often impenetrable bamboo. The biogeographic point is worth
pausing on: Nothofagus also grows in New Zealand, Tasmania, New Guinea and New
Caledonia, which is one of the older and more elegant lines of evidence that
these landmasses were once joined.

East of the divide the flora is entirely different. Tussocks of coirón grass,
low cushion shrubs like neneo, thorny calafate with its dark fruit, and vast
areas where bare gravel shows between the plants. Grazing has thinned it further;
much of the Santa Cruz steppe carries visible scars of desertification, with
deflation hollows and shifting sand where the vegetation mat was broken.

The characteristic animal of the steppe is the guanaco, a wild camelid that
moves in bands, sometimes at a startling pace, and whose alarm call is a kind of
indignant laugh. With it go the lesser rhea, a flightless bird that runs with its
neck low; the Patagonian mara, a long-legged rodent that behaves like a small
antelope; armadillos; foxes; and above everything the puma, which in the
protected valleys of the far south has become unusually visible after decades of
persecution eased. The coast has its own economy: Magellanic penguins in
colonies of hundreds of thousands, southern right whales calving in the gulfs of
Península Valdés, elephant seals hauling out to moult, and the orcas of Punta
Norte, which have learned to drive themselves partway up a shingle beach to take
sea lion pups and then wriggle back into the surf.

## How People Have Made a Living Here

The steppe supported hunting peoples for many thousands of years — the Aónikenk,
usually called Tehuelche, who followed guanaco on foot and later on horseback,
and the Selk'nam of Tierra del Fuego. The channels and fjords supported something
rarer: the Kawésqar and the Yámana, canoe peoples who lived largely on the water,
diving for shellfish in near-freezing seas and carrying a fire on a bed of sand
in the bottom of the boat. It was those fires, seen from a passing ship, that
gave Tierra del Fuego its name.

The nineteenth century ended that world with brutal speed. Argentina's campaign
against the southern Indigenous nations broke Tehuelche society and opened the
land to settlement; in Tierra del Fuego, sheep companies paid bounties for
Selk'nam lives. What replaced them was wool. Sheep were brought in from the
Falklands and from Europe, holdings were measured in tens of thousands of
hectares, and Punta Arenas became a genuinely wealthy port because every ship
rounding the Horn had to pass its door. The Panama Canal took most of that trade
away. Immigration came from Britain, Croatia, Spain and Chile, and, in one
peculiar case, from Wales: a colony settled the lower Chubut valley in the 1860s
looking for a place to keep their language, irrigated the floodplain, and left
behind chapels, place names, and a surviving tradition of tea houses.

Ranching is no longer the whole story, and in much of the steppe it is barely
half of it. Wool prices, overgrazing and the Hudson ashfall emptied many
estancias. What has grown instead is unevenly distributed: oil and gas in
Neuquén and Chubut, aluminium and shipping at Puerto Madryn, hydroelectricity on
the Limay and Santa Cruz rivers, salmon farming through the Chilean fjords with
all the disputes that industry brings anywhere, and tourism concentrated in a few
places — El Chaltén, El Calafate, Torres del Paine — that are crowded for four
months and shuttered for the rest. Large conservation properties bought from
failing ranches have been converted to parkland and stocked back up with the
species the sheep displaced.

The ice, meanwhile, is going. Nearly every glacier in both icefields is thinning
and retreating, some of them dramatically, and the lakes at their snouts are
growing. The exception everyone photographs is Perito Moreno, which has held
roughly its position and periodically pushes across a narrow arm of Lago
Argentino, sealing it off until the impounded water undercuts the ice and brings
an arch of it down in front of an audience. It is a good show. It is also not
representative of anything, which is worth remembering when you see the pictures.
