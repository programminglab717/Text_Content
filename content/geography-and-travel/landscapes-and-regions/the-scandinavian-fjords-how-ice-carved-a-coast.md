# The Scandinavian Fjords: How Ice Carved a Coast

A ferry crossing the Sognefjord passes over water more than a kilometre
deep, with rock walls rising a kilometre above it on either side, and the
whole structure is narrow enough that you can hear a tractor working on the
far shore. Two vertical kilometres of relief and perhaps two horizontal
ones. Nothing in a river's repertoire produces a cross-section like that. A
river cuts a V, because its erosive power is concentrated in a thin,
turbulent thread at the bottom of the channel. The fjord is a U, steep-
walled and flat-floored, and it is a U because the thing that cut it was
filling the valley from wall to wall.

Fjords are glacial valleys drowned by the sea. That one sentence covers the
outcome but skips everything interesting about the process, including the
part that seems impossible: much of a fjord floor lies far below sea level,
and glaciers, unlike rivers, are perfectly capable of digging downward past
it.

## Setting the Stage: Old Mountains, Old Surfaces

Norway's bedrock is old. The Caledonian mountains were thrown up something
like four hundred million years ago when the continental fragments of
Baltica and Laurentia collided, and they were subsequently worn down over
an enormous span of time to low, rolling terrain. The whole edge of
Scandinavia was then uplifted again, tilting that ancient landscape
upward. Remnants of it survive as the paleic surface — the broad, gentle,
almost boring plateaus you find on top of the fjord walls, where the
country looks like moorland until you walk to the edge and it drops away.

On that raised block, rivers did the preliminary work. They cut valleys
along the lines of least resistance: fracture zones, shear zones, softer
bands of rock. Those valleys set the pattern, which is why fjord systems
have the branching, angular geometry of a drainage network rather than the
random arrangement you might expect from ice. The glaciers that came later
did not choose where to go. They inherited the route.

## What Ice Does That Water Cannot

Once the Quaternary ice ages began, ice accumulated on the plateaus and
drained seaward down those pre-existing valleys. Where a broad ice sheet
funnels into a confined valley, the ice speeds up dramatically — an ice
stream — and erosion goes roughly with flux and velocity. A valley that
already carried water now collects ice from a huge catchment and forces it
through a narrow gate. The more it deepens, the more ice it captures, and
the faster it deepens. The feedback is savage, and it is why fjords are so
out of proportion to the land around them.

Glacial erosion works by two mechanisms. Abrasion is the grinding of rock
flour and larger clasts dragged along in the basal ice, which polishes and
scratches bedrock into the striations and smoothed roches moutonnées you
see on every scoured outcrop in Norway. Plucking is more violent: meltwater
works into joints, refreezes, welds the ice to blocks of rock, and rips
them out as the ice moves on. Plucking is what steepens the walls and
squares off the profile.

Now the sea-level problem. A river cannot cut below the level of the water
body it drains into, because at that point it has no gradient left and
stops flowing. A glacier is not driven by the slope of the ground but by
the slope of its own upper surface. As long as the ice surface keeps
descending toward the terminus, the ice at the bottom will keep moving —
uphill if necessary. So a glacier can gouge a basin hundreds of metres
below sea level and shove the debris out ahead of it. This is overdeepening,
and it is the defining trick of fjord formation.

It also explains the shallow bar at the mouth. Near the outer coast the
valley opens out, the ice spreads, thins, slows, and loses its erosive
grip. The result is a sill or threshold across the entrance, often only a
hundred or two hundred metres deep, guarding an inner basin more than a
kilometre deep. Sognefjord is the classic case: over two hundred kilometres
long, well over a kilometre deep in its interior, and far shallower where
it meets open water.

The accessory features follow from the same physics. Tributary glaciers
were smaller and cut less deeply, so their valleys are left hanging on the
walls — which is why the fjords are lined with waterfalls dropping straight
into salt water, and why the Seven Sisters above Geirangerfjord exist at
all. Ridges between tributary valleys were shaved off into truncated spurs,
which is why the walls are so nearly straight. Bowl-shaped cirques sit high
in the side valleys where small glaciers rotated in place.

When the ice finally left, the crust it had been pressing down began to
rise. That isostatic rebound is still going on; parts of the Gulf of
Bothnia are emerging from the sea at a rate that visibly changes harbours
over a human lifetime, and raised beaches and old shorelines sit well above
the present waterline along much of the coast.

## A Fjord Is Not Just a Shape

The sill does more than mark the entrance. It controls the water inside.
Rivers and snowmelt pour freshwater into the head of a fjord, where it
floats as a brackish layer over dense ocean water, and the two circulate in
an estuarine pattern — outflow at the surface, a compensating inflow
beneath. But the deep basin below sill depth is cut off from that exchange.
It is renewed only when unusually dense water gathers on the outer coast,
spills over the threshold, and sinks. In fjords where that happens rarely,
oxygen in the deep water is slowly used up, and the bottom goes anoxic.

For life, this makes fjords idiosyncratic. Cold, dark, sheltered walls host
reefs of stony cold-water coral — Norway has some of the largest known,
sitting in water far too cold and deep for any tropical relative. Kelp
forests fringe the outer coast. Herring use deep fjords as overwintering
grounds, and killer whales come in after them, which is why one of the best
places in Europe to watch orcas is a Norwegian fjord in the dark of
November.

The regional climate is equally particular. The North Atlantic Drift keeps
the coast far warmer than its latitude deserves, so harbours stay ice-free
north of the Arctic Circle while ports at similar latitudes in Canada
freeze solid. Moist Atlantic air hitting the coastal mountains is forced
up and wrings itself out, which is why Bergen has a national reputation for
rain. Push inland past two or three ranges, though, and the same air has
nothing left. The inner valleys at the heads of the long fjords are among
the driest places in Northern Europe, dry enough that farms there have
irrigated their fields for centuries.

## Living on a Ledge

Flat land in fjord country is scarce and therefore precious. Farms cluster
on the strandflat — the low, wave-cut bench along the outer coast — and on
alluvial fans and terraces at the heads of the fjords and the mouths of
side valleys, where streams have dumped enough sediment to make a shelf. On
the slopes between, hay was cut by hand on ground too steep to stand on
comfortably, and stored in barns reachable only on foot. Hardanger grows
apples, plums, and cherries at a latitude that should not permit them,
because deep water moderates the frosts and long midsummer days make up in
light what the season lacks in heat.

Until recently the water was the road. Boats served villages that had no
land connection at all, and the mail boat was the timetable a community
lived by. Modern Norway has attacked that isolation with engineering of
remarkable ambition for a country of its size: ferry links, submarine
tunnels under the fjords, and the Lærdal Tunnel, which runs nearly
twenty-five kilometres through the mountains and remains the longest road
tunnel in the world.

The same terrain provides the country's other great asset. High rainfall,
steep drops, and natural lake reservoirs make Norway close to ideal for
hydroelectricity, and it generates almost all of its power that way.
Energy-hungry industries were deliberately planted at the fjord heads in
the early twentieth century, next to the turbines — the aluminium and
carbide towns of Odda, Høyanger, and Årdal grew up as single-employer
settlements at the end of a fjord, wired to a waterfall. Salmon farming
later added a second fjord industry, and with it a running argument about
sea lice, escapes, and what intensive aquaculture does to the wild stocks
in the rivers.

The same walls that make the view also make the hazard. Rock slides off
steep fjord sides displace enormous volumes of water, and the resulting
waves have destroyed communities — Loen and Tafjord both suffered
catastrophic slide-generated waves in the first half of the twentieth
century, with heavy loss of life. Several unstable mountainsides are now
monitored continuously with instruments that watch for millimetres of creep,
because the question is not whether a large slab will eventually fall but
when, and how much warning the villages below will get.
