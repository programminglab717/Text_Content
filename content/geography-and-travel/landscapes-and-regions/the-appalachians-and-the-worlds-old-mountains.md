# The Appalachians and the World's Old Mountains

The rock under your boots on a ridge in the Great Smokies was, for a long stretch
of its existence, ten or fifteen kilometres below the surface. It is schist and
gneiss because it was cooked and squeezed at pressures you only get at that
depth. Everything that used to lie above it — the peaks, the volcanic cover, the
unmetamorphosed sediment, the kilometres of overburden that supplied the
pressure — has been taken away grain by grain and delivered to the Atlantic
coastal plain and the Gulf of Mexico. What you are standing on is not a mountain
range. It is the root of one, exhumed.

That is the defining condition of old mountains everywhere. Young ranges show you
their construction: the Himalaya and the Andes are still being pushed up faster
than erosion can take them down, and their shapes are tectonic shapes. Old ranges
show you their interiors, and their shapes are carved rather than built —
sculpted out of whatever the rock happened to leave behind when it was worn into
the arrangement we see.

## Four Collisions and an Ocean That Closed

The Appalachians were not made in one event. They are the cumulative result of a
sequence of them, stretched across hundreds of millions of years, and the
underlying story is a complete Wilson cycle: an ocean opening, then closing
again, with the continents on either side eventually welded together.

The oldest rocks in the range predate all of it. The basement exposed in the Blue
Ridge and in the Adirondacks belongs to the Grenville orogeny, a continental
collision of around a billion years ago that built a mountain chain of Himalayan
scale across what is now eastern North America. That range was long gone before
the Appalachian story began. In its place a rift opened, widened, and became the
Iapetus Ocean, and a broad carbonate shelf accumulated along the passive margin
in warm shallow water — limestones that would later become the floors of valleys
and the caves beneath them.

Then Iapetus began to close. First an offshore volcanic island arc rode into the
continental margin, crumpling the shelf and shoving slices of ocean crust onto
it. Later, a microcontinent geologists call Avalonia arrived from the other side,
adding terranes that now make up much of coastal New England and the Canadian
Maritimes. Finally, in the late Palaeozoic, Africa collided with North America as
Pangaea assembled, and the accumulated pile was driven inland along enormous
low-angle thrust faults, sheets of rock tens of kilometres across sliding
westward over younger strata like cards pushed across a table.

Pangaea did not last. When the Atlantic opened in the Mesozoic, the suture tore
open slightly to the east of where it had formed, which is why the far side of
the same orogenic belt is now on the far side of the ocean. The Scottish
Highlands, the Norwegian Caledonides, and parts of Ireland are the Appalachians'
severed northern continuation. Further south, the Variscan belts of Iberia,
France and Morocco's Anti-Atlas record the same Pangaean collision from the other
plate. The Urals, the Cape Fold Belt of South Africa, and the ranges of eastern
Australia are the same age and the same kind of thing: worn-down welds where
continents were joined.

## Why the Ridges Run in Rows

The Appalachians are conventionally divided into belts that run the length of the
chain, and the division is not arbitrary. West of the crystalline core lies the
Valley and Ridge, where the sedimentary cover was folded and faulted but never
deeply metamorphosed, and where erosion has done something beautifully legible
with it. The folded layers alternate between resistant quartz sandstone and weak
shale and limestone. Weathering eats the weak rock down into long valleys and
leaves the sandstone standing as long, narrow, remarkably continuous ridges. Look
at a relief map of central Pennsylvania and you see a set of hairpins and
zigzags, which are simply the plunging noses of folds intersected by the ground
surface.

West of that, the Appalachian Plateau — Allegheny in the north, Cumberland in the
south — was lifted without being folded much at all. Its rocks are nearly
horizontal, and streams have dissected them into a maze of steep-sided hollows
with no through-valleys, which is why the plateau's settlement pattern consists
of ribbons of houses along creek bottoms. Among those flat-lying beds are the
coal seams, laid down in the Carboniferous swamps of the foreland basin that
sagged in front of the rising mountains.

One of the better puzzles in the range is that several rivers cut straight
through the ridges instead of running around them. The Susquehanna, the Potomac
and the New River all slice across the structural grain in deep water gaps. A
river cannot erode a ridge that already blocks it. The explanation has to be that
the drainage was established first — on a surface that has since been stripped
away — and that the rivers cut down into the resistant structure as it was
exposed beneath them, holding their courses the whole time.

## The Problem of a Range That Won't Go Away

Here is the thing that should bother you. The last major mountain-building event
in the Appalachians ended roughly a quarter of a billion years ago. Erosion at
even modest rates should have reduced the range to a low plain many times over in
that interval. Mount Mitchell, in North Carolina, still stands over two thousand
metres.

Part of the answer is isostasy. The crust floats on the mantle, and a mountain
range has a deep low-density root beneath it. Strip material off the top and the
root rises to compensate, so removing a kilometre of rock lowers the summit by
considerably less than a kilometre. That buys time, but not this much time.

The rest of the answer is that the Appalachians appear to have been rejuvenated.
Measured erosion rates across much of the range are genuinely slow — tens of
metres per million years — yet river profiles show knickpoints and incision that
suggest relatively recent uplift. Seismic imaging has found a region of hot, slow
mantle beneath the central Appalachians, and one live hypothesis is that
buoyant upwelling there has been lifting the surface long after the tectonics
that built the range went quiet. Old mountains are not necessarily finished.

## A Forest That Was Never Under Ice

The Pleistocene ice sheets reached into Pennsylvania and stopped. Everything
south of that limit — the whole southern Appalachians — went unglaciated, and
that single fact is why the region is biologically extraordinary.

Plants and animals in Europe, pushed south by advancing ice, ran into the
Alps and the Mediterranean and were trapped. In eastern North America the
mountains run north-south, so species simply shifted down the chain and back
again with each glacial cycle. Nothing was eliminated by geometry. The result is
the mixed mesophytic forest of the Cumberland Plateau and the southern Blue
Ridge, which the botanist E. Lucy Braun documented in detail in the mid-twentieth
century: a canopy with no single dominant, in which a dozen or more tree species
share the top layer, one of the richest temperate forests anywhere.

The salamanders are the signature. The southern Appalachians hold the greatest
diversity of salamanders on Earth, most of them lungless plethodontids that
breathe through their skin and therefore need cool, permanently damp ground.
Those requirements make every high ridge an island, and species have diverged on
them accordingly, so that neighbouring mountains hold different animals. The
rivers are equally distinctive: the Tennessee and Cumberland drainages support
the world's richest temperate assemblage of freshwater mussels, darters and
crayfish, a fauna that has been quietly devastated by dams and silt.

The highest summits carry spruce and fir left behind from the glacial-era boreal
forest, marooned above the hardwoods. Those relicts have been hit hard by
introduced insects — the balsam woolly adelgid killing Fraser fir, the hemlock
woolly adelgid working through the hemlock groves below. And running under all of
it is the memory of the American chestnut, once a dominant canopy tree across the
whole range, which a fungal blight introduced around the beginning of the
twentieth century reduced within a few decades to root sprouts that die before
they fruit.

## Living in the Folds

The valleys were Cherokee, Shawnee and Haudenosaunee country before European
settlement pushed through the gaps; the Cherokee were removed west in 1838,
though a community remained in the mountains of western North Carolina and holds
land there still. The settlers who followed farmed bottomland, ran hogs in the
woods, and built an economy that worked on a small scale and had very little
surplus.

Industry arrived from outside and extracted. The timber boom around the turn of
the twentieth century cut the mountains over so comprehensively — and the floods
and fires that followed were bad enough — that Congress authorised the federal
purchase of eastern watersheds, which is the origin of the national forests here.
Coal followed the same pattern: company towns, company scrip, mine wars fought
with rifles in West Virginia, unionisation, black lung, and then mechanisation
that removed the jobs while leaving the extraction in place. Mountaintop removal
mining later took the process to its logical end by disposing of the summits into
the valley heads.

What is left is a region with the country's oldest mountains, some of its
richest forests, its thinnest rural infrastructure, and a long habit of sending
away both its resources and its young people. The gas being drilled out of the
Marcellus shale beneath Pennsylvania and West Virginia is Devonian black mud
deposited in the basin in front of the rising Appalachians — the range, in the
end, still being mined for a mountain-building event that finished before there
were reptiles.
