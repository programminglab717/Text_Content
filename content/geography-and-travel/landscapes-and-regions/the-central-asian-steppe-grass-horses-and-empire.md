# The Central Asian Steppe: Grass, Horses, and Empire

For two or three weeks in April, parts of the Kazakh steppe turn red. Wild
tulips — the ancestors of the ones sold in Dutch bunches — come up out of the
thawing ground, flower hard and fast on a landscape that is otherwise the colour
of straw, and are gone by the time the heat arrives. It is the one moment of the
year when the steppe looks generous. The rest of it is an exercise in living off
something that is thin, seasonal, and spread very widely.

The belt of grassland that produces this runs from the Hungarian plain across
Ukraine and southern Russia, past the Volga and the southern Urals, over the
whole width of Kazakhstan, and on through Dzungaria into Mongolia and Manchuria:
roughly eight thousand kilometres of more or less continuous open country. It has
no significant north-south barriers along most of its length. That single
geographical fact — a corridor, not a set of basins — determined the history of
half of Eurasia.

## Old Rock, Thin Soil, No Ocean

The core of the steppe is not flat in the way a plain is flat. Northern and
central Kazakhstan is underlain by the Saryarka, an eroded Palaeozoic massif
whose mountains have been worn down to low hills and to knots of weathered
granite that stand up out of the grass in rounded blocks. To the west the ground
falls away into the Caspian Depression, a good deal of which is below sea level.
Between the southern Urals and the Kazakh uplands lies the Turgay corridor, a
broad low gap that has served for millennia as the easiest route between the
Siberian forest and the dry lands of Turan, for animals and armies alike.

The soils run in bands that track the rainfall. In the north, where moisture is
adequate and grass grows tall and dies back thickly every autumn, centuries of
root decay have built chernozem — deep black earth, among the most fertile soil
on the planet, and the reason this latitude grows wheat. Further south the grass
thins, the organic layer thins with it, and chernozem gives way to chestnut soils
and then to the grey, salt-crusted ground of semi-desert like the Betpak-Dala,
and eventually to the true deserts of the Kyzylkum and Karakum.

Climate does the sorting. Central Asia sits about as far from any ocean as land
can get, walled off from the Indian Ocean monsoon by the Himalaya and the Pamir.
Summers are hot and evaporative; winters are dominated by the Siberian high, an
enormous anticyclone of dense cold air that settles over the interior and
produces weeks of clear, windless, ferocious cold. Most of the year's
precipitation falls in spring and early summer, which is exactly when the grass
needs it and why the growing season is short and front-loaded. Snow is dry and
shallow and gets redistributed by wind into drifts, leaving some slopes swept
bare — which, as it happens, is what makes winter grazing possible at all.

That arrangement fails catastrophically every so often. The Mongolian word is
dzud: a winter that kills livestock. It can come as snow too deep for animals to
paw through, or as a thaw-freeze cycle that glazes the grass in ice, or as the
delayed consequence of a dry summer that left the herds without enough fat to
burn. A bad dzud can kill millions of animals in a season and bankrupt families
who were prosperous the previous autumn. It is the single fact that most shapes
how pastoral households think about risk, and it explains the old logic of
spreading animals among relatives across distant pastures rather than
concentrating them.

## What Lives on Grass

The steppe evolved with large grazers, and the grasses show it: feather grass and
fescue grow from a crown at the base, so being cropped short does not kill them,
and the regrowth is more nutritious than the standing dead material it replaced.
Wormwood and other aromatic shrubs increase as you go south and dry.

The signature wild grazer is the saiga, an antelope with a bulbous, downturned
nose that filters and warms air, which migrates in herds across Kazakhstan and
Kalmykia. Its recent history is a compressed lesson in everything that can happen
to a steppe animal. Poaching for horn collapsed the population after the Soviet
collapse. In 2015 a bacterial infection, apparently triggered by unusual warmth
and humidity acting on a normally harmless gut organism, killed an enormous
fraction of one calving aggregation within days. And then, under sustained
anti-poaching effort, numbers rebounded so strongly that Kazakhstan began
arguing about culls. Alongside the saiga are marmots and ground squirrels whose
burrowing turns over the soil across vast areas, corsac foxes, wolves, steppe
eagles hunting the rodents, demoiselle cranes, and bustards. Przewalski's horse,
which went extinct in the wild, has been returned to Mongolia from zoo stock and
is breeding on the Hustai grasslands.

## What the Horse Made Possible

People were on the steppe long before they could use it properly. The
transformation began with horses. Northern Kazakhstan's Botai sites, from around
the fourth millennium BCE, preserve evidence of horse keeping — bit wear on
teeth, residues in pottery consistent with mare's milk — although genetic work
has since complicated the picture, and the lineage from which almost all modern
domestic horses descend now appears to trace to the steppes of the lower Volga
and Don somewhat later. Wheeled wagons came in around the same broad period, and
by roughly 2000 BCE the Sintashta settlements on the southern Ural fringe were
burying men with spoke-wheeled chariots and teams of horses.

What followed was one of the more consequential shifts in human history. Riding
turned a low-productivity landscape into a viable one: a mounted household can
manage far more animals over far more ground than a walking one, can move a whole
camp between seasonal pastures, and can reach markets, allies and enemies
hundreds of kilometres away. Add a composite bow that can be drawn and shot from
the saddle, and the same mobility becomes military. The sequence of steppe powers
that followed — Scythians, Xiongnu, Huns, Türks, Khazars, Kipchaks, and finally
the Mongols under Chinggis Khan — were not a series of unrelated invasions but
recurring expressions of the same advantage.

The relationship with the settled world was never simply predatory. The oasis
cities of Transoxiana — Samarkand, Bukhara, Merv, Otrar — needed the steppe for
horses, hides, wool and safe passage; the herders needed grain, metal, and
textiles. The caravan routes we collectively call the Silk Road ran on that
symbiosis, and the periods of greatest trade were generally the periods when a
single steppe power was strong enough to make the roads safe.

The material culture is a direct response to movement. The dwelling is the felt
tent — ger in Mongolian, kiiz üy in Kazakh — a lattice of wooden walls, a ring of
roof poles pinned into a wooden crown, and layers of felt beaten from the sheep's
own wool, the whole thing collapsible and loadable in about an hour. The herds
are traditionally reckoned as five kinds: sheep, goats, cattle, horses and
Bactrian camels, each tolerating different forage and weather, together providing
a portfolio rather than a bet. Milk is preserved by souring, drying and
fermenting — kumis from mares, shubat from camels, rock-hard curd balls that keep
for a year. Almost nothing is wasted, because almost nothing can be replaced
locally.

## The Plough, the Famine, and the Grass Coming Back

The steppe's political independence ended with gunpowder and fortresses. Russian
expansion pushed fortified lines south through the eighteenth and nineteenth
centuries, Cossack and peasant settlers took the best-watered northern land, and
grazing ranges were progressively cut. The most violent rupture came with Soviet
collectivisation in the early 1930s, when the forced seizure of herds and the
forced settlement of nomads produced a famine that killed something on the order
of a third of the Kazakh population and permanently ended mobile pastoralism as
the society's organising form.

Two decades later, Khrushchev's Virgin Lands campaign put the plough to tens of
millions of hectares of northern Kazakh steppe, importing labour and machinery on
a huge scale. The first harvests were enormous. Then the topsoil began to leave
in dust storms, as it had in the American plains a generation earlier, and much
of the marginal land was found not to be wheat country after all. Elsewhere on
the steppe the Soviet state placed things that required emptiness: the
Semipalatinsk nuclear test site, where hundreds of detonations left a legacy of
contamination and illness, and the Baikonur cosmodrome.

What happened after 1991 was almost the opposite. State and collective farms
dissolved, subsidies vanished, livestock numbers crashed, and millions of
hectares of marginal cropland were simply abandoned. Grass came back. Some of the
recovery has been substantial enough to register as a carbon sink and to give
saiga, bustards and steppe eagles room they had not had in decades, and large
conservation initiatives now work to link those recovering ranges into corridors
along the old migration routes.

The people mostly did not come back with it. Kazakhstan's wealth is in oil,
uranium and grain, and its new capital rises out of the flat steppe at a latitude
that guarantees a brutal winter. In Mongolia, roughly half the population now
lives in Ulaanbaatar, a great deal of it in ger districts on the city's edge —
households who kept their tents but lost their animals, often to a single bad
dzud, and who heat with coal through a winter that traps the smoke in the valley.
The tents are the same. The distances they were designed for are gone.
