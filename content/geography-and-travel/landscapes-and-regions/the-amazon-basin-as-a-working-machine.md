# The Amazon Basin as a Working Machine

A few kilometres downstream of Manaus, two rivers run side by side inside one channel without mixing. The Rio Negro arrives black as strong tea; the Solimões arrives the colour of milky coffee. They travel together for several kilometres, the boundary between them sharp enough to photograph, before turbulence finally folds one into the other. Tour boats sell the spectacle as a curiosity. It is actually a readout: two rivers with different densities, different temperatures, and radically different chemistry, draining two parts of a basin that work in completely different ways.

Read across its whole extent, the Amazon behaves less like scenery than like plumbing and metabolism. Water arrives, gets used several times over, and leaves. Nutrients arrive rarely and are hoarded ferociously. Almost everything distinctive about the place follows from those two facts.

## The River That Used to Run the Other Way

The basin is bounded north and south by two ancient blocks of rock, the Guiana Shield and the Brazilian Shield, both among the oldest surfaces on the planet and both worn down to rolling uplands studded with table mountains. Between them lies a long structural trough. For much of its history, drainage in that trough ran west, toward the Pacific, because there was nothing in the west to stop it.

Then the Andes went up. Subduction along South America's western margin raised a mountain wall across the old outlet, and the trapped drainage backed up into an enormous system of lakes, swamps, and brackish embayments that persisted for millions of years. Fossil beds from that period contain freshwater dolphins, giant caimans, and molluscs found nowhere else. Eventually the ponded water broke through eastward across a low structural high in the middle of the continent, and the modern Amazon was established, flowing from the young mountains to the Atlantic across the flattest continental surface of comparable size anywhere.

The flatness is easy to underestimate. At Iquitos, some three thousand kilometres from the sea, the water surface sits little more than a hundred metres above sea level. Over most of its lower course the river falls a few centimetres per kilometre, which is why it meanders so extravagantly, abandons channels, and spreads sideways across a floodplain tens of kilometres wide rather than cutting down. Tides reach far inland, and at the mouth an incoming spring tide can pile into a bore, the pororoca, that runs upstream against the current. The volume moving through all this is close to a fifth of all the river water entering the world's oceans, enough to leave a detectable freshwater lens on the Atlantic hundreds of kilometres offshore.

## Three Kinds of Water

The colour difference at Manaus reflects where each tributary has been. Whitewater rivers — the Solimões, the Madeira, the Ucayali — rise in the Andes and carry a heavy suspended load of freshly ground rock: silt, clay, and with it phosphorus, calcium, and other nutrients in usable quantities. They are the fertilizing rivers. The floodplains they build, called várzea, receive a fresh coat of Andean mud every year and are by a wide margin the most productive land in the basin.

Blackwater rivers like the Negro drain ancient, leached sandy lowlands where there is nothing left to erode but quartz. What they carry instead is dissolved organic matter, tannins and humic acids leached from slowly decomposing litter, which stains the water and makes it acidic. Blackwater floodplains, the igapó, are nutrient-poor and support a distinctly different forest. Clearwater rivers such as the Tapajós and the Xingu drain the old shields, carry little sediment and little dissolved organic matter, and run transparent and green.

Over the mainstem, the whole system breathes once a year. The flood pulse raises the river by something like ten metres at Manaus and pushes water kilometres back into the forest, where trees stand submerged to the lower branches for months at a stretch. Fish move into the flooded woods and feed there on fruit and seeds dropping from the canopy. The tambaqui, a deep-bodied relative of the piranhas, has molar-like teeth for cracking hard seeds, including those of rubber trees, and passes many of them intact — making it one of the more important seed dispersers in the basin and a good illustration of how thoroughly the aquatic and terrestrial halves of this place are the same system. Communities time their year to that rise and fall: gardens go in as the water drops, cattle move to higher ground as it climbs, houses are built on floats or stilts, and the fishing calendar follows the fish into and out of the trees.

## The Forest Runs Its Own Weather

Rain falling on the western basin has usually fallen several times already. A mature rainforest returns a large fraction of the water it receives to the atmosphere through transpiration and evaporation from wet leaves, and because the prevailing winds blow inland off the Atlantic, that moisture is carried further west and rains out again. The same water can be recycled repeatedly on its way across the continent, which is why the interior of the basin stays wet despite being enormously far from the ocean. When the airflow reaches the Andes it turns south, and much of the moisture that eventually falls on the agricultural heartland of southern Brazil, Paraguay, and northern Argentina has passed through Amazonian leaves on the way.

The forest also shifts the timing of its own rainy season. Toward the end of the southern dry season, trees in parts of the basin flush new leaves while the soil still holds deep water, and the extra transpiration adds enough moisture to the air to help trigger the transition to wet-season convection weeks before the large-scale circulation alone would deliver it. A forest that behaves this way has a stake in its own continuity: remove enough of it and the dry season lengthens, which stresses what remains.

## Wealth Held in the Living, Not the Ground

Visitors are often startled to learn that the soil under the world's greatest forest is mostly poor. Much of the lowland basin is covered by deeply weathered, acidic clays from which rain has stripped the soluble nutrients over millions of years, leaving iron and aluminium oxides behind. In patches of white sand the leaching has gone further still, producing bleached podzols that support a stunted, scrubby vegetation quite unlike the surrounding forest.

The forest copes by keeping its capital above ground and recycling it fast. Leaf litter on the floor is thin because it decomposes within months in the heat and damp. Dense mats of fine roots and fungal threads sit in and just above the litter layer, intercepting nutrients as they are released and moving them back into living tissue before rain can wash them down past the root zone. Many trees carry fungal partners on their roots that extend the network of interception. Cut and burn the forest and that capital goes up as smoke and ash: for two or three seasons the ash supports crops, and then the underlying poverty of the ground reasserts itself.

Diversity follows a pattern that took a long time to recognize. A single hectare can hold several hundred tree species, yet across the basin a small minority of species accounts for something like half of all individual trees. Most Amazonian tree species are genuinely rare, represented by scattered individuals; a relative handful — palms, certain legumes, Brazil nut and its relatives — are everywhere. Several of the most abundant are species people have been planting, tending, and favouring for thousands of years, which complicates any clean separation between wild forest and cultivated landscape.

## The Basin Has Always Been Inhabited

The idea of the Amazon as untouched wilderness is a colonial-era artefact, largely produced by epidemics that swept the river well ahead of the Europeans who recorded it. Along the bluffs of the mainstem lie deposits of terra preta, dark earth enriched with charcoal, pottery fragments, fish bone and household waste, that remain fertile centuries after they were made and are still sought out by farmers today. In Acre, deforestation has exposed geometric earthworks cut into the ground; in the Bolivian lowlands there are raised fields, causeways, and fish weirs across seasonally flooded savanna. These are the remains of substantial populations who worked the floodplain.

The staple that made it possible is bitter manioc, a root that stores well in the ground but contains enough cyanogenic compounds to be dangerous raw. The processing chain — grating, then squeezing the pulp in a woven tube press that tightens as it is pulled, then toasting the meal over a fire — removes the toxin and yields flour that keeps for months in a hot, wet climate where almost nothing else does. The pressed liquid, boiled long enough to drive off the poison, becomes a sauce in its own right.

Later arrivals reorganized the basin around extraction. The rubber boom drew tens of thousands of workers from Brazil's drought-stricken northeast into debt-bonded tapping across the forest, and financed an opera house in the middle of Manaus, before seeds smuggled out to Kew and on to plantations in Southeast Asia collapsed the price and left the whole economy stranded. What followed was more roads: highways driven through from the south and east in the second half of the twentieth century, and along them cattle pasture, then soy, then towns.

That frontier is still moving, and the southeastern edge of the basin now has a longer dry season than it did a generation ago and burns more readily. Forest that is thinned, fragmented, and dried behaves differently from forest that is intact — it transpires less, recycles less moisture, and passes drier air to the next stretch downwind. Nobody knows exactly where the threshold sits between a forest that makes its own rain and a landscape that cannot, but the machinery is legible enough to see which direction the southeastern basin is being pushed.
