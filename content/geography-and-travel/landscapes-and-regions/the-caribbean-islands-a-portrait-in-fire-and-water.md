# The Caribbean Islands: A Portrait in Fire and Water

Stand on the eastern shore of Guadeloupe's Grande-Terre and you are on a low,
dry, limestone table with white sand and cane fields. Cross the narrow salt
channel to Basse-Terre and within twenty minutes you are climbing a steep green
volcano through dripping rainforest toward a summit that makes its own weather,
onto beaches the colour of ash. The two halves are joined by a bridge and are
administratively one island. Geologically they belong to different worlds, and
the difference explains a great deal about the Caribbean.

The Lesser Antilles are a volcanic island arc, thrown up where the Atlantic
seafloor dives westward beneath the Caribbean plate. In the northern half of the
chain the arc is doubled. There is an older, outer line — Anguilla, St Martin,
Barbuda, Antigua, Grande-Terre — whose volcanoes died millions of years ago,
subsided, and were capped by growing coral reef, so that today they are flat,
porous, rainless and ringed with pale carbonate beaches. And there is a younger,
inner line — Saba, St Kitts, Montserrat, Basse-Terre — where the volcanoes are
still alive. Almost everything visitors think of as Caribbean character, from
rainfall to soil fertility to whether an island has rivers, follows from which
arc it sits on.

## Fire, and the Ground That Moves

The live volcanoes are not a historical curiosity. Mount Pelée destroyed
Saint-Pierre on Martinique in 1902 with a pyroclastic flow that killed nearly the
entire population of what was then the most cosmopolitan town in the French
Antilles. The Soufrière Hills volcano on Montserrat began erupting in 1995,
buried the capital at Plymouth, and forced most of the island's people to leave
for Britain or Antigua; the southern half of Montserrat remains an exclusion
zone. La Soufrière on St Vincent erupted explosively again in 2021, coating the
north of the island in ash. Offshore from Grenada, Kick 'em Jenny grumbles
underwater and occasionally closes a shipping exclusion radius.

The Greater Antilles — Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica, Puerto Rico — are older,
larger and structurally more complicated, assembled from ancient arc rocks,
scraped-up seafloor and thick carbonate cover, and they carry real mountains:
the Sierra Maestra, the Blue Mountains, and Hispaniola's Cordillera Central,
which rises to the highest point in the islands. Their danger is not volcanic but
seismic. The northern edge of the Caribbean plate is a strike-slip boundary,
grinding east-west, and it runs directly through the region's most densely
populated ground. The Enriquillo fault system produced the 2010 Haiti earthquake,
a moderate-magnitude event that killed a catastrophic number of people because of
where it struck and what the buildings were made of.

A few islands refuse the pattern entirely. Barbados is not volcanic at all: it is
a wedge of ocean-floor sediment scraped off the descending plate, uplifted and
capped with coral limestone. Trinidad is a detached piece of South America,
separated only by shallow water, with South American geology, South American oil
and asphalt, and — crucially for naturalists — South American animals, including
monkeys and ocelots that no true oceanic island in the chain possesses.

Limestone shapes daily life on the islands that have it. Rain sinks rather than
runs, so Barbuda, Anguilla and the Caymans have essentially no surface streams;
fresh water sits as a lens floating on denser salt water within the rock, and
over-pumping draws the salt upward into the wells. Jamaica's Cockpit Country is
an extreme version of the same chemistry, a dense field of limestone hollows and
conical hills, hard to cross and harder to police, which is exactly why
self-liberated Maroons were able to hold it against British forces long enough to
win treaty terms.

## The Wind Does the Zoning

The trade winds blow steadily out of the east-northeast, and every mountainous
island is therefore divided into a wet windward side and a dry leeward one. On
Dominica the windward slopes are drenched rainforest with a river for every day
of the year, by local reckoning, while the leeward coast is scrubby and sunny —
which is where the towns and the hotels are. The seasonal rhythm is a dry period
from roughly December into April and a wet one from May into November.

The wet season is also hurricane season, peaking in the late summer. Most major
Caribbean hurricanes begin as easterly waves rolling off West Africa, organise
over warm open Atlantic water, and arrive from the southeast having had two
thousand kilometres of ocean to intensify over. Another African export moderates
them: the Saharan Air Layer, a plume of hot, dry, dust-laden air that crosses the
Atlantic every summer, suppressing storm formation when it is thick, hazing the
sky over the islands, fertilising thin soils with mineral dust, and aggravating
asthma when it settles.

The arithmetic of small islands makes storms a different kind of event than they
are on a continent. A hurricane that damages a corner of a large country damages
a whole nation here. When Maria crossed Dominica in 2017 it stripped the forest
canopy to bare sticks and destroyed a large share of the country's economic
output in a night; the same storm left Puerto Rico without electricity for
months. Recovery is not a regional transfer but an international appeal, and the
debt taken on to rebuild arrives just in time for the next season.

## Evolution's Repeated Experiment

Islands are where evolutionary biology goes to test itself, and the Caribbean
offers an unusually clean set of replicates: four large islands, similar
climates, colonised separately.

The classic case is the anole lizards. On each of the Greater Antilles, anoles
have diversified into the same set of specialists — a long-legged one that runs
on the ground and tree trunks, a short-legged one that creeps along twigs, a
large green one in the canopy, a slender one on grass stems — and in each case
those forms evolved independently on that island from local ancestors rather than
dispersing between islands. The same ecological problem, solved the same way,
four times. It is about as direct a demonstration as nature provides that
adaptation is predictable when the selective pressures are.

The rest of the native fauna is a mixture of the very old and the very reduced.
Bats aside, the surviving land mammals are down to hutias, large rodents hunted
for centuries, and the solenodons of Cuba and Hispaniola — nocturnal, shrew-like,
venomous through a grooved tooth, and the last of a lineage that split from other
mammals deep in the Cretaceous. The ground sloths and monkeys that once lived on
these islands disappeared after people arrived. Bird endemism remains high: the
todies, a tiny family of bright green kingfisher relatives found nowhere else,
and Cuba's bee hummingbird, the smallest bird in the world. The frogs are
mostly Eleutherodactylus, which skip the tadpole stage entirely and hatch
directly from eggs laid in damp leaf litter — a neat adaptation to islands with
no reliable ponds, and the reason Puerto Rico's coquí can call from a bromeliad
halfway up a mountain.

The reefs have had a bad half-century. White-band disease wiped out most of the
branching elkhorn and staghorn corals that once built the shallow reef crest.
Shortly afterward, a pathogen swept the Caribbean's dominant grazing sea urchin
to near-disappearance in about a year, and with both the reef-builders and the
main algae-cropper gone, seaweed took over surfaces that had been coral for
millennia. Repeated bleaching in hot years and a fast-moving stony coral tissue
loss disease have added to the pressure. So has the arrival, since the early
2010s, of huge floating mats of sargassum weed from a new belt in the tropical
Atlantic, which rot on the beaches in stinking windrows and smother turtle
nesting grounds.

## What the Sugar Did

The first people came up the arc from South America, ceramic-making farmers
following an earlier wave, and by the time Columbus arrived the Greater Antilles
supported Taíno societies growing cassava on mounded fields and the Lesser
Antilles were Kalinago. Within a few generations of 1492 that population had
collapsed under disease and forced labour.

What replaced it was a plantation system of a severity without much precedent.
Sugar cane demands flat land, heavy labour, and a mill; it rewards scale
brutally. Barbados was cleared of almost all its forest within a few decades of
English settlement, and the model spread through the Leewards, Jamaica and French
Saint-Domingue. The great majority of Africans carried across the Atlantic in the
slave trade were landed in the Caribbean and Brazil, not in North America, and
the islands' demography, languages, music and food all begin there. Haiti's
revolution, the only slave uprising to found a state, ended in 1804 and was
answered with an indemnity and a long diplomatic quarantine whose effects are
still legible. After emancipation, planters recruited indentured labour from
India, which is why Indo-Trinidadians are today one of that country's two largest
communities and why
its street food involves roti.

The ecological legacy is as durable as the social one. Forests were cut, slopes
eroded, and mongooses were introduced to control rats in the cane and instead
ate their way through ground-nesting birds, snakes and lizards. The economies
that followed sugar have been similarly narrow and similarly exposed: bananas on
the Windward Islands until European trade preferences were struck down, bauxite
in Jamaica, oil and gas in Trinidad, offshore finance in the Caymans, and
everywhere tourism, which brings foreign currency, imports most of what it feeds
its guests, and depends on the reef and the beach staying where they are.
