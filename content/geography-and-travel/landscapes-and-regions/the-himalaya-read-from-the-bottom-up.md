# The Himalaya Read from the Bottom Up

In the shops around Muktinath, in the upper Kali Gandaki valley of Nepal, you can buy a shaligram: a black stone, usually the size of a fist, which when cracked open reveals the coiled shell of an ammonite. Pilgrims treat these as manifestations of Vishnu and carry them home to household shrines. A geologist would describe the same object as the remains of a marine cephalopod that died on the floor of a tropical ocean, was buried in mud, and now sits in a riverbed roughly three and a half kilometres above sea level, several hundred kilometres from the nearest coast. Both descriptions are accurate, and the second one is the short version of everything that follows.

## A Collision Nobody Has Called Off

The ocean in question was the Tethys, and it closed because India, having broken away from the southern supercontinent, drifted north at a rate that was unusually brisk for a continent and ran into Asia. Continental crust is too buoyant to be pushed down into the mantle for long, so nothing subducted cleanly. Instead the seafloor sediments that had accumulated along India's northern margin were scraped off, stacked, and shoved southward over the Indian plate along a series of enormous, shallow-angled thrust faults — a pile of slices, each one riding over the one in front of it. This is why marine limestone ends up on summits, including the top of Everest, and why the fossil-bearing rock of the Tethyan sequence forms the dry, pale country north of the main peaks rather than the crystalline core of the range itself.

The stack is still growing forward. The youngest and southernmost of the major thrusts runs beneath the Siwaliks, the low, crumbling foothills that face the Indian plain — hills made almost entirely of the eroded debris of the higher range, dumped in a trough and then, with a kind of geological thrift, uplifted and folded into mountains of their own. South of them lies the Terai, a belt of gravel and fine silt where rivers emerging from the hills lose their gradient and drop their load. Strain accumulates on the thrust beneath all of this until it releases, which it does regularly and hard. The great Nepal-Bihar earthquake of 1934 and the Gorkha earthquake of 2015 were both expressions of India continuing to move underneath Tibet.

One consequence worth noticing on any map: several major rivers cut straight through the highest part of the range rather than flowing away from it. The Kali Gandaki runs between Dhaulagiri and Annapurna in a trench far deeper than either summit is distant. The Arun, the Sutlej, and the Tsangpo do comparable things. These rivers predate the mountains they cross, and as the rock rose, they sawed down through it fast enough to keep their courses. Which is a tidy summary of the range's whole condition: raised from below, shredded from above, gaining a little more than it loses.

## One Wall, Two Climates

The Himalaya sit broadside to the summer monsoon. Moist air moving north off the Bay of Bengal meets the front of the range, is forced up, and unloads. The eastern and central foothills are among the wettest inhabited places on the planet, and the rain arrives on a schedule reliable enough to organize a civilization around. Cross to the north side and the air has nothing left. Mustang, Dolpo, Ladakh, Spiti, and the Tibetan Plateau beyond are high desert, with ochre badlands, whitewashed villages clinging to irrigated green strips, and skies that stay open for most of the year.

The range is also a water tower, holding more ice than anywhere outside the polar regions and metering it out through the dry months. The western end of the system works on a different schedule from the eastern end: the Karakoram and the western Himalaya get a substantial share of their snow in winter, from disturbances tracking in on the westerlies, while the eastern Himalaya are overwhelmingly fed by summer monsoon snowfall. That difference shows up in how the glaciers are behaving. Most Himalayan glaciers are thinning and retreating; the Karakoram, fed by winter snow at very high elevations, has been conspicuously slower to follow, an anomaly that has occupied glaciologists for two decades.

## Walking Up Through the Bands

A traverse from the plains to the snowline passes through a sequence of vegetation belts compressed into a horizontal distance a fit walker can cover in a week, and it is the quickest way to understand the range.

At the base, in the Terai and the lower Siwaliks, sal forest dominates: tall, straight, deciduous, with a grassy understorey that once carried tiger, rhinoceros, and enough malarial mosquito to keep the belt sparsely settled until spraying campaigns opened it up in the middle of the last century. Climb into the mid-hills and sal gives way to a patchwork of subtropical broadleaf forest, chir pine on dry south-facing spurs, and terraced farmland occupying everything that can be levelled. Around two thousand metres the character changes again, into oak and rhododendron forest that intercepts cloud most mornings, hangs with moss and epiphytes, and flowers scarlet and pink through the spring. This is where the Himalayan monal goes about its business, a pheasant with iridescent plumage improbable enough that it seems designed for a different climate, and where langurs work through the canopy.

Higher still, fir, hemlock, and blue pine take over, then thin into birch — bhojpatra, whose papery bark was peeled for manuscripts for centuries — mixed with juniper and rhododendron scrub bent low by snow load. Treeline sits somewhere near four thousand metres on the southern flank and notably higher on the Tibetan side, where the sheer bulk of the plateau warms the air above it. Above that come alpine meadows, brilliant with primula and gentian for the few weeks they are not frozen, grazed in summer by sheep, goats, and yak, and hunted by snow leopard, which follow blue sheep and Himalayan tahr up and down the crags and are seen mostly as tracks. Beyond the meadows lies rock, moraine, and permanent ice, crossed twice a year by bar-headed geese that migrate straight over the range at altitudes where the air holds a fraction of the oxygen at sea level.

## Farming a Slope

Nearly all Himalayan agriculture begins by converting a hillside into a staircase. Terraces cut into the mid-hills are levelled, bunded, and fed by contour channels tapped off streams sometimes kilometres away, and the labour of maintaining them — repairing walls after the monsoon, clearing channels, moving manure uphill — is the fixed cost of living there. Irrigated terraces on the lower slopes grow rice in the summer and wheat or mustard in winter; the drier rainfed terraces above carry maize, millet, buckwheat, barley, and potatoes, the last of which arrived relatively recently and changed what altitudes could support a village.

Above the farming belt the economy turns to animals, and to movement. Households in the high valleys have long run a seasonal circuit, taking yak, dzo — the yak-cattle hybrid that tolerates lower elevations better than a pure yak — sheep, and goats up to summer pasture and back down before the passes close. Until the middle of the twentieth century, many of the same families ran the trade that made the high valleys viable: Tibetan salt carried south over the passes on animals, exchanged for grain grown in the middle hills, with the ratio negotiated at fixed trading places. Political change in Tibet, cheap iodized salt from India, and roads that made lowland goods available everywhere took most of that business away within a generation.

What replaced it varies. Some valleys turned to fruit, apples especially, which grow superbly in the western mid-hills and travel well once a road arrives. Some turned to the summer harvest of yartsa gunbu, the caterpillar fungus that fetches extraordinary prices in Chinese medicine markets and empties whole villages onto the high meadows for a month each year. Some turned to trekkers and climbers, an industry that made Sherpa households in Solukhumbu prosperous by Nepali standards while also making them the ones who carry loads through the icefall. Very many turned to migration: men and increasingly women working in Gulf states, Malaysia, or Indian cities, sending money back to hill villages where the terraces are progressively falling out of cultivation for want of hands.

## The Range's Bad Days

Building anything permanent here means accepting a schedule of destruction. Monsoon rain on steep, freshly uplifted, heavily fractured rock produces landslides as a matter of routine, and roads cut into those slopes without adequate drainage produce many more, since each cut undermines the hillside above it and dumps spoil onto the one below. Cloudbursts in the upper catchments have swept away towns built on old debris fans, as at Kedarnath. Glacial retreat has left meltwater ponded behind loose moraine dams, and when such a dam fails — or when a mass of rock and ice detaches high above and lands in the lake, as happened in Chamoli — the resulting flood travels down the valley with enough force to remove bridges, hydropower intakes, and settlements in a single afternoon.

Hydropower is nonetheless where much of the region's development money goes, because the combination of steep gradient and reliable flow is extremely valuable, and because dams are one of the few assets a mountain state can export. Meanwhile in the mid-hills, a quieter problem: springs that fed villages for generations are yielding less, as recharge areas are built over and rainfall arrives in fewer, harder bursts.

All of the material stripped off by that rain has somewhere to go. Carried down through the gorges, across the plains by the Ganges and the Brahmaputra, and out into the Bay of Bengal, it has built the largest submarine sediment fan on Earth — a cone of eroded Himalaya thousands of metres thick, still receiving deliveries, waiting on the seafloor for whatever the next collision does with it.
