# Varanasi on the Turning of the River

Before first light the boatmen are already out, working small wooden craft
along the western bank, and the far shore is nothing but a pale line of sand.
Then the sun comes up directly out of the river, and for perhaps twenty minutes
the entire city is lit from the front — five kilometers of stone steps,
towers, painted guesthouses and half-sunk temples, all facing east, all catching
the light at once. People turn toward it, cup water in their hands and let it
fall. This is the thing Varanasi is built to do, and it does it every clear
morning of the year.

## The Geography of a Bend

The arrangement is not an accident of architecture. It is a fact about the
Ganges. Across most of the north Indian plain the river runs broadly southeast.
At Varanasi it swings, for a stretch of several kilometers, almost due north
before resuming its course. The outside of that curve is the city's bank, cut
steep and high by the current; the inside is a broad depositional sandbank on
the far side, low and shifting. So the city stands on a bluff looking east
across the water at sunrise, with the river running northward past it, back
toward its source — a reversal that Hindu tradition reads as auspicious and
that pilgrims still remark on.

The two banks could hardly be less alike. The western side holds everything: a
continuous wall of masonry, the steps called ghats descending from it in
flights wide enough to seat a crowd, palaces and temples built right to the
water's edge. The eastern side holds almost nothing. Tradition holds the far
bank inauspicious, and the annual flood would destroy anything permanent built
on it, so it remains sand — grazed by buffalo, camped on in the dry months,
crossed by the occasional rowboat carrying someone who wants to look back at
the skyline.

Older than either is the name. The city is Kashi in the oldest usage, the place
of light, and Banaras or Benares in common north Indian speech. Varanasi is a
compound of the two tributaries that bracket the sacred stretch — the Varuna in
the north and the small Assi in the south — so the name is in effect a set of
boundary markers. Whatever the correct etymology, people have lived on this
bank without interruption for something like three thousand years, which puts
it among the small handful of cities in the world that can claim continuous
occupation on that scale.

## Stone Steps, Built by Strangers

The ghats look ancient and mostly are not. Almost all of the stone embankment
you see today was built or rebuilt in the eighteenth and early nineteenth
centuries, when Maratha and Rajput rulers from across India competed to endow
the city they could not govern. Ahilyabai Holkar of Indore, a widowed queen
with a formidable record as a builder of temples and rest houses, rebuilt the
Kashi Vishwanath temple in 1780 after more than a century in which the site had
been occupied by a mosque raised on the ruins of its predecessor. The Scindias
funded ghats near the main cremation ground; the Rajput house of Amber built
Man Mandir Ghat, where an eighteenth-century observatory of stone instruments
still sits on the roof, siblings to the ones at Jaipur and Delhi. Bengali
patrons, Nepali patrons, monastic orders and merchant families all bought
frontage. The result reads from a boat like a subscription list of eighteenth
century India.

There are something like eighty ghats along the crescent, running from Assi in
the south to Raj Ghat in the north, and their characters differ sharply. Some
are municipal and crowded; some belong to a single family or monastery and are
quiet all day. Wrestlers train in mud pits at a few of them, swinging weighted
clubs under images of Hanuman. Barbers work on wooden platforms. Dhobis beat
laundry on flat stones and lay it out across the steps in long colored
rectangles. Dashashwamedh, the busiest, hosts a nightly aarti in which priests
in matching robes raise tiered brass lamps in unison — a ritual of real
antiquity that has also, in the last few decades, become a nightly spectacle
watched by hundreds from boats moored ten deep.

## The Business of Dying

Two of the ghats are burning ghats, Manikarnika and the smaller Harishchandra,
and their fires do not go out. Bodies arrive wrapped in cloth on bamboo
stretchers, carried at a jog through the lanes by male relatives; they are
dipped in the river, laid on a pyre of stacked wood weighed out by the
kilogram, and lit with fire brought from a hearth that the Dom community,
which has held the right for generations, maintains and charges for. Sandalwood
costs more. A mango-wood pyre costs less. The negotiation over the quantity of
wood is conducted openly and is part of the scene.

Underneath is a belief stated plainly in the city's own literature: that to die
within the sacred boundary of Kashi is to be released from the cycle of rebirth
altogether, because Shiva himself whispers a liberating syllable into the ear
of the dying. People act on it. There are guesthouses near the burning ghats
that accept the terminally ill and their families for a limited stay, at
minimal cost, on the understanding that the guest has come to die. Families
arrive from hundreds of kilometers away by train with an elderly relative and
a bundle of bedding.

For a visitor the effect is disorienting less because death is visible than
because it is unremarkable. Twenty meters from a pyre, boys are playing
cricket. A tea seller works the steps. Goats eat the marigolds from a
discarded garland. Photography is not welcome at the cremation grounds, and
anyone who offers to guide you closer for a donation to a nonexistent hospice
is running a well-worn scam, but simply sitting at a distance and watching is
neither forbidden nor resented.

## Looms, Lanes and Breakfast

Away from the water the city becomes a warren. The lanes behind the ghats —
galis — are often too narrow for a car and sometimes too narrow for two people
with luggage to pass. They are shared with motorbikes, cows that know exactly
how much space they are entitled to, handcarts, and enormous volumes of
pedestrian traffic funneling toward the Vishwanath temple. Navigation by map is
hopeless; navigation by descending toward the river always works.

Sound gives away the other Banaras. Down a side lane you hear a rhythmic
wooden clatter from an upper window: a handloom, weaving the brocade silk that
has carried the city's name for centuries. Banarasi saris, with their patterns
of gold and silver zari thread, are woven very largely by Muslim weaver
families in neighborhoods like Madanpura and Alaipura, and sold very largely by
Hindu merchants in the bazaars. The craft has been squeezed hard by power looms
and by Chinese synthetic yarn, and a lot of the old jacquard looms are silent,
but the trade and the arrangement behind it are among the oldest continuous
industrial facts in the country.

The city's reputation for pleasure is as firm as its reputation for
renunciation, and both are old. Banarasi paan — betel leaf folded around lime
paste, areca nut and a long list of optional sweet and aromatic fillings — is
prepared at stalls whose proprietors have opinions. Breakfast is kachori with a
spiced potato curry and a jalebi, eaten standing up before nine. In the cold
months, and only in the cold months, vendors sell malaiyo: milk whipped into a
foam so light it barely registers on the tongue, traditionally left out
overnight to catch the winter dew, dusted with pistachio and gone by mid-
morning. It cannot be made in the heat, so it exists for a few weeks a year.

The same doubleness runs through the music. Varanasi supports a distinct
classical tradition, the Banaras gharana, known for its tabla and for a
particular style of light classical singing; Ravi Shankar was born here, and
Bismillah Khan, the greatest shehnai player of the twentieth century, spent his
life in the city — a devout Muslim who played for decades at a Hindu temple and
declined every invitation to move somewhere more lucrative. Just outside town
at Sarnath, the Buddha gave his first sermon after enlightenment, and a brick
stupa the size of a small hill still marks the deer park where he did it.

## When the River Comes Up

Everything described above is seasonal, and the season that rules is the
monsoon. From July the Ganges rises — not by centimeters but by many meters —
until the lower flights of every ghat are underwater and the river laps at
doorways that in April stood thirty steps above it. Boats tie up near the top
of the stairs. The cremation platforms move upward to whatever stone is still
dry. Lanes flood. Then the water falls back through autumn, leaving a fine grey
silt over the steps that has to be shoveled off, and by the following May the
heat is in the mid-forties, the sandbank across the river is enormous, and the
whole thing looks as though it could never have been otherwise.

The river itself is in poor condition, and the city knows it. Decades of
cleanup programmes have produced treatment plants that flood or lose power,
interceptor sewers that fill faster than planned, and water at the ghats whose
bacterial load is not a matter of dispute. Pilgrims drink it anyway, carry it
home in sealed brass pots, and pour it over stone lingams. Reconciling those
two facts is not something the city has ever found necessary. Kashi has
outlasted a great many people who arrived with a plan for it.
