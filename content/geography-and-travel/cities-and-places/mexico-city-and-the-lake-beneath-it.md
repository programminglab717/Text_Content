# Mexico City and the Lake Beneath It

The floor of the Palacio de Bellas Artes sits well below the sidewalk outside
it. When the marble opera house was designed at the turn of the twentieth
century, its entrance was level with the street. It has been sinking ever
since, and the city eventually gave up and built a sunken forecourt around it
with steps leading down. Walk a few blocks in any direction and you find
variations on the same problem: a colonial doorway bricked up at the bottom and
reopened higher, a church nave that tilts perceptibly toward one transept, a
nineteenth-century facade whose row of windows describes a shallow wave, as
though the building had been photographed through moving water. The old center
is not settling evenly. It is subsiding, in places by several centimeters a
year, and the reason is that the whole city sits on a lake that people spent
four centuries trying to get rid of.

## A Basin With No Way Out

The Valley of Mexico is not really a valley. It is a closed basin, ringed on
every side by volcanic mountains, with no river draining out of it. Rain that
falls on the Ajusco to the south or on the flanks of Popocatépetl and
Iztaccíhuatl to the southeast runs down into the bottom of the bowl and stays
there. Before people interfered, it collected in a chain of shallow, connected
lakes sitting at about 2,240 meters above sea level — high enough that the air
is noticeably thin to a visitor arriving from the coast, and thin enough that
water boils several degrees cool.

The lakes were not uniform. The lowest and largest, Texcoco, was brackish,
fed by runoff that evaporated in place and left its salts behind. The southern
lakes, Xochimilco and Chalco, were fresh, fed by springs at the foot of the
mountains. The Mexica founded Tenochtitlan in the fourteenth century on an
island in the saline part and then engineered their way around every
disadvantage of the site. They ran three broad causeways out to the mainland,
with removable bridges for defense. They piped drinking water in from the
springs at Chapultepec along a double aqueduct, so one channel could be cleaned
while the other ran. And they built a long dike across the lake, credited to
the poet-king Nezahualcoyotl of Texcoco, to hold the salt water off and keep a
freshwater lagoon around the city.

They also farmed the water. Chinampas — rectangular plots built up from lake
mud and vegetation inside frames of woven stakes, anchored by willows whose
roots knit the whole thing together — turned marsh into some of the most
productive cropland anywhere, with canals for streets and a water table right
at the root zone. A few square kilometers of them survive in Xochimilco, still
worked, still reached by flat-bottomed boat.

## Four Hundred Years of Pulling the Plug

The Spanish took the city in 1521 and made a decision that still governs daily
life: they rebuilt on the ruins rather than moving to dry ground. A city of
canals and canoes does not suit carts, horses, or heavy stone cathedrals, and
without the indigenous dikes and sluices — which fell out of maintenance almost
immediately — the new capital flooded again and again.

The response was the Desagüe, the drainage, one of the longest-running public
works projects in the hemisphere. Work began in the first decade of the
seventeenth century on a cut and tunnel through the low northern rim of the
basin at Huehuetoca, an attempt to give the closed basin an artificial outlet.
It was under-engineered, repeatedly collapsed, and was eventually abandoned in
favor of an open trench dug by conscripted labor over generations. It did not
prevent the catastrophe of 1629, when the city flooded and stayed flooded for
years; people moved between buildings by canoe, and there was serious
discussion of relocating the capital entirely.

The work continued under the republic and accelerated under Porfirio Díaz,
whose Gran Canal and its tunnel finally gave the basin a functioning outlet
around the turn of the twentieth century. It was celebrated as a triumph of
modern engineering. Within decades it stopped working, because the ground it
was cut into began to drop, and a canal that sinks below its own outlet becomes
a bathtub. Successive deeper tunnels followed — the Emisor Central in the
1970s, and more recently a second deep tunnel on the eastern side — each one
built lower than the last to stay ahead of the sinking city. The lakes
themselves are essentially gone. What remains is the Xochimilco canal network,
a shrunken saline flat out by the airport, and a floodplain full of twenty-odd
million people.

## What the Clay Is Doing

The lakebed left behind is not ordinary soil. It is a lacustrine clay, much of
it derived from volcanic ash, with a structure so open that its water content
can exceed its solid content several times over. Suspended in place, it holds
up buildings adequately. Drained, it compacts like a wrung sponge.

Mexico City draws most of its water from the aquifer beneath that clay, and
has pumped it faster than rain can recharge it for generations. The clay
consolidates as the pressure below drops, and the surface comes down with it.
Over the past century the historic center has descended on the order of ten
meters. Because the clay is thicker in some places than others, and because
heavy colonial churches were often founded on the rubble platforms of Mexica
temples, adjacent structures sink at different rates — which is where the
tilted naves and wavy facades come from. The Metropolitan Cathedral's twentieth
century tilt became severe enough that engineers undertook a remarkable
correction: rather than jack the low side up, they carefully mined soil out
from under the high corners and let the building settle back toward level.

The clay does something else, too. Soft, saturated sediment amplifies seismic
waves and rings at a characteristic period of around two seconds — close to the
natural sway period of a reinforced concrete building of ten to twenty stories.
In September 1985, an earthquake centered off the Michoacán coast, hundreds of
kilometers away, was barely noticed in some intervening towns and flattened
mid-rise buildings in the old lakebed. Neighborhoods on volcanic rock to the
south came through nearly untouched. The disaster also reshaped the city
politically: with the government slow and evasive, residents organized their
own rescue brigades, and the volunteer tunnelers known as the topos became a
permanent institution. The earthquake of September 2017 — by coincidence on the
anniversary — was closer and shallower, with energy at shorter periods, and it
took down a different population of buildings, mostly six to eight stories, in
Roma, Condesa and the south.

## A City That Floods and Runs Dry

The result of all this is a water system that runs in two contradictory
directions at enormous expense. Because the local aquifer cannot keep up,
roughly a quarter to a third of the city's supply is lifted from the Cutzamala
reservoirs outside the basin and pumped more than a kilometer uphill, one of
the most energy-hungry water transfers anywhere. Meanwhile rainwater that falls
inside the basin, which would once have recharged the lakes, lands on asphalt,
mixes with sewage and is expelled through the deep tunnels into the Tula valley
to the north.

Neither half works evenly. A large share of the piped water is lost to leaks in
mains that crack as the ground deforms beneath them. In the flat eastern
boroughs, taps run on a rotation of a few days a week, and households that fall
outside the schedule buy from tanker trucks at several times the metered price.
Those same boroughs sit lowest in the basin, so they are also the first to flood
when a summer storm overwhelms the drains.

## Neighborhoods Keyed to Geology

You can read the underlying ground off the streetscape once you know what to
look for. The Centro Histórico occupies the footprint of the island, its grid
still following the Mexica causeways: Calzada de Tlalpan and the road to Tacuba
run where the embankments ran. Roma and Condesa, laid out on drained lakebed in
the early twentieth century, are full of Art Nouveau and Art Deco apartment
blocks, jacaranda-lined medians, and, in Condesa, an oval street called
Amsterdam that traces the track of a demolished racecourse. Coyoacán and San
Ángel to the south were separate villages on the old shoreline, standing on
firmer volcanic ground, and they still feel like villages — cobbles, walled
gardens, plazas with two churches. Further south again, the university campus
and the Pedregal district are built directly on the black lava field laid down
when the small volcano Xitle erupted about two thousand years ago, a surface so
hard and strange that the architects of the 1950s simply designed around the
rock and left it exposed in the gardens.

Above it all, on a clear winter morning when the wind has scoured out the
basin, the two volcanoes appear on the southeastern horizon with snow on them,
and it becomes obvious that the city is sitting in a bowl.

The texture of ordinary days has its own acoustics. The sweet potato vendor's
cart carries a small steam boiler, and the whistle it lets off is a long,
mournful shriek that can be heard three streets away. A truck circulates with a
recorded call offering to buy old mattresses and refrigerators. A man in a
brown uniform cranks a barrel organ on a corner near the cathedral while a
second man works the crowd with a hat. Somewhere nearby, at the Templo Mayor,
archaeologists are excavating the ceremonial center that electrical workers
stumbled into in 1978 when they hit a carved stone disc of the dismembered moon
goddess. The dig floor sits below the water table. To keep working on it, they
have to pump.
