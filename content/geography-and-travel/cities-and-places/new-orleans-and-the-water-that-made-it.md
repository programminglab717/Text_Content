# New Orleans and the Water That Made It

The strangest thing about New Orleans is not that much of it sits below sea
level. It is that the high ground is the riverbank. Walk from the Mississippi
levee at the foot of Canal Street away from the water, northward toward Lake
Pontchartrain, and you are walking downhill the whole way, even though the
gradient is so slight you would never notice it without a bicycle or a heavy
rain. The river is up there, behind the earthen wall, running at a level that in
spring can put the decks of passing ships above the rooftops of the French
Quarter. The city lies in the bowl behind it.

This is not a planning failure. It is what deltas do. A river carrying a
continent's worth of silt slows as it nears the sea, and when it tops its banks
during high water the heaviest particles drop out first, right at the edge of
the channel. Repeat that for a few thousand years and the river builds itself a
pair of low natural ridges, one on each bank, standing a few feet above the
marsh behind them. Those ridges are the only dry land in a delta. Every early
settlement in southern Louisiana — French, Spanish, Isleño, Acadian — sits on
one, which is why the old farm lots run as long narrow ribbons perpendicular to
the water, each one claiming a little river frontage and a long tail of
backswamp nobody wanted.

## Eighteen Blocks of Dry Land

Bienville chose the site in 1718 for reasons that had nothing to do with comfort.
It was the point where the river came closest to Lake Pontchartrain, and where a
portage along Bayou St. John gave a back door to the Gulf that avoided a hundred
miles of the Mississippi's lower reaches. Whoever held it held the continent's
drainage. The original town was a rectangle of a few dozen blocks laid on the
crest of the natural levee, and that rectangle is the French Quarter, which is
the reason the Quarter stayed dry in 2005 while neighbourhoods built later and
lower filled to the eaves.

The architecture there is mostly Spanish, not French, a detail that surprises
people standing under the wrought-iron galleries. Spain held Louisiana from the
1760s until just before the Louisiana Purchase, and two catastrophic fires late
in the eighteenth century burned nearly everything the French had put up. The
rebuilding happened under Spanish building codes: brick or stuccoed masonry
instead of wood, tile roofs instead of shingles, flush street walls with the
living space wrapped around an interior courtyard. The courtyard was not
decorative. It was a way to get light and moving air into a deep lot on a hot,
airless afternoon, and the same instinct runs through the rest of the local
vernacular — the tall shuttered windows, the twelve-foot ceilings, the shotgun
house with every room opening onto the next so a breeze can travel the length of
it.

## What the Pumps Bought

For nearly two centuries the city stayed on the ridges because there was nowhere
else to go. The back of town was cypress swamp, saturated to the surface, and
digging a ditch there produced a ditch full of water. That changed in the early
twentieth century when a local engineer named A. Baldwin Wood designed a screw
pump of unprecedented capacity — an impeller in a wide barrel that could shift
enormous volumes of debris-laden water without clogging. Wood's pumps, combined
with a network of canals, let New Orleans drain the backswamp and build on it.
Lakeview, Gentilly, Broadmoor and much of Mid-City exist because of that
machinery. Versions of Wood's design were exported to the Netherlands, to China,
to anywhere with the same problem.

The cost arrived slowly and was not obvious for a generation. Delta soil behind
the natural levee is largely organic — peat and partly decayed cypress, held up
by the water in it. Take the water out and the organic matter oxidizes and the
clay consolidates, and the ground simply goes down. Neighbourhoods that were at
or just above sea level when they were laid out sank several feet over the
following decades, so that today a single street can run from two feet above the
Gulf to eight feet below it. Houses show it: raised piers tilted, doorframes
racked into parallelograms, live oak roots heaving the sidewalk into waves.
Every foot of subsidence made the pumps more necessary and the consequences of
their failure worse.

What the failure looked like became clear in August 2005. Katrina's surge did not
overtop the main river levees. It pushed into the drainage canals that ran
straight into the heart of the city — the very canals that carried water out to
the lake — and the floodwalls along them gave way at their foundations. Roughly
four fifths of the city went under. The water that drowned Lakeview came from the
lake, through infrastructure built to protect it. It is difficult to look at the
subsequent decades of reconstruction, the rebuilt walls and the new surge barrier
east of the city, without also noticing what was never solved: the coast that
once absorbed storms is dissolving, cut to pieces by navigation channels and
starved of sediment by the same levees that keep the river in its bed.

## A City Assembled From Elsewhere

None of this explains why people stayed, and the reason is not scenic. New
Orleans developed a culture unlike anything else in North America because of who
was funnelled through the port and what they were permitted to keep.

Under French and Spanish law, enslaved people in Louisiana retained certain
customs that Anglo-American slave codes suppressed, including the Sunday off.
They used it. For decades, on Sundays, hundreds gathered in an open area at the
edge of the old town — Congo Square, in what is now Tremé — to trade, to talk,
and above all to drum and dance in forms carried from West and Central Africa
and the Caribbean. That continuity of rhythm, maintained in public, in a city
that also had opera, military brass bands, and a large free population of colour
with access to formal musical training, is the soil jazz grew out of. The mix was
specific to this place, and so was the result.

You can hear the inheritance on any ordinary Sunday. A second line is a parade
put on by one of the neighbourhood social aid and pleasure clubs, organizations
that began in the nineteenth century as burial societies and mutual insurance for
people the insurance companies would not cover. The club members dance out front
in matching suits; a brass band plays; the second line is everyone else, the
crowd that attaches itself and walks four hours through the streets behind the
music. Jazz funerals work on the same logic, dirges on the way to the cemetery
and everything cut loose on the way back.

## Heat, Calendar, Table

The climate is subtropical and it dictates the schedule. Summer is five months
long, humid in a way that makes shade almost useless, and broken most afternoons
by a thunderstorm that arrives at four, dumps an inch of rain in twenty minutes,
and leaves the streets steaming. For most of the nineteenth century summer also
meant yellow fever, and anyone with money left town from June until the first
cold snap. What remains of that pattern is a city that does its real living
outdoors in the mild months and treats late August as something to be endured.

The calendar is organized around this. Carnival season opens on Twelfth Night
and runs to Mardi Gras itself, with parades put on by private clubs called
krewes; the Black Masking Indians, who spend a year hand-beading and feathering
suits of extraordinary elaboration, come out on Mardi Gras morning and again on
St. Joseph's night. Crawfish season peaks in the cool spring, and boils happen
in back yards on newspaper-covered tables. Red beans are a Monday dish because
Monday was wash day and a pot of beans could sit on the stove unattended. The
food, like the music, is a record of who was in the kitchen: a French roux, West
African okra, the ground sassafras the Choctaw called filé, Spanish rice,
Caribbean peppers, German butchers' sausage, Sicilian olive salad on a
muffuletta.

Even the burials are climatic and cultural at once. The old cemeteries are
walled cities of above-ground tombs, partly a Mediterranean habit brought by
French and Spanish settlers, partly a response to a water table that historically
made a deep grave into a small pond. The tombs are reused across generations,
the heat of a Louisiana summer inside a sealed brick oven doing the work that
elsewhere takes much longer.

Most American cities were built where building was easy. This one was built where
the river was useful, and it has spent three centuries paying that bill in
pumping stations, levee boards, insurance premiums and evacuations. The
arithmetic has never once worked out. Nobody who lives there seems to consider
that an argument.
