# Lisbon After the Earthquake

At about twenty to ten on the morning of the first of November, 1755, the
churches of Lisbon were full. It was All Saints' Day, candles were lit on every
altar in a city of tens of thousands of candles, and the first shock arrived
while the congregations were standing. It lasted long enough for people to get
into the streets. The second and third brought the buildings down on top of
them. Survivors ran downhill toward the open ground along the Tagus, which was
sound reasoning and fatal, because perhaps forty minutes later the water
withdrew from the harbor far enough to expose old wrecks on the mud, and then
came back over the quays in a series of waves. What the sea did not take, the
fire did. Candles and kitchen hearths in a city of collapsed timber burned for
the better part of a week, and the wind pushed the flames through the districts
the shaking had spared.

Nobody knows how many died. Estimates run from ten thousand to several times
that, and the honest answer is that the record-keeping burned along with
everything else. What is certain is that the capital of a seaborne empire — the
place where the gold of Brazil was landed and counted — lost its royal palace,
its opera house, its customs house, most of its churches, and the archives of
two centuries of exploration in a single morning.

## The Argument the Disaster Started

The scale of it broke something in eighteenth-century European thought. A
devout city, on a holy day, in a country that had fought hard for orthodoxy,
had been destroyed at the hour of worship while the brothel districts along the
waterfront reportedly came through better than the cathedrals. Voltaire wrote a
long poem about it within weeks, and then wove it into *Candide*, using Lisbon
to batter the proposition that we live in the best of all possible worlds.
Rousseau answered him with an argument that is now startlingly modern: the
earthquake did not kill those people, twenty thousand houses of six and seven
stories packed together did. Kant, still a young man, wrote several essays
trying to find a physical mechanism, and in doing so helped push earthquakes out
of theology and into natural science.

The Portuguese response was practical and it was fast. The king's secretary of
state, Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo — later the Marquis of Pombal — took
effective control within days. The order traditionally attributed to him is to
bury the dead and feed the living, and whether or not he said it in those words,
that is what was done. Bodies were taken out to sea and dumped, over clerical
objection, to head off plague. Troops sealed the city to stop looting and to
keep the surviving labor force from scattering into the countryside. Grain
prices were fixed. Gallows were put up in prominent places, and used. Within a
month the chief engineer had been asked for plans, and within a year the
principle had been settled: the lower town would not be patched. It would be
demolished flat and rebuilt from nothing.

## The Grid in the Baixa

What went up is one of the first thoroughly planned quarters in modern Europe,
and it is still the center of Lisbon. The Baixa Pombalina runs in a tight
rectilinear grid from a vast square on the river — the old palace site, renamed
for commerce — to the rounded space of the Rossio at the inland end, with
straight parallel streets between them and cross-streets at right angles.
Façades were standardized: the same window proportions, the same iron balconies,
the same cornice height, street after street. Ground floors were given
shopfronts with wide arched openings. Streets were named for the trades assigned
to them, and the names survive even though the goldsmiths and shoemakers have
mostly been replaced by banks and souvenir shops.

The engineering was more radical than the appearance. Behind the masonry of each
new building stands a three-dimensional lattice of timber — braced squares of
wood, pinned together, running through the walls and tied into the floors. The
idea was that in a quake the masonry might crack and spill but the wooden cage
would hold the box together long enough for people to get out. The elements were
cut to standard dimensions in yards outside the city and assembled on site,
which is about as close to prefabrication as the eighteenth century got. The
story that troops were marched around scale models to simulate ground shaking is
repeated in every account of the reconstruction; whether or not the detail is
exact, the intent behind it was genuine, which makes the Baixa one of the
earliest districts anywhere designed against a known hazard. Piles of green pine
were driven into the soft alluvium beneath it, and they are still down there,
waterlogged and holding.

## What the Earthquake Spared

Walk east out of the grid and the city changes character within a block. Alfama,
the oldest quarter, sitting on the flank of the castle hill, came through 1755
comparatively intact — partly because it stands on firmer ground, partly because
its stacked, mutually buttressing houses behaved as a single mass. Its plan is
the plan of a Moorish town: lanes a meter and a half wide that turn back on
themselves, staircases doubling as streets, vaulted passages under houses,
washing lines overhead. Cars cannot enter most of it. Deliveries are made by
hand truck and shoulder. It is the one part of central Lisbon where you can
still get thoroughly lost, and it is where fado is generally said to have taken
its modern shape — a music of plain voice, twelve-string Portuguese guitar and a
vocabulary of longing that resists translation.

Out west at Belém, five kilometers downstream and on different ground, the great
Manueline monuments also survived: the monastery raised on the wealth of the
spice route, with its stone carved into ropes, anchors, seaweed and armillary
spheres, and the little fortified tower in the shallows that once guarded the
approach. The aqueduct that had been completed only a few years before the
earthquake, striding across a valley on enormous pointed arches, took the shock
without damage and went on delivering water to the city for the next century and
a half. It is a useful reminder that eighteenth-century Lisbon knew how to build
well when it chose to; the problem was the rest of the housing stock.

## Hills, and the Machinery for Climbing Them

Lisbon is built on a series of steep hills falling south toward the river, and
the whole city is therefore tilted toward the light coming off the water. This
is the thing visitors notice first and find hardest to describe: a low,
reflective brightness that makes the pale limestone and the tiled façades look
slightly overexposed, and that turns copper for an hour before sunset. The
Portuguese pavement, black basalt and white limestone chips set by hand into
wave patterns and anchors and compass roses, throws the light back up. It is
also treacherous after rain, polished to a shine by two centuries of shoe
leather.

Getting up and down the hills produced an entire local technology. Three
funiculars, counterweighted and running on short steep streets, have been
hauling passengers up since the end of the nineteenth century. A cast-iron
passenger lift with a neo-Gothic viewing platform on top connects the Baixa to
the Bairro Alto in a single vertical move. And the trams — small four-wheeled
cars built to take gradients and corners that modern rolling stock cannot —
still grind up through Graça and Alfama on routes that were laid out for
commuters and are now mostly full of tourists, to the loud irritation of people
who need the tram to get to work.

Where the hills flatten into terraces, the city has put in *miradouros*:
formalized lookout points with a kiosk, some benches, a few trees, and a view
over an orange-tiled roofscape down to the river and the long low suspension
bridge. On the far bank a concrete statue of Christ stands with its arms out,
modeled on the one in Rio, which is the sort of reciprocal gesture the Atlantic
has produced repeatedly between these two cities.

## The Current Pressure

Lisbon spent most of the twentieth century poor, under a dictatorship that ended
in 1974 when a column of soldiers moved on the capital and the population came
out to meet them with carnations from the flower market. It absorbed hundreds of
thousands of Portuguese returning from the African colonies the following year,
and it has since absorbed Cape Verdeans, Angolans, Brazilians, Ukrainians and
South Asians, most visibly in Mouraria, where the grocery shops and the Hindu
and Muslim prayer rooms sit a few doors from the oldest fado houses.

The pressure now is money. Rent laws that had frozen tenancies for decades were
overhauled in 2012, residency-for-investment schemes drew in foreign buyers, and
short-term rentals hollowed out whole staircases in Alfama and Bairro Alto.
Buildings that stood derelict for forty years have been rebuilt beautifully,
which is a genuine gain, and the families who lived in them are largely in the
suburbs, which is a genuine loss. The arithmetic of a city with a modest local
wage structure and an international property market has not resolved itself.

In June the city forgets the arithmetic for a fortnight. The feast of Saint
Anthony fills Alfama and Graça with charcoal grills dragged into the street,
sardines eaten off slices of bread, paper garlands strung between windows, and
neighborhood marching troupes rehearsing their routines for weeks beforehand.
The smoke drifts up through the same lanes that the fire of 1755 never reached.
