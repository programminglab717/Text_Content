# Cape Town Under the Table Mountain

The cloud arrives in the early afternoon and it always comes from the same
direction. A white lip forms along the back edge of the mountain, thickens, and
then begins to pour forward over the flat summit and down the front face,
tumbling and rolling like something spilled, and stopping dead about two thirds
of the way down. It does not reach the city. It never does. Locals call it the
tablecloth and treat it as scenery, but it is a piece of physics performed daily
in front of half a million people: humid southeasterly air off False Bay is
forced up the rear slope, cools past its dew point and condenses, then spills
over the top into air that is warmer and drier on the descent, and evaporates
back to nothing in mid-fall.

Everything about Cape Town begins with that mountain. It is not a backdrop. It
occupies the middle of the metropolitan area like a wall, shapes the wind and
the rain and the water supply, and dictated which parts of the city could be
built on and by whom.

## A Slab of Sandstone on a Granite Plinth

The flat top is the giveaway. Table Mountain is capped by a thick sequence of
hard quartzitic sandstone, laid down in shallow water hundreds of millions of
years ago and left lying almost perfectly horizontal ever since. That sandstone
resists weathering far better than the older granite and shale beneath it, so the
softer rock erodes away from the flanks while the cap holds its level, producing
a plateau with cliffs on every side. The highest point, a little over a thousand
metres, is marked by a cairn built in the nineteenth century by Thomas Maclear,
the astronomer who came to the Cape to re-measure the curvature of the Earth and
needed a fixed station he could see from a long way off.

The mountain does not stand alone. Lion's Head and Signal Hill curl around to
the northwest, Devil's Peak to the east, and between them they enclose a natural
amphitheatre open toward the harbour. This is the City Bowl, and it is where the
town started and where the money largely still is. The range then runs south down
the peninsula in a long spine, splitting the Atlantic side from False Bay, and
ends in the cliffs at Cape Point.

Two oceans are said to meet at that point. They do not, quite — the genuine
boundary between the cold Benguela system and the warm Agulhas wanders around
well to the southeast — but the peninsula does produce one of the most abrupt
sea-temperature gradients anywhere near a city. The summer southeaster pushes
surface water away from the Atlantic shore and cold water rises from below to
replace it, so the beaches at Clifton and Camps Bay are spectacular and almost
unswimmable, numbing within a minute. Drive thirty minutes over the neck of the
peninsula to Muizenberg and the water is warm enough to stay in all afternoon.

## Fynbos and the Necessity of Fire

The vegetation on the slopes looks, from a distance, like unremarkable grey-green
scrub. It is one of the richest plant communities on the planet. The Cape Floral
Region is the smallest of the world's floral kingdoms and holds an absurd
concentration of species, a large share of which grow nowhere else; the
peninsula alone carries something over two thousand kinds of plant. The
dominant vegetation is fynbos — fine bush — built from three main groups: the
proteas with their heavy architectural flowerheads, the ericas or heaths, which
run to hundreds of species of tiny bell-shaped flowers, and the restios, wiry
reed-like plants that do the work grasses do elsewhere.

Fynbos is adapted not merely to tolerate fire but to require it. Many proteas
hold their seed in woody cones on the plant for years and release it only when
heat kills the parent. Bulbs lie dormant underground through decades and flower
spectacularly in the season after a burn. Some seeds germinate in response to
chemicals in smoke. A slope that has not burned in twenty or thirty years is not
a healthy slope; it is an ageing one losing diversity. This makes managing a
mountain that sits inside a city genuinely difficult, because a necessary fire
and a disastrous one look identical from a suburb.

The problem has been made worse by trees that do not belong there. Pines,
eucalypts and Australian wattles were planted for timber and dune stabilization,
escaped, and now colonize catchments where they crowd out fynbos, burn far hotter
than fynbos does, and drink a great deal of water that would otherwise reach the
dams.

## A Garden for Passing Ships

The Dutch East India Company put a station here in 1652 with a narrow purpose:
ships on the Asia run were arriving at Batavia with crews destroyed by scurvy,
and the Company wanted a place halfway to grow vegetables and trade for cattle.
The plot they cleared for that vegetable garden is still there in the middle of
town, now shaded and full of squirrels, with the parliament and the national
museum built around its edges.

The refreshment station became a colony almost immediately, because feeding
ships required farms, and farms required land and labour. The land came from
Khoikhoi herders who had grazed the peninsula for centuries and who lost access
to their pasture through a mixture of treaty, war and, decisively, the smallpox
epidemic of 1713 that tore through communities with no prior exposure. The
labour came by sea. The Company imported enslaved people from Madagascar,
Mozambique, India and above all the Indonesian archipelago, and it also exiled
political prisoners from its eastern possessions to the Cape, some of them
scholars and nobles.

That is the origin of the Cape Muslim community, and of the Bo-Kaap, the
steeply sloping quarter on the flank of Signal Hill where the first mosque in
southern Africa was founded in the late eighteenth century by a prince exiled
from the Moluccas who had spent years imprisoned on Robben Island. The famous
saturated colours of the Bo-Kaap houses are a recent habit rather than an
ancient one; the neighbourhood's real inheritance is its food, its Arabic-script
Afrikaans manuscripts, and the shrines scattered around the peninsula. The
minstrel carnival that fills the streets on the second of January traces back to
the single day of the year that enslaved people at the Cape were given off.

## The Map That Was Drawn on Purpose

There is one more layer, and it is the one that still determines where people
live. Under the Group Areas Act, neighbourhoods were assigned by race, and the
convenient, well-served land close to the mountain and the harbour was assigned
to whites. District Six, a dense, poor, mixed quarter on the slope of Devil's
Peak with a long musical tradition, was declared a white area in 1966. Something
like sixty thousand people were removed over the following years and the
buildings were bulldozed. Then, for the most part, nothing was built. The scar
of empty grass below the mountain is visible from the freeway, and it has stayed
visible for decades because the moral problem of building on it was never
resolved.

The removed populations went to the Cape Flats: the low, sandy, wind-scoured
plain east of the mountain where the peninsula joins the mainland. It floods in
winter, the southeaster lifts the sand in summer, it is far from where the jobs
are, and it was laid out with the wide buffer strips and limited access roads
that make a place easy to seal off. Langa, Gugulethu, Mitchells Plain and later
Khayelitsha grew there. Cape Town has remained one of the most spatially
segregated cities in the world not by drift but by design, and the design is
still legible in a satellite image.

## Wind and Water

The climate is Mediterranean and inverted relative to most of Africa: rain
comes in winter, delivered by cold fronts sweeping in off the South Atlantic,
and summer is dry, bright and fiercely windy. The southeaster that makes the
tablecloth also scours the city for days at a time, tips over café furniture,
and earned the name the Cape Doctor for supposedly blowing the bad air away.
Winter brings the northwester instead, which comes straight into Table Bay, and
it wrecked enough ships at anchor that the Company eventually ordered its fleets
to spend the wet months around the corner in Simon's Bay.

That seasonal rain is the city's entire water supply, stored in dams behind the
mountains inland, and between 2015 and 2018 it failed three years running. The
municipality began counting down to what it called Day Zero, the date the
reservoirs would fall low enough to shut off household taps and force people to
queue at distribution points. Residents were held to fifty litres per person per
day, which is enough for a ninety-second shower, a load of dishes and the
toilet, and the city measured its remaining supply in public, in percentage
points, week by week. The rains returned in time. The counting stopped. But
Cape Town had spent two years learning exactly how much of a city runs on water
falling on a mountain, and nobody there has entirely unlearned it.
