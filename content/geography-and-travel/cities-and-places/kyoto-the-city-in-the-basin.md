# Kyoto: The City in the Basin

Stand at almost any intersection in central Kyoto and look north along the
street. At the end of it, six or seven kilometers away and floating a little in
the haze, there is a mountain. Look east and there is another ridge, closer.
Look west, the same. Only southward does the view run out flat toward Nara and
the Inland Sea. This is not an accident of viewpoint. It is the single fact that
explains the city's plan, its water, its food, its weather and a good deal of
its temperament: Kyoto sits in a basin, closed on three sides, and its streets
run true north-south and east-west, so the mountains are framed at the end of
almost every one of them.

The basin was chosen deliberately in 794, when Emperor Kanmu moved his court
here after two decades of unsatisfactory capitals and named the place Heian-kyō,
the capital of peace and tranquility. Court geomancers approved the site on the
Chinese model — protective hills at the back and to the sides, water on the
flanks, an open aspect to the south — and the surveyors laid out a grid copied
from Chang'an, then the largest city on earth. A great avenue ran down the
middle from the palace enclosure at the north to a gate at the south, with
numbered cross-streets stepping down from First Avenue to Ninth. Kyoto children
still learn the east-west streets as a singsong list, and the list still works,
which is a useful reminder that the plan of 794 is not a historical curiosity
but the operating manual for getting around.

## The Grid That Bent

The copy of Chang'an was never completed and never quite fit. The western half
of the grid lay on the low, wet ground of the Katsura floodplain; it was
malarial, it flooded, and the aristocracy quietly declined to live there. Over a
few centuries the city drifted east and north, toward the higher, drier ground
along the Kamo River, abandoning the western avenues to rice fields. The
ceremonial central axis lost its centrality. What had been drawn as a perfect
rectangle became an L-shaped, lopsided settlement clinging to one side of its
own diagram.

Then it burned. The Ōnin War, which began in 1467 as a succession dispute among
warlords and degenerated into a decade of street fighting, destroyed most of the
capital. Afterward the survivors regrouped into two effectively separate towns —
an upper city around the court and a lower city of merchants — each ditched and
palisaded, each governed by its own neighborhood associations. That habit of
tightly organized blocks, run by residents who knew exactly who belonged and
who did not, outlived the danger that produced it and still shapes how Kyoto
neighborhoods handle everything from festival duties to garbage collection.

Toyotomi Hideyoshi rebuilt the city at the end of the sixteenth century with the
brisk authority of a man who had conquered the country. He drove new north-south
streets through the long blocks to double the amount of street frontage, moved
temples into a single district to get them out of the way, and threw an earth
rampart around the whole settlement, fragments of which survive in odd corners
behind houses and schoolyards. The doubled frontage is still legible: central
Kyoto's blocks are noticeably narrower in one direction than the other.

## Water Under the Floorboards

Rain falling on the surrounding mountains soaks into the gravel fan beneath the
basin and moves slowly through it, so the city sits on an enormous reservoir of
clean, soft groundwater. Kyoto's food is essentially a set of techniques for
exploiting that fact. Soft water makes good tofu and good yuba, the skin lifted
off simmering soymilk, both of them pillars of the vegetarian temple cooking
that became the ancestor of kaiseki. It makes the delicate stock, thin and
almost clear, that a Kyoto cook will defend against the darker broths of Tokyo
with genuine heat. South of the city in Fushimi, where the water is particularly
soft, brewers have made a famously mild sake for centuries. Dyers of kimono silk
used the Kamo for rinsing, and the Nishijin district's looms wove the results
into brocade. Several of the old wells are still in use, and a few shrines keep
one open for anyone who shows up with a bottle.

Surface water was harder. The Kamo flooded so reliably that a twelfth-century
retired emperor is said to have listed it among the three things in the world he
could not control. It has since been embanked into a well-behaved shallow river
with gravel bars and herons, and its long grassy banks function as the city's
informal living room — joggers and buskers and students, and in the evenings
couples sitting along the water spaced out at intervals so consistent that
locals joke about the regulation distance.

The most consequential water project came after the worst blow the city ever
took. When the emperor moved to Tokyo in 1869, Kyoto lost the court, the
government and a large share of its population in a few years, and faced the
real possibility of becoming a provincial town with famous temples. The response
was a canal driven through the mountains from Lake Biwa, designed by a young
engineer barely out of school and finished around 1890. It brought in water for
industry and irrigation, carried boats over a cable-hauled incline at Keage, and
fed Japan's first commercial hydroelectric plant, which in turn powered the
country's first electric streetcar. A city that had been running on imperial
prestige reinvented itself on hydropower. The canal still runs, and a branch of
it flows past the temples on the eastern hills along the walking route now known
as the Philosopher's Path.

## Machiya, Lattice, Noren

The traditional townhouse, the *machiya*, is long, narrow and dark at the
street. Frontages are tight and the buildings run back thirty meters or more,
which earned them the nickname "eel beds"; the usual explanation is that taxes
were once assessed on street width, though the doubled frontages of Hideyoshi's
rebuilding probably encouraged the shape as well. Inside, a packed-earth
corridor runs the full depth of the house from the shop at the front to the
kitchen at the back, with a high vent above it to pull smoke out. Small interior
gardens — sometimes only a few square meters, a stone lantern, a maple, moss —
puncture the plan at intervals to bring in light and to drive a breeze through
the house in summer.

The street face is a lesson in graded privacy: wooden lattice screens that let
those inside see out but not the reverse, a bamboo fender along the base of the
wall, a split cloth curtain hung in the doorway that says the shop is open, and,
in some quarters, a small shrine mounted at roof level. Thousands of machiya
have been lost to inheritance taxes, fire regulations and the simple economics
of land value; thousands more have been converted into cafés, guesthouses and
shops for foreign visitors, a transformation that residents regard with feelings
that are honestly mixed. Kyoto's answer has been regulatory rather than
sentimental: strict height limits across most of the city, rules on rooftop
advertising and signage, and subsidies for keeping old timber frames standing.

## A Year Measured in Crowds

Kyoto's climate is the basin again. Air settles in it and does not leave. July
and August are brutally humid, with nights that barely cool; winter brings a raw
cold and, a few times a season, wet snow that transforms the temple roofs and
draws photographers out before dawn. In between are the two seasons the city
sells: cherry blossom in early April, and maples in the second half of November,
each lasting roughly two weeks and each drawing a volume of people that makes
the buses unusable.

The festival calendar is older than the tourism and less negotiable. In July the
Gion Matsuri, which began as a plague-dispelling rite more than a millennium
ago, occupies the whole month and culminates in wheeled floats several stories
tall being hauled through the streets by teams on ropes, pivoted around corners
on wet bamboo because the wheels do not steer. On the evening of August 16, five
enormous fires are lit in sequence on the hillsides — the first of them shaped
into the character for "great" — to see the spirits of the dead back out of the
world. People watch from riverbanks and rooftops and from the flat top of a
department store, and the whole thing is over in half an hour.

What is easy to miss under all of this is that Kyoto is an ordinary working city
of well over a million people, with a large student population, an electronics
industry, a traffic problem and a shortage of family housing. The visitor sees
the eastern hills and the geisha districts; the resident sees the covered
shopping arcades, the bicycle parked illegally and ticketed, the standing noodle
counter at the station, the elderly neighbor sweeping the strip of pavement in
front of her gate at seven in the morning because that is the arrangement and
has been for four hundred years. The grid holds it together. Give a Kyoto
resident a street name in each direction and they will tell you exactly where
you are standing, which is what the surveyors of 794 were aiming at.
