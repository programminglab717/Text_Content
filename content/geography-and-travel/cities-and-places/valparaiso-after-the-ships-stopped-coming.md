# Valparaíso After the Ships Stopped Coming

The wooden car of the Ascensor Reina Victoria holds about a dozen people if
they stand close. It leaves from a doorway on a busy street in the flat part of
town, climbs a slope steep enough that the floor has to be built in steps like
a staircase, and delivers you ninety seconds later onto a terrace of painted
corrugated-iron houses with the whole bay laid out below — cranes, container
stacks, a naval frigate, the curve of the shore running away north toward Viña
del Mar. The fare is small change. The machinery is well over a century old.
Most days it works.

Valparaíso is a city that has been running on old machinery, literally and
otherwise, since the thing it was built for stopped happening.

## A Shelf of Flat Land and Forty-Odd Hills

The site is barely a site. Along this part of the Chilean coast the land rises
out of the Pacific almost immediately, and Valparaíso occupies the one place
where a bay offers usable shelter. Between the water and the slope there is a
narrow shelf of level ground, called simply El Plan, and a good deal of even
that is artificial — reclaimed from the sea in the nineteenth century to make
room for warehouses, customs houses, rail track and the port itself.

Everything else is vertical. Behind El Plan the ground climbs three hundred
meters in an arc of steep hills, more than forty of them by local reckoning,
each with its own name and its own identity: Cerro Alegre, Cerro Concepción,
Cerro Artillería, Cerro Bellavista, Cerro Polanco. Between the hills run
quebradas, the ravines that carry rainwater down to the sea and that were
filled, over the years, with the houses of people who could not afford a ridge.
The hills form a rough amphitheater around the bay, which means that from
almost anywhere in the city you can see a great deal of the rest of it, tilted
toward you like seating.

The street pattern makes sense only in that light. Valparaíso was never
formally laid out. Spanish colonial cities were founded by ceremony, with a
plaza staked out and a grid drawn around it, but this was a landing place for
Santiago's cargo rather than a town in its own right, and it simply accumulated.
The flat part eventually got something approximating a grid; the hills got
whatever fit. Streets end in staircases. A named avenue can narrow to a walkway
between two walls. Houses are entered from their roofs.

## The Port at the End of the Horn Route

For roughly a century Valparaíso was one of the most important harbors in the
Pacific, and the reason was Cape Horn. A ship sailing from Europe or the east
coast of the United States to anywhere on the Pacific rim had to round the
bottom of South America and then beat north, and Valparaíso was the first
serious port it reached — the first place to take on fresh water, repair
damage, unload, refit, and settle accounts. The California gold rush multiplied
the traffic. Chilean wheat went north to feed San Francisco; everything else in
the world came south.

The money and the people arrived together. British, German, Italian and
Croatian communities established themselves on the hills and brought their
institutions with them. Chile's first stock exchange opened here. So did the
Spanish-speaking world's longest-running daily newspaper, founded in 1827 and
still publishing. The city built gas lighting, streetcars and telegraph lines
early, generally before the capital did. Because timber buildings burned
regularly, residents organized a volunteer fire service in the mid-nineteenth
century, its companies formed along national lines — a German company, a
British one, a French one — and the whole service is volunteer to this day, run
by companies that still carry those names and treat membership as an
inheritance.

The immigrants also left the cemeteries. On Cerro Panteón the Catholic burial
grounds sit next to the Cementerio de Disidentes, the dissidents' cemetery,
created for the Protestant merchants who could not be buried in consecrated
Catholic ground. The inscriptions are in English and German, the family names
are Liverpool and Hamburg shipping names, and the view over the bay is
excellent.

## 1906, Then 1914

Two blows landed eight years apart. On a winter evening in August 1906 a large
earthquake struck the central Chilean coast and wrecked Valparaíso. Thousands
died. Fire followed the collapse, as it always does, and the reconstruction of
the flat town dates largely from the years just after.

The city rebuilt confidently, because it had every reason to expect the ships
to keep coming. They did not. The Panama Canal opened in 1914 and erased the
Cape Horn route in a single stroke. Traffic that had made Valparaíso rich now
had no reason to come within four thousand kilometers of it. The great trading
houses wound down or moved to Santiago. The banks went with them. What was left
was a regional port serving Chilean exports, and a built environment scaled for
something much larger.

The decline was long and not quite terminal. The city kept its navy, which has
its national headquarters on Plaza Sotomayor, looking out across the square at
a monument to the dead of a nineteenth-century naval battle. It kept its
universities, including a technical institution founded on a shipping fortune
whose campus looks like a gothic abbey misplaced onto a coastal hillside. In
1990 the Chilean Congress was relocated here from Santiago, a deliberate act of
decentralization that deposited an enormous concrete building at the edge of
the flat town and brought several hundred staff who mostly go home at weekends.
And in 2003 UNESCO listed the historic quarter, which had the effect of
converting a certain amount of picturesque dereliction into boutique hotels on
Cerro Alegre and Cerro Concepción while the hills further out stayed poor.

## Riding the Hills

The funiculars — ascensores, though most of them are inclined railways rather
than lifts — began in the 1880s as a commercial solution to a commuting
problem. Workers lived up the hills and worked down on the flat, and walking up
three hundred meters twice a day is a serious tax on a person. At the peak
there were around thirty of them, privately built and privately run, each a
short track with two counterweighted cars passing at the midpoint, originally
driven by water ballast and later by electric motors.

About half the structures survive and rather fewer run at any given time,
depending on maintenance money and which agency currently owns them. They are
national monuments now, which guarantees them attention and not necessarily
funding. One of the strangest, on Cerro Polanco, is a genuine vertical
elevator: you walk into a tunnel driven horizontally into the hillside, ride up
a shaft inside a yellow tower, and come out on a footbridge at the top. Another
climbs to Cerro Bellavista, where the walls of a neighborhood were painted in
the 1990s as an open-air mural museum, an idea that has since escaped and
colonized most of the city. Valparaíso is now painted more or less continuously
— stairs done as piano keys, whole gable ends given over to murals, tags layered
over everything, all of it renewed and defaced on a cycle of months.

The other surviving antique is the trolleybus. A fleet of them still works the
flat town, including cars built in the United States in the late 1940s, some of
them acquired secondhand from Switzerland decades ago and kept alive by
mechanics who fabricate what they cannot buy. They are officially protected
historic objects that are also, inconveniently for preservationists, public
transport that people use to get to work.

## Paint, Fire, and What the City Runs On

The colors have a practical origin. Houses here were built as light timber
frames — safer in an earthquake than masonry — and clad in corrugated iron
sheets that arrived as ballast in British ships and were sold off cheap at the
dock. Bare iron rusts in salt air, so it had to be painted, and what got used
was whatever marine paint was left in the chandlers' stores. The result is a
city of pink, turquoise, ochre and oxblood walls, still repainted in the same
improvised spirit.

Iron and timber burn, and the hills burn regularly. The quebradas fill with dry
brush in summer; informal settlements have crept up into them; the roads are
too narrow for engines and the water pressure at the top of a three-hundred-
meter hill is poor. A fire in April 2014 destroyed thousands of homes across
several hills in a single night. Another in February 2024, in the hills between
Valparaíso and Viña del Mar, killed more than a hundred people. The volunteer
companies still turn out, as they have since the 1850s, and the structural
problem is unchanged.

What keeps the place going is ordinary. The port still works, shipping fruit
and copper, though a rival harbor to the south now handles more containers.
Commuter trains run up the coast. Students fill the cheap flats. Fishing boats
land at small coves where pelicans and sea lions wait for scraps directly
beside the cleaning tables. In a bar off Plaza Aníbal Pinto with an old vermouth
sign outside, someone will be singing boleros to a room that has heard them
before, and a plate of chorrillana — fries under beef, onions and fried eggs —
will arrive designed for four people and ordered by two.

Pablo Neruda kept a house on Cerro Florida with windows angled at the harbor,
and wrote an ode to the city that is mostly a list of its collapses: the
earthquakes, the fires, the ships that left. He liked it anyway. So does
everyone who stays.
