# Hanoi: A Day in the Old Quarter

At five in the morning the lake belongs to people over sixty. They come out of
the lanes in windbreakers and sandals, three and four abreast, and walk the
circuit of Hoàn Kiếm at a pace that never varies. On the paving by the water
somebody has set up a portable speaker and a dozen women are doing a routine
that is half tai chi and half aerobics. Badminton games go on under the
streetlights without a net, two players and a shuttlecock and a great deal of
shouting. A man in his seventies hangs from a tree branch by both hands. By half
past six all of them are gone, the speakers packed away, and the first
motorbikes begin to arrive.

This is the hinge of a Hanoi day, and it is worth catching, because the city
that follows is loud enough to make you forget the quiet one existed.

## Delta, Dike, Dragon

Hanoi sits in the Red River delta, on flat alluvial ground a little above sea
level and some ninety kilometres inland. The river that made the delta is named
for its colour: it carries an enormous load of iron-rich silt down off the
mountains of Yunnan and northern Vietnam, and in the wet season it runs the
brown-red of strong tea. That silt has been the region's fortune and its
standing threat. It built the fertile plain that supports one of the densest
rural populations on earth, and it also raises the riverbed year by year, so that
in places the water flows above the level of the fields beside it, held in only
by earth.

Those dikes are among the oldest continuously maintained public works in Asia.
Dynasties rose and fell, but the dike corvée went on, because a breach in August
meant a drowned harvest for a province. You can still walk long stretches of the
embankment in the city, a raised road with houses crowded up its inner flank,
and understand very quickly that everything you are looking at exists at the
pleasure of the river.

The city itself was founded in 1010, when the emperor Lý Thái Tổ moved his
capital here and named it Thăng Long, the ascending dragon, after a vision he
reported on arriving. It stayed the seat of Vietnamese power for most of the
next eight centuries. The name Hanoi came later, in the nineteenth century, and
is a plainer piece of geography: the place inside the rivers.

## Thirty-Six Streets

The Old Quarter grew up outside the citadel walls as the commercial quarter, and
it organized itself by trade. Craftsmen came from villages in the surrounding
delta, settled together by specialty, brought their village patron deities with
them and built small temples on the corners. The streets took the names of what
was sold on them, each one prefixed with hàng, meaning goods or wares. Hàng Bạc
is silver. Hàng Gai is hemp, though it long ago became silk. Hàng Thiếc is tin.
Hàng Chiếu is sleeping mats. Hàng Mã sells the paper votive objects burned as
offerings to the dead — paper motorbikes, paper mobile phones, paper stacks of
dollars, all of it in colours visible from a block away.

Tradition calls the quarter the thirty-six streets, and the number is more
poetic than accurate, but the principle still holds to a degree that surprises
people. Go looking for a hinge or a hasp and you will find yourself on a street
where nine consecutive shops sell nothing but hinges and hasps, their owners
sitting on stools in the doorway, entirely relaxed about the competition.
Clustering is not a failure of the market here. It is the market: customers know
where to come, and a shop that lacks the exact size sends you two doors down.

## The Tube House and the Sidewalk

The buildings are the other inheritance. A classic Hanoi tube house is perhaps
three metres wide and thirty or forty deep, a shaft of rooms running back from
the street with one or two small light wells punched through the middle. The
usual explanation is that property was once taxed on street frontage, which
rewarded anyone willing to live in a corridor. Whatever the origin, the form
suits the way commerce works: the front room is the shop, the back rooms are the
family, and the stairs are steep and narrow and lead to bedrooms stacked three
storeys up.

Because the house has no yard, the sidewalk does the work a yard would do. This
is the single most important thing to understand about street life in Hanoi. The
pavement is not a place for walking. It is where the family parks the
motorbikes, where the grandmother shells beans, where the noodle stall sets out
its low plastic stools at dawn and clears them at ten, where the tea seller
keeps a thermos and four glasses, where the barber hangs a mirror on a wall and
cuts hair in the open air, where the card game happens. Pedestrians walk in the
road, and the traffic accommodates them, because the traffic is mostly
motorbikes moving at the speed of a bicycle, and a motorbike can go around a
person without anybody breaking stride.

Crossing a street here is the thing every visitor fails at first and then
suddenly understands. You do not wait for a gap, because no gap is coming. You
step off the kerb and walk at a slow constant speed without stopping, and the
riders read your trajectory and flow around you. The horn is used constantly and
means, almost always, I am here, not get out of the way.

## A Layer of France

Southeast of the Old Quarter the streets abruptly get wide and straight and
lined with trees, and the buildings turn into shuttered ochre villas set behind
gates. This is the French quarter, laid out after Hanoi was made the capital of
French Indochina at the turn of the twentieth century. There is an opera house
modelled on Paris, a gothic cathedral with stone blackened by a hundred years of
damp, and a great many civil service buildings now occupied by ministries.

The most eloquent French object is the Long Biên bridge across the Red River, a
long, spindly cantilever of riveted steel finished in the early 1900s by a
French engineering firm, and credited by nearly every guidebook to Gustave
Eiffel, who had nothing to do with it. It was bombed repeatedly during the war
with the United States and repaired repeatedly in between, and the
mismatched spans are visible from the riverbank — the graceful original trusses
alternating with plainer wartime replacements. It now carries a single railway
track, two lanes of motorbikes and a footpath, and at dusk it fills with
teenagers taking photographs of the sun going down over the sandbanks.

## Four Seasons on the Same Street

Northern Vietnam has real seasons, which surprises people who expect uniform
tropical heat. Summer is brutal, humid, upper thirties, with afternoon
thunderstorms that flood the low streets to knee height in half an hour. Autumn
is the season Hanoians write songs about: three or four weeks of dry air and
clear light, when the hoa sữa trees flower and release a scent so heavy and
sweet that people argue about whether they like it. Winter is genuinely cold,
not freezing but damp and grey, and since almost no building has heating it gets
into everything. Late winter brings mưa phùn, a fine persistent drizzle that
does not so much fall as hang, and then nồm, warm wet southerly air that
condenses on cool tile floors and makes the whole house sweat.

The trees track this. The old sấu trees along the boulevards drop a sour green
fruit that gets pickled, or dropped into soup, or soaked in sugar for a summer
drink. Flame trees go scarlet at the end of the school year.

## Evening

Food in Hanoi runs on a schedule and the schedule is not negotiable. Phở is
breakfast, a clear beef or chicken broth over rice noodles, served with very
little on top — a few slivers of spring onion, some chilli and vinegar on the
table, none of the mountain of herbs and sprouts you get further south. Bún chả
is lunch, and only lunch: charcoal-grilled pork patties and pieces of belly
floating in a bowl of sweetened, diluted fish sauce with pickled kohlrabi, eaten
with cold rice vermicelli and a pile of lettuce and perilla. By two in the
afternoon the bún chả smoke is gone from the lanes and the grills are cold.

Late afternoon is bia hơi. Fresh, unpasteurized draft beer is brewed and
delivered the same day in steel kegs, poured into scuffed glasses, and sold for
a sum too small to think about at corner stalls that spill onto the intersection.
The stools are plastic and about twenty centimetres high. The tables are the
same. Everybody sits down there together, office workers and builders and
students, knees up around their ears, and the noise carries three streets.

Later still the Old Quarter thins out, the shutters roll down over the hinge
shops, and the lanes go quiet enough to hear a kettle. Around the lake the
paving cools. Someone is already thinking about five in the morning.
