# Marrakesh: A City Engineered Against Heat

In July the air over the plain outside Marrakesh is hot enough that the
mountains to the south swim and sometimes disappear entirely. Then in December
they come back hard-edged and white, the High Atlas rising to well over four
thousand meters within sight of the city walls, holding a snowpack that will
melt into the plain over the following months. Everything about how Marrakesh
was built follows from those two facts. The place is hot, in a way that is
seriously hostile for four months of the year, and the water that makes it
possible is up in the mountains rather than under the city.

There is no proper river here. The Tensift, a few kilometers north, runs
seasonally and unreliably. What the site has is an alluvial fan: gravel washed
down out of the Atlas over geological time, with water moving slowly through it
underground. The Almoravids, a Berber confederation out of the Sahara, founded
their capital on this fan around 1070, and within a generation their engineers
had worked out how to get at the water.

## Tapping the Fan

The technique is the *khettara*, the Moroccan version of a technology found from
Iran to western China under other names. You start where the water table comes
close to the surface, high up on the piedmont, and you dig a mother well down to
it. Then you drive a tunnel from the well back toward the city, at a gradient
slightly shallower than the slope of the ground, so that the tunnel emerges into
daylight several kilometers away. Water enters along the galleries and runs down
under gravity. There is no pumping and there is nothing to break. Every twenty
or thirty meters a vertical shaft is sunk for ventilation and for hauling out
spoil, and those shafts, seen from the air, leave a line of small craters
marching across the desert like the track of a machine.

Dozens of these lines once converged on Marrakesh. They filled the basins of the
Agdal and Menara gardens, irrigated the olive groves, and watered the
*palmeraie*, the belt of date palms north and east of the city that was, by
tradition, seeded from the stones spat out by an Almoravid army camped there. It
is a genuinely elegant system: self-regulating, since a khettara can only ever
deliver what the aquifer supplies, and maintained collectively by the users, who
had to send men down the shafts every year to clear silt and shore up the
galleries. It is also almost entirely dead. Motor pumps drilled into the same
aquifer from the 1960s onward dropped the water table below the level of the old
tunnels, and most khettaras ran dry within a few decades. The gardens and the
golf courses are now on boreholes and on canal water brought from the mountains,
and the palm grove is thinner and patchier than the photographs of a century ago
show.

## Building with the Ground You Stand On

The city wall is nearly twenty kilometers of rammed earth. The technique, called
*tabia*, is simple and extremely old: pack a mixture of the local soil with
lime and a little water into wooden shuttering, tamp it down hard in layers,
move the shuttering up and repeat. The horizontal seams between lifts and the
regular holes left by the scaffolding poles are visible in every stretch of the
Marrakesh walls. The soil here is loaded with iron oxide, which is why the whole
city reads as a single dusty pink-orange, a color the municipality now enforces
on new construction. Mud wall does not weather well — it needs a cap to keep
rain off the top and regular resurfacing — but it has one enormous advantage in
this climate. It is thick and dense, so it absorbs heat all day and releases it
slowly at night, which flattens out the daily swing between a scorching
afternoon and a cold desert night.

The same logic governs the houses. The classic Marrakesh dwelling turns its back
on the street: blank earth wall, one modest door, no windows at ground level.
Everything faces inward onto a courtyard, which in the larger houses — the
*riads* proper, the word means garden — is planted in four quarters around a
central fountain, often with bitter orange or pomegranate. The courtyard is a
chimney and a cistern of cool air at once. Overnight it fills with air chilled
by the exposed sky; the surrounding rooms, shaded by deep arcades and finished
in lime plaster, draw on that reservoir through the hottest part of the day.
Interior surfaces do real work: mosaic *zellige* cut tile on the lower walls,
and *tadelakt*, a lime plaster polished with stone and sealed with olive-oil
soap, which is waterproof and stays cool to the touch. Put a hundred of these
houses side by side with only narrow lanes between them and you get the medina —
a dense thermal mass with shaded slots cut through it, perhaps ten degrees
cooler at street level than the open plain outside the gates.

## The Machinery of a Quarter

Traditional medina neighborhoods were organized around a short list of shared
institutions, and in many quarters of Marrakesh the list still functions. There
is a mosque. There is a fountain. There is a *ferran*, the communal wood-fired
oven, where households still send round trays of dough in the morning — each
marked in some way so the family gets its own loaves back — and where a tray of
stew can be left to cook for a few dirhams. There is a hammam, the public bath,
and behind the hammam is the *farnatchi*, the furnace room, where a man feeds a
fire that heats the bath water. The whole arrangement is a heat cascade: the
oven and the furnace burn the offcuts of the city's carpentry shops and the
crushed pits from the olive presses, the flue runs under the bath floor, and
the waste heat that is left over is where Marrakesh cooks its signature dish.
*Tanjia* is not a tagine. It is beef or lamb with preserved lemon, cumin and
smen sealed in a tall clay urn, handed to the furnace man, and buried in the
embers for half a day. It is traditionally a bachelor's dish, cooked by men,
collected on the way to a picnic.

Between these fixed points run the *derbs*, residential dead-end lanes with a
single entrance, which made a quarter defensible and also made it socially
legible: nobody walks up your derb without a reason. The commercial souks
occupy a separate zone north of the great square, sorted by trade in a rough
ring — the noisy and smelly crafts pushed outward, the clean and valuable ones
kept close to the main mosque. Iron is hammered in one alley and the sound
carries two streets. In the dyers' souk, skeins of freshly dipped wool in
alarming synthetic colors hang across the lane overhead, dripping. The tanneries
sit near a gate on the northeast edge, where the stink and the runoff can leave
town. The overhead lattice of palm slats that shades the main souk streets casts
a striped, moving light that photographers love and that serves the entirely
practical purpose of keeping the merchandise out of the sun.

## The Square, the Two Cities, the Cracks

At the south end of the souks is the Jemaa el-Fna, an irregular open space that
functions as the hinge of the whole city. It empties and refills over the course
of a day. Morning is orange juice carts, water sellers in fringed hats and
copper cups who now make their living mainly from photographs, and men with
teeth spread out on cloths. Afternoon brings the snake handlers and the monkeys.
Then at dusk a hundred food stalls are wheeled in and assembled within about
twenty minutes, numbered and lit and smoking, and rings of listeners form around
Gnawa musicians with iron castanets and three-stringed lutes, around acrobats,
around boxers, and — fewer every year — around storytellers working a repertoire
that came down orally. It was the storytellers, as much as anything else, that
led UNESCO to recognize the square's living oral culture rather than its
buildings.

West of the medina, across a boulevard, is a different city entirely. Gueliz was
laid out during the French Protectorate on a policy of leaving the old town
alone and building a new one beside it: straight avenues, plane trees, cafés
with terraces, apartment blocks. The two halves have never merged. Middle-class
Marrakshis shop in Gueliz supermarkets and visit the medina for hardware and
weddings; medina families rent to tourists and work in the hotels of the
Hivernage district. Mopeds are the thread that ties it all together — thousands
of them, threading through the souk at implausible speed, the rider's shout of
*balak* the only warning you get.

The mountains that supply the water are also a fault zone. Late one evening in
September 2023 a strong earthquake struck in the High Atlas south of the city.
The worst of it fell on stone-and-mud villages in the mountains, where whole
settlements came down and thousands died. Marrakesh itself, farther from the
epicenter, got off comparatively lightly, but minarets cracked, a stretch of the
old rammed-earth wall slumped, and people slept in the square and in the parks
for several nights. Repair work on the medina has gone on since, slowly, in
earth and lime, by the same methods the walls were built with in the first
place.
