# Buenos Aires on the River Plate

The river that gives Buenos Aires its reason for existing is not really a river
and is not, from most of the city, visible. The Río de la Plata is the merged
estuary of the Paraná and the Uruguay, and at the point where it meets the
Atlantic it is something like two hundred kilometers across — a sheet of brown
freshwater so wide that from the waterfront you see no far bank, only a horizon.
It is also shallow, silt-choked and short of anywhere to shelter a ship. The
pampa that runs up to it is dead flat. There is no bay, no headland, no island,
nothing that says: here.

So the city had to be argued into place twice. Pedro de Mendoza's expedition
planted a settlement in 1536 and abandoned it within five years, having starved
and fought with the people already living there. Juan de Garay came back down
the rivers from Asunción in 1580 and tried again with a more realistic plan and
more cattle, and this time it held. For the next two centuries it held
marginally. Spanish law routed all legal trade through Lima and Panama, which
meant that the obvious port for the whole southern cone was officially a
backwater, and Buenos Aires lived instead on contraband: hides and silver going
out to Portuguese and British ships that were not supposed to be there,
manufactured goods coming in the same way. The habit of treating official
economic rules as a starting point for negotiation is very old here.

## The Flattest Possible Site

Flatness gave the city one thing: nothing stopped the grid. The colonial
surveyors laid out square blocks of roughly a hundred meters a side around a
central plaza, exactly as Spanish ordinance instructed, and the grid then
expanded outward for four centuries without meeting an obstacle worth the name.
Avenues run arrow-straight for twenty kilometers. One of them, Rivadavia, is
long enough that street numbers climb past twenty thousand. A visitor's first
impression is of relentless regularity; the second is of how much variety a
strict grid can carry, because the buildings on it were put up by successive
waves of people with entirely different ideas about what a city should look
like.

The one topographic feature that matters is easy to miss. A low bluff, the
*barranca*, runs through the northern barrios — the old shoreline of the
estuary, a few meters high. Everything below it, including the parks along the
waterfront and the airport, is either reclaimed ground or was once marsh. A
handful of small streams once drained across the plain into the river; they were
culverted and built over during the twentieth century, and the largest of them
now runs in a concrete box beneath a major avenue. Every few years a
*sudestada* — a persistent southeasterly that piles estuary water back against
the shore — coincides with a summer downpour, the buried streams overflow their
culverts, and the neighborhoods on top of them flood. The flat city has a
drainage problem that its own street plan pretends is not there.

The port, too, had to be invented. A first set of enclosed docks was built along
the downtown waterfront in the 1890s with British money and engineering, and was
obsolete almost immediately because ships grew faster than the design allowed
for. A second port was dug to the north and took over the work. The first one
sat derelict for most of a century, close enough to the financial district to
see from an office window, before being converted in the 1990s into the glass
towers and expensive apartments of Puerto Madero, with the old brick warehouses
kept as a façade along the water.

## The Arrival

Between roughly 1880 and 1930 Argentina received one of the largest immigration
flows in the world relative to its existing population, and most of it came
through Buenos Aires. The great majority were Italians and Spaniards, with
smaller but substantial numbers of Jews from the Russian Empire, Syrians and
Lebanese, Poles, Welsh, Germans and Japanese. By the time of the census taken on
the eve of the First World War, something close to half the people in the city
had been born somewhere else.

They arrived into a housing stock that could not hold them, and the result was
the *conventillo*: an old courtyard mansion abandoned by a wealthy family after
the yellow fever epidemic of 1871 pushed the elite north toward Recoleta, now
subdivided so that each room held a family, with a shared patio, shared washing
and one privy. Purpose-built versions followed. The conventillo is where a great
deal of what the world recognizes as Argentine culture was actually assembled.
*Lunfardo*, the city's slang, took shape there and in the port districts, built
largely on Italian dialect words plus a game of inverting syllables, and it
passed into ordinary speech so thoroughly that porteños use dozens of its words
without knowing the origin. Tango came out of the same margins — the dance halls
and courtyards of the outer edges, with a German-made accordion called the
bandoneón giving it that wheezing, sacred sound — and was disreputable for a
generation until it went to Paris, became fashionable, and returned home
acceptable.

What the immigrants built, once they had money, was European in an exuberant,
unsupervised way. Italian masons and French-trained architects produced blocks
where an ornate Beaux-Arts apartment house with a slate mansard stands next to
an Art Nouveau confection next to a stripped 1970s slab, all of them the same
height and all of them with the corner cut off. That last detail is a municipal
ordinance and one of the quiet pleasures of the place: at every intersection the
building corners are chamfered into a diagonal face, the *ochava*, originally so
that carriages could see around them. It means that every crossing in Buenos
Aires opens into a small octagonal room of sky, and that corner cafés get a
wide, triangular window looking both ways down the street.

## Underground, Overhead, and on the Bus

The subway opened in 1913, the first underground railway in the southern
hemisphere, and its oldest line ran varnished wooden carriages with brass
fittings until they were finally retired about a decade ago. Above ground the
real system is buses — hundreds of routes, running all night, historically
decorated with *fileteado*, the local ornamental painting of curling acanthus
scrolls, dragons and mottoes in a particular kind of dimensional lettering,
which migrated from horse carts to buses to tattoos and is now a recognized folk
art.

The streets are lined with trees chosen a century ago and now enormous: plane
trees that peel in patches, tipas that drip sticky sap onto parked cars all
summer, and jacarandas that spend two weeks in November turning entire avenues
violet and then drop the flowers to be trodden into the pavement. In the few
large parks there are ombús, which are not really trees but giant herbs with
swollen grey trunks that spread across the ground like something from an earlier
world.

Daily life runs late and in public. Lunch is at one, dinner starts at ten and
dinner with friends can finish at two in the morning on a weeknight. The café
is an institution with legal standing — a register of historic cafés protects
the old ones from renovation — and it is normal to occupy a table for two hours
over one coffee and a glass of soda water, reading, arguing, working. Bookshops
survive in absurd density along Corrientes, open past midnight, including one
built inside a converted 1920s theater where the stalls are shelves and the
stage is a café. The asado is less a meal than an afternoon: a fire of hardwood
burned down to coals, a grill loaded in a deliberate order, and hours of
standing around it. Mate circulates continuously, one gourd, one metal straw,
refilled and passed in a fixed direction, and refusing your turn requires a
specific word.

## The Cycle

The other constant is economic turbulence, and it has shaped the city's habits
as much as the grid has. Argentina has spent much of the last eighty years
cycling through inflation, devaluation, capital controls and default, and
porteños have developed a practical, almost technical relationship with money:
prices renegotiated mid-project, salaries converted into dollars and stored
physically, informal exchange houses operating in plain sight along downtown
streets while men murmur the day's rate at passers-by. The collapse of 2001 —
bank deposits frozen, several presidents in a fortnight, crowds in the street
banging pots — is the reference point everybody over thirty-five measures new
crises against.

Politics is equally close to the surface, because the physical center of the
country's power is a square you can walk into. The Plaza de Mayo has the
presidential palace at one end and the colonial town hall at the other, and it
has absorbed two centuries of demonstrations, including the Thursday circuits
walked by the mothers of the disappeared through the worst years of the
dictatorship and continued long after. On an ordinary weekday it is pigeons,
office workers and a man selling flags. On any given month it may be a hundred
thousand people. Buenos Aires does not keep these things at a distance; the
protest, the café, the bus route and the jacarandas all occupy the same block.
