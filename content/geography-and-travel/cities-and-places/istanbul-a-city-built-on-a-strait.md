# Istanbul: A City Built on a Strait

The men fishing from the lower deck of the Galata Bridge drop their lines into
water that moves faster than it looks. From a café table the Bosphorus reads as
a broad, placid harbor with ships parked on it. The people who work the water
know better. Ferry captains crab their boats upstream of the berth they are
aiming for. Fishermen shift along the railing as the eddies shift. Tanker
pilots, taking a loaded ship down through a channel that bends twice at nearly
ninety degrees, treat it as what it functionally is: a river with a serious
current, running through the middle of a city of some fifteen million people.

That current is the reason the city exists, and it is stranger than it appears.
The Black Sea receives enormous volumes of fresh water from the Danube, the
Dnieper, the Don and half a dozen other rivers, far more than evaporates off it.
It sits higher than the Mediterranean and its water is much less salty. So the
top layer of the Bosphorus flows south, out of the Black Sea, at a steady
walking pace and faster in places. Underneath it, dense Mediterranean water runs
the other way, north along the bottom, a submerged current flowing uphill in
salinity terms toward the Black Sea. Anchovies, bonito and bluefish ride these
flows on their seasonal migrations, which is why the fishing calendar in
Istanbul is so precise, and why a plate of grilled *hamsi* in December tastes
like a local event rather than a menu item.

## Why Anything Was Ever Built Here

Look at the shape of the land and the site almost insists on a city. A strait
roughly thirty kilometers long joins the Black Sea to the Sea of Marmara, which
drains in turn through the Dardanelles to the Aegean. The only land route
between Europe and Anatolia that does not involve a long detour around the Black
Sea crosses here. Halfway down the European shore, a drowned river valley cuts
inland for several kilometers: the Golden Horn, deep, sheltered from every wind
that matters, and easy to close with a chain across its mouth. A triangular
peninsula sits between the Horn and the Marmara, water on two sides, high ground
in the middle, and only one landward approach to defend.

The Greeks from Megara who founded Byzantion in the seventh century BC settled
on that peninsula. A story told since antiquity has the Delphic oracle
instructing them to build opposite the land of the blind, the blind being the
earlier colonists at Chalcedon on the Asian side, who had somehow looked at the
two shores and chosen the wrong one. The story is too neat to be history, but it
captures something real. Kadıköy, as Chalcedon is now called, is a pleasant
place with no harbor worth the name.

Constantine refounded the town as his eastern capital in 330 and the site did
the rest. For eleven centuries the city was the administrative and commercial
hinge of the eastern Mediterranean, and for most of that period the best
defended place on earth. The land walls that Theodosius II built in the fifth
century across the neck of the peninsula were a layered system — a ditch, a low
outer wall, a terrace, then an inner wall nearly five meters thick studded with
towers. Attackers who got over one line found themselves in a killing ground
below the next. Those walls turned back Avars, Arabs, Bulgars, Rus and Turks
across a thousand years. They still run for kilometers through the western
districts, with vegetable gardens growing in the old ditch, laundry lines strung
between towers, and highways punched through them at intervals.

What finally broke them was artillery. In 1453 Mehmed II brought guns capable of
throwing stone balls heavy enough to shake a wall apart over weeks of sustained
fire, and hauled ships overland to get them into the Golden Horn past the chain.
The city he took was a shrunken thing, its population a fraction of what it had
been, with fields inside the walls. He repopulated it deliberately, moving in
Muslims, Greeks, Armenians and Jews — including, within a few decades, refugees
expelled from Spain — and the pattern of a city organized into distinct religious
communities, each with its own courts and schools, lasted into the twentieth
century.

## Domes, and the Problem of Water

Two building campaigns gave Istanbul the skyline it still has. The first was
Justinian's. Hagia Sophia, finished in the 530s under two mathematicians rather
than builders, put a dome more than thirty meters across on top of a square
hall, carrying it on pendentives so that the weight funnels down to four piers
and the space beneath stays open. The first dome came down in an earthquake
within a generation and was rebuilt steeper. What survived is an interior that
does something no photograph reproduces: a ring of windows at the base of the
dome undercuts the masonry with light, so the whole ceiling appears to float
rather than press.

The second campaign belongs to Mimar Sinan, chief architect to three sultans in
the sixteenth century, who spent fifty years working out variations on the
problem Hagia Sophia had posed — and, in the Süleymaniye above the Golden Horn,
solving it with a clarity the older building never had. Sinan's mosques are not
isolated monuments. Each anchors a *külliye*, a complex with a soup kitchen, a
hospital, a school, baths and a cemetery, endowed as a charitable trust and
functioning as a neighborhood's civic center. The domes that stack up the hills
of the old city are the visible part of what was essentially a welfare system.

Sinan also spent years on something invisible. A city on a peninsula between two
seas has no fresh water of its own worth mentioning. The Romans had solved this
with aqueducts reaching far into Thrace and with vast covered cisterns — the
Basilica Cistern under Sultanahmet, with its forest of recycled columns, is only
the most theatrical survivor. The Ottomans rebuilt and extended the system,
Sinan's Kırkçeşme lines bringing water down from the Belgrade Forest through
dams, conduits and the tall arcades that still stride across the valleys north
of the city. Public fountains, some of them small architectural masterpieces,
distributed it at street corners. Supplying water to strangers was a pious act,
and it was also the thing that let the city grow.

## Concrete, Migration and the Fault

The Istanbul that most of its residents actually live in was built after 1950,
and it was built fast. Mechanization pushed people off the land in Anatolia and
industry pulled them to the city; they arrived faster than housing could be
planned. The response was the *gecekondu*, literally "placed by night," a house
run up on unclaimed land between dusk and dawn on the strength of an old
tradition that a dwelling with a roof on it could not simply be torn down. Whole
districts began this way, were regularized in waves by governments hunting for
votes, and were later sold to developers who replaced them with towers. The
hills that were bare in photographs from the 1960s now carry apartment blocks to
the horizon in every direction.

The city crossed to Asia in earnest when the first suspension bridge over the
Bosphorus opened in 1973. Two more bridges followed, along with a rail tunnel
bored beneath the strait, and a metro system that has been extending steadily
for two decades. None of it has caught up. The commute from the outer districts
can run two hours, and the ferries — cheap, frequent, faster than the traffic
they are parallel to — remain the pleasantest way to move, which is why they are
full at rush hour with people drinking tea from tulip glasses on the open deck
while gulls hang motionless off the stern waiting for bread.

Underneath all of it runs the North Anatolian Fault, which crosses the Sea of
Marmara a short distance south of the city and has been unzipping westward
through the twentieth century in a sequence of large earthquakes. The 1999 rupture
near İzmit, an hour east, killed tens of thousands, most of them in apartment
buildings that failed because the concrete was poor and the ground floors were
opened up for shops. Since then Istanbul has lived with an engineering problem
it cannot avoid: an enormous stock of mid-century buildings put up without
serious seismic detailing, and a fault segment offshore that most geologists
expect to slip. Retrofitting and demolition proceed, unevenly, against the
economics of who owns what.

## Weather, and the Rhythm of the Day

Istanbul's residents talk about wind the way other cities talk about rain. The
*poyraz* comes down out of the northeast off the Black Sea, cold and hard,
stacking whitecaps in the strait and sometimes stopping the ferries outright.
The *lodos* blows warm and damp from the southwest, brings haze and headaches,
and is blamed for bad moods with a seriousness that is only half a joke. A
lodos in February can put people in shirtsleeves on the seawall at Kumkapı; a
poyraz in April can put snow on the domes.

The daily texture is small and repetitive and completely particular to the
place. Tea arrives constantly, carried on swinging trays by boys who run it to
shopkeepers up staircases and across streets. Simit sellers push their red carts
into position before seven. Cats, fed by everybody and belonging to nobody,
occupy the warm spots. In the old Greek and Armenian quarters of Kurtuluş and
Kumkapı you can still read the layered communities in doorways and church walls,
though the communities themselves thinned drastically after the violence of 1955
and the expulsions that followed. Fishermen on the bridge pull up small silver
fish in the failing afternoon light while the call to prayer starts from one
minaret, then another slightly out of phase, and the ferries keep crossing
against the current, angled upstream, correcting all the way.
