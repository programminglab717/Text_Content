# Venice: A City Standing in Mud

There is a moment on the causeway from the mainland, somewhere past the oil
tanks and container cranes of Marghera, when the water on both sides of the
train goes shallow and strange. Wooden posts stick out of it in bundles of
three, lashed together and leaning. Between them, in low water, you can see
brown mud with grass growing on it. This is the lagoon, and it is worth looking
at properly for two minutes before the train reaches Santa Lucia, because almost
nothing about Venice makes sense until you understand that the city is sitting
in a tidal marsh roughly a metre deep.

The posts mark the navigable channels. Outside them the bottom comes up to your
knees. The whole basin is about fifty kilometres long, closed off from the
Adriatic by a pair of long barrier islands with three gaps in them, and it
exists only because two processes have been balanced against each other for a
very long time: rivers bringing silt in, and tides washing it out.

## Keeping the Lagoon a Lagoon

Left alone, a lagoon fed by Alpine rivers fills up. Sediment settles, mudflats
rise above high water, marsh becomes meadow, and within a few centuries you have
a coastal plain with a city marooned in the middle of it. The Venetian Republic
understood this early and spent several hundred years preventing it. The Brenta,
the Piave, the Sile — all the rivers that emptied into the basin were, one after
another, excavated into new channels and sent out to sea somewhere else. It was
one of the largest sustained hydraulic engineering programmes in pre-industrial
Europe, undertaken not to build anything but to stop something from happening.

The tides, meanwhile, were the city's sanitation. Twice a day the Adriatic pushes
in through the three inlets and twice a day it drains out, flushing the canals.
The rise is modest, usually well under a metre, but it is enough to move an
enormous volume of water through a shallow basin, and it is the reason a
pre-modern city with no sewers was survivable at all. Everything went into the
rio and the rio emptied.

## Standing on a Forest

The islands the first settlers occupied were mudbanks, and mud will not carry a
church. The solution was piles: trunks of alder, oak and larch, stripped, cut to
a few metres, and driven down side by side in a dense mat until they hit a layer
of compacted clay a short distance below the mud. Venetians call that layer
caranto, and it is the floor the entire city rests on. On top of the pile heads
went a raft of horizontal timbers, on top of that several courses of Istrian
limestone — a dense white stone from across the Adriatic that barely absorbs
water and therefore stops salt from wicking upward — and only then brick.

The piles have not rotted, because rot is biological and the organisms that do
it need oxygen. Buried in saturated anoxic mud, the wood stays waterlogged,
excludes air, and over centuries takes on minerals from the surrounding
sediment. What is under Venice is not decayed timber but something closer to a
forest converted slowly into stone. The scale is hard to hold in the head: the
foundations of the great plague church of Santa Maria della Salute, at the mouth
of the Grand Canal, are recorded as having taken more than a million individual
piles, all of them driven by hand.

The Grand Canal itself was not dug. It follows the bed of an old watercourse
that wound through the mudbanks before anyone built here, which is why it makes
that lazy reversed S instead of running straight, and why it is deep enough for
working traffic when the water on either side of it is not.

## Rain in a Sand Filter

Here is the paradox that shaped daily life for eight centuries: a city
surrounded by water had no fresh water at all. Wells sunk into the ground pull
up brine. The rivers had been deliberately sent elsewhere. So Venice built
cisterns, and it built them in the middle of its open spaces.

Almost every square in the city is a campo, a field, and originally that is what
it was — a bare, slightly convex space serving the parish church and the
families around it. Under it, the Venetians dug a pit, lined it with waterproof
clay, filled it with sand, and sank a shaft of porous brick in the centre.
Rainwater falling on the campo ran to stone drain grates at the corners, soaked
down through the sand, was filtered on the way, and collected in the shaft. The
carved wellhead in the middle of the square, the thing tourists now sit on with
their sandwiches, is the top of that shaft. In dry years boats brought water in
from mainland rivers and topped up the cisterns by hand. It was only in the
1880s that a mainland aqueduct arrived and the wells were sealed.

The street vocabulary is similarly specific, and learning six words turns the
maze into something readable. A calle is an ordinary lane. A salizada was one of
the first streets to be paved, so it is usually old and important. A fondamenta
runs alongside a canal, and a riva is a broad one on open water. A rio is a small
canal; a rio terà is a small canal that was filled in, which is why it is
suspiciously straight and flat. A sottoportego is a passage under a building.
Note also that addresses do not belong to streets. Numbers run sequentially
through an entire district, into the six thousands, in an order that made sense
to some sixteenth-century official and to nobody since.

## What the Republic Was For

All of this infrastructure existed to support a trading state that lasted about
eleven hundred years, which is an unusual run for any political arrangement. The
Republic began as a refuge for mainland populations fleeing invasion in the
centuries after Rome, became a salt monopolist, then a carrier of eastern goods
into Europe, then a genuine maritime empire with possessions down the Dalmatian
coast and across the Aegean.

Its most remarkable physical institution was the Arsenale, the state shipyard in
the east of the city: a walled complex of basins, ropewalks and sheds where
galleys were assembled from standardized, interchangeable components moving past
fitting-out stations in sequence. Thousands of workers were employed there, with
their own privileges and their own district, and the yard could reportedly put
together and equip a complete warship in a single day when the Republic wanted to
impress a visiting dignitary. It was industrial production several centuries
before there was a word for it.

The political machinery was equally deliberate and equally strange. The doge was
elected through a sequence of roughly ten alternating lotteries and ballots,
designed over generations to make it impossible for any faction to control the
outcome. In 1297 membership of the governing council was closed to a fixed list
of families, freezing the ruling class in place. It was not a democracy in any
sense, but it was a machine built to distrust ambitious individuals, and it
outlasted every rival.

## Acqua Alta and the Gates

Napoleon ended the Republic in 1797. The water problem outlived it and got worse.

Acqua alta happens when several things line up: a high spring tide, a sirocco
blowing up the length of the Adriatic and piling water against its closed
northern end, low atmospheric pressure letting the sea surface rise, and
sometimes a slow sloshing oscillation of the whole basin. Any one of these is
survivable. Together they put water over the pavements, and since San Marco is
the lowest point in the city, the basilica floods first.

The floods have become more frequent for two reasons that are easy to separate.
Through the twentieth century, industrial wells at Marghera pumped groundwater
out from under the lagoon and the ground subsided; that pumping was stopped in
the 1970s. At the same time the sea has been rising. Between them, the water
stands something like a quarter of a metre higher relative to the city than it
did a century ago, which converts what used to be a rare event into a routine
winter nuisance and a routine nuisance into a disaster. The great flood of
November 1966 reached nearly two metres above datum. In 2019 a tide came within
centimetres of matching it.

The answer, after decades of argument, cost overruns and a corruption scandal
that reached the mayor's office, is MOSE: rows of hollow steel flap gates lying
flat in caissons on the seabed at each of the three inlets. Normally they are
full of water and invisible. When a dangerous tide is forecast, compressed air
forces the water out, and the gates pivot up on their hinges to stand as a
barrier across the mouths of the lagoon. They have been raised in earnest, and
they have held. What they cannot do is run often, because closing the inlets
also stops the tidal flushing the city has depended on since it was founded, and
the more the gates are used the more Venice trades one kind of drowning for
another.

Meanwhile the resident population of the historic centre has fallen from well
over a hundred thousand after the Second World War to under fifty thousand, and
the wash from motorboats eats at the brickwork of foundations designed for a
world of oars.
