# Edinburgh: The City on the Crag and Tail

Stand on the North Bridge on a clear morning and the entire argument of the
city is visible in one slow turn of the head. To the west, a black cliff with a
castle welded onto its top. To the east, a green hump of moorland rising out of
the rooftops like something the builders forgot to remove. Below your feet,
running south, a canyon of soot-darkened stone where a street called the Cowgate
sits two storeys beneath the street you are standing on. And to the north, past
a railway station sunk into a hole where a lake used to be, a grid of pale
Georgian terraces laid out with the confidence of people who had decided the
medieval experiment was over.

Edinburgh is a city whose shape was decided long before anyone lived in it. Most
old towns grew along a river, or around a crossroads, or outward from a market.
This one grew along a ridge of rock left behind by a glacier, and almost every
peculiarity of the place — the vertical living, the tunnels, the dramatic
sightlines, the stairs — follows from that.

## The Rock and the Ridge

Roughly 340 million years ago, in the Carboniferous, this part of Scotland was
volcanically busy. Magma pushed up through the sedimentary rock and, in one
particular spot, hardened inside the throat of a vent into a mass of dolerite far
tougher than anything around it. The volcano itself eroded away entirely. The
plug did not. That plug is Castle Rock, and its sheer western and southern faces
are the cooled interior of a vent that last did anything interesting before the
first reptiles existed. Arthur's Seat, a mile to the east, is the deeply eroded
remains of a separate volcanic centre from about the same period, and the curved
cliff of Salisbury Crags below it is the exposed edge of a sill — a sheet of
magma that was injected sideways between existing beds of rock rather than
erupting.

The glaciers came much later, and they came from the west. When the ice sheet
ground eastward across the Midland Valley it met the dolerite plug and had to
go around and over it. In the lee of the rock, the softer sedimentary layers
were protected from scouring, so instead of being planed flat they survived as a
long tail of material sloping gently away to the east. Geologists call the
formation a crag and tail, and Edinburgh's Old Town is the textbook example.
The crag is the castle. The tail is the Royal Mile, running down through the
Lawnmarket, the High Street and the Canongate to the Palace of Holyroodhouse at
the bottom. The whole medieval town is a single street on the spine of a
glacial deposit, with steep ground falling away on both sides.

That geometry made the site nearly perfect for defence and nearly impossible
for expansion. The castle guarded the only easy approach. The valleys on either
flank — one of them deliberately flooded to make the Nor' Loch, a defensive
moat that gradually became the town's sewer — kept enemies out and kept the
citizens in.

## Life in the Tall Town

Hemmed in, Edinburgh did the only thing left. It built upward. By the
seventeenth century the Old Town had tenements — locally called lands — rising
to heights that astonished visitors from cities where three storeys was normal.
Some reached ten or eleven floors on the downhill side. The closes and wynds
running off the High Street were not streets in any generous sense but roofed
and unroofed slots barely wide enough for two people, dropping steeply toward
the valleys in flights of worn steps.

Density of that kind produced a social arrangement that has few parallels. A
single stair might hold a shopkeeper in the cellar, a respectable merchant on
the second floor, a judge on the third, and progressively poorer tenants above,
with the garrets under the roof housing the destitute. The desirable floors were
the low ones — no lift existed, water had to be carried up, and waste had to be
carried down or, more commonly, thrown out of the window at ten o'clock at night
after the traditional shouted warning. The nickname Auld Reekie, old smoky, came
from the pall of coal fires that hung over the ridge, but the smell that
visitors recorded came mostly from the street.

Something of that world survives physically. When the city built the Royal
Exchange in the eighteenth century it did not demolish the closes underneath but
simply capped them and built on top, leaving whole lanes of houses entombed in
the foundations. When George IV Bridge and South Bridge were thrown across the
valleys, they were built as long series of arches, and the arches filled with
vaults that were used as workshops, storage and eventually slum housing. Walk
down Victoria Street or the Cowgate and you are in the bottom of the valley,
looking up at the underside of the city's ordinary streets.

## The Rational Half

The escape came in 1766, when the town council ran a competition for a plan to
extend Edinburgh onto the farmland north of the loch. It was won by James Craig,
a young and otherwise little-known architect, whose scheme was as different from
the Old Town as it is possible to imagine: three parallel streets on the
ridge opposite, terminating in a square at each end, with two service lanes
between them and a strict uniformity of frontage. The Nor' Loch was drained.
The Mound was formed, more or less by accident, out of the spoil dug from the
New Town's foundations and dumped across the valley to make a shortcut. What had
been a swamp became gardens, and later the trench that carries the main line
railway into Waverley.

The New Town was built over about seventy years and it remains one of the
largest surviving pieces of Georgian urban planning anywhere. Its logic is
relentlessly horizontal where the Old Town is vertical: wide streets, even
cornice lines, pale sandstone quarried at Craigleith, and an order of precedence
running from the grand principal rooms on the first floor down to the servants'
quarters in the basement area behind the railings.

The two halves were built by the same generation that made Edinburgh briefly one
of the intellectual capitals of Europe. David Hume, Adam Smith, the chemist
Joseph Black, and the geologist James Hutton were all working in the same small
town at the same time, drinking in the same taverns off the High Street. Hutton
in particular could hardly have chosen a better laboratory. His argument that
the Earth was vastly older than scripture allowed, and that its rocks recorded
cycles of deposition and uplift repeating without visible beginning or end, was
worked out partly by examining the junction where the Salisbury Crags sill has
shouldered its way into the sedimentary rock beneath it. The evidence for deep
time was lying in a cliff twenty minutes' walk from his house.

## Haar, Dark and Light

Edinburgh sits at about 56 degrees north, further from the equator than Moscow,
and the year is lopsided in a way that shapes the mood of the place more than
the temperature does. Midsummer evenings never go properly dark; the sky above
the Forth stays a pale blue-green until midnight and the birds treat two in the
morning as dawn. In December the sun clears the rooftops late, tracks low across
the south, and is gone by half past three, which makes the winter feel less like
cold weather than like a long indoor season punctuated by short grey outings.

The other local phenomenon is the haar. When mild air moves over the cold North
Sea in spring and early summer, it condenses into a dense low fog, and an
easterly breeze pushes it up the Forth and into the city. It arrives fast. A
warm blue afternoon on Princes Street can turn into a chilled grey murk in
twenty minutes, with the castle vanishing and the tops of the church spires
floating loose. It rarely reaches far inland, so it is entirely possible to
drive ten miles west into sunshine.

## The Month Everything Changes

For eleven months Edinburgh is a working city of half a million people, with a
large legal establishment, two ancient professions' worth of institutions, a
university woven through the Old Town, and a population that goes about its
business in the rain. In August it becomes something else. The Edinburgh
International Festival was founded in 1947 as a deliberate act of postwar
cultural repair; a handful of companies that had not been invited turned up
anyway and performed on the fringes of it, and the Fringe they accidentally
started long ago outgrew its parent. For three weeks the city's population
roughly doubles. Every church hall, function room, basement vault and temporary
tent becomes a venue, the Royal Mile fills with performers handing out flyers,
and rents for a spare room reach absurd levels.

Locals are divided on this, and reasonably so. But the thing August exposes is
how well the Old Town's geometry suits crowds: a single long spine with dozens
of side closes, every one of them a natural stage, and an audience funnelled
uphill or downhill by the slope. The glacier that shaped the ridge was not
planning a theatre district. It made a very good one anyway.
