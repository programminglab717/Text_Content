# Montreal Between the Rapids and the Mountain

Upriver from the old port, the St. Lawrence stops behaving like a navigable
river. For a stretch of a few kilometers it drops over a shelf of rock into a
churn of standing waves — the Lachine Rapids, still violent enough that rafting
companies sell tickets to ride them. For two centuries after Europeans arrived,
this was where everything going west had to come out of the water and be
carried. That single geographic inconvenience is the whole reason a city of two
million people sits on this island rather than fifty kilometers in either
direction.

## Why Anyone Stopped Here

When Jacques Cartier came up the river in 1535 he found a fortified Iroquoian
town called Hochelaga near the base of the hill, climbed the hill, and named it
Mont Royal. By the time Champlain returned seven decades later the town was
gone, for reasons still argued over. The permanent French settlement came in
1642, and it was not founded as a trading post at all: Ville-Marie was a
missionary colony, financed by a devout society in France, established by Paul
de Chomedey de Maisonneuve alongside Jeanne Mance, who set up the hospital that
became the Hôtel-Dieu and who kept the venture financially alive more than
once.

Piety lost to commerce fairly quickly. The island's position at the meeting of
the St. Lawrence and the Ottawa rivers, just below the point where boats had to
be portaged, made it the natural clearinghouse for the fur trade of half a
continent. Canoe brigades left from Lachine each spring for the Great Lakes and
beyond. Diplomacy followed trade: in 1701 Montreal hosted the Great Peace, a
treaty negotiated over weeks with delegations from dozens of Indigenous
nations, which ended a long period of warfare and let the trade run.

The British took the city in 1760, and the fur money changed hands rather than
disappearing. A largely Scottish merchant class built the Beaver Club, then the
banks, then the railways. By the middle of the nineteenth century Montreal was
the commercial capital of British North America, and a compact district of
mansions on the southern slope of the mountain — the Golden Square Mile —
housed families who controlled a startling proportion of the country's wealth.
McGill University sits in the middle of it, founded on the bequest of a fur
merchant. The Bank of Montreal's domed head office faces the old parade
ground, and Windsor Station, from which the transcontinental railway was run,
is a block away.

Two pieces of engineering fixed the city's shape. The Lachine Canal, opened in
1825, finally bypassed the rapids — and its banks, with cheap water power and
direct shipping, filled with flour mills, foundries, sugar refineries and rail
yards. The working-class districts that grew up around them are still legible:
Griffintown, largely Irish, Pointe-Saint-Charles, Saint-Henri. The novelist
Gabrielle Roy set her portrait of Depression-era poverty in Saint-Henri, with
the trains going past the bottom of the street.

## The Mountain That Is Not One

Mount Royal is not a volcano, though it is often called one. It is the eroded
root of an igneous intrusion — magma that stalled underground, cooled into hard
rock, and was left standing when the softer sedimentary plain around it wore
away. A chain of similar humps runs southeast across the farmland toward
Vermont, each one rising abruptly out of flat country. The Montreal example is
a little over two hundred meters high, which is not much, and utterly dominant,
which is the point.

The city bought the summit in the 1870s after a landowner began logging it, and
hired Frederick Law Olmsted to make a park. Olmsted's plan resisted the urge to
build a grand road to the top; he wanted the ascent to feel like a gradual
passage into a different kind of place, and he got it, more or less. Much of
the rest of the mountain is occupied by two enormous cemeteries lying side by
side, one Catholic and one Protestant, which is as clear a map of nineteenth
century Montreal as any document. A steel cross, lit at night, stands near the
eastern summit, put up in the 1920s in memory of a wooden one Maisonneuve is
said to have carried up in 1643.

The mountain also governs the skyline, because the city has long refused to let
buildings rise above it. And it governs direction. Montrealers use a compass
rotated some forty-five degrees from true: the river is "south," the mountain
is "north," and streets run "east-west" in a way no satellite will confirm.
Everyone uses it, including the transit maps.

## Two Cities on One Street

Saint-Laurent Boulevard runs from the port straight up the island, over the
shoulder of the mountain, and out the other side. Street addresses in Montreal
count outward from it in both directions, east and west. For most of the
nineteenth and twentieth centuries it was also the social frontier: anglophone
money to the west, francophone working-class districts to the east, and in the
middle, on the street itself, everybody who belonged to neither. Jewish
immigrants from Eastern Europe arrived by the tens of thousands and built the
garment trade along its lower blocks, with the delis, bagel bakeries and
Yiddish theatres that went with it. Later came Portuguese, Greek, Italian,
Chinese, Haitian, Vietnamese, Lebanese and South Asian neighborhoods, some
still exactly where they started. Mordecai Richler and Leonard Cohen both came
out of that world and both wrote about it at length.

The larger relationship between the city's two founding languages went through
its decisive change after 1960. The Quiet Revolution replaced the Catholic
Church's control of schools, hospitals and welfare with a secular provincial
state in about a decade; church attendance collapsed so thoroughly that
Montreal now has more deconsecrated churches turned into condominiums,
concert halls and climbing gyms than most cities have churches. Hydroelectric
power was nationalized. A francophone business class was deliberately built.
In 1970 the October Crisis — kidnappings by a small revolutionary cell,
followed by troops in the streets — marked the violent edge of the same
transformation. In 1977 the Charter of the French Language made French the
language of work, commerce and public signage, and required the children of
most newcomers to attend French schools.

Businesses that disliked the direction left, and Toronto, already growing
faster, took over as the country's financial center; a major insurer moving its
head office out in 1978 became the symbol of the shift. Montreal spent two
decades with high vacancy and low rents. It also ended up with something
unusual: a metropolis where a large share of residents move between French and
English without much ceremony, where a shop greeting of "Bonjour, hi" can still
start a political argument, and where cheap space allowed circus companies,
record labels, game studios and a very large student population to take root.

## Stairs, Alleys, and Two Meters of Snow

The residential signature is the plex — two or three flats stacked, each with
its own front door, built in long brick rows between the 1890s and the 1930s —
and the exterior staircase. The stairs curve up from the sidewalk in painted
steel, sometimes in a full spiral, and they exist because bylaws requiring a
setback from the street combined with builders' determination not to give up
interior floor area to a stairwell. They are treacherous in January and the
city banned them in new construction in 1940, by which time the housing stock
was largely built. Behind the rows run the ruelles, narrow service alleys, many
of them now dug up by residents and replanted as shared green lanes.

Winter is not an inconvenience here; it is an operation. Something like two
meters of snow falls in a season, and removing it involves a fleet of sidewalk
tractors, loaders, and truck convoys that haul the snow to chutes and disposal
sites, street by street, with signs and towing and a phone line to find out
where your car went. In January 1998 a freezing rain event coated the region in
several centimeters of ice, folded steel transmission towers like wire, and
left parts of the area without power for weeks in the cold.

Against that, the city treats summer as a public entitlement. Festivals occupy
downtown streets for most of July. On Sunday afternoons a drum circle
assembles around the monument on the eastern slope of the mountain and plays
until dark. Terraces appear on sidewalks that had snowbanks in April.

## The Fair That Rebuilt the City

The most concentrated period of building came in the 1960s. The Metro opened in
1966 on rubber tires, running quietly through tunnels bored well below the
frost line, with each station given to a different architect — so the system
reads as a sequence of rooms rather than a corridor. Expo 67 followed, the
world's fair built on islands in the river, one of them constructed out of the
rock excavated for the Metro. Buckminster Fuller's geodesic dome is still there
on Île Sainte-Hélène, its acrylic skin long since burned away, and Habitat 67,
Moshe Safdie's stack of precast concrete boxes, is still occupied.

Downtown, the same decade produced the beginnings of the underground city, a
network of climate-controlled passages knitting together Metro stations, office
towers, university buildings and shopping concourses. It started with a single
tower complex built as a lid over the railway trench that had split the
downtown in two, and grew by accretion into something like thirty kilometers of
connected interior. In February nobody thinks it is strange.
