# Copenhagen, and the Decisions That Made It

At around eight in the morning on a wet Tuesday in October, the cycle track on
Nørrebrogade is busier than the road beside it. Not metaphorically busier —
physically fuller, three and four abreast, moving at a steady unhurried pace
that never quite stops. Parents pedal cargo bikes with two children and a
thermos strapped into the box in front. Nobody is wearing lycra. A woman in a
long wool coat holds an umbrella in one hand. The rain is the ordinary
horizontal Danish kind and nothing about the scene suggests anyone has made a
decision about it at all.

That is the trick of Copenhagen, and it is worth resisting. The city is
routinely described as though its qualities were meteorological — as though
Danes simply are like this, sensible and communal and fond of daylight. They
are not, particularly. What the city has instead is a long accumulation of
specific choices, some made in the seventeenth century by an autocratic king,
some by nineteenth-century sanitary reformers, some in the 1970s by people
sitting down in the street to block traffic. Most were contested at the time.
Several were reversals of the opposite decision taken a decade earlier.

## Why the Streets Bend

The physical city begins with a shallow harbor on the wrong side of an island.
Copenhagen sits on the eastern shore of Zealand, facing the Øresund — the
narrow strait between Denmark and Sweden, with Malmö visible across the water
on a clear day. The channel is the gateway to the Baltic, and whoever held both
sides of it could tax everything that passed. That is the city's whole reason
for existing: a fishing harbor that became a customs post that became a
capital. Its name preserves the fact plainly. Købmandshavn means merchants'
harbor.

Bishop Absalon fortified the spot in the twelfth century on the small island
now called Slotsholmen, where the parliament and the old stock exchange stand.
Everything medieval grew inward from that point, which is why the streets of
the old center wander — they are cowpaths and property lines, not a plan.
Then, in the early seventeenth century, Christian IV spent the treasury on
building. He is responsible for a startling share of what tourists photograph:
the Round Tower with its spiral ramp wide enough to ride a horse up, the
Renaissance stock exchange with its spire of four twisted dragon tails, the
ochre naval rowhouses of Nyboder, and the entire district of Christianshavn,
laid out on reclaimed mudflats with Dutch engineers and Dutch canals. He also
bankrupted the country in a series of wars it lost. Copenhagen's most beloved
architecture and Denmark's decline as a great power are the same project.

## Fire, Rockets, and Cholera

Three catastrophes finished the medieval city. Fires in 1728 and again in 1795
burned through great swathes of timber housing. In 1807 the Royal Navy, worried
that Napoleon would seize the Danish fleet, bombarded the civilian city with
incendiary rockets for three nights and then sailed away with the ships. What
went back up afterward was the restrained yellow-and-grey neoclassicism you see
now on street after street — flat facades, regular windows, tile roofs — built
to fire regulations, on the old crooked street lines. That combination of
orderly buildings and disorderly geometry gives the center its particular
feeling of intimacy without monotony.

The fourth catastrophe was cholera. Copenhagen was still ringed by earthwork
ramparts, forbidden to build outside them, and by the mid-nineteenth century it
was densely and lethally overcrowded. The epidemic of 1853 killed thousands in
a few months and broke the political resistance to demolishing the walls. What
followed was the fastest growth in the city's history: the bridge quarters,
Nørrebro, Vesterbro and Østerbro, thrown up in a couple of decades as
five-storey perimeter blocks around dark interior yards full of workshops,
privies and sheds.

Two long-lived features came out of that moment. The defensive lakes were kept
rather than filled, so a chain of rectangular water — Sortedams, Peblinge,
Sankt Jørgens — still curves through the middle of the city marking exactly
where the ramparts ran, with a footpath on each side. And the block interiors
were eventually reclaimed. Through the 1980s and 1990s the city ran a slow,
sometimes brutal urban renewal programme that cleared sheds out of the
courtyards and merged them into shared green gårde, one per block, often with
a locked gate and a residents' committee that argues about the grill. A lot of
what people experience as Copenhagen's calm is simply this: a dense city whose
noise is on the street side and whose bedrooms and playgrounds face a communal
garden.

## The Bicycle Was Not Inevitable

In the early 1960s Copenhagen was going the way of every other Western city.
Plans existed to run motorways into the center; the lakes were considered as a
route. Squares were being converted to parking. Cycling numbers, high before
the war, were falling year on year as people bought cars.

The reversal came in pieces. The main shopping street, Strøget, was closed to
traffic in 1962 as an experiment for the Christmas season, over loud objections
from shopkeepers who predicted ruin; it was made permanent when the takings
went up. The architect Jan Gehl began counting, literally counting, how many
people sat on benches and how long they stayed, producing the evidence that
public life responds to design rather than to national character. Then the oil
shocks of the 1970s arrived, along with car-free Sundays and a genuine popular
movement — demonstrations, occupied streets, the deaths of children in traffic
made into a public argument. The city began building cycle tracks again.

The infrastructure is specific and it matters. A Copenhagen cycle track is not
paint on asphalt; it is a separate surface at its own level, curbed down from
the sidewalk and up from the roadway, usually with parked cars on the outside
acting as a shield. Left turns are made in two stages, rolling across to the
far corner and waiting, so nobody has to cross a lane of traffic. Signals on
the main radials are timed in sequence at roughly the speed of an unhurried
rider, so a commuter who holds a steady pace gets a run of greens. In winter,
the tracks are cleared of snow before the roads are. The payoff is that riding
a bicycle in Copenhagen requires no athleticism, no special equipment and no
courage, which is why something close to half of all trips to work and school
in the city are made that way, and why the riders include eight-year-olds and
eighty-year-olds.

## Swimming in the Harbor

The second reversal is easier to see. Within living memory the harbor was an
industrial sewer — shipyards, coal, and combined storm drains that dumped
untreated waste into the water whenever it rained hard. Nobody swam in it. From
the 1990s the city spent heavily and unglamorously on the boring half of the
problem: separating stormwater from sewage, building huge underground overflow
basins to hold the first flush of a downpour, upgrading treatment. By 2002 the
water was clean enough that the first public harbor bath opened at Islands
Brygge, a wooden pontoon with diving boards in the middle of the city. There
are several now, and on a hot July evening the harbor is full of teenagers
jumping off bridges into water that container ships used the same century.

The same instinct — put the pleasant thing on top of the necessary thing —
produced the waste-to-energy plant on Amager with a dry ski slope running down
its roof and a hiking path up the side. It is a real incinerator, burning the
city's household rubbish for district heating. It also has a climbing wall.

## Fingers and Wedges

The region grew to a plan, and the plan has a name and a shape. Drawn up just
after the Second World War, the Fingerplan treated the historic city as the
palm of a hand and directed all new development outward along five radial
corridors, each one following a suburban rail line, with green wedges of
farmland, forest and sports fields left between them. It has been amended
constantly and violated at the edges, but it held well enough that you can
still ride a train twenty minutes out of the center, walk five minutes from the
platform, and be in a beech wood. It also means the suburbs are strung along
transit rather than spread evenly, which is why a city of this size has never
needed an inner motorway.

Tenure matters as much as geography. A large fraction of Danish housing is
either non-profit social housing, allocated by waiting list and governed by
tenant democracy, or andelsbolig — cooperatives where residents buy a share of
the building rather than an apartment, at prices historically held below the
market. Neither model is a utopia, and both have been squeezed hard by rising
land values. But between them they slowed the sorting of the city by income,
and they are the reason a nurse and a lawyer can still live on the same
staircase in Nørrebro.

Copenhagen also tolerates its own exceptions. Christiania, the self-declared
free town founded by squatters in abandoned military barracks in Christianshavn
in 1971, has spent half a century in unresolved negotiation with the Danish
state — sometimes raided, sometimes half-legalized, never quite ended.

## The Dark Half of the Year

None of this makes Copenhagen easy. It is a northern city, far enough up that
midsummer evenings never fully darken and December days give perhaps seven
hours of grey light, with the sun rising late enough that children go to school
before dawn and setting in the middle of the afternoon. The weather is not
dramatic; it is persistent. Wind off the strait, rain that is more of a state
than an event, and a long shoulder season on either side of a short, glorious
summer.

The famous domestic culture follows directly. Candles burned by the crate,
lamps at table height rather than overhead, heavy curtains, long meals. Rye
bread dense enough to build with, topped and eaten with a knife and fork.
Pastry that the Danes call wienerbrød, Vienna bread, because it was introduced
by Austrian bakers brought in to break a strike. And a restaurant culture that,
since a handful of cooks published a manifesto in the mid-2000s committing
themselves to northern ingredients and seasons, has made a virtue of the
region's limits — sea buckthorn, fermented vegetables, things dug up rather
than flown in.

Copenhagen is not a large city. Walking across the old center takes half an
hour, and the whole metropolitan region holds fewer people than a mid-sized
suburb of Shanghai. Its influence comes from being legible: a place small
enough that a visiting mayor can see the whole argument in an afternoon, and
carry home the curb detail that separates a cycle track from a road.
