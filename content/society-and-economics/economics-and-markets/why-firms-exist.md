# Why Firms Exist

Somewhere in a building this morning, a person was told what to do. Not
persuaded, not offered a price, not invited to submit a competing quote — told.
Move to the other project. Take the Tuesday shift. Stop working on that and
start working on this. No money changed hands at the moment of instruction, no
contract was renegotiated, and nobody thought the episode remarkable.

This is strange if you have spent any time with the standard account of how an
economy allocates resources. In that account, everything is directed by prices.
Labour flows towards its highest-value use because wages signal where it is
wanted; materials go where they fetch the most. Yet inside a firm, that
mechanism is switched off. The design team does not bid for the engineer's time
against the sales team. A supervisor simply decides. The economist Dennis
Robertson caught this in a line that Ronald Coase later borrowed to open his
argument: firms are islands of conscious power in an ocean of unconscious
co-operation, like lumps of butter coagulating in a pail of buttermilk.

Coase asked the obvious question that nobody had thought to press. If the price
mechanism is so efficient, why is any of it suspended? And if central direction
works so well inside a firm, why is the whole economy not one enormous firm? The
answer had to explain both why firms exist at all and why they stop growing.

## Using the Market Costs Something

Coase published his answer in 1937, when he was in his mid-twenties, having
worked out the core of it while touring American factories on a travelling
scholarship a few years earlier. The insight was that transacting is not free.
Before you can buy something on a market you must discover that the relevant
market exists and who is in it, find out what the going price is, satisfy
yourself that the counterparty is competent and honest, negotiate terms, write
them down in a form a court would recognise, and then monitor performance and
chase up failures. Each of these consumes time, attention and money. They are the
costs of using the price mechanism, and they had simply been assumed away.

Once you admit them, the firm has a purpose. It is a device for avoiding some of
those costs by replacing a series of separate bargains with a single continuing
relationship governed by authority. An employment contract is remarkably vague
precisely because that is the point: the worker agrees, within limits, to do what
she is directed to do, and both sides are spared the cost of renegotiating every
morning. Herbert Simon later described this as accepting a zone within which
instructions will be followed without further argument, which is a fair account
of what almost everyone signs.

The boundary then falls out of the same logic. Organising an activity internally
also costs something — supervision, meetings, reporting lines, the slow
degradation of information as it travels up a hierarchy, the risk that a manager
will back a favourite project because he wants it rather than because it pays.
As a firm grows, these internal costs rise, and at some point the next activity
is cheaper to buy than to run. That margin is the boundary of the firm, and it is
where the make-or-buy decision actually gets settled. Firms are not the opposite
of markets. They are what happens where markets are expensive.

## The Problem of the Specific Asset

Coase's argument sat admired and largely unused for decades, partly because
transaction costs were hard to observe and therefore hard to test. Oliver
Williamson gave the idea teeth from the 1970s onward by identifying the specific
circumstance in which market contracting breaks down worst.

His key concept is asset specificity. Some investments are worth roughly the same
to anybody: a delivery van, a warehouse near a motorway junction, a standard
lathe. Others are worth a great deal in one particular relationship and very
little outside it. A die cast for one manufacturer's body panels. A chemical
plant built beside a single refinery because piping the feedstock any distance is
uneconomic. An engineer who has spent five years learning one company's legacy
systems. Human capital counts as much as steel.

Where such an investment exists, the parties begin in a competitive market and
end up locked together. Before the plant is built, a dozen firms might bid for
the contract. Afterwards there is one buyer and one seller, and the sunk value
sitting between them is available to be fought over. Either side can threaten to
walk away in order to extract better terms, knowing the other has far more to
lose. Williamson called this the hold-up problem, and the anticipation of it is
what does the damage: fearing it, a rational supplier under-invests in exactly
the specialised capacity that would have made the relationship valuable.

Long-term contracts help but cannot solve it, because contracts are necessarily
incomplete. No document can specify what should happen in every state of the
world, and the states that matter most — a technology shift, a demand collapse,
a regulatory change — are the ones nobody foresaw. Vertical integration is the
blunt remedy. Buy the supplier, and the dispute becomes an internal argument
settled by fiat rather than a contractual standoff settled by lawyers. The
standard illustration is the American car industry's absorption of its body
suppliers in the 1920s, though economic historians have argued vigorously about
how well that particular case fits the theory. Williamson shared the Nobel prize
in 2009.

## The Team and the Monitor

A different explanation arrived from Armen Alchian and Harold Demsetz in the
early 1970s, and it locates the firm's purpose in measurement rather than in
bargaining.

Consider four men lifting a heavy crate onto a truck. The output is the crate
being loaded. There is no way to determine, from the output alone, how much of
the effort came from each man. Production is a team effort in the strict sense
that individual contributions cannot be separated out of the joint product. Under
those conditions, each person has a quiet incentive to lean rather than lift,
since the cost of slacking is spread across everyone while the benefit is
private.

The solution is to hire somebody to watch. But then who watches the monitor, who
has the same incentive to shirk as everyone else? Alchian and Demsetz answered by
giving the monitor the residual: the money left over after all the contracted
payments have been made. Now his own income depends directly on how well the team
performs, and his incentive to monitor is self-enforcing. Grant him the right to
hire, fire, revise terms and sell the whole position to somebody else, and you
have described the classical owner-manager of a firm. On this view authority is
almost beside the point. The employer cannot order an employee to do anything a
grocer could not order of a customer; he can only stop dealing with her. What
distinguishes the firm is the concentration of contracts through a single central
party who bears the consequences.

## Who Owns the Machine

The third major answer took the incompleteness of contracts as its starting point
rather than as a complication. Sanford Grossman, Oliver Hart and John Moore asked
what ownership actually confers when a contract cannot cover every eventuality.
Their answer: residual control rights. To own an asset is to hold the right to
decide how it is used in all the situations the contract failed to mention.

That is a modest-sounding definition with sharp consequences. If contracts were
complete, ownership would be irrelevant, since every decision would already be
written down. Because they are not, ownership determines who has the stronger
hand when the unforeseen arrives, and therefore who can capture more of the
surplus when terms are renegotiated. Anticipating that, each party invests more
when they expect to hold the bargaining power afterwards, and less when they do
not. The efficient assignment of ownership is the one that puts the asset in the
hands of whoever's investment matters most to the value created.

This framework does something the earlier theories could not: it explains why
integration has costs, not merely benefits. Buying the supplier strengthens the
buyer's incentives and weakens the supplier's, because the supplier's former
owner is now an employee who no longer controls the assets he once invested
himself into. Integration is a transfer of incentive, not a free addition of
control, which is a reasonable summary of why so many acquisitions disappoint
everyone involved. Hart shared the Nobel prize in 2016 with Bengt Holmström,
whose own work on incentives established, among other things, that paying hard
for a measurable task reliably pulls effort away from the unmeasured tasks
sitting beside it — a result that explains a great deal about schools, hospitals
and sales floors.

## The Boundary Keeps Moving

These explanations are complements rather than rivals, and the practical value of
all of them shows in how firm boundaries have shifted. The vertically integrated
giant of the mid-twentieth century, owning its ore, its steel, its glass and its
dealerships, made sense when coordinating across firms was slow and expensive.
Cheap communication, containerised shipping, standardised interfaces and better
contracting law have all lowered the cost of dealing at arm's length, and firms
have accordingly shed functions they once owned: manufacturing, logistics,
payroll, data centres.

Yet the same decades produced organisations of extraordinary scale in businesses
where the critical assets are data, reputation and network position — assets that
are specific in Williamson's sense and nearly impossible to contract over
cleanly. And the platform, which contracts with drivers or sellers as
independents while directing their work through software with a specificity no
foreman ever managed, sits awkwardly across the whole framework. Courts and
legislatures keep being asked to decide whether a given arrangement is a firm or
a market, and they keep finding that the question was never as binary as the
textbooks made it look.
