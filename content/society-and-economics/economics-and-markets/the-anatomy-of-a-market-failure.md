# The Anatomy of a Market Failure

A delivery truck idles outside a primary school for twenty minutes while the
driver unloads crates. He pays for the diesel he burns. He does not pay for the
fine particulate drifting over the playground fence, or for the fraction of an
asthma case it will eventually contribute to somewhere in the neighbourhood. No
contract covers that transfer. No invoice records it. The bread arrives at a
price that is, from the point of view of everyone who agreed to the trade,
perfectly accurate — and from the point of view of the town hosting it, too low.

That gap is the whole subject. The case for markets rests on a genuinely
powerful argument: prices carry information about scarcity and desire that no
central office could assemble, and they carry it continuously, cheaply, and
without requiring anyone to understand the system as a whole. A rise in the
price of copper tells a Chilean mine to dig harder and a cable manufacturer to
economise, and neither party needs to know why the other is behaving as it does.
But the argument has a load-bearing assumption tucked inside it. It assumes that
everyone whose welfare is affected by a transaction is a party to that
transaction. Where that fails, the price stops summarising the cost of an
activity and begins summarising only the portion of the cost that happens to
fall on the people doing the trading. The market then works beautifully, and
produces the wrong quantity of things.

## The Wedge Between Private and Social Cost

Arthur Pigou, writing at Cambridge in the years after the First World War, gave
the phenomenon its durable vocabulary. His examples were homely: sparks from a
railway locomotive setting fire to crops along the line, smoke from a factory
chimney blackening laundry and lungs in the streets below. In each case a
producer makes his decision by comparing his own marginal cost against his own
marginal revenue, and gets the answer right for himself. The cost landing on the
farmer or the laundress never enters his arithmetic, so he runs more trains, or
burns more coal, than the sum of everyone's interests would justify.

The logic runs equally in the other direction, and this half is routinely
forgotten. A landowner who drains a marsh reduces flood risk for people
downstream who pay her nothing. A firm that trains an apprentice creates a
skilled worker whose next employer captures much of the value. Someone who gets
vaccinated protects not only herself but everyone she would otherwise have
infected. Positive externalities are supplied in quantities that are too small
for exactly the structural reason that negative ones are supplied in quantities
that are too large: the person deciding captures only a slice of what she
creates.

Notice what is not being claimed here. Pollution is not inefficient because it
is unpleasant; it is inefficient because it is unpriced. An economy producing no
pollution whatsoever would be one producing almost nothing, since nearly every
useful act imposes some cost on somebody. The efficient level of a harm is the
level at which one more unit of abatement would cost more than the damage it
prevents, which is almost never zero and is also almost never whatever an
unregulated market happens to deliver. This is an unsentimental way to frame the
question and it makes people uneasy, but it is what allows the question to be
answered rather than merely shouted about.

## Pigou's Remedy and Coase's Objection

Pigou's fix was direct. Tax the activity at a rate equal to the damage it does
at the margin, and the producer's private arithmetic silently becomes the social
arithmetic. He does not need to care about the laundress. He needs only to
respond to a cost, which he was always good at. Subsidies do the mirror-image
job for beneficial spillovers. The elegance of the scheme is that the regulator
need not know how much abatement any particular firm ought to do, or by what
method — only the size of the damage. Each firm then finds its own cheapest
response, which might be a scrubber, a change of fuel, a different production
schedule, or shutting down.

Ronald Coase attacked the framing in 1960, in an essay that has been misread
ever since. His point was not that taxes are unnecessary. It was that
externalities are reciprocal. The railway damages the farmer only because the
farmer grows wheat beside the line; the harm is jointly produced, and asking who
caused it is less useful than asking which arrangement of activities is worth
the most. From this he drew the famous result: where property rights are clearly
assigned and bargaining is costless, the parties will negotiate their way to the
efficient outcome regardless of who initially holds the right. The farmer pays
the railway to fit spark arresters, or the railway pays the farmer to plant
further back, and the same acreage ends up burning either way.

The real content of that theorem sits in its premise. Bargaining is not costless.
When the affected parties number in the millions, are not yet born, or cannot
identify which of ten thousand emitters harmed them, the cost of assembling the
negotiation swamps any gain it would produce. Coase understood this perfectly
well; his essay is mostly an argument that the choice lies between imperfect
institutional arrangements rather than between a flawed market and a flawless
government. But the corollary is genuinely useful where it applies. When a
handful of identifiable parties are involved, assigning a clear right and
letting them sort it out often beats writing a rule, because those parties know
things about costs that no agency will ever learn.

## The Other Failures

Externalities absorb most of the attention, but they are one item on a list, and
the others share a family resemblance: in each case the price loses its grip on
the underlying reality.

Market power is the simplest. A seller facing no close competitor maximises
profit by restricting output and holding price above marginal cost, which means
trades both parties would have valued never happen. Natural monopoly is the
harder version — water mains, transmission grids, railway track — where average
costs fall across the entire range of demand, so a single supplier really is
cheapest, and the regulator is left policing a monopolist it cannot afford to
break up.

Information asymmetry corrodes markets from a different angle. George Akerlof's
used-car argument showed that if sellers know the quality of their goods and
buyers do not, buyers will offer only an average price, the better sellers will
withdraw, the average will fall, and the market can unravel down to nothing.
Insurance faces the mirror problem: the people keenest to buy flood cover are
those who already know their cellar fills every spring. A great deal of the
institutional furniture of commerce — warranties, brand names, professional
licensing, credit ratings, medical underwriting, the whole apparatus of
independent audit — exists to patch these holes, and every patch carries costs
of its own.

Then there are the markets that simply do not exist. You cannot insure against
being born with an expensive inherited condition, because by the time you are
able to sign anything the relevant information is already known. You cannot
easily sell a claim on your own future earnings to fund an education, because
the contract is close to unenforceable and uncomfortably resembles indenture.
Where a market cannot come into being, it cannot be efficient.

## What Has Actually Worked

The theory is a century old; the record of applying it is shorter and more
instructive. Tradable permit systems for sulphur dioxide, introduced in the
United States after the 1990 amendments to the Clean Air Act, are the standard
success story. Emissions fell as required, and compliance turned out to cost far
less than either industry or the regulators had forecast, largely because the
scheme let firms discover cheap abatement options — switching to low-sulphur
coal from newly accessible mines, for one — that no rule-writer had thought to
mandate. The Montreal Protocol on ozone-depleting substances worked for somewhat
different reasons: a short list of chemicals, a short list of producers, and
substitutes that turned out to exist.

Congestion pricing is the clearest everyday case. Road space at rush hour is
scarce and priced at zero, so it gets rationed by queueing, which wastes the one
commodity nobody can manufacture. Singapore began charging for entry to its
central area in the mid-1970s and does it electronically now; London followed
early in this century; Stockholm ran a trial and then held a referendum on
whether to keep the charge. The Stockholm sequence is the interesting one,
because public support was low before the trial and higher after people had
spent months driving in the resulting traffic. Road charges tend to be unpopular
in prospect and considerably less unpopular in retrospect.

Carbon pricing applies the same instrument to a harm that is global, cumulative
and slow, which is why it has proved so much harder. The damage done by a tonne
of carbon dioxide depends on assumptions about events decades away and on how
much weight one gives to the welfare of people not yet born — a question about
discount rates that is partly ethical and cannot be settled by measurement.
Careful economists produce estimates of the social cost of carbon that differ by
an order of magnitude, not because any of them is sloppy, but because the number
is a compound of a physical estimate and a moral judgement.

## The Standard of Comparison

The move that wrecks most arguments about market failure is comparing a real
market against an idealised government. Regulators work with incomplete
information, respond to political incentives, and are lobbied hardest by exactly
the firms they supervise. A pollution tax set at the wrong level misallocates
resources as surely as no tax at all, and a badly designed permit scheme can
hand windfalls to incumbents while achieving very little. Demonstrating a market
failure establishes that some intervention could improve matters. It does not
establish that any particular intervention will.

What it does establish is that the question is empirical rather than doctrinal.
Once you accept that prices can be wrong in specific, describable ways, "leave
it to the market" and "the state should handle it" both stop functioning as
answers and become hypotheses about which imperfect institution handles a
particular defect more cheaply. The truck idling outside the school is not an
argument against deliveries. It is evidence that somewhere in the chain between
the depot and the loaf, a real cost went unrecorded, and that a well-built
institution would put it back on the books.
