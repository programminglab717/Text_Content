# The Invention of the Central Bank

The Bank of England was not founded to manage an economy. It was founded in
1694 because the English Crown had lost a naval engagement to the French and
needed a fleet it could not pay for. A syndicate of merchants agreed to lend
the government a large sum at eight per cent, and in exchange received a
charter to operate as a joint-stock bank and to issue notes against the debt
they held. The loan was never repaid. It was refinanced, extended, and
eventually became the permanent national debt, and the institution created to
hold it became, over the following two centuries and largely by accident, the
model for every central bank in the world.

Nothing about that origin suggests the job as it is now understood. The
functions accumulated one crisis at a time, each added because the previous
arrangement had failed in some specific and expensive way.

## Learning to Be the Lender of Last Resort

The first thing a bank of that size discovered was that other banks kept their
reserves with it. Country banks in England found it convenient to hold
balances in London, and London banks found it convenient to hold them at the
Bank. This was a commercial arrangement, not a policy one. But it meant that
when a panic arrived, everyone's claim on cash converged on a single building.

Panics arrived regularly. The pattern was always the same: a speculative
episode, a failure, a scramble by depositors to convert deposits into cash, and
then the discovery that a banking system by design does not hold enough cash to
satisfy everyone at once. Perfectly solvent banks with good loan books failed
because they could not sell those loans quickly enough. The failures propagated,
because each bank's assets were other people's promises.

Walter Bagehot, editing *The Economist* in the mid-nineteenth century, watched
several of these episodes and wrote down the rule that came out of them. His
1873 book *Lombard Street* argued that in a panic the Bank should lend freely,
at a high rate of interest, against any collateral that would be considered
good in normal times. Each clause does work. Lend freely, because half-measures
confirm the fear. At a penalty rate, so that nobody uses the facility except in
genuine distress and everyone repays it quickly. Against good collateral, so
that the lender is rescuing the illiquid rather than subsidising the insolvent.
The Bank's directors at the time disliked being told they had public
obligations. Every central banker since has operated some version of Bagehot's
rule, usually while insisting on its limits in advance and abandoning most of
them in the event.

The United States resisted the whole idea longer than anyone. Two attempts at
a national bank were killed by political hostility to concentrated financial
power, and the country spent the second half of the nineteenth century with
thousands of independent note-issuing banks and a recurring cycle of autumn
panics tied to the agricultural payments calendar. The crisis of 1907 was
resolved by J. P. Morgan personally convening bankers in his library and
strong-arming them into a rescue fund, which impressed everyone at the time and
alarmed them afterwards. The realisation that the financial stability of a
continental economy depended on the health and temper of one private banker
produced, after six years of argument, the Federal Reserve Act of 1913.

The role kept widening. Bagehot had been writing about banks, and for a
century the rule was applied to banks. By 2008 a large share of short-term
credit ran through institutions that took no deposits and held no central bank
account — investment banks, money market funds, the commercial paper market,
the securities dealers who financed themselves overnight against collateral.
When that funding froze, the authorities improvised facilities to lend directly
into each of those markets, and the Federal Reserve extended swap lines to
foreign central banks so that they could supply dollars to their own banks,
which had borrowed heavily in a currency no one but the Fed could create. The
rule survived. Its perimeter turned out to be wherever the runs actually were,
which is not a boundary anyone can draw in advance.

## The Constraint That Used to Be Gold

For most of this history the central bank operated under an external discipline
it did not choose. Under the classical gold standard of the decades before the
First World War, a note was a claim on a fixed weight of metal, and a country
losing gold had to raise interest rates until the outflow stopped, regardless of
what that did to employment at home. The system delivered remarkable price
stability across generations and remarkably brutal adjustment within them, and
the interwar attempts to rebuild it collapsed under exactly that tension.

Bretton Woods, negotiated in 1944, kept a diluted version: other currencies
were pegged to the dollar, and the dollar alone was convertible into gold at a
fixed price. It held until the volume of outstanding dollars grew far beyond the
American gold stock and the arithmetic became obvious to everyone holding them.
The convertibility was suspended in 1971 and never restored. From that point
the value of money rested on nothing but the conduct of the institution issuing
it, which is why the decades since have been spent constructing substitutes for
the discipline gold used to impose — targets, mandates, published forecasts,
and the careful cultivation of a reputation that can be lost.

## The Balance Sheet Is the Instrument

Strip away the vocabulary and a central bank is a bank with one unusual
privilege: its liabilities are the economy's settlement asset. Commercial banks
hold accounts there, called reserves, and payments between banks are finally
settled by moving reserves from one account to another. The central bank can
create those reserves at will, by buying something and paying for it with a
credit to the seller's bank.

That is the whole mechanism, and everything else is a variation on it. To push
short-term interest rates down, the central bank buys government securities,
which adds reserves to the system and makes overnight funds more plentiful and
therefore cheaper. To push rates up, it sells, or simply announces a higher rate
on the reserves banks already hold. In the older corridor arrangement, reserves
were kept genuinely scarce and the policy rate was hit by fine daily adjustments
of their quantity. In the floor systems most large central banks now run,
reserves are abundant and the rate is set administratively, by paying interest
on reserves at the desired level — no bank will lend at less than it can earn
risk-free from the central bank, so that rate becomes the floor for everything
else.

Quantitative easing extends the same logic to longer maturities. Once the
overnight rate has been cut to approximately zero there is nothing left to cut,
so the bank buys long-dated bonds instead, paying for them with newly created
reserves. It is best understood as swapping one government liability for
another — a ten-year bond for an overnight deposit — and thereby taking
duration risk out of private portfolios and pushing investors toward riskier
assets. Whether it accomplishes much beyond signalling the bank's intentions
remains genuinely disputed.

## How a Rate Change Reaches a Factory

The overnight rate at which banks lend each other reserves is, by itself, of
interest to almost no one. Its power comes from what it implies. A long-term
interest rate is roughly an average of expected future short-term rates plus a
premium, so a credible signal about the path of policy moves mortgage rates,
corporate bond yields and the discount rate applied to every future cash flow
in the economy. This is why the words surrounding a decision usually matter
more than the decision, and why central banks now spend enormous effort on
forward guidance about the direction of travel.

From there the effects fan out along several channels at once. Higher
borrowing costs delay business investment and the purchase of houses and cars.
Falling asset prices reduce household wealth and the collateral against which
firms borrow, which tightens credit beyond the direct price effect. A higher
policy rate attracts foreign capital, lifts the exchange rate, and makes
imports cheaper and exports dearer. Banks themselves become more selective
about whom they lend to.

All of this happens slowly. The conventional estimate is that monetary policy
affects output after something like a year and prices after longer still, which
means a central bank is always steering by a forecast rather than by what it can
see. The consequences of tightening arrive after the situation that prompted it
has changed. This lag, more than any disagreement about theory, is what makes
the job difficult, and it is why central bankers talk constantly about risks
rather than predictions.

## Independence and Its Price

The argument for insulating all this from elected politicians is narrower than
it is usually made to sound. It rests on a timing problem. A government facing
an election has a strong incentive to stimulate now and accept higher inflation
later; if the public anticipates that incentive, it builds the expected
inflation into wages and prices in advance, and the government ends up with the
inflation and none of the stimulus. Delegating the decision to an institution
with a statutory mandate, long terms of office and no electoral horizon is a way
of making a promise that cannot be conveniently broken.

The bargain that made this politically acceptable was a narrow mandate and
public accountability. New Zealand pioneered the explicit numerical inflation
target at the end of the 1980s; most of the advanced world had adopted some
version within a decade, typically around two per cent, with published
forecasts, minuted votes and appearances before legislatures. The Federal
Reserve carries a dual mandate covering employment as well as prices, which
gives it more latitude and more exposure to criticism.

The arrangement is not stable in all conditions. When a government's debt is
large and its deficits persistent, a central bank that raises rates increases
the state's interest bill directly, and the political pressure not to becomes
severe. Economists call the resulting condition fiscal dominance, and it is the
state in which the central bank's independence exists on paper and not in fact.
The scale of government bond buying in the 2010s and early 2020s made this less
theoretical than it had been for decades, since a central bank holding a large
share of its own government's debt is no longer obviously a neutral referee.

Nor does independence expand the range of what the instrument can do. A
central bank cannot raise productivity, build houses, or make oil cheaper. It
can change the price and availability of credit, and through that the level of
demand, and it can stand behind the payment system when confidence fails. Ask
it to solve a supply problem and it will deliver a recession, because a
recession is what it has. That limitation was well understood in the 1970s,
partly forgotten in the long calm that followed, and relearned again the last
time supply chains broke.
