# How a Price Finds Its Level

In the auction halls at Aalsmeer, outside Amsterdam, cut flowers travel through
the building on trolleys while buyers sit in banked rows watching a clock. The
hand starts high and falls. The first buyer to press a button stops it, takes
the lot at whatever number the hand had reached, and the trolley rolls on. A
bucket of tulips that fetched one figure at six in the morning may go for a
fraction of it by eight, because the roses that flew in from Kenya overnight
came in heavier than anyone expected and the German wholesalers have already
filled their vans. Nothing about the flowers changed. What changed was the
relationship between how many there were and how badly anyone still wanted them.

The descending clock does quickly and visibly what most markets do slowly and
out of sight. It is worth being precise about what is actually happening,
because the two-curve diagram that gets drawn on a thousand whiteboards is
frequently misread as a statement about what things are worth. It is not. It is
a statement about information and about margins.

## Two Schedules, Not Two Numbers

Demand is not a quantity. It is a list of conditional answers: at this price,
buyers would collectively take this much; at a lower price, more. Line up every
potential buyer of a good in order of what they would be willing to pay, from
the person who wants it most to the person who is nearly indifferent, and you
have traced the demand schedule. Supply is the mirror image, with sellers
ordered by the cost of producing one more unit — the grower with the efficient
greenhouse and cheap gas at one end, the grower who would have to run extra
lighting at the other.

The crossing point is the only price at which no one is left frustrated. Above
it, sellers who would happily produce at that price cannot find takers. Below
it, buyers who would happily pay cannot find goods. At the crossing, the last
buyer to get a unit values it at almost exactly what it cost the last seller to
make it, and every buyer and seller behind them in the queue does better than
break even. Those accumulated differences are what economists call consumer and
producer surplus, and they are the whole case for letting the exchange happen.

The critical word is *last*. The clearing price reflects the valuation of the
marginal buyer, not the average one. Water is priced low not because people
place little value on water but because the final litre consumed in a
well-supplied city goes on a lawn. This is the resolution of the old paradox
about diamonds and water, and it is also why a market price tells you almost
nothing about how much a good matters in aggregate.

## Movement Along, and Shifts

The most common error in reading the diagram is treating a price change as a
cause of a demand change. It is not. If coffee gets more expensive and people
buy less of it, demand has not fallen; the schedule is exactly where it was, and
buyers have merely slid down it. Demand itself moves only when something
outside the price changes — incomes rise, a substitute gets cheaper, a
complement gets scarcer, a health story circulates, the weather turns. Supply
shifts when input costs change, when a technology arrives, when a frost takes
out a growing region, when sellers enter or leave.

Keeping this straight matters because the two cases predict opposite things.
A rise in price accompanied by a rise in quantity traded means demand shifted
outward. A rise in price accompanied by a fall in quantity means supply
contracted. Look only at the price and the two are indistinguishable, which is
why arguments about whether a shortage is caused by greed or by scarcity can be
settled by looking at volumes.

## Elasticity Decides Who Pays

The slope of each schedule carries most of the practical content. Elasticity is
just the responsiveness of quantity to price, expressed as a ratio of
percentage changes, and it varies enormously by good and by horizon. Insulin,
salt, a funeral: the quantity barely moves whatever the price. Restaurant
meals, air travel, a particular brand of anything with close substitutes: the
quantity moves a great deal.

Time is the underappreciated variable. When fuel prices spike, drivers can do
almost nothing in the first week except keep driving, so short-run demand looks
rigid. Over several years the same price increase reshapes car purchases,
commutes, housing choices and freight logistics, and the long-run response is
far larger. Energy analysts who forecast using short-run elasticities have been
embarrassed repeatedly for exactly this reason.

Elasticity also settles the arithmetic of revenue, which is why the answer to
"should we raise the price?" is never a matter of taste. If demand is
inelastic, a ten per cent increase costs the seller less than ten per cent of
volume and total takings rise. If it is elastic, the same increase empties the
shop. Airlines and hotels invest heavily in guessing which regime they are in
for each seat and each night, which is the entire business of yield management:
the same physical product sold at a dozen prices to buyers sorted by how
flexible their travel dates are.

Elasticity also determines who bears a tax, regardless of who hands over the
money. If a tax is levied on sellers of a good whose buyers have nowhere else
to go, sellers raise the price by nearly the full amount and buyers pay it. If
buyers can easily substitute away, sellers must absorb most of it. The legal
assignment of the tax is close to irrelevant; the relative stubbornness of the
two sides is what settles the incidence. The same logic explains why a tariff
does not necessarily fall on the exporting country and why payroll taxes
nominally split between employer and employee tend, over time, to land on
wages.

## Nobody Is Actually Running the Auction

Here the textbook model quietly departs from most of economic life. The neat
account of a price converging on equilibrium was formalised by Léon Walras
using an imaginary auctioneer who calls out prices, collects bids, and permits
no trade until the books balance. There is no such person. Aalsmeer has a
clock; a fish market has a shouting crowd; a stock exchange has a central limit
order book where bids and offers meet continuously. Almost everything else has
posted prices set by a firm that guesses, watches, and adjusts.

Firms adjust slowly, and for good reasons. Changing a price has direct costs —
reprinting, reprogramming, renegotiating contracts — and indirect ones, since
customers read frequent increases as opportunism and read decreases as an
invitation to wait for the next one. So the first response to a demand surge is
usually not a price rise but a fall in inventory, then a lengthening lead time,
then overtime, and only then a repricing. Inventories and order backlogs are
the shock absorbers that let quantities adjust while prices sit still. When
shipping containers became scarce, spot freight rates moved within weeks while
the retail price of the goods inside them took the better part of a year to
respond.

Where continuous trading does exist, someone is usually paid to stand in for
the missing auctioneer. A market maker quotes a price at which they will buy
and a slightly higher price at which they will sell, absorbing the mismatch
between arriving buyers and arriving sellers into their own inventory. The gap
between the two quotes is their compensation for that risk, and it widens
exactly when the market is least certain what the right price is. A thin
market has a wide spread not because dealers are greedy but because they are
guessing.

This stickiness is not a flaw in the model so much as the reason the model's
long-run predictions can be right while its short-run ones are useless. Prices
do carry the information the theory says they carry. They just carry it late.

## What Happens When Clearing Is Blocked

Hold a price below its clearing level and the shortfall has to be settled some
other way. Rent control produces queues, key money, discrimination in tenant
selection, and a slow deterioration of the housing stock, because the landlord's
incentive to maintain an apartment falls with the rent they are allowed to
charge. Fuel price caps produce petrol lines, which convert a money price into
a time price that is paid by everyone and collected by no one. Ticket price
caps for popular events produce resale markets. In each case the good still
goes to whoever is willing to give up the most, but the currency changes from
money to waiting, connections, or willingness to break a rule.

Price floors do the reverse: a minimum above the clearing level creates a
surplus that someone must store, destroy, export, or pay people not to produce.
European agricultural policy spent decades demonstrating this with butter and
wine.

Markets can also fail to settle for reasons internal to their own timing.
Hog farmers, seeing high prices, expand their herds; the animals take months to
raise; by the time they reach market the extra supply has crushed the price;
farmers cut back; scarcity returns. The oscillation is self-generated and can
persist for years. The same cobweb appears in petroleum drilling, in commercial
property, in the supply of newly trained engineers and physicians, and anywhere
else that the production decision must be made long before the price that will
reward it is known.

None of which makes the crossing-point diagram wrong. It makes it a statement
about tendency: prices move in the direction that closes the gap between what
people want and what exists, and they carry, compressed into a single number,
a quantity of dispersed information about scarcity and desire that no
administrator could assemble. That is a claim about coordination. It is not a
claim that the resulting distribution is just, or that the marginal buyer is the
person who needed the thing most, and the argument gets muddled whenever those
two things are run together.
