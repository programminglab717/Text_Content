# The Economics of Public Goods

On a bad night off the Cornish coast, a light on a headland is worth a great
deal to the master of a collier running for shelter. It is worth exactly as much
to the second ship behind him, and the third, and it costs the keeper nothing
extra to serve them. He could not keep the beam off any particular hull if he
wanted to. A lighthouse is therefore a peculiar sort of commodity: consuming it
does not use it up, and refusing it to non-payers is impossible. Those two
awkward properties turn out to define an entire class of goods that markets
handle badly and that societies have nevertheless been supplying, one way or
another, for as long as there have been societies.

Paul Samuelson formalised the idea in the mid-1950s in a paper of barely three
pages, which is one reason it is so often quoted and so rarely read carefully.
His definition rested on non-rivalry: one person's consumption of the good does
not diminish what is available to anyone else. Non-excludability — the practical
impossibility of keeping out those who have not paid — came to be treated as the
second criterion, though it is really a statement about technology and law
rather than about the good itself. Encryption made broadcast television
excludable. A fence makes a park excludable. Neither change alters whether the
good is rival.

## Four Boxes, Not Two

Crossing the two properties gives a grid that is far more useful than the
familiar public-versus-private dichotomy. Rival and excludable goods are
ordinary private goods: a sandwich, a haircut, a pair of boots. Non-rival and
excludable goods are club goods, a category James Buchanan set out in the
1960s — a swimming pool, a satellite channel, a toll bridge in light traffic.
Once the thing is built, an extra member costs nothing much to serve, but
membership can be sold, and so private provision works tolerably well.

The interesting box is the fourth: rival but not excludable. These are
common-pool resources, and they behave nothing like public goods despite being
constantly confused with them. A fishery, an aquifer, a grazing common, an
atmosphere with a finite capacity to absorb carbon — each is genuinely depleted
by use, and each is hard to fence. The failure mode is over-consumption rather
than under-provision. Garrett Hardin's 1968 essay gave this the name that stuck,
though his herdsmen were not really sharing a commons in the historical sense;
they were sharing an open-access resource with no rules at all, which is a
different and rarer thing.

Most real goods sit somewhere in the middle of the grid rather than in a corner,
and they move around it. A road is non-rival at three in the morning and fiercely
rival at eight. Knowledge is the purest non-rival good there is — a theorem used
by one mathematician is not thereby unavailable to another — but patents and
trade secrets make particular applications of it excludable by deliberate
design, precisely because the alternative would leave nobody with a reason to
fund the research.

## The Free Rider and the Samuelson Condition

The reason markets struggle is straightforward once stated. If I cannot be
excluded from the benefit, my rational contribution towards the cost is zero,
since the flood barrier either gets built without my money or does not get built
with it. Everyone reasons the same way, and a barrier that everyone wanted goes
unbuilt. The perversity is that nobody has behaved irrationally and nobody has
lied; the incentives simply do not aggregate into the outcome that all parties
prefer.

Samuelson's contribution was to state the efficiency condition precisely. For a
private good, efficiency requires that each consumer's valuation at the margin
equals the cost of producing one more unit. For a non-rival good, everyone
consumes the same quantity, so the correct test is whether the *sum* of what
everybody would pay for one more unit exceeds the cost of providing it. The
change from equality-per-person to a summed valuation sounds like a technicality.
It is the difference between a good the market can price and a good the market
cannot see, because no individual's willingness to pay carries the information
the decision requires.

Erik Lindahl had already sketched a solution in the 1920s: charge each person a
personalised price equal to their own marginal benefit, and voluntary
contributions would exactly fund the efficient quantity. The scheme is coherent
and entirely unusable, because it requires people to announce truthfully how
much they value something when understating it lowers their bill. Later work on
demand-revealing mechanisms, building on William Vickrey's insights about
auctions and developed further by Edward Clarke and Theodore Groves, found
clever payment rules under which honesty becomes the best strategy. These
schemes are real and occasionally used, but they tend to be complex, to leave
money on the table, and to strike ordinary people as a swindle when explained.
Taxation, crude as it is, survives partly because it does not require anyone to
be honest about anything.

## The Lighthouse, Reconsidered

The lighthouse is the standard classroom example, and it has a history worth
knowing because of how thoroughly it has been fought over. John Stuart Mill used
it. Henry Sidgwick used it. Samuelson used it. Then in 1974 Coase published a
short historical paper arguing that the example was largely wrong as a matter of
fact: lighthouses around the English coast had for centuries been built and run
by private individuals, who recovered their costs through dues levied on ships
at the ports they served.

The finding was a genuine embarrassment to a lazy textbook habit, and it is
often over-read. Those private builders operated under patents granted by the
Crown, and the dues were collected with the full force of law by harbour
officials; Trinity House, a chartered corporation, held authority over the
system and eventually absorbed it. What the episode really demonstrates is
subtler and more interesting than either side's slogan: the beam itself remained
non-excludable, but a ship wanting to unload cargo *was* excludable, and the
port made a convenient tollbooth. Solving a public-goods problem usually means
finding some rival, excludable good that travels alongside the non-rival one and
charging for that instead. Commercial radio sells listeners to advertisers.
Open-source software companies sell support contracts. The trick is old and it
is everywhere, and it only works when a suitable companion good happens to exist.

## Ostrom's Correction

The dominant assumption through the middle of the twentieth century was that
unpriced shared resources faced a binary fate: state ownership or privatisation,
Leviathan or enclosure. Elinor Ostrom spent decades demonstrating that this was
an artefact of studying models rather than places. Working through field studies
of Swiss alpine pastures, Japanese village forests, Spanish irrigation
associations, and inshore fisheries in several countries, she catalogued
communities that had governed common-pool resources sustainably for generations
without either a state monopoly or private title.

What she extracted was not a formula but a set of recurring features in the
arrangements that lasted. Boundaries — of both the resource and the group
entitled to use it — were clearly defined. Rules about extraction fitted local
conditions rather than being imported. The people bound by the rules had a hand
in writing them. Monitoring was done by people accountable to the users, often
by the users themselves. Sanctions started small and escalated for repeat
offenders rather than beginning with expulsion. Cheap local forums existed for
settling disputes. And the external authorities, crucially, refrained from
overriding the local arrangement.

Ostrom shared the Nobel prize in 2009, and her work is sometimes enlisted as
evidence that formal governance is unnecessary. It shows nothing of the sort.
The successful cases she studied were thick with governance; it was simply
polycentric, locally authored, and largely invisible to anyone counting
government agencies. The systems that failed were typically those where a
distant authority had dissolved the local institution and replaced it with a
permit regime nobody believed in.

## Where the Category Gets Slippery

Calling something a public good is a claim about its technical properties, not a
verdict on whether the state should provide it, and the two get conflated
constantly. Basic research, national defence, mosquito eradication, a stable
currency, herd immunity and the rule of law are close to textbook public goods.
Schools and hospitals are not: a classroom seat is rival and a patient can be
turned away. The case for public provision of those rests on quite different
arguments — about spillovers, insurance failures, capital-market imperfections
and plain distributive justice — and pretending otherwise weakens it.

The category also strains at the largest scale. A stable climate is non-rival and
non-excludable for the entire planet, but there is no global tax authority, and
any single country that abates pays the full cost while capturing a small slice
of the benefit. That is the free-rider problem with the enforcement mechanism
removed, which is why international environmental agreements lean so heavily on
reciprocity, monitoring, reputation, and trade measures — the same tools Ostrom
found her villagers using, scaled up beyond anything her villagers would have
recognised, and correspondingly more fragile.

Meanwhile a genuinely new class of cases has arrived. Digital goods are non-rival
in the strongest sense ever seen, since copying is essentially free, but they are
excludable by technical means that did not exist a generation ago. A body of
software, a map, an encyclopaedia, a genome database: each could be provided to
everyone at no additional cost, and each is capable of being locked down. The
decision about which to do is not forced by the nature of the good. It is a
choice, made through law and licensing, and it is made freshly every time.
