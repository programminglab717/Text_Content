# Comparative Advantage and the Case for Trade

The mathematician Stanislaw Ulam once challenged Paul Samuelson to name one
proposition in all of the social sciences that was both true and non-obvious.
Samuelson's answer was comparative advantage — true, he said, in the way a
theorem is true, and non-obvious in that thousands of intelligent people had
failed to grasp it after it was explained to them. The story is repeated partly
because it is a good story and partly because the second half keeps being
confirmed. The argument is roughly two hundred years old, it is not difficult,
and it still loses every public debate it enters.

The reason is that the intuition it overturns is very strong. Everyone knows
what it means to be outcompeted. A country that can make nothing more cheaply
than its rivals feels, self-evidently, like a country with nothing to sell. The
whole content of the theorem is that this feeling is wrong, and that the
relevant comparison is not between countries at all.

## The Comparison That Matters Is Internal

Consider a solicitor who types faster and more accurately than any assistant
she could hire. She has an absolute advantage in both lawyering and typing.
It does not follow that she should do her own typing, because an hour spent
typing is an hour not spent on billable legal work, and the value of that
forgone hour is the true cost of the typing. Her assistant, who is worse at
everything, is nonetheless cheaper at typing in the only sense that matters:
what the assistant gives up to type is far less.

That is the entire argument. Substitute countries for people and it does not
change. What a country gives up to produce a tonne of steel is the wine, or
software, or wheat it could have produced with the same labour and capital.
Trade is worthwhile whenever those internal trade-offs differ, and they almost
always differ. Absolute productivity determines how rich a country is. The
*ratio* of its productivities determines what it should specialise in, and the
two have nothing to do with each other.

Work it through with invented numbers. Suppose one country's workers can
produce, in a year, either ten units of cloth or ten of wine, while another's
can manage six of cloth or two of wine. The second country is worse at both.
But in the first, a unit of wine costs a unit of cloth; in the second, a unit of
wine costs three units of cloth. Wine is expensive in the second country and
cheap in the first. Move wine from where it is cheap to where it is dear, move
cloth the other way, and both countries end up with more of both than they could
have produced alone. No one has been exploited and no one has won at anyone
else's expense. The gain comes from the difference in the ratios, and it would
vanish only if the ratios were identical.

David Ricardo published this in 1817, using England and Portugal, cloth and
wine. He was explicit about an assumption that later readers often skip: that
capital stays home. If capital could move freely to wherever labour was
absolutely most productive, the logic would shift toward absolute advantage and
the tidy result would weaken. In a world of genuinely global capital, that
caveat has more bite than it used to.

## Where the Differences Come From

Ricardo took the productivity differences as given. Explaining them became the
next century's work. The Heckscher-Ohlin framework located them in factor
endowments: a country abundant in arable land will find land-intensive goods
relatively cheap to produce, a country abundant in low-cost labour will find
labour-intensive goods cheap, a country thick with engineers and capital will
find complex machinery cheap. Trade in goods is then, indirectly, trade in the
services of land, labour and capital that went into them.

This explains a great deal of the trade between rich and poor countries, and
very little of the trade that actually dominates world commerce. Most trade
happens between wealthy economies with similar endowments, and much of it
involves shipping goods in both directions within the same category — Germany
sells cars to France while France sells cars to Germany. No theory of
endowments predicts that.

The resolution, developed in the late 1970s and early 1980s and associated most
closely with Paul Krugman, added increasing returns to scale and consumer
appetite for variety. If making cars has large fixed costs, no country can
afford to produce every model, so each specialises in a few and trades for the
rest. The gains come not from differences between countries but from longer
production runs and wider choice. This also explains why the pattern of
specialisation is often historically arbitrary: an industry that gets started
somewhere accumulates suppliers, skilled workers and know-how, and the advantage
becomes real by being acted upon. Switzerland has no natural endowment in
watches. Korea had no endowment in shipbuilding before it decided to have one.

Underneath all of it sits a stubborn empirical regularity. Trade between two
countries rises with the size of their economies and falls sharply with the
distance between them, in a relationship close enough to Newton's that it is
called the gravity equation. Distance still matters enormously, which is
awkward for anyone who thinks geography stopped mattering when container ships
got large.

## What the Theorem Does Not Promise

Here is where the public argument earns its stubbornness, because the critics
are not simply confused. The theorem says that trade raises total output. It
says nothing whatsoever about how that output is distributed, and the standard
model itself predicts that some people lose.

The Stolper-Samuelson result makes this explicit: opening to trade raises the
return to a country's abundant factor and lowers the return to its scarce one.
In a rich country with plenty of capital and skilled workers and relatively few
low-skilled ones, the prediction is that low-skilled wages come under pressure.
That is a conclusion of the theory, not an objection to it.

Economists long assured themselves that the losses were diffuse and the
adjustment quick — workers would move from a declining industry to a growing
one, and that would be that. Research on the rapid growth of Chinese
manufacturing exports after the turn of the century, particularly work by David
Autor and his co-authors on American local labour markets, made that
complacency untenable. The losses turned out to be geographically concentrated
in towns built around a single industry, and they persisted for many years
rather than resolving in a few. People did not move as readily as the models
assumed, and those who did not move stayed poorer. The aggregate gains were
real and the local damage was also real, and the aggregate gains were collected
mostly by people who were not in those towns.

The textbook answer is that winners can compensate losers and still come out
ahead. That is arithmetically sound and politically almost never done. A trade
agreement is a concentrated loss and a diffuse gain, which is a reliable recipe
for opposition, and the opposition is not irrational just because the sum is
positive.

## The Errors That Keep Coming Back

Set the distributional argument aside and what remains in most trade debates is
a small set of mistakes that have been refuted for centuries and refuse to die.

The oldest is mercantilism: exports are winnings, imports are losses, and a
trade surplus is a scoreboard. But the point of exporting is to be able to
import. Goods that arrive are the benefit; goods that leave are the price. A
country that ran a permanent surplus would be permanently shipping out real
things in exchange for accumulating claims it never cashed.

The second is treating a trade deficit as a verdict on competitiveness. A
country's current account balance equals the gap between what it saves and what
it invests, by accounting identity. A nation that invests more than it saves
must import capital, and importing capital means importing goods. That balance
is driven by budget deficits, demographics and investment opportunities far more
than by anything happening at a factory gate or in a tariff schedule.

The third is the notion that jobs are a fixed pool to be divided. Employment
in an economy is set by labour supply, demand and institutions, not by the
volume of imports. Trade changes which jobs exist and where, sometimes
violently, which is a serious matter and a different one entirely.

None of the three is a fringe view. All three are the default assumption in
most political argument about trade, which is why Samuelson's reply to Ulam has
kept its sting. It is not that the theorem is hard. It is that it asks people
to believe that a country can be worse at everything and still have something
worth selling, and no amount of demonstration has ever made that feel true.
