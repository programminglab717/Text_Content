# What Inflation Is and Where It Comes From

Somebody has to walk into the shop. In every country that publishes a
consumer price index, a small army of price collectors visits supermarkets,
petrol stations, dentists, barbers and car dealerships on a fixed schedule,
finding the same specific item — not "shampoo" but a particular bottle of a
particular brand in a particular size on a particular shelf — and writing down
what it costs this month. When the bottle is discontinued, they have to decide
what replaces it, and whether the replacement is better or worse. Inflation is
not a natural quantity waiting to be measured with an instrument. It is a
construction, assembled monthly out of hundreds of thousands of small
judgements, and understanding it starts with understanding what the builders
are trying to do.

## The Thing the Index Is Trying to Isolate

Prices change all the time for reasons that have nothing to do with inflation.
Tomatoes are dear in February. Televisions get cheaper every year because
manufacturing them gets easier. A drought raises the price of coffee and
lowers, indirectly, the price of everything the coffee drinker now cannot
afford. These are relative price movements: the market doing its job, telling
people where scarcity has moved.

Inflation is the residue left when you strip the relative movements out — a
sustained rise in the general level of prices, which is the same thing as a
sustained fall in what a unit of currency buys. To extract it, statisticians
build a basket meant to stand in for what a representative household actually
purchases, weight each item by its share of spending, and track the cost of the
whole basket over time.

Every step of that is contestable. If beef gets expensive and shoppers switch
to chicken, a basket with fixed weights keeps pricing the beef and overstates
the pain; chained indices that update weights continuously correct for this but
lag. If this year's laptop costs the same as last year's but runs twice as
fast, has the price risen, fallen, or held steady? Statistical agencies apply
quality adjustments, sometimes using hedonic regressions that decompose a
product's price into the value of its characteristics, and the results are
genuinely uncertain and occasionally accused of understating inflation.
Housing is worse still: most people in owner-occupied homes do not pay rent,
so agencies impute one, typically by asking what the property would let for.
That imputed number often carries the largest single weight in the index, and
it is not a price anyone actually paid.

There is also more than one index, and they do not agree. A consumer price
index tracks a fixed basket bought out of pocket; a producer price index tracks
what firms pay at the factory gate; a national accounts deflator covers
everything the economy produces, including the medical care paid for on a
household's behalf by an insurer or the state. Analysts also strip out food and
energy to produce a core measure, which is not an attempt to pretend that
groceries and petrol do not matter but an attempt to see the persistent signal
underneath two of the noisiest series in the basket. Central bankers watch core
because it forecasts next year better; households experience headline because
it is what they pay.

The upshot is that no household experiences the published figure. A young
renter in a city with a tight housing market and a long commute faces a
different inflation rate from a mortgage-free retiree who drives rarely and
spends heavily on medical care. Both are correctly measured. Neither is the
average.

## Too Much Money, or Not Enough Goods

The oldest account of inflation is monetary and mechanical: the amount of money
in circulation, multiplied by the rate at which it changes hands, must equal
the volume of transactions multiplied by their average price. Hold velocity and
output roughly steady and prices rise in proportion to the money supply. The
identity is true by construction, and the history of debased coinage, of silver
arriving in sixteenth-century Spain from the Americas, and of every
government that has funded itself with a printing press gives the mechanism
real force.

Its weakness is the assumption of steady velocity. Money's circulation speed
turns out to shift substantially with interest rates, payment technology, and
confidence, which is why central banks that tried to target monetary aggregates
in the early 1980s found the relationship dissolving under them almost as soon
as they leaned on it. The monetary story explains the big episodes superbly and
the ordinary ones poorly.

The modern working account distinguishes pressure from the demand side and from
the supply side. Demand-pull inflation happens when spending outruns the
economy's capacity to produce: orders arrive faster than factories can fill
them, job vacancies outnumber applicants, and firms discover they can raise
prices without losing customers. Cost-push inflation comes from the other
direction — an oil embargo, a crop failure, a currency collapse that raises the
price of everything imported — and it is nastier in one specific way. A demand
boom raises prices and output together, which is uncomfortable but coherent. A
supply shock raises prices while lowering output, leaving policymakers with no
lever that improves both.

There is an important distinction buried here that public argument routinely
misses. A one-off shock — a tariff, a war premium on energy, a pandemic
scrambling supply chains — raises the price level and therefore shows up as a
burst of inflation for about a year, then drops out of the annual comparison
even if nothing gets cheaper. Prices stay high; inflation falls. That gap
between the two statements explains a great deal of the frustration people
express when officials announce that inflation has been beaten.

## Expectations Do Most of the Work

What converts a shock into sustained inflation is anticipation. If everyone
expects prices to be five per cent higher next year, unions bargain for it,
firms build it into next year's price lists, and landlords write it into
leases. The expectation is then satisfied by the behaviour it produced. Run
the same logic with two per cent and the economy settles there instead. The
level is close to arbitrary; the self-confirming quality is not.

This is why the mid-century belief in a stable trade-off between unemployment
and inflation broke down. The original Phillips relationship, drawn from
decades of British data, suggested that a government could buy lower
unemployment with a little more inflation. Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps
argued, before the fact, that the trade-off would only hold while the inflation
was unexpected — that once workers learned to anticipate it, the same
unemployment rate would return alongside a higher inflation rate. The 1970s
delivered exactly that, with rising prices and rising unemployment together,
and the phrase coined for it, stagflation, marked the end of the old
confidence.

Economies that live with chronic inflation eventually build it into their
institutions. Brazil in the 1980s and early 1990s ran an economy in which
wages, rents, savings accounts and tax brackets were all linked to a price
index and adjusted on a rolling basis, which made high inflation survivable and
simultaneously made it almost impossible to stop, since every price was now
mechanically chasing every other. Israel's inflation of the same era had a
similar indexed architecture. The lesson drawn from both was that indexation
buys relief from the pain of inflation at the cost of removing the friction
that might otherwise have slowed it.

Breaking an entrenched expectation is expensive. Paul Volcker's Federal
Reserve did it at the turn of the 1980s by driving interest rates high enough
to force a severe recession, and the disinflation held partly because the
willingness to tolerate that recession was itself the message. Countries that
tried to disinflate without establishing credibility found themselves paying
the cost in lost output and getting no reduction in expectations for it.

## Why the Target Is Not Zero

Given the costs, it seems odd that most central banks aim for low positive
inflation rather than none. The reasons are practical. Measured inflation
probably overstates the true figure slightly, because quality improvements are
hard to capture fully. More importantly, wages are very difficult to cut in
nominal terms — workers accept a real pay cut delivered by a below-inflation
rise far more readily than an explicit reduction — so a little inflation gives
employers a way to reduce real labour costs in a downturn without the morale
damage of announced cuts. And a target of zero would mean nominal interest
rates sitting close to zero in normal times, leaving no room to cut them in a
slump. A small positive rate buys manoeuvring space.

The costs of inflation, when it stays moderate, are correspondingly modest:
the nuisance of repricing, the effort spent economising on cash balances, the
erosion of savings held in currency, and a tax code whose brackets and capital
gains thresholds quietly tighten unless they are indexed. It also transfers
wealth from lenders to borrowers, since debts are repaid in cheaper money — one
reason inflation has always had a political constituency among the indebted,
and why nineteenth-century American farmers campaigned for it under the banner
of free silver.

## The Runaway Case

Hyperinflation is a different phenomenon, not merely a larger one, and it is
almost always fiscal in origin. A government that cannot borrow and will not
tax funds itself by creating money, which is a tax on everyone holding the
currency. But the base of that tax shrinks as people flee it. Holdings are
spent faster, contracts switch to a foreign currency, wages are paid daily, and
the government must print faster still to extract the same real resources.
The spiral is self-reinforcing and, once it is running, has very little to do
with the state of demand or supply.

Weimar Germany in the early 1920s, Hungary immediately after the Second World
War, Zimbabwe in the 2000s and Venezuela more recently all followed that
pattern: a fiscal hole, a captive central bank, a collapse in the willingness
to hold the currency. The endings are also consistent. Hyperinflations stop
abruptly, not gradually, and they stop when the fiscal source is closed off and
a new monetary arrangement is made credible — a currency reform, an independent
issuing authority, a peg, or outright adoption of somebody else's money. The
price level does not drift back down. It simply stops climbing, on the day the
government stops needing it to.
