# What Makes a Labour Market Strange

On the docks of Liverpool, New York and a hundred other ports, the working day
used to begin with the shape-up. Men gathered in a half-circle before dawn and
a foreman walked along the line pointing at the ones he wanted. The rest went
home unpaid, or waited for the afternoon call. It was a market in the plainest
sense — supply and demand meeting in a physical space every morning, prices and
quantities set on the spot — and almost nobody involved in it thought it worked
well. The men who got picked were often the ones who had bought the foreman a
drink. The system was eventually replaced in most ports by registered hiring
halls and guaranteed weekly minimums, not because the economics were wrong but
because a market that reallocates human beings from scratch every twelve hours
turns out to be a poor way to organise a life.

That tension runs through the whole subject. The apparatus built for markets
in wheat and steel applies to labour, and it explains a good deal. But the
commodity here is inseparable from a person who has to show up, who learns, who
resents being treated unfairly, and who goes home at night. Every one of those
facts breaks something in the standard model.

## Why It Is Not a Spot Market

Start with what is actually being bought. An employer does not purchase output;
it purchases time, and hopes for effort. Effort cannot be fully specified in
advance and cannot be fully observed afterwards, which means no employment
contract is ever complete. What fills the gap is an ongoing relationship —
supervision, reputation, promotion prospects, professional norms, and the
mutual expectation that the arrangement will continue.

That relationship generates value of its own. A worker who has been somewhere
three years knows the systems, the customers, the informal workarounds and the
people to ask. This firm-specific human capital is worth something to the
employer and nothing to anyone else, and it is one reason both sides are
reluctant to break the match even when outside conditions change. Recruiting
and training a replacement is genuinely expensive, on the order of months of
salary for a skilled role once lost productivity is counted.

The consequence is that most jobs are not repriced continuously. Wages are set
in a negotiation at the start, adjusted at intervals, and otherwise left alone
while conditions move around them. A labour market is a market in
relationships, not in hours.

## Search, Matching, and Why Unemployment Never Reaches Zero

The oddest recurring feature of any labour market is that unfilled vacancies
and unemployed workers exist at the same time, in large numbers, permanently.
In a market that cleared like a commodity exchange, that would be a
contradiction. In a market where every job and every worker is different and
neither side can see the whole field, it is simply the cost of looking.

The search-and-matching framework developed by Peter Diamond, Dale Mortensen
and Christopher Pissarides treats the meeting of worker and job as a production
process in its own right, with inputs — the stock of searchers, the stock of
vacancies, the effort each puts into searching — and an output of new matches
per month. Anything that makes matching harder reduces hires without changing
the underlying willingness to work on either side. A worker with a reservation
wage, the lowest offer they will accept, turns down early offers in the
reasonable hope of a better one, and the resulting unemployment is productive
rather than wasted: the economy gets better matches for it.

This is also why the headline unemployment rate is a narrower statistic than
most people assume. The standard international definition counts someone as
unemployed only if they are without work, available to start, and have actively
looked recently. Someone who has given up looking is not unemployed; they have
left the labour force, and the unemployment rate falls when they go. A
part-time worker who wants full-time hours is counted as employed. Two
countries with the same unemployment rate can therefore have very different
shares of their working-age population in work, and the participation rate —
who is in the market at all — often carries more information about a slump than
the rate everyone quotes.

Behind the monthly net figures sits an enormous churn. Even in a quiet month,
the number of people hired and the number who leave jobs both run to many
millions in a large economy, and the reported change in employment is the small
difference between two very large flows. Most of that movement is not the
unemployed finding work; it is employed people moving to better jobs, and that
ladder is how wages actually rise for most workers over a career. When the
ladder stops — when quits collapse because nobody dares move — wage growth
stalls even for people whose jobs were never at risk. Economists watch the quit
rate for precisely this reason: it is a measure of confidence that a headline
unemployment number cannot supply.

Plot vacancies against unemployment and you get the Beveridge curve, named for
the British economist and welfare architect. It slopes downward, because
plentiful vacancies coincide with low unemployment. When the whole curve shifts
outward — more vacancies than usual for a given unemployment rate — something
has gone wrong with matching itself. Perhaps the jobs are in cities the
unemployed cannot afford to move to, or require skills they do not have, or
demand schedules incompatible with the childcare they can arrange. The curve is
a diagnostic, not an explanation, but it separates a shortage of demand from a
mismatch of kinds, which are two problems with opposite remedies.

## Wages That Will Not Fall

In a slump, the model says wages should drop until everyone who wants work has
it. They do not. Nominal pay cuts are rare even in severe downturns, and
employers reduce hours, freeze hiring, or lay people off instead.

The explanation is partly about morale. Surveys of managers have found, fairly
consistently, that they avoid pay cuts because they expect the damage to effort
and the departure of their best staff to cost more than the saving. A worker
who takes a below-inflation rise grumbles; a worker handed an explicit
reduction concludes the relationship has changed character. Fairness norms are
not a decoration on the labour market. They are load-bearing.

Some employers go further and pay deliberately above the going rate. The
efficiency wage idea has several versions. Carl Shapiro and Joseph Stiglitz
formalised the disciplinary one: if being fired costs a worker a premium they
cannot get elsewhere, the threat of dismissal becomes real, and supervision gets
cheaper. George Akerlof described a reciprocal version, in which generous
treatment is answered with discretionary effort that no contract could have
required. Henry Ford's decision in 1914 to roughly double the daily wage at his
plants is the standard illustration: he was losing workers to the monotony of
the assembly line almost as fast as he could hire them, and the higher wage cut
turnover sharply enough to pay for itself. The general point is that if a
higher wage raises productivity, the employer will not cut pay to clear the
market, and involuntary unemployment persists as a feature of the equilibrium
rather than a temporary disturbance.

## Who Actually Has the Power

The textbook picture has many small employers competing for workers, so that
anyone paid less than their contribution simply walks. Search frictions wreck
that. If finding another job takes months, involves moving, or means losing
accumulated seniority and the doctor your family has seen for a decade, an
employer can hold pay below what a competitive market would deliver and lose
only a few people.

Economists call this monopsony, a term coined by Joan Robinson: a single buyer
facing many sellers, the mirror image of monopoly. The historical version was
the company town with its own store and its own scrip. The modern versions are
subtler — non-compete clauses, occupational licensing that does not transfer
across state lines, rural hospitals that are the only employer of nurses for
fifty miles, the simple fact that most people will not relocate for a small
raise.

Monopsony also resolves an empirical puzzle that dogged the minimum wage
debate. Simple supply and demand predicts that a wage floor above the market
rate must reduce employment. David Card and Alan Krueger's comparison of
fast-food restaurants in New Jersey, after that state raised its minimum, with
restaurants just across the border in Pennsylvania found no such effect, and a
large literature since has found effects that are generally small at moderate
minimum levels. Under monopsony this is exactly what theory predicts: an
employer holding wages below the competitive level is also employing fewer
people than they otherwise would, and forcing the wage up can raise employment
as well as pay. The argument is about where the threshold sits, not about
whether one exists.

Collective bargaining is the other historical answer to the same imbalance, and
its reach varies enormously. In much of continental Europe, sectoral agreements
still set the terms for most workers in an industry whether or not they belong
to a union. In the English-speaking world, coverage has fallen for decades, and
wage-setting has moved back toward the individual firm.

## What a Wage Is Measuring

Given all this, the number on a payslip is a compound of several things that do
not resolve neatly. Some of it is productivity, augmented by education and
experience — though economists still argue how much schooling raises a worker's
capability and how much it simply signals capabilities that were already there,
an ambiguity Michael Spence set out in the early 1970s and which the data have
never fully settled.

Some of it is compensation for the job's disagreeable features. Adam Smith
noticed that unpleasant, dangerous, irregular or dishonourable work commands a
premium, and the effect is visible in night shifts and offshore rotations,
though it is frequently swamped by the fact that the worst jobs are also the
ones taken by people with the fewest alternatives.

And a surprisingly large share of it is simply the identity of the employer.
Studies tracking workers who move between firms consistently find that some
firms pay systematically more than others for observably identical people, and
that the premium follows the firm rather than the worker. Where you work turns
out to matter nearly as much as what you can do, which is not a conclusion the
model of a single market-clearing wage can accommodate at all.
