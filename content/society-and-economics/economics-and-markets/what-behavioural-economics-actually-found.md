# What Behavioural Economics Actually Found

A group of day-care centres in Israel had a problem familiar to every nursery in
the world: parents turning up late to collect their children, leaving staff
sitting in an empty building past closing time. Some of the centres introduced a
small fine for late pickup. Lateness went up. It stayed up even after the fine
was withdrawn, as though something had been broken that could not be unbroken.
The most plausible reading is that the fine converted a social obligation into a
priced service. Before, arriving late meant imposing on a teacher who had her
own children to collect. Afterwards it meant buying an extra fifteen minutes,
rather cheaply, with no apology owed.

That experiment is a decent introduction to the field, because it shows both
what behavioural economics is good at and what it is easily mistaken for. It is
not the discovery that people are stupid. The parents in the priced centres
responded sensibly to the incentives they were shown; it was the designers of
the fine who had modelled the situation badly, assuming that adding a monetary
cost to an act could only reduce it. The finding is about the structure of
motivation, not the failure of intellect.

## The Model Being Departed From

To see what was being revised, you have to be fair to what came before. The
standard economic agent is not a caricature of a selfish calculator, whatever
its critics say. It is a set of consistency requirements. Preferences are
complete and transitive; choices do not flip when irrelevant options are added
or when the same situation is described differently; beliefs update in
proportion to the strength of new evidence; the future is discounted at a
consistent rate. From these modest axioms a great deal follows, and the payoff is
enormous — a framework in which behaviour can be predicted from prices without
knowing anything about the person.

Herbert Simon began chipping at the foundation in the 1950s. His argument was
that optimisation is expensive. Real decision-makers have limited attention,
limited memory and limited time, and so they satisfice: they search until they
find something good enough against an aspiration level, then stop. Simon won a
Nobel for this and was widely admired and largely ignored, because bounded
rationality told modellers what to abandon without telling them what to write
down instead.

The breakthrough came from two psychologists who supplied the missing thing: a
tractable alternative model. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky published prospect
theory in 1979, and its genius was that it had the same shape as the theory it
replaced. It could be fitted to data. It made point predictions. Economists
could use it without ceasing to be economists.

## Reference Points and the Asymmetry of Losing

Prospect theory's central move is that people do not evaluate outcomes as final
states of wealth. They evaluate changes, measured from a reference point that is
usually the status quo but can be an expectation, a recent peak, a purchase
price, or whatever the question happens to suggest. This sounds minor. It is not,
because it means the same objective outcome can be a gain or a loss depending on
where you start counting, and gains and losses are not weighted alike.

Losses loom larger. Giving up something hurts noticeably more than acquiring the
identical thing pleases — an asymmetry with a factor typically estimated at
somewhere around two, though the exact number depends heavily on the domain and
the measurement. From this asymmetry a long list of anomalies falls out
naturally. The endowment effect: hand someone a mug and their asking price
exceeds what an otherwise identical person will pay for it, because selling is
coded as a loss. The status quo bias: any change has a loss component, so
inertia gets a systematic advantage. The disposition effect among investors, who
hold losing stocks too long because selling would realise a loss and make it
real.

The theory also proposed that people misweight probabilities, treating small
chances as larger than they are and near-certainties as less than certain — which
is why the same individual will buy both a lottery ticket and an insurance
policy, a combination that looks incoherent under the standard model. And it
explained a robust reversal: people tend to be risk-averse over gains and
risk-seeking over losses. Offered a sure gain or a gamble with the same expected
value, most take the sure thing. Facing a sure loss or a gamble with the same
expected value, most take the gamble, which is a fair description of how firms
behave on the way down and how gamblers behave at two in the morning.

## The Future Belongs to Someone Else

The other durable finding concerns time. The standard treatment discounts the
future at a constant rate, which has the tidy implication that plans made today
remain optimal tomorrow. Actual discounting appears steeply front-loaded: the
gap between today and next week looms far larger than the gap between a year and
a year-and-a-week, even though both are seven days.

The consequence is preference reversal over time, and it is the mathematical
skeleton of nearly every self-control problem anyone has. Sitting in January, you
genuinely prefer to save more, exercise more, and start the report early. Arriving
at the moment of action, the immediate cost is present and the benefit is not,
and you reverse. Neither self is irrational in any simple sense; they just
disagree, and the one holding the pen keeps changing. This is why commitment
devices work — the Christmas savings club that pays no interest and charges for
early withdrawal, the deposit forfeited if you skip the gym, the writer who gives
away the router before sitting down. All of them are the January self placing
constraints on the March self, and their popularity is evidence that people know
perfectly well what is going on.

## Nudges, and the Modesty That Followed

The most consequential practical result came from the mildest-looking finding.
Defaults matter enormously. When employees are automatically enrolled in a
retirement plan with an option to leave, participation is dramatically higher
than when they must actively opt in, and the effect persists for years. Nothing
has been forbidden and nothing has been made more expensive; only the setting
that applies when nobody does anything has changed.

Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein packaged this and a cluster of related results
into the argument of *Nudge* in 2008, coining the awkward phrase "libertarian
paternalism" for interventions that steer without restricting. Governments took
it up with unusual speed. Britain set up a behavioural insights team inside the
Cabinet Office around 2010; dozens of similar units followed elsewhere. Tax
letters were rewritten to mention that most neighbours had already paid. Organ
donation registration forms were redesigned. Pension enrolment was made automatic
by statute in several countries.

Then came the corrective. Part of it was the wider replication crisis in
psychology, which fell hard on exactly the kind of clever laboratory
demonstration the field had built its reputation on. Ego depletion — the idea
that willpower is a finite resource drained by use — failed to survive large
pre-registered replication attempts. Various social priming results proved
similarly fragile. Kahneman himself publicly acknowledged that a chapter of his
own book had leaned on evidence weaker than he had realised. None of this touched
the core findings, which have been replicated across countries, stakes and
decades. It did puncture the assumption that any striking result in a good
journal was real.

The second correction was about size. Nudges work, but many produce effects far
smaller than the early enthusiasm suggested, and published estimates were
inflated by the ordinary machinery of selective publication. A reminder text
message might lift attendance at a clinic by a few percentage points, which is
worth having at the price, but it is not a substitute for building the clinic
somewhere people can reach. Critics argued, reasonably, that a focus on choice
architecture let policymakers off the hook: it is cheaper to redesign a form than
to raise a wage, and it flatters the belief that outcomes are mostly the result
of individual decisions.

## What Is Left Standing

A field that has been absorbed stops being a movement, and that is roughly what
has happened. Nobody now writes a contract-theory paper assuming perfectly
disciplined attention. Consumer protection regulators study how mortgage terms
are presented, not merely whether the terms are disclosed, because disclosure
that nobody can parse is not information. Firms have exploited the same findings
with enthusiasm — subscriptions that renew by default, drip-priced airfares,
anchoring by struck-through list price — which is a reminder that choice
architecture was never a government invention. Every form has a default. The
only question is who chose it and in whose interest.

Gerd Gigerenzer's long-running objection deserves a hearing here, because it
sharpens what the findings mean. He argues that many so-called biases are simply
heuristics evaluated against the wrong benchmark: fast rules that perform well in
the noisy, sparse environments organisms actually inhabit, and that look foolish
only when scored against a laboratory problem with clean probabilities. Ignoring
most available information is often the right call when the information is mostly
noise. Whether one calls a shortcut a bias or an adaptation depends on the
environment you think it was built for, and that is an empirical question, not a
matter of temperament.

What survives from the whole enterprise is less a catalogue of errors than a
methodological instruction. Preferences are constructed, not merely revealed, and
they are constructed partly by the situation in which the question is put. That
makes the frame part of the economics rather than noise around it, which is a
considerably less comfortable place to stand than the one the field occupied
before.
