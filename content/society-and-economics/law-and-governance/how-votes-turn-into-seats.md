# How Votes Turn Into Seats

In 1812 the Massachusetts legislature redrew its state senate districts, and
one of them came out shaped like a long-necked creature curling around the
northern edge of Boston. A newspaper cartoonist added a head, wings, and claws,
and someone attached the name of the governor who had signed the bill, Elbridge
Gerry. The composite word outlived both the district and the man. Two centuries
later it is the standard term in every English-speaking democracy for drawing a
boundary to determine an outcome in advance.

What the cartoon captured is that an election has two separate stages, and
almost all public attention goes to the first. Voters cast ballots. Then a rule
converts those ballots into seats, offices, and governing power. The conversion
rule is not a technicality. Run the same set of ballots through different
conversion rules and you can get different parliaments, different governments,
and different policies — without a single voter changing their mind.

## The Same Ballots, Different Governments

The simplest rule is plurality in single-member districts, often called
first-past-the-post. The country is carved into constituencies, each elects one
representative, and whoever gets the most votes in a constituency wins it.
Nothing is required beyond finishing first; a candidate can win with thirty-five
percent of the vote in a crowded field.

This rule has consequences that run deep into a country's party structure.
Votes cast for losing candidates elect nobody, and votes cast for a winner
beyond the margin needed do nothing either, so a party whose support is thinly
spread everywhere can win a substantial share of the national vote and almost
no seats, while a party with regionally concentrated support wins seats far out
of proportion to its total. Voters learn this and adjust, abandoning candidates
they judge unable to finish first. Parties learn it too and merge rather than
split. The tendency of plurality systems to settle into two dominant parties is
robust enough that it carries a name — Duverger's law — though it is a strong
tendency rather than an iron rule, as the survival of regionally based third
parties in Canada and the United Kingdom shows.

Proportional representation takes the opposite approach: seats are allocated to
parties in proportion to votes across multi-member districts or a national
pool. Small parties survive, coalition government becomes normal, and the
bargaining that a two-party system does inside each party before the election
happens instead among parties after it. Most PR systems impose a threshold — a
party must clear some minimum share, commonly around five percent, to receive
any seats — to prevent fragmentation into unworkable numbers of parties.

Between these lie hybrids. Mixed-member proportional systems, used in Germany
and adopted by New Zealand in the 1990s, give each voter two votes: one for a
local representative and one for a party. Local winners take their seats, and
additional list seats are then distributed so the overall composition of the
legislature matches the party vote. Voters keep a constituency member and the
chamber comes out roughly proportional. The single transferable vote, used in
Ireland and Malta and for the Australian Senate, achieves proportionality
without party lists by having voters rank candidates in multi-member districts,
with surplus votes above the winning quota and votes for eliminated candidates
transferring down the rankings. Ranked-choice voting in single-member seats is
the same counting machinery applied to one winner; it guarantees majority
support for the victor but produces no proportionality at all, since the
district still elects only one person.

## Where District Lines Come From

Any system using districts has to draw them, and then redraw them, because
populations move. In the United States the cycle is anchored to the decennial
census. Seats in the House of Representatives are reapportioned among the
states according to the new population counts, and then each state redraws its
internal district boundaries — for Congress and for its own legislature — to
match.

For most of American history the redrawing was optional in practice, and many
states simply declined to do it. Rural districts emptied out and urban
districts filled, and legislatures dominated by rural members saw no reason to
correct the imbalance. By the mid-twentieth century some state legislative
districts contained many times the population of others, meaning a vote in one
place carried a small fraction of the weight of a vote in another. Federal
courts had long treated apportionment as a political question outside their
competence. That changed with *Baker v. Carr* in 1962, which held such
challenges justiciable, and then with *Reynolds v. Sims* and *Wesberry v.
Sanders*, which established that districts must contain substantially equal
populations — the principle summarized as one person, one vote.

Equal population is now the hardest constraint, enforced to within very small
percentages for congressional districts. The other constraints are softer and
vary by jurisdiction: contiguity, meaning a district must be a connected piece
of territory; compactness, which has a dozen competing mathematical definitions
and no agreed one; respect for existing political subdivisions like counties
and municipalities; and preservation of communities of interest, a genuinely
useful idea that is also elastic enough to justify nearly anything. Layered on
top is the Voting Rights Act, which restricts maps that dilute the voting
strength of racial minorities, and which has generated decades of litigation
over when a district must be drawn to give a minority community a realistic
opportunity to elect its preferred candidate, and when drawing one goes too
far.

## Packing and Cracking

The techniques of partisan mapmaking are simple enough to state in a sentence
each, which is part of why they persist.

Packing concentrates the opposing party's voters into a small number of
districts they win overwhelmingly. Every vote above the winning margin is
wasted, and the surrounding districts become safer for the mapmaker's side.
Cracking does the reverse: it splits a concentration of opposing voters across
several districts so that they form a substantial minority in each and a
majority in none. Used together across a state, the two techniques can produce
a delegation whose partisan balance diverges sharply from the statewide vote,
and can make that divergence stable for a decade.

A third variant is bipartisan and less discussed: incumbent protection, where
both parties agree to a map that makes nearly every seat safe for whoever
currently holds it. This produces very few competitive general elections, which
shifts the real contest to primaries, which are typically low-turnout and
dominated by the most committed voters — a mechanism that pushes
representatives toward the ends of their own coalitions regardless of which
party benefits from the map overall.

Measuring all this has become a serious technical field. Analysts compare a
map's seat outcome against the distribution of outcomes produced by generating
thousands of alternative maps at random under the same legal constraints, which
reveals whether the enacted plan is an ordinary draw or a statistical outlier.
Simpler summary statistics exist too, such as the efficiency gap, which
compares each party's wasted votes. None of these has been adopted as a
definitive legal test.

## Who Holds the Pen

The identity of the mapmaker matters more than any formal criterion. In most
American states, district lines are drawn by the state legislature and signed
by the governor — which is to say, by the people whose own jobs depend on them.

Other democracies resolved this differently and mostly earlier. The United
Kingdom assigns the task to permanent, politically neutral Boundary
Commissions, which propose maps, hold public hearings, and revise. Canada
adopted independent commissions in the 1960s, one per province, chaired by a
judge. Australia's electoral commission conducts redistributions on defined
triggers. In none of these countries is boundary-drawing a partisan contest in
the way it is in the United States, and the absence is not because the
politicians there are more restrained. The function was removed from their
hands.

A number of American states have moved the same direction by ballot
initiative, creating independent or bipartisan commissions with varying degrees
of insulation from the legislature. The design details determine whether they
work: who appoints the members, whether elected officials and lobbyists are
excluded, what happens on deadlock, and whether the legislature can reject the
result.

Federal courts have stepped back from the partisan question. In 2019 the
Supreme Court held in *Rucho v. Common Cause* that claims of excessive partisan
gerrymandering present political questions federal courts cannot adjudicate,
while leaving racial gerrymandering claims and state-court claims intact.
Several state supreme courts have since struck down maps under provisions of
their own constitutions, making state constitutional law the main venue for
these disputes.

## What Fair Maps Cannot Fix

It is tempting to treat districting as the whole problem, but geography does
some of the work on its own. Where one party's voters cluster densely in
cities and the other's spread evenly across suburbs and countryside, even maps
drawn by a scrupulous neutral commission will tend to pack the urban party and
waste its votes. The effect is a property of settlement patterns interacting
with single-member districts, not of anyone's intent.

Which points at the deeper issue. Almost every pathology of districting —
packing, cracking, wasted votes, safe seats, the outsized effect of where lines
fall — is a consequence of electing one person per territory by plurality.
Systems that elect several representatives per district from the same ballots
are far less sensitive to boundaries, because a district that elects five
members will send a mixed delegation almost regardless of how its edges are
drawn. The salamander of 1812 was possible because the seat inside it belonged
entirely to whoever won it.
