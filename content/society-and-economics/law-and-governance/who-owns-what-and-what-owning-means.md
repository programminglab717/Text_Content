# Who Owns What, and What Owning Means

Consider a flat in a city with an active rental market. The landlord owns it
and cannot enter without notice. The tenant does not own it and can exclude
the landlord, the landlord's relatives, and everyone else. A bank that has
never seen the building can force its sale if payments stop. The upstairs
neighbour has a right to have their floor supported by the landlord's ceiling.
The council can refuse permission to knock out a wall, and a preservation
listing may prohibit changing the windows. A utility has a permanent right to
run a pipe beneath the garden. Ask who owns the flat and the answer is
straightforward. Ask what owning it consists of and the answer fragments
immediately.

This is the first thing to understand about property: it is not a relationship
between a person and a thing. It is a set of relationships among people,
concerning a thing, which the law sorts into rights that can be held
separately and traded separately.

## A Bundle, With Exclusion at the Centre

Blackstone described ownership as sole and despotic dominion, and the phrase
has been quoted mockingly ever since, because even in his own day it was
false. The rights an owner holds can be listed and separated: to possess, to
use, to exclude others, to take the income, to transfer, to consume or
destroy, to use as security. Each of these can be given away, temporarily or
permanently, without the owner ceasing to be the owner. A lease hands over
possession and exclusion for a term. A mortgage hands over a right of sale on
default. An easement hands over a specific use. A licensing deal hands over
the income.

Of the whole bundle, the right to exclude does the most work. It is the one
that lets an owner ignore everyone else's opinion about the best use of the
thing, and it is the one that makes investment rational, because a person who
cannot keep others off will not bother improving. It is also the right that
attracts the most regulation, since exclusion is how owners impose on
strangers — a landowner who excludes the public from a beach, an employer who
excludes union organizers from a car park, a platform that excludes a user
from an account.

## The Law Starts From Possession, Not Title

Legal systems care about who is actually holding the thing, independent of who
is entitled to. Possession is protected as a fact, which is why a thief can
sue a second thief, and why the law generally forbids an owner from seizing
property back by force even from someone with no right to it. This looks
strange until you consider the alternative: a rule that lets anyone with a
plausible claim of right use self-help produces violence and a great deal of
mistaken violence.

Possession also generates ownership. Original acquisition rules assign
unowned things to the first taker, and the litigation over what counts as
taking is old and entertaining — the pursued fox that a rival killed and
carried off, the whale struck with a marked harpoon, the baseball caught and
dropped in a scrum. Different industries settled on different customs, and
courts generally adopted whatever rule the relevant trade already used,
because the point is predictability rather than metaphysics.

More consequentially, possession maintained long enough and openly enough
becomes title. Adverse possession in common law systems and prescription in
civil law ones transfer ownership to a person who has occupied land as an
owner would, without permission, for a statutory period. It sounds like
rewarding a trespasser. Its actual functions are to quiet stale claims so that
land can be sold without infinite historical research, to align legal title
with the facts on the ground after boundaries have drifted for decades, and to
penalize owners who neither use nor monitor their property while someone else
builds a life on it.

## Slicing a Thing Across Time and Space

The English common law developed a distinctive trick: dividing ownership
across time. A fee simple is ownership without a time limit. A life estate is
ownership for the duration of somebody's life, after which the property passes
to whoever holds the remainder. Future interests of this kind let land be
settled across generations, and the resulting doctrines became so intricate
that they remain a rite of passage in legal education. Civil law took a
different approach, insisting on a single unified ownership with lesser real
rights carved out of it — a usufruct entitling the holder to use and income for
a period, a servitude entitling a neighbour to cross, a long lease of a kind
that approaches ownership without becoming it.

Both traditions restrict how many kinds of property right may exist. You
cannot invent a novel form of ownership and bind the world to respect it; you
must use a recognized category. The reason is information cost. A buyer, a
lender, a surveyor and a tax authority all need to know what they are dealing
with, and if every parcel could carry bespoke obligations of unlimited
variety, checking would become impossible and the whole market would slow
down. The closed list has a cost — genuinely useful new forms have to wait for
legislation, which is how condominium and strata title regimes came into
existence, since the idea of owning a cube of air with shared ownership of the
structure around it fit nothing in the traditional menu.

The same logic explains the rules about obligations that run with land. A
promise between neighbours is an ordinary contract binding only them. For the
promise to bind whoever buys the house next, it must satisfy stricter
conditions and usually be registered, because it is being imposed on people
who never agreed to it and who need a way to discover it.

## Making Ownership Legible

None of this works unless claims can be checked. Older systems recorded deeds:
a public archive of transactions, from which a buyer's lawyer reconstructs a
chain of title back far enough to be reasonably confident. The method is
laborious and never conclusive, which is why title insurance became a
substantial industry where the deeds system persists. Title registration
inverts the logic. The register does not record transactions; it states who
owns what, and that statement is the legal position, generally guaranteed by
the state with compensation if the register turns out to be wrong. It is
faster, cheaper and easier to lend against, and the transition to it has taken
most countries decades of surveying and dispute resolution.

Formalizing title is not a neutral technical exercise. Where people hold land
under customary or communal arrangements — seasonal grazing rights, lineage
claims, layered entitlements in which several families hold different
recognized uses of the same ground — a registration programme must decide
which of those claims becomes the registered one. The process frequently
converts overlapping rights into a single name, often a man's, often the most
literate or best-connected claimant, and the people whose secondary rights
vanish are usually the ones with the least capacity to object. Arguments that
formal title unlocks credit for the poor have run into this repeatedly: the
paperwork is the easy part, and a registry that does not match what the
community actually recognizes gets ignored or produces dispossession.
Recognition running the other way is possible — Australian courts eventually
accepted that indigenous title had survived colonization rather than being
extinguished by it — but translating a customary relationship to land into a
registrable interest is genuinely difficult, not merely bureaucratic.

## The Limits Are Part of the Right

Every system of property is riddled with constraints, and they are not
exceptions to ownership so much as its terms. Nuisance law stops uses that
unreasonably interfere with neighbours. Planning and zoning determine what may
be built. Environmental rules restrict clearing, draining and emitting. The
state may take land outright for public purposes, on payment of compensation,
and the perpetual argument is over the edges: when regulation so reduces value
that it amounts to a taking requiring payment, and what counts as a public
purpose.

Then there are the resources that resist private ownership altogether. The
standard story about commons — that shared pasture is inevitably destroyed by
each herder's incentive to add one more animal — turned out to describe
unmanaged open access rather than commons as they historically existed.
Detailed fieldwork on irrigation systems, fisheries and alpine grazing found
communities that had sustained shared resources for centuries using their own
monitoring, boundaries, graduated sanctions and dispute forums, and that often
did worse after a government replaced those arrangements with formal
ownership. The opposite failure exists too: when rights over one resource are
split among too many holders who can each veto, nothing can be assembled and
the resource sits idle. A city block owned by forty parties, a drug that
requires licences from a dozen patent holders. Too much fragmentation blocks
use as effectively as too little control.
