# Common Law and Civil Law: Two Ways of Making Law

Put a judge in Lyon and a judge in Leeds in front of the same dispute — a
builder who walked off a half-finished house, leaving the owner to hire
someone else at greater cost — and watch what each one reaches for first. The
French judge reaches for a code: a numbered article on the obligations of a
contractor, sitting inside a single book that also governs sales, leases,
marriage, inheritance, and the liability of a landowner whose wall falls on a
passerby. The English judge reaches for cases: decisions written by other
judges, most of them about disputes that had nothing to do with building,
which between them settled what counts as repudiating a contract and what a
disappointed party may recover.

The two will probably arrive at similar answers. Builders who abandon jobs
lose almost everywhere. The difference is not the outcome but the location of
authority — where the binding statement of a rule is kept, who is entitled to
issue it, and what a lawyer has to read in order to find out what the law
currently is.

## Circuits, Writs, and a System Nobody Designed

English common law was not drafted. It accumulated. After the Norman conquest
the crown had an interest in pulling litigation away from local, baronial and
ecclesiastical courts, and under Henry II it did so by sending royal justices
out on circuit to hear pleas in the shires. What made the royal courts
attractive was procedure: a sworn body of local men to settle disputed facts,
and enforcement backed by the king. What made them a system was the writ — a
standard form of command, purchased from the Chancery, that started a case.
There was a writ of debt, a writ of trespass, a writ of right. If no writ fit
your grievance, you had no case, and so the forms of action quietly determined
the shape of substantive English law for centuries. Contract law grew out of a
writ originally meant for wrongs, because lawyers stretched trespass on the
case to cover a craftsman who did a job badly, and then one who never did it
at all.

Because the law lived in decided cases, it had to be recorded, and the
recording habit ran from the medieval Year Books through the named reporters
of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries to the official series of the
nineteenth. Reliable reports plus a clear court hierarchy are what turned
loose respect for earlier decisions into the hard doctrine of binding
precedent, which is younger than most people assume — a Victorian tightening,
not a medieval inheritance. Alongside it grew equity, the separate
jurisdiction of the Chancellor, which existed precisely because the common law
courts had become rigid: if the writ system gave you no remedy, or a remedy
that produced injustice, you petitioned the Chancellor for conscience-based
relief. Injunctions, trusts, and specific performance all come from that
parallel track.

## Rome, Bologna, and the Codes

The other tradition began as a text. Justinian's jurists compiled the
accumulated writing of Roman lawyers into the collection later called the
*Corpus Juris Civilis*, and when scholars in eleventh-century Italy recovered
the Digest, they treated it the way theologians treated scripture: something
to be glossed, reconciled, systematized and taught. Bologna became a magnet
for students from across Latin Christendom, who went home carrying a body of
learning that was academic before it was national. For several centuries
educated European lawyers shared a common legal language, the *ius commune*,
layered over local custom.

Codification came later and was explicitly political. The French code of 1804
was meant to sweep away a patchwork of regional customs, Roman rules, royal
ordinances and clerical jurisdiction, and to replace it with one accessible
statement a literate citizen could in principle read. Its drafters wrote in
plain, unfussy French and kept the articles short. The German code that took
effect a century later was the opposite in temperament: the product of decades
of professorial system-building, opening with a general part of abstract
concepts — legal persons, declarations of will, conditions, time limits — that
apply across the rest of the book. Between them those two models were adopted,
adapted or imitated across Latin America, much of Africa and Asia, and, by
deliberate choice rather than conquest, in places like Japan, which studied
German law intensively while modernizing, and Turkey, which took over the
Swiss civil code wholesale.

Codification did not end judicial lawmaking; it relocated and disguised it.
The French code devoted only a handful of short articles to liability for harm
— the famous one says in a single line that a person who causes damage to
another by fault must repair it — and out of that sentence French courts built
an entire modern law of accidents, including strict liability for things in
one's custody, which the drafters plainly did not have in mind when railways
and motor cars did not exist. The code's brevity, initially a virtue of
accessibility, became an invitation. German courts did something comparable
with the general clauses of their code, using the requirement that obligations
be performed in good faith as a hook for whole doctrines the legislature never
wrote, from duties of pre-contractual disclosure to the renegotiation of
contracts wrecked by hyperinflation.

## What a Judge Is For

The deepest practical difference is the judge's job description. A common law
judge decides a live dispute and, in explaining the decision, makes law. The
reasoning necessary to the result binds later courts; the asides do not.
Lawyers spend their professional lives arguing about which is which, and
about whether the present case is meaningfully like or unlike the earlier one
— the craft of distinguishing, which allows a system formally committed to
precedent to change direction without admitting it. Judgments are long,
signed, personal in style, and often accompanied by dissents that later
generations quarry for material.

Who becomes a judge follows from this. In England, and in most systems
descended from it, judges are recruited late, from senior practitioners who
have argued before the courts for decades; judging is a capstone, often a
single rank held for the rest of a career, and the bench is small. On the
continent, judging is a career entered young. A law graduate sits a
competitive examination, trains in a judicial school, takes a junior post, and
is promoted through a hierarchy by a body that evaluates performance. That
produces a judiciary that is numerous, professional, salaried, and
institutionally cautious — well suited to applying a code and managing a file,
poorly suited to the frank policy-making that a common law appellate judge is
expected to do in public and under their own name.

A civil law judge, in the classical account, applies the code to the facts and
is not supposed to be making law at all. The French Cour de cassation
traditionally issued judgments of extraordinary brevity, impersonal and
syllogistic, with no dissent and no authorial voice. The intellectual work of
organizing the law was done elsewhere, by professors writing treatises and
case notes — the *doctrine* — which carry a weight in continental practice
that would strike an English barrister as peculiar. In practice the highest
courts do follow their own prior decisions, and a settled line of them,
*jurisprudence constante*, functions much like precedent without being
described as binding.

Procedure diverges just as sharply. The common law trial is a concentrated
event, a single hearing at which evidence is produced live and tested by
cross-examination, and that shape is a legacy of the jury: you assemble
ordinary people once, so everything must happen at once, and you need rules of
evidence to keep unreliable material away from them. Continental civil
procedure typically has no trial in that sense. A file accumulates through a
sequence of hearings, the judge takes an active role in deciding what
questions to pursue and which experts to appoint, witnesses are questioned by
the court rather than dueled over by counsel, and there is far less pretrial
disclosure because the judge, not the parties, drives the search for facts.

## The Systems Lean Toward Each Other

Neither tradition survives in pure form. Legislation now dominates common law
countries so thoroughly that most litigation is really statutory
interpretation, and judges spend more time construing statutes than mining old
cases. Civil law systems, meanwhile, have acquired constitutional courts that
write long, reasoned, openly creative opinions with dissents, which is very
nearly the common law style transplanted into the one place the old model had
no answer for. European Union law and the European Convention on Human Rights
push national systems toward shared doctrines and toward reasoning by
precedent, because the Luxembourg and Strasbourg courts themselves work that
way.

Some jurisdictions have been mixed from the beginning. Scotland kept Roman-
based private law after the union with England while absorbing English
commercial and public law. Louisiana and Quebec preserved civilian codes
inside common law federations. South Africa layers Roman-Dutch private law
under a constitutional and procedural apparatus that looks Anglo-American.
International commercial arbitration has produced a working hybrid of its own,
with document production narrower than American discovery and broader than a
continental judge would order, and written witness statements standing in for
much of the live testimony.

Real differences remain, and commercial lawyers feel them. German and French
law impose a general duty of good faith in performing contracts; English law
has resisted any such open-ended obligation, preferring specific doctrines. In
civil systems a court ordering performance of the contract is the natural
remedy and money the substitute; in common law systems it is the reverse, with
specific performance reserved for land and genuinely unique goods. The notary,
a quasi-public drafter who authenticates transactions and whose deeds carry
evidentiary force, is central to continental property and family dealings and
has no real Anglo-American counterpart. The trust — splitting legal and
beneficial ownership — grew in equity and does not fit a civilian theory of
unitary ownership, though France, Germany and others have built functional
substitutes.

What each tradition worries about is revealing. A codifier fears the gap: the
situation the drafters did not foresee, for which no article supplies an
answer, and the system responds with general clauses, analogy, and the
expectation that a judge will reason from principle. A common lawyer fears the
opposite — not silence but noise, a thicket of decisions pointing different
ways, out of which no clear rule can be extracted. Both fears are well
founded, and most of the institutional machinery each system has built over
the past few centuries exists to answer them.
