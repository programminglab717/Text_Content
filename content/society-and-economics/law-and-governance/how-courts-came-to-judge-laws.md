# How Courts Came to Judge Laws

The case that established the most consequential power in American law began
with an undelivered envelope. In the last days of his presidency John Adams
appointed a batch of justices of the peace for the District of Columbia; the
Senate confirmed them, the commissions were signed and sealed, and then, in
the rush of a departing administration, several were simply never handed out.
One of the men left waiting was William Marbury. The incoming Jefferson
administration saw no reason to deliver commissions to its opponents, and
Marbury went to the Supreme Court asking for an order compelling the new
Secretary of State to hand his over.

The Chief Justice hearing the case was John Marshall, who had been Adams's
Secretary of State and whose own office had failed to deliver the envelopes —
a conflict of interest that troubled nobody much at the time. Marshall was in
an awkward spot. Order delivery and Jefferson would ignore him, exposing the
Court as powerless. Deny Marbury and the Court would look supine. What he did
instead was hold that Marbury had a legal right to his commission and was
entitled to a remedy, but that the section of the Judiciary Act under which he
had come directly to the Supreme Court gave that court a kind of original
jurisdiction the Constitution did not permit. The statute therefore could not
be applied. Marbury lost, Jefferson got the outcome he wanted and had nothing
to defy, and the Court announced in passing that it was the business of the
judiciary to say what the law is, including when the law is a statute that
conflicts with the Constitution.

The idea was not invented that morning. Edward Coke had suggested in the early
seventeenth century that the common law might control an act of Parliament,
the Privy Council had long reviewed colonial legislation for conformity with
charters, a few state courts had already refused to apply statutes, and
Alexander Hamilton had argued for judicial review in the Federalist papers.
What the case supplied was an occasion and a memorable sentence.

## Two Ways to Build the Power

The American design spread the power thinly. Any court, at any level, hearing
an ordinary lawsuit, may conclude that a statute is unconstitutional and
decline to apply it. There is no special constitutional tribunal and no
separate constitutional procedure; the question arises inside a dispute about
a contract, a prosecution, a tax bill. Formally the ruling resolves only that
case, but because lower courts follow higher ones, a Supreme Court holding
effectively removes the statute from operation. The design carries built-in
limits: someone must have been injured and must sue, which produces the
doctrines of standing, ripeness and mootness, and courts cannot answer
constitutional questions in the abstract no matter how urgent.

That model travelled badly to Europe, for a structural reason. Continental
judiciaries are large career services without binding precedent, so diffuse
review would have meant thousands of judges reaching inconsistent conclusions
about the same statute with no mechanism for settling the matter. There was
also, after the 1930s and 1940s, a well-earned distrust of the ordinary
judiciary, much of which had served authoritarian regimes without visible
discomfort. The alternative was designed by Hans Kelsen for the Austrian
republic after the First World War: a single specialized constitutional court,
standing outside the regular judicial hierarchy, with a monopoly on the
question. Ordinary judges who suspect a statute is unconstitutional do not
decide the point; they stop and refer it. After 1945 that model was adopted in
Germany and Italy, then in Spain and Portugal after their dictatorships ended,
then across post-communist Europe, and in South Korea, South Africa and
elsewhere.

Concentrated courts can do things diffuse ones cannot. They hear abstract
challenges with no underlying dispute — in France, a bill may be referred
before it is promulgated, originally only by a handful of officials and, after
a reform in the 1970s, by a group of legislators, which turned the procedure
into a routine weapon of parliamentary opposition. France later added a route
for constitutional questions to be raised in ordinary litigation and passed
upward, closing the gap that had kept enacted laws immune. Germany allows an
individual constitutional complaint after ordinary remedies are exhausted,
which gives citizens direct access and has buried the court in petitions ever
since. And a concentrated court's ruling typically annuls the statute for
everyone, with effect from a stated date, which is legislative in a way an
American judgment formally is not.

A confusing feature of the phrase is that in Britain and much of the
Commonwealth, judicial review usually means something else entirely: the
review not of statutes but of decisions made by ministers, agencies, councils
and tribunals under statutes. That jurisdiction is far older and far busier
than constitutional review, and it asks different questions. Did the official
act within the powers Parliament actually granted? Was the procedure fair —
was the affected person told the case against them and given a chance to
answer, and was the decision-maker free of personal interest? Was the
conclusion one no reasonable authority could have reached? Were relevant
considerations taken into account and irrelevant ones excluded? Every
developed legal system has some version of this, whether through ordinary
courts or, as in France, through a separate administrative jurisdiction with
its own hierarchy. For most citizens it matters more than constitutional
litigation, because it is the mechanism that reaches planning permissions,
benefit denials, licensing decisions and deportation orders.

## Weak Versions, Deliberately

Some systems wanted rights review without giving courts the final word. The
British Human Rights Act is the clearest example: courts must read legislation
compatibly with the protected rights so far as it is possible to do so, and
where that is impossible, a senior court may issue a declaration of
incompatibility. The declaration does nothing to the statute. It remains in
force, and Parliament decides whether to amend it. In practice governments
have usually responded, which is the point — the arrangement was meant to
produce a dialogue rather than a veto. Canada's charter took a different
route, allowing legislatures to override certain rights guarantees by
expressly invoking a notwithstanding clause, subject to renewal every few
years so that the override must be defended again. New Zealand went weaker
still, with an interpretive obligation and no invalidation power at all.

## How the Question Gets Decided

Having the power raises the harder problem of exercising it. Outside the
United States the dominant method is proportionality analysis, a structured
sequence rather than a judgment call. A court asks whether the law pursues a
legitimate aim, whether the measure is capable of advancing that aim, whether
a less restrictive means would have achieved it about as well, and finally
whether the benefit gained is worth the cost imposed on the right. The
sequence does not remove disagreement, but it forces it into the open at the
step where it actually lives, usually the last two. American doctrine reaches
similar terrain through tiers of scrutiny — strict, intermediate, rational
basis — where choosing the tier does most of the work, and much of the
litigation is about which tier applies.

Courts also have techniques for not deciding. Constitutional avoidance reads a
statute narrowly to escape the question. Political question doctrine declares
certain issues committed to other branches. Standing requirements keep
challengers out. Margin-of-appreciation reasoning, developed in the European
human rights system, defers to national authorities on contested moral
questions where practice across countries diverges. These are not evasions so
much as budget management: a court's authority is a finite resource, and
spending it on a fight it will lose damages its ability to win later ones.

Remedies have become more inventive as the power has spread. A Canadian or
South African court may declare a law invalid but suspend the declaration for
a year so the legislature can fix it without leaving a legal vacuum. German
practice includes declaring a statute incompatible with the constitution
without voiding it, and setting a deadline. Courts read words into statutes,
read them down, sever the offending clause, or order the government to report
back on implementation. Each of these is an attempt to solve the problem that
striking a law down entirely often produces a worse situation than the one
complained of.

How the judges are chosen follows the model. Where review is concentrated,
constitutional judges are usually selected by an explicitly political process
with an explicitly political balance — in Germany, elected by large
supermajorities in the two chambers, which forces agreement across party
lines, and serving a single long term with no possibility of reappointment, so
that nobody is auditioning for renewal. Terms are staggered so that no single
government appoints the whole bench. Life tenure, as in the United States,
produces the opposite incentives: vacancies arrive at random, each one is
enormous, and the timing of retirements becomes a strategic decision. Neither
arrangement removes politics from the selection. They distribute it
differently, and the difference shows up in how far a court is willing to get
ahead of the political branches.

## The Objection That Never Goes Away

The standing criticism is that unelected judges overriding elected
legislatures is hard to square with democracy — the counter-majoritarian
difficulty, in the phrase that stuck. The replies are familiar and partial.
One is that courts mainly police the preconditions of democratic politics:
voting, speech, access to the process, the protection of groups the process
reliably ignores. Another is that constitutional review is a kind of insurance
bought by whoever writes a constitution, since every faction knows it may one
day be out of power and wants something standing between it and a hostile
majority — which explains why judicial review tends to be strongest in
societies whose founding bargain was struck among rivals none of whom expected
permanent dominance.

The most practical reply is that courts do not, in the long run, have the last
word. Constitutions get amended. Judges get appointed, and appointment
processes get fought over precisely because everyone understands what is at
stake. Legislatures rewrite statutes to achieve the same end by other means.
Governments slow-walk compliance. Courts that push too far against a settled
public consensus have historically been curbed — by packing threats,
jurisdiction-stripping, budget pressure, or simple disregard. A court has no
soldiers and no revenue. Its judgments are obeyed because officials have
formed the habit of obeying them and expect other officials to be scandalized
if they do not. That habit took a very long time to build and can be spent
much faster than it accumulates.
