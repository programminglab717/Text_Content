# How a Criminal Case Moves Through the Courts

A patrol car pulls in behind a sedan at one in the morning because a taillight
is out. That is a small thing, legally speaking — a traffic infraction, the
kind of violation that produces a written warning and nothing else in most of
the encounters where it occurs. But the stop opens a door. If the officer
smells something, or sees something on the back seat, or the driver answers a
question badly, a sequence begins that can run for years and end in a prison
term. Criminal procedure is the set of rules governing that sequence: not what
conduct is a crime, which is substantive criminal law, but who may do what to
whom along the way, and what happens when someone skips a step.

The rules described here are mostly those of the American system, which is
unusually adversarial and unusually court-centered. Continental systems built
on the inquisitorial model distribute the same functions differently — an
investigating magistrate supervises the file, the trial is less a contest than
a public verification of an investigation already conducted under judicial
oversight — but the underlying problems are shared. Every system has to decide
when the state may intrude on a person, who decides to prosecute, how facts get
tested, and how errors get corrected.

## Suspicion, Cause, and the Moment of Arrest

Police authority escalates in tiers, and each tier requires a stronger factual
basis than the last. An officer may approach anyone and ask questions; that is
a consensual encounter and requires nothing. A brief investigative detention —
a stop — requires reasonable suspicion, meaning specific facts that would lead
a reasonable officer to suspect criminal activity, not a hunch. If the officer
also reasonably suspects the person is armed, a pat-down of the outer clothing
for weapons is permitted. That framework comes from *Terry v. Ohio*, and the
practice still carries the name: a Terry stop.

Arrest requires more. The standard is probable cause: facts sufficient for a
reasonable person to believe a crime has been committed and this person
committed it. It is a lower bar than the trial standard by a wide margin, and
deliberately so, because arrest is a preliminary judgment, not a final one.
Searches follow a parallel logic. The default rule is that a search of a home
requires a warrant issued by a neutral magistrate on a sworn showing of
probable cause and describing the place and items with particularity. The
default has accumulated a long list of exceptions — consent, exigent
circumstances, plain view, search incident to lawful arrest, the reduced
expectation of privacy in a vehicle — and much of the practical work of
criminal litigation consists of arguing about which exception applies.

The remedy for a violation is what gives these rules teeth. Evidence obtained
unconstitutionally is generally inadmissible against the defendant, and
evidence derived from it may be excluded as well under the doctrine usually
called fruit of the poisonous tree. The exclusionary rule is not a
compensation scheme for the person searched; it is a deterrent aimed at police
departments, which is why it has been narrowed where deterrence seems
unlikely to be achieved.

## Charging, the Decision Nobody Watches

Arrest does not begin a prosecution. A prosecutor does. Once a case file
reaches a district attorney's office, someone decides whether to file charges
at all, which charges, and how many. This is the least visible and arguably
most consequential discretion in the entire system. Prosecutors are not
obliged to charge every provable offense, and the choice among overlapping
statutes — a single incident may support a half-dozen chargeable offenses at
different severity levels — shapes everything downstream, including the
sentencing exposure that drives plea negotiation.

For serious offenses, a screening step follows. In the federal system and in
some states, a grand jury reviews the prosecutor's evidence in secret and
returns an indictment if it finds probable cause. Grand juries hear only one
side and almost always indict, which has made them a frequent target of
criticism; their more significant function in practice is investigative, since
they can compel testimony and documents. Other jurisdictions use a preliminary
hearing instead, an adversarial proceeding before a judge where the defense can
cross-examine.

Between arrest and any of this, the defendant appears before a judicial officer
— promptly, usually within a day or two — to be informed of the charges and to
have release conditions set. Bail was originally a device to secure appearance
at trial, and the debate that has grown up around money bail turns on whether
conditioning release on the ability to pay accomplishes that, or simply sorts
defendants by wealth before guilt has been determined.

## The Machinery Before Trial

Most of what happens in a criminal case happens in writing, in a courthouse
nobody is watching. The defense receives discovery: police reports, lab
results, witness statements, in scope varying enormously by jurisdiction.
Layered on top is a constitutional obligation with no equivalent on the defense
side — the prosecution must disclose evidence favorable to the accused and
material to guilt or punishment, a duty derived from *Brady v. Maryland*.
Because the prosecutor decides what is favorable and material, Brady violations
are structurally difficult to detect and are a recurring source of exonerations.

Then come motions. A motion to suppress asks the court to exclude evidence on
the ground that it was obtained in violation of the constitution. A motion to
dismiss attacks the charging document or the sufficiency of the case. Motions
in limine set the boundaries of what the jury will be allowed to hear. A
suppression hearing is often the real trial: if the gun comes in, the case is
over, and if it stays out, the charges usually evaporate.

The right to counsel runs through all of it. *Gideon v. Wainwright* established
that states must provide counsel to defendants who cannot afford it in felony
cases, and the right was later extended to any case carrying actual
imprisonment. Establishing the right proved easier than funding it. Public
defender offices in many places carry caseloads that make meaningful
investigation of each case arithmetically impossible, which is a quiet
structural fact about the system that no doctrine addresses.

## Why Most Cases End Without a Trial

The jury trial is the organizing image of criminal justice and the rare
exception in its actual operation. The great majority of convictions — well
over nine in ten in many jurisdictions — come from guilty pleas. Plea
bargaining is not a loophole; it is the system's ordinary mode. The defendant
trades the trial right for a reduced charge or an agreed sentence
recommendation; the prosecutor trades sentencing exposure for certainty and
docket capacity.

What makes the bargain work, and what makes it uncomfortable, is the same
mechanism. The gap between the sentence offered and the sentence exposed after
a trial conviction — sometimes enormous where mandatory minimums or
consecutive counts are available — supplies the pressure. A judge must confirm
that the plea is knowing and voluntary and supported by a factual basis, and
defendants are entitled to competent advice about the consequences of pleading,
including immigration consequences. But no procedure can fully separate a
rational calculation from a coerced one when the differential is large enough
that an innocent person might reasonably take the deal.

## The Trial and the Long Tail After It

A trial begins with jury selection, where lawyers question prospective jurors
and remove some for cause and some peremptorily, subject to the rule that
peremptory strikes may not be exercised on the basis of race or sex. The
prosecution carries the burden of proving every element beyond a reasonable
doubt; the defendant need prove nothing, may decline to testify, and the jury
may not draw an adverse inference from silence. Witnesses against the accused
must generally testify in person and be subject to cross-examination, which is
what the confrontation right protects and why out-of-court statements are
restricted.

Conviction opens a second proceeding. Sentencing in most systems is guided —
by statutory ranges, by guidelines that translate offense severity and criminal
history into a recommended term, by mandatory minimums that remove judicial
discretion for particular offenses. Judges typically hear from both sides, from
the probation office, and from victims before imposing a term.

Appeal is not a second trial. An appellate court reviews a written record for
legal error, gives deference to the trial court's factual findings, generally
considers only issues that were preserved by objection below, and will affirm
despite error if the error was harmless. A defendant who loses on direct appeal
may still file a collateral challenge — habeas corpus in the American
vocabulary — which is the vehicle for claims that could not be raised on the
record, most commonly ineffective assistance of counsel and newly discovered
evidence. The procedural barriers there are formidable and have grown, on the
theory that at some point judgments must become final.

Which leaves the system with its permanent tension. Every safeguard added at
one stage is a cost imposed somewhere else: delay, expense, cases resolved by
negotiation instead of adjudication. Every safeguard removed buys speed at the
price of a slightly higher chance that the wrong person is standing there when
the sentence is read.
