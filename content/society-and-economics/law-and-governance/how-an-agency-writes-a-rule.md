# How an Agency Writes a Rule

In the 1880s an American farmer shipping grain a hundred miles to a river port
might pay more per ton than a manufacturer shipping freight a thousand miles
between two cities served by competing lines. The short haul cost more than the
long haul, and the reason was structural: on the short route one railroad held
a monopoly, and on the long route several fought for the traffic. Farmers
organized. States passed rate laws and the courts struck them down as
interference with interstate commerce. Congress responded in 1887 by creating
the Interstate Commerce Commission, a body with a mandate to require that rates
be just and reasonable and with the authority to investigate, hold hearings,
and issue orders.

That combination was the novelty. Congress did not attempt to write a national
freight tariff into statute — it could not have, since the information changed
weekly and the expertise sat with people who spent their careers in rail
economics. It created an institution, gave it a standard, and delegated the
job of filling the standard in. Nearly every regulatory agency since has been
built on the same template: a legislature writes an objective it can articulate
but cannot operationalize, and hands the operationalizing to a body with
technical staff and continuous jurisdiction.

## Why Legislatures Delegate

The delegation is not laziness. A legislature has a few hundred members, meets
in sessions, votes on everything from defense procurement to postal rates, and
possesses no laboratory. Deciding the permissible concentration of a solvent in
workplace air requires toxicology, exposure modeling, and engineering estimates
of what controls are achievable at what cost. Deciding whether a drug may be
sold requires reading clinical trial data. Deciding the separation standard
between aircraft on approach requires knowing the performance envelope of the
equipment in service this year.

Delegation also buys continuity. A statute passed once must govern conditions
nobody anticipated; an agency can revise a rule when the facts change without
requiring a new majority in a legislature. That is the great advantage and the
great objection in a single sentence, because the officials doing the revising
are usually not elected, and the rules they issue carry the force of law.

Legislatures manage the tension by constraining the grant. A statute typically
specifies the subject matter, sets a standard the agency must meet, prescribes
procedures, and identifies what a reviewing court may examine. Agencies operate
only within that grant. An agency with authority over air pollutants cannot
regulate mortgage disclosure, however convinced it may be that mortgage
disclosure needs regulating, and an action outside the statutory grant is void
regardless of its merits.

## Notice and Comment

The procedural spine of modern rulemaking, in the American system, comes from
the Administrative Procedure Act of 1946, which was a settlement of a
constitutional argument. The New Deal had produced agencies faster than anyone
had produced rules for agencies, and the APA imposed a common procedure on all
of them.

It begins with a decision to act — prompted by new legislation, by a court
order, by an incident, by a petition from an outside party, or by an internal
review finding an old rule obsolete. Staff assemble a record: technical
studies, industry data, inspection findings, economic modeling. In the American
federal system, an economically significant proposal then goes to a review
office within the executive branch, which examines it against the
administration's priorities and its cost-benefit analysis before it may be
published.

The proposal appears in the government gazette — in the United States, the
Federal Register — as a notice of proposed rulemaking. This is not a press
release. It states the statutory authority, describes the problem, explains the
agency's reasoning, lays out the regulatory text, and frequently asks specific
questions about alternatives the agency is considering. A comment period opens,
usually running one to three months, and anyone may file: trade associations,
labor unions, state governments, academics, affected companies, individual
citizens. On contested rules the docket can run to tens or hundreds of
thousands of submissions, though volume and influence correlate poorly — a
single technically detailed comment from someone who understands the equipment
can reshape a rule that a hundred thousand form letters do not touch.

Then comes the part that surprises people. The agency must respond. When it
issues the final rule, it publishes a preamble that addresses the significant
comments, explains why it accepted some and rejected others, and justifies the
choices it made. This preamble is often vastly longer than the rule itself,
sometimes hundreds of pages for a few paragraphs of regulatory text, and it
exists because it is the record on which a court will later judge whether the
agency acted reasonably. A rule that ignores a serious objection in the docket
is vulnerable. A rule that changes substantially from proposal to final without
adequate notice can be sent back on the ground that the public never had a
chance to comment on what was actually adopted.

The final rule is published with an effective date, sometimes with a compliance
schedule stretching years for capital-intensive requirements, and it enters the
codified body of regulations, where it sits alongside decades of accumulated
rules from the same agency.

## Enforcement, and the Agency as Court

Writing the rule is half the job. Agencies also enforce, and this is where they
acquire the shape that makes lawyers uneasy, because the same institution
investigates, charges, and adjudicates.

Enforcement runs through inspections, audits, required reporting, and
complaints. When an agency finds a violation it typically has a ladder of
responses: a warning letter, a demand for a corrective plan, a civil penalty, a
license suspension, an order to cease. Contested penalties are heard in the
first instance by an administrative law judge, an official inside the agency
who is insulated from its enforcement staff and from ordinary removal. The
agency head can often review the judge's decision, and the losing party can
then appeal into the ordinary courts.

A great deal of regulation also operates below the level of binding rules
altogether. Agencies issue guidance documents, interpretive bulletins,
enforcement priorities, and advisory opinions that are formally non-binding but
practically decisive, because a regulated firm rarely wants to test whether its
regulator meant it. This shadow body of quasi-law is fast, flexible, and
largely escapes both notice-and-comment procedure and judicial review — which
is exactly why it is contested.

## Who Watches the Watchers

Four constraints operate on agencies, and all four are imperfect.

Courts review. A challenger can argue that a rule exceeded statutory authority,
that the agency skipped a required procedure, or that the rule was arbitrary
and capricious — meaning the agency failed to consider an important aspect of
the problem, relied on factors the statute did not permit, or offered an
explanation that runs counter to the evidence. For forty years American courts
also deferred to an agency's reasonable interpretation of an ambiguous statute
it administered, the doctrine associated with the Chevron decision; the Supreme
Court overruled that framework in 2024, shifting more interpretive authority
back to judges. The practical consequences are still working themselves out,
but the direction is toward more litigation over what statutes mean and less
room for an agency to change course by reinterpretation.

Legislatures oversee. They hold hearings, demand documents, confirm senior
appointees, and — the sharpest tool — set budgets. An agency whose enforcement
division is not funded cannot enforce. Legislatures can also amend the
authorizing statute, and in some systems can pass a resolution disapproving a
recently issued rule outright.

The executive controls. Agency heads are appointed, priorities are set
centrally, and the review of proposed rules before publication gives the
political leadership a check on the technical staff. Some agencies are
structured as multimember commissions with staggered terms and party-balance
requirements specifically to blunt this.

And the public participates, unevenly. The comment docket is open to everyone
and read mostly by those with the resources to write usefully.

## Capture, Ossification, and the Trade

Two failure modes recur. The first is capture: the regulator's staff and the
regulated industry share an information base, a professional network, and a
career path in both directions, and over time the agency's view of the problem
converges on the industry's view. Capture rarely looks like corruption. It
looks like an agency that has internalized what is feasible from the standpoint
of the firms it oversees.

The second is ossification, the opposite pathology. Each procedural safeguard
added to guard against arbitrary regulation raises the cost of regulating at
all. When a significant rule takes the better part of a decade to issue and
must survive a hostile record and near-certain litigation, agencies start
choosing the fights they can win rather than the problems that most need
addressing, and they migrate toward guidance and enforcement discretion, where
the procedural burden is lighter and the accountability thinner.

Both failures trace back to the same underlying bargain the farmers of the
1880s struck without quite knowing it: expertise and speed in exchange for a
certain distance from the ballot box. Every procedural reform since has been an
attempt to adjust the exchange rate.
