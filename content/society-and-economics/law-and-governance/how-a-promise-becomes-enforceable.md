# How a Promise Becomes Enforceable

You bought a coffee this morning and formed a contract. Offer, acceptance,
exchange of value, complete performance on both sides — the whole transaction
born and extinguished in forty seconds, with no writing, no lawyer and no
thought given to any of it. This is the normal condition of contract law. It
governs an enormous share of daily life and is almost never noticed, because
noticing it means something has gone wrong.

The interesting question is not why the coffee transaction works. It is why
some promises attract the machinery of the state and others do not. A friend
who agrees to help you move on Saturday and then does not show up has broken a
promise as real as any commercial one, and the law will do nothing. A supplier
who agrees to deliver steel in March and does not will face a bill. Somewhere
between the two the law draws a line, and the shape of that line is most of
what contract doctrine is about.

## What Turns a Promise Into an Obligation

The classic common law answer is that a promise binds when it was bought.
Consideration — something given in exchange, a payment, a counter-promise, a
forbearance from doing something you were entitled to do — marks the promise
as part of a bargain rather than a favour. The courts have never required the
exchange to be fair; a nominal sum is enough, which is why old contracts
recite a peppercorn or a dollar. What consideration screens out is the purely
gratuitous promise, on the theory that a person who gives without getting has
not committed in the way a trader has. The doctrine produces awkward results
often enough that two escape hatches exist alongside it: a promise made in a
formal deed binds without consideration, and a promise that the recipient
reasonably relied on to their detriment may bind through estoppel even though
nothing was exchanged.

Continental systems never adopted consideration and do not miss it. Serious
gratuitous promises can be enforced, typically if they are made in a required
form — before a notary, in writing — which performs the same cautionary
function as the bargain requirement by making the promisor stop and think. The
civilian tradition asked instead about *cause*: whether the obligation had a
proper reason behind it, a concept broad enough to cover gifts and narrow
enough to exclude commitments made for illegal or nonexistent purposes.

Formation itself is assessed objectively, and this matters more than it
sounds. The law does not ask what a party privately intended; it asks what a
reasonable person in the other party's position would have understood from the
words and conduct. If you sign a document you did not read, you are generally
bound by it. If you make an offer as a joke that nobody could reasonably take
as a joke, you have made an offer. Contract law is built for a world where
people must be able to rely on appearances, because they cannot see inside
each other's heads.

## Nobody Writes a Complete Contract

Every agreement is unfinished. Parties cannot anticipate every contingency,
and it would be wasteful to try — the cost of negotiating a clause about an
unlikely event usually exceeds the expected cost of the event. So contract law
supplies defaults: rules that apply unless the parties say otherwise. Sale of
goods legislation says when risk passes, what quality is implied, how long a
buyer has to reject. Courts imply terms from trade usage, from the parties'
prior dealings, and occasionally from necessity, where an agreement would make
no sense without them. A separate, much smaller category of rules is
mandatory, applying whatever the contract says, which is where consumer
protection and employment law mostly live.

Interpretation is where most commercial disputes actually sit. Systems differ
in how far they will look beyond the words. English courts lean textual, with
a parol evidence rule limiting the use of prior negotiations and entire
agreement clauses reinforcing it; American practice varies by state and is
generally more willing to admit context; German and French courts start from
the parties' real common intention and treat the text as strong evidence of it
rather than the whole of it. Underneath sits good faith, which continental
codes state as a general obligation in performance and which English law has
long refused to adopt in that open-ended form, preferring targeted doctrines
and, more recently, implying cooperative duties into long-term relational
contracts.

Then there is the problem that most contracts are not negotiated at all. The
terms attached to a software licence, an insurance policy, a parking garage
ticket or a mobile phone plan are drafted by one side and presented to the
other on a take-it-or-leave-it basis, and essentially nobody reads them. The
legal response has been a set of counterweights rather than a rejection of the
practice: unusually onerous terms must be brought fairly to the other party's
attention, ambiguity is construed against the drafter, and statutes in most
developed countries let courts strike unfair terms in consumer contracts
outright.

## When Consent Was Not What It Looked Like

A contract can be formed and still be vulnerable. Misrepresentation — a false
statement of fact that induced the agreement — lets the deceived party unwind
it, with the remedy depending on whether the lie was fraudulent, careless or
innocent. Duress covers threats, including in many systems economic threats,
such as refusing to perform an existing contract unless paid more at a moment
when the other side has no alternative. Undue influence addresses relationships
of trust and dependence where pressure need not be explicit. Mistake is
narrower than laypeople expect: a shared and fundamental error about the
subject matter may void an agreement, but a party who simply misjudged the
market gets no relief. Incapacity protects minors and people unable to
understand what they are doing. Illegality bars enforcement of agreements the
law will not countenance at all.

Unconscionability draws these threads together in some systems, asking both
how the bargain was made and how one-sided it turned out. Courts are
cautious with it, because a doctrine that lets judges rewrite bad deals
undermines the point of having deals.

Separately, events after formation can excuse performance. If the thing
contracted for is destroyed, or the purpose is defeated, or performance
becomes legally impossible, common law doctrines of frustration and
impracticability may discharge the contract — though the bar is high, and mere
expense is not enough. German law has long recognized relief when the
foundation of a transaction collapses, and France added a doctrine for
radically changed circumstances in its modern reform of the code. Most
commercial parties do not rely on any of this and write their own force
majeure clause instead.

## What a Broken Promise Costs

The default remedy in common law systems is money, measured by the
expectation interest: enough to put the injured party in the position
performance would have produced, which is why the buyer of undelivered steel
recovers the difference between the contract price and the cost of covering
elsewhere. That measure is bounded in several directions. Losses too remote to
have been within the parties' contemplation are not recoverable, a limit
settled in the nineteenth-century case of a mill whose broken crankshaft was
delayed by a carrier who had not been told the mill was idle without it. A
claimant must take reasonable steps to mitigate. Punitive damages are almost
never available for breach alone, and clauses fixing damages in advance are
enforced only if they are a genuine attempt to estimate loss rather than a
penalty designed to frighten.

Civil law systems invert the priority. There, ordering the defaulting party to
actually perform is the natural response, and damages are the fallback; in
common law systems specific performance is exceptional, reserved for land and
genuinely unique goods, on the reasoning that courts should not be supervising
unwilling performers when money will do. That difference feeds an old argument
about efficient breach — the idea that a party who can profit by breaking a
contract and paying full compensation should be free to do so, since the
promisee ends up no worse off and resources move to higher-value uses. Critics
answer that it treats a promise as a priced option and ignores that being
compensated after a lawsuit is not the same experience as being performed to.

The critics have the better of it empirically, because damages systematically
undercompensate. Litigation costs money, takes years, and produces sums that
rarely account for the disruption. Which is why most contracts are not
enforced by courts at all. Firms perform because they want repeat business,
because their reputation circulates in a small industry, because the person
across the table will be across the table again next year. Studies of business
practice have found repeatedly that managers negotiate settlements with
reference to the relationship rather than the agreement, and that many firms
will cheerfully do substantial business on a purchase order nobody has read
closely. Contract law's most important function may be to sit in the
background as the thing neither side wants to resort to.
