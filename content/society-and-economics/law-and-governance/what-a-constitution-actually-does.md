# What a Constitution Actually Does

A constitution is a piece of paper, and pieces of paper have no army. Nothing
in the text of any founding document physically prevents a president from
staying past the end of a term, a legislature from cancelling an election, or
a police force from searching a house without a warrant. James Madison, who
knew this perfectly well, worried in print about the weakness of what he
called parchment barriers — written guarantees that depend for their force on
the willingness of powerful people to be bound by words. Two centuries later,
the world is full of constitutions that are ignored and a smaller number that
are obeyed, and the difference between the two categories is not mainly a
matter of drafting quality.

Which raises the question of what the document is actually for, given that it
cannot enforce itself.

## Rules About How Rules Get Made

The first job is constitutive, in the plain sense of the word: the document
brings offices into existence and says who may speak in the name of the state.
Before it, there is no such thing as a Senate, or a Chancellor, or a Supreme
Court — there are only people and armies and habits. Afterward there is an
office with defined powers, occupied under defined conditions, and a procedure
for handing it to somebody else. The constitution answers questions that
cannot be answered by ordinary legislation without circularity, because
ordinary legislation has to be passed by somebody, and something must already
have established who that somebody is.

From this follows the idea of higher law. If the legislature exists by virtue
of the constitution, and holds only the powers the constitution grants, then a
statute exceeding those powers is not a bad law but, strictly speaking, not a
law at all. The hierarchy has teeth only if some institution is willing to say
so out loud, which is a separate problem, but the logic is what gives
constitutional argument its distinctive shape. Almost every constitutional
dispute is at bottom about authority: not whether a policy is wise, but
whether this body was entitled to adopt it by this procedure.

## Written, Unwritten, and the Space Between

The United States produced the first durable single-document constitution, and
its success made codification look like the normal form. It is common but not
universal. The United Kingdom has no such document; its constitutional
arrangements are scattered across statutes of very different ages — Magna
Carta, the Bill of Rights of 1689, the Acts of Union, the Parliament Acts, the
devolution settlements, the Human Rights Act — along with common law
principles developed by judges and a thick layer of convention. New Zealand is
similar. Israel has never completed a constitution and operates through a
series of Basic Laws passed over decades.

Conventions are the strange and underrated part. These are binding rules that
no court will enforce: that the monarch grants assent to bills passed by
Parliament, that a prime minister who loses the confidence of the Commons
resigns, that ministers accept collective responsibility. They are obeyed
because the political cost of breaking them is severe, and because the actors
involved understand that the alternative is a crisis nobody can control. Every
system has them, codified or not. The American document says nothing about
political parties, presidential primaries, the cabinet, the filibuster, the
size of the Supreme Court, or the practice of presidents not seeking a third
term — which was convention for a century and a half before an amendment made
it law. A written constitution is always shorter than the constitutional order
it anchors.

Constitutions also differ wildly in length and ambition. Some are terse
frameworks that describe institutions and stop; others run to hundreds of
articles and include commitments about education, health, labour, language and
the environment. India's includes a set of directive principles that are
explicitly declared non-justiciable — goals the state is instructed to pursue
but which no citizen can sue to enforce. South Africa took the opposite
approach with several social rights, and its courts have enforced them in a
measured way, ordering governments to produce reasonable plans rather than
ordering particular outcomes. The drafting choice is a real one: writing a
programme into the constitution commits future majorities and gives advocates
a lever, but it also risks devaluing the document if large parts of it are
visibly not honoured.

## Entrenchment, and the Cost of Rigidity

The technical feature that distinguishes a constitution from an ordinary
statute is that it is harder to change. Amendment rules are therefore the most
consequential clauses in any such document, and they vary enormously. The
American rule requires supermajorities in Congress plus ratification by
three-quarters of the states, a bar so high that the document has been amended
only a handful of times outside the founding era and the aftermath of the
Civil War. Ireland requires a referendum for every change, which makes
amendment frequent, public and occasionally dramatic. Australia requires a
national majority plus majorities in a majority of states, and most proposals
fail. India's constitution is long, detailed and amended often, yet its Supreme
Court has held that certain features form a basic structure that amendment
cannot destroy. Germany goes further and puts the point in the text itself:
human dignity, the federal structure and the democratic and social character
of the state are placed beyond the reach of amendment altogether.

Rigidity has a price, and the price is usually paid in interpretation. When
formal amendment is effectively unavailable, pressure moves to whoever
construes the text. American constitutional law has absorbed enormous change —
the scope of federal commerce power, the reach of the equality guarantee, the
application of the Bill of Rights to the states — through judicial reading
rather than textual revision, which is one reason appointments to the court
became a central political prize. Flexible constitutions face the mirror-image
risk: if a governing majority can rewrite the rules of the game with the same
majority it uses to pass a budget, the constitution constrains nobody.

How a constitution gets written shapes what it can later do. Some emerge from
elected constituent assemblies convened for the purpose and dissolved
afterward. Some are negotiated settlements ending a conflict, in which each
side's main concern is protection against the other, and the text reads like a
peace treaty because it is one. Some are imposed by an occupying power, as in
Japan and West Germany after 1945 — an origin that critics never stop raising
and that has not, in either case, prevented the documents from becoming
genuinely owned. Some are drafted behind closed doors by a small group and
ratified in a referendum with no real alternative on offer. Processes that
include the eventual losers, and that take long enough for bargains to be
struck rather than dictated, tend to produce arrangements the losers will
still accept ten years later; processes run by a single faction in a hurry
tend to produce documents that last exactly as long as that faction does.

## Structure Does More Work Than Rights

Bills of rights get the attention. Structural provisions do most of the
protecting. Whether a country has an independent judiciary, whether the
legislature controls the budget, whether elections are administered by
somebody other than the incumbent, whether there is a second chamber or a
federal layer with resources of its own, whether emergency powers expire
automatically — these determine whether the rights clauses will ever be
honoured. The rights-listing part of a constitution is a promise; the
structural part decides who will be in a position to insist on it.

The distinction between parliamentary and presidential systems belongs here
too, and it is less about dignity than about what happens when the executive
and the legislature disagree. A parliamentary system resolves the deadlock
structurally: the government falls, or parliament is dissolved, and there is
an election. A presidential system has fixed terms and no such valve, so a
president facing a hostile legislature simply serves out the term, which
concentrates attention on the outer limits of executive power — decrees,
vetoes, emergency declarations, prosecutorial control — because those are
where the conflict goes when it cannot be resolved by a vote of confidence.

Federalism is a related structural bet. Dividing power between levels creates
a second set of officials with their own electorates and their own reasons to
resist central overreach, at the cost of duplication, legal complexity and the
permanent difficulty of deciding which level may act. Almost all large,
diverse countries adopt some version of it, and almost all of them spend their
constitutional litigation budget on boundary disputes between levels.

## Why Anyone Complies

Back to Madison's problem. The answer that has held up best is that a
constitution works largely as a coordination device. Any regime can be
overthrown if enough of the people who staff it — soldiers, civil servants,
judges, tax collectors, provincial governors — decline to cooperate, but each
of them will resist only if confident that others will too, and that
confidence requires a shared signal. A constitution provides one. It makes
certain acts publicly and unambiguously violations, so that when a leader
crosses the line, opponents who otherwise distrust one another can recognize
the same moment as the moment. Vague or endlessly reinterpretable rules
destroy that function, which is part of why bright-line provisions about terms
of office and election timing matter out of proportion to their intellectual
interest.

Courts help, when they are strong enough, but they are downstream of the same
dynamic: a court order is obeyed because officials expect other officials to
treat defiance as intolerable. Where that expectation has not been built, the
document sits in the archive, formally in force, describing a country that
does not exist. Comparative research on constitutions around the world has
found most of them to be surprisingly short-lived — the median national
constitution is replaced within a generation or two, often after a coup, a war
or a change of regime, and the ones that endure tend to be those flexible
enough to be amended, specific enough to be enforced, and inclusive enough
that losing an election does not feel like losing everything.

That last condition is the one drafters can least control. A constitution is
in part a bargain among people who expect to alternate in power, and its
stability depends on each side continuing to believe that it will get another
turn. When that belief goes, no amendment formula, however clever, holds the
arrangement together.
