# What Makes International Law Binding

A diplomat walks through an airport carrying a sealed bag, and the customs
officer does not open it. Nothing physical prevents the officer from opening
it. There is no world police force standing behind that bag, no supranational
sheriff who will arrive if the seal is broken. And yet the bag goes through,
in Lagos and Lima and Lisbon, essentially every day, essentially without
incident. This is worth sitting with, because the usual objection to
international law is that it cannot be real law since nobody enforces it. The
diplomatic pouch suggests the objection is at least incomplete.

The skeptical position has a distinguished pedigree. John Austin, writing in
the nineteenth century, defined law as the command of a sovereign backed by
sanction, and concluded that the law of nations was not law but "positive
morality" — a set of conventions states observed out of convenience. Since
there is no sovereign above states, there can be no law binding them. The
argument is clean. It also fails to describe what practitioners actually
observe, which is a dense, technical, heavily litigated body of rules that
governments spend enormous resources complying with, arguing about, and
occasionally violating in ways they take pains to justify in legal terms.

## Where the Rules Come From

International law has no legislature, so its sources are unusual. The standard
enumeration, drawn from the statute of the International Court of Justice,
lists treaties, custom, and general principles of law common to national legal
systems, with judicial decisions and scholarly writing as subsidiary means of
determining what the rules are.

Treaties are the easy case. A treaty is a written agreement between states,
governed by its own meta-law — the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties,
which sets out how treaties are concluded, interpreted, reserved to, suspended,
and terminated. The governing maxim is ancient: *pacta sunt servanda*,
agreements must be kept. Treaty obligations bind only parties, which is why
the map of international law is patchy rather than uniform; a rule that
applies to eighty states may not apply to the ninety-first.

Custom is stranger and more interesting. A customary rule arises when states
generally behave in a certain way and do so out of a sense of legal obligation
— the two elements being state practice and *opinio juris*. The second is what
separates law from habit. States exchange ambassadors as a matter of
convenience and also refrain from arresting them because they believe they are
legally required not to; only the second attitude generates a rule. Custom
binds states that never signed anything, which makes it powerful and also
contested, since proving what states believe is an exercise in reading
diplomatic notes, domestic statutes, votes in international bodies, and
official protests.

A small set of customary rules has been elevated to *jus cogens*: peremptory
norms from which no derogation is permitted, and which override contrary
treaties. The prohibitions on genocide, slavery, torture, and aggressive war
are the standard examples. Two states may not validly agree between themselves
to conduct a slave trade. That is a remarkable claim for a system supposedly
built entirely on consent, and it marks the point at which international law
asserts that some obligations are owed to the international community as a
whole rather than to particular counterparties.

## The Enforcement Problem, Reconsidered

There is no global police force. What exists instead is a set of decentralized
mechanisms that turn out to do a substantial amount of work.

The first is reciprocity. Most international law regulates relationships that
recur. A state that mistreats foreign diplomats can expect its own diplomats
to be mistreated; a state that ignores the rules on airspace notification will
find its own aircraft unwelcome. Where the interaction is continuous and
mutual, defection is expensive in a way that needs no external enforcer.

The second is reputation, which is not a soft consideration in a world where
capital, trade access, and alliance membership are allocated partly on
assessments of reliability. A government that treats its treaty commitments as
provisional discovers that the price of its next commitment rises.

The third, and quietly the most important, is domestic incorporation. Most
international law is enforced not by international bodies but by ordinary
national institutions. Customs officers apply tariff schedules negotiated in
Geneva. National courts apply extradition treaties and the conventions
governing cross-border child custody and the recognition of arbitral awards.
Central banks apply capital standards agreed in Basel. Aviation authorities
apply technical annexes drafted by an international organization in Montreal.
Once an obligation has been translated into domestic law, it is enforced by the
same police, judges, and inspectors who enforce everything else, and the
absence of a world sheriff stops mattering.

States differ in how that translation happens. Monist systems treat ratified
treaties as directly part of domestic law; dualist systems require implementing
legislation before a treaty has any effect on a citizen. The distinction
determines whether an individual can walk into a national court and invoke a
convention directly.

## Courts That Nobody Has to Attend

International adjudication is real but consent-based, which visitors from
domestic legal systems find disorienting. The International Court of Justice
resolves disputes between states, and its jurisdiction depends on the parties
having accepted it — through a clause in the treaty at issue, a special
agreement, or a standing declaration that may be hedged with reservations. A
state cannot simply be hauled in. When the ICJ does rule, compliance is
generally good, in part because states that agreed to litigate have already
decided to accept the outcome.

Other bodies vary. The International Criminal Court prosecutes individuals
rather than states for a defined set of grave offenses and operates on
complementarity: it acts only where national systems are unwilling or unable to
prosecute genuinely. Several major powers are not parties, which limits its
reach and fuels the argument that its docket is skewed. Regional human rights
courts, most notably in Europe, exercise a far more intrusive jurisdiction —
individuals can petition directly, and judgments regularly require states to
change legislation. Investor-state arbitration lets a foreign investor sue a
host government before a tribunal under a treaty the investor never signed, an
arrangement that has generated both large awards and substantial political
backlash. The World Trade Organization built the most legalized dispute system
of all, with binding panels and authorized retaliation for non-compliance, and
then demonstrated its fragility when the appellate stage lost its quorum
because member states declined to appoint judges.

## Where It Holds and Where It Snaps

The honest picture is one of sharp asymmetry rather than uniform weakness.

In the technical domains, compliance is close to total and nobody discusses
it. Mail crosses borders under the Universal Postal Union. Radio spectrum is
allocated so that broadcasts do not collide. Ships are registered, flagged, and
inspected under conventions on safety of life at sea. Aircraft separation
standards, undersea cable protection, the delimitation of territorial waters
and exclusive economic zones under the law of the sea, the mutual recognition
of patents — these function as reliable infrastructure. They are unglamorous
precisely because they work.

At the other end sit the rules on the use of force, and this is where the
system earns its reputation for impotence. The United Nations Charter prohibits
the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity of any state, with
narrow exceptions for self-defense against armed attack and for action
authorized by the Security Council. But the Council's permanent members hold
vetoes, which means that enforcement is structurally unavailable against them
or their close partners. The result is a rule that is violated conspicuously
and irregularly, and a body of doctrine that mostly operates as a vocabulary of
justification.

That vocabulary is not nothing. States that invade do not say they are
indifferent to the Charter; they construct legal arguments — anticipatory
self-defense, intervention by invitation, protection of nationals, humanitarian
necessity — that are frequently unconvincing but are always made. A system in
which even the violators argue in its terms is exercising some constraint on
what can be openly claimed, and constraints on claims eventually constrain
conduct, because arguments have to be defended in front of allies, parliaments,
and domestic courts.

The law of armed conflict occupies a middle position. The Geneva Conventions
and their protocols govern treatment of prisoners, civilians, and the wounded,
and they are violated in every war and also taught in every professional
military, embedded in rules of engagement and targeting procedures, and
enforced through courts-martial far more often than through international
tribunals. The Nuremberg and Tokyo trials after the Second World War
established the principle that individuals, not only states, can be held
criminally responsible for acts of state, and that following superior orders is
not a complete defense. That principle has been applied unevenly since. It has
also never been retracted.

International law, then, is not a weak version of domestic law waiting for a
world government to complete it. It is a different kind of system: horizontal
rather than hierarchical, built out of mutual commitment among entities that
recognize no superior, enforced mostly by the parties themselves and by their
own institutions. It works best where interests recur and reciprocity bites,
and worst where a state's survival or a great power's ambition is on the table
— which is to say, it works in inverse proportion to how much attention it
receives.
