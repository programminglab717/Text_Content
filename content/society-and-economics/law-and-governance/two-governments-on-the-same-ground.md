# Two Governments on the Same Ground

Stand at an intersection in a mid-sized American city and count the governments
with authority over that square of asphalt. The city paves it and sets the
speed limit. The county may run the sheriff's patrol and the property
assessment. The state licenses the driver, defines the traffic offenses, and
pays for the arterial road two blocks over. The federal government funded part
of the interstate that the arterial feeds, sets the emissions standard for
every vehicle crossing the intersection, and enforces the civil rights statutes
that constrain how the city polices it. Add a school district with its own
elected board and taxing power, a transit authority, possibly a water district
with boundaries that match nothing else. Half a dozen sovereigns and
quasi-sovereigns, overlapping on one piece of ground, each with its own
revenue, its own officials, and its own claim on the same citizen.

That is federalism in its practical form, and it is stranger than it looks.
Most of the world's states are unitary: a single national government holds
sovereignty, and whatever regional or local authorities exist do so at its
pleasure, exercising powers that can be expanded, contracted, or abolished by
ordinary legislation. France, Japan, and Sweden all devolve substantial
administration downward without any level below the national one possessing
independent constitutional standing. Federations are the minority, perhaps two
dozen of them, but they include some of the largest and most diverse countries
on earth — the United States, India, Brazil, Germany, Canada, Australia,
Nigeria, Mexico — and together they govern a large share of humanity.

## Coming Together and Holding Together

Federations arrive by two roughly opposite routes, and the route shapes
everything afterward.

Some are formed by aggregation. Separate polities that already existed decided
to pool sovereignty while keeping a share of it: the American states under the
Constitution of 1787, the Swiss cantons, the Australian colonies at
federation, the German states under the empire and again under the postwar
Basic Law. In these cases the constituent units predate the center and their
powers are understood as retained rather than granted. The residual authority
— everything not expressly assigned — stays with the units, and the center's
powers are enumerated.

Others are formed by devolution, usually because a large and diverse state
concluded that holding together required conceding real autonomy to regions
that might otherwise leave. India built a federation on the framework of a
centralized colonial administration and reorganized its states along linguistic
lines in the decades after independence. Belgium transformed itself
incrementally from a unitary kingdom into one of the most decentralized
federations in Europe as the Flemish–Walloon divide sharpened. Spain, without
adopting the label, created autonomous communities with asymmetric powers, so
that the Basque Country and Catalonia hold competences that other regions do
not. In holding-together federations the residual power typically stays at the
center, and the regions have enumerated competences.

The two designs behave differently under stress. Aggregative federations tend
to argue about whether the center has overstepped its list. Devolved
federations tend to argue about whether regional competences are being hollowed
out, and their disputes more often carry the language of national identity.

## How the Powers Get Split

Every federal constitution has to answer the same questions, and there are only
a few families of answers.

The first is the division of subject matter. Defense, currency, and foreign
affairs go to the center almost universally. Education, policing, land use, and
family law usually sit with the units, though rarely exclusively. Between these
lies a large zone of concurrent authority where both levels may legislate, and
where a supremacy rule determines the outcome of a conflict — the center's
valid law prevails, and the unit's inconsistent law is displaced to the extent
of the inconsistency.

The second question is who administers. Here the American and German models
diverge sharply. The United States operates two parallel bureaucracies: federal
law is largely carried out by federal officials in federal buildings, and state
law by state officials. Germany does something different and, to American eyes,
counterintuitive: most federal legislation is executed by the Länder, using
their own civil servants. Berlin writes a great deal of the law; the states run
most of the administration. This produces a federation that is legislatively
centralized and administratively decentralized, and it means the states have
enormous practical leverage over how national policy actually lands.

The third question is what the center may demand of the units. American
doctrine has developed a firm answer in one direction: the federal government
may regulate individuals directly and may attach conditions to money it offers,
but it may not commandeer state legislatures into enacting a federal program or
conscript state officials into administering one. The anti-commandeering
principle emerged from decisions in the 1990s and has since been invoked across
the political spectrum, since a state that cannot be forced to help enforce one
federal policy also cannot be forced to help enforce another.

## The Money

Formal competences describe far less of real federal practice than the
financing does.

Nearly every federation runs a vertical fiscal imbalance: the center collects
the most productive taxes — income, corporate, value-added — because they are
easiest to administer nationally and because bases that move across borders are
hard for subnational units to tax. Meanwhile the units carry the expensive
obligations, above all health, education, and social services. The gap is
closed by transfers, and the terms of the transfers become a second
constitution that nobody wrote.

Unconditional or block transfers preserve autonomy. Conditional grants do not;
they buy compliance. A center with no constitutional power over a subject can
often achieve its aims by offering money for programs that meet its
specifications, and a unit that refuses forgoes revenue its taxpayers have
already contributed. American courts have policed the edges of this — the
condition must be stated clearly enough for states to choose knowingly, must
relate to the purpose of the spending, and at some point becomes compulsion
rather than inducement — but the spending power remains the most effective tool
the center has for reaching into areas otherwise closed to it.

Federations also differ on equalization: whether wealthier units subsidize
poorer ones so that comparable services can be provided at comparable tax
rates. Canada, Germany, and Australia operate substantial explicit equalization
schemes, and in each the formula is a chronic political sore point. The United
States has no general equalization program, achieving a partial and unplanned
version through the distributional effects of federal programs and taxes.

## The Umpire, and the Second Chamber

Disputes between levels need a referee, and in most federations it is a court —
a supreme or constitutional court with authority to decide whether a law falls
within the enacting level's competence. Courts in this role are not neutral
instruments; their doctrines move over time, and the history of any federation
can be written as a sequence of judicial expansions and contractions of central
power. American constitutional history turns substantially on the reach of the
commerce clause, which was read narrowly, then very broadly from the late
1930s, and then somewhat more restrictively again from the 1990s.

The other structural safeguard is representation of the units at the center,
usually through a second legislative chamber. The designs vary in ways that
matter enormously. The American Senate gives every state equal representation
regardless of population, with members directly elected and functionally
independent of state governments. The German Bundesrat is composed of delegates
of the Land governments themselves, voting in instructed blocs with weights
that partially reflect population; because state governments sit in it
directly, the states bargain with the federation as governments rather than as
electorates. Where a second chamber is weak or its members owe nothing to
regional authorities, the formal federal structure tends to erode toward
practical centralization.

## What It Buys and What It Costs

The arguments for federalism are well worn and mostly sound. Policy can be
matched to local preference in large, heterogeneous countries. Units can
experiment, and successful experiments propagate — the phrase about states as
laboratories comes from a dissent by Justice Brandeis and has outlived the case
it was written for. Power is divided, which complicates its capture. Regions
with distinct languages or histories can govern themselves in matters closest
to identity without seceding.

The costs are equally real and less often stated plainly. Variation in
provision means that where a child lives determines the quality of their
schooling and the coverage of their family's medical care. Units competing for
mobile capital face pressure to lower taxes and weaken regulation, and the
resulting race is not always to the top. Problems that cross boundaries —
river pollution, pandemic response, a regional labor market — fall between
jurisdictions and require coordination that nobody is in charge of producing.
And the division of responsibility fogs accountability: when a service fails,
each level can plausibly point at another, and voters trying to assign blame
face a genuinely difficult research problem.

That fog is not a bug that better design will eliminate. It is the direct
consequence of the arrangement's central promise, which is that no single
government gets to decide everything.
