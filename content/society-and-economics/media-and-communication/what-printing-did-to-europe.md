# What Printing Did to Europe

The object that changed everything was not the press. Screw presses had been
squeezing olives and wine grapes and pressing linen for centuries, and the
Rhineland workshops that adapted one for paper did not have to invent anything
to do it. The genuinely hard part of Johannes Gutenberg's achievement was much
smaller and easier to overlook: a hand mould that could cast a piece of type
with the same body width and the same height-to-paper as the thousand pieces
cast before it, out of an alloy that shrank predictably as it cooled and did
not deform under the pressure of a few hundred impressions. Gutenberg was
trained as a goldsmith. The Bible he printed at Mainz around the middle of the
1450s is remembered as a book, but its real content was a manufacturing
process: punch, matrix, mould, interchangeable parts, tolerances measured in
fractions of a millimetre.

None of the components were unprecedented on their own. China had printed from
carved wood blocks for centuries and Bi Sheng had experimented with ceramic
movable type in the eleventh century. Korean foundries were casting metal type
generations before Mainz; the Buddhist text known as the Jikji was printed that
way in the 1370s. What Europe supplied was a set of conditions that made the
technique pay: an alphabet of a few dozen characters rather than thousands of
logographs, a cheap substrate in the form of rag paper whose mills had been
spreading north from Italy and Spain, an existing commercial book trade around
the universities, and an oil-based ink borrowed from painters that would cling
to metal instead of beading off it. Remove any one of those and the press stays
a curiosity.

## The Speed of the Spread

Printing propagated across Europe faster than almost any technology before it,
and it did so through a very specific mechanism: the trained workman who
leaves. Mainz was sacked in 1462 during an archbishops' quarrel, and its
printers dispersed. Within a generation presses were running in well over two
hundred towns, concentrated along the trade routes and the river valleys and
especially in Venice, which had capital, paper, shipping, and a tolerant
enough government to become the printing capital of the continent.

The economics were unforgiving in a way manuscript production had never been. A
scribe copying a book on commission was paid per unit of work and took no risk.
A printer had to buy paper for the entire edition up front, tie up a workshop
for weeks, and then find several hundred buyers for identical copies of the
same text. That single fact reorganized European intellectual life. Printers
became speculators in demand. They needed titles that would move, which meant
schoolbooks, devotional manuals, legal formularies, almanacs, romances,
practical guides to surveying and accounting and horsemanship — and it meant
publishing in the vernacular far sooner than the prestige of Latin would
suggest. It also meant bankruptcies. A great many early printers went broke on
handsome editions nobody wanted.

The book itself changed shape under this pressure. Manuscripts had been
idiosyncratic; a reader found their way around by memory and by the physical
feel of a particular copy. Printed books acquired title pages, so a bookseller
could identify stock on a shelf. They acquired printed page numbers, running
heads, tables of contents, and alphabetical indexes — apparatus that only makes
sense when every copy is paginated identically. Aldus Manutius in Venice cut a
compressed italic face and issued classical texts in small octavo volumes a
person could carry, which is roughly the invention of the portable book.
Scholars could begin citing a passage by page rather than describing where to
look for it.

## Fixity, and Its Limits

The most influential argument about what print changed concerns this
standardization. When a hundred astronomers own copies of the same star table
with the same errors, those errors become visible and correctable; the next
edition improves; knowledge accumulates instead of decaying. Manuscript
transmission ran the other way, with every copy introducing fresh corruption,
so that a scholar's first task was often the restoration of a text rather than
its extension. Exactly reproducible diagrams mattered even more than exactly
reproducible words. Anatomy, botany, machine design, and cartography are
disciplines in which a picture carries the argument, and a woodcut or engraving
could carry it identically to every reader for the first time.

This picture needs a qualification that historians have pressed hard. Fixity
was an achievement rather than an automatic property of print. Early printed
books were riddled with errors, editions were pirated across jurisdictions
within weeks, texts were abridged and padded and falsely attributed, and the
errata leaf became a standard feature precisely because nobody trusted the
setting. Readers of the period were often intensely suspicious of what they
held. Trustworthiness had to be manufactured through reputation, privileges,
printers' devices, and eventually institutions like learned societies that
would vouch for what appeared under their name. The reliability we associate
with print came later than the press did, and it came from social arrangements
rather than from the machine.

Print amplified whatever it was given, which included a great deal of error.
The most effective witch-hunting manual of the period was an early printed
bestseller, and it circulated far more widely than any manuscript denunciation
could have. Astrological prognostications and miracle pamphlets sold
magnificently. There is no version of this history in which the press is simply
a solvent of superstition.

## The Reformation as a Publishing Event

The clearest demonstration of what the new medium could do arrived in 1517,
when an Augustinian friar in Wittenberg objected to the sale of indulgences.
Theological disputes of that kind had occurred before and had stayed local.
This one did not, because Martin Luther turned out to be an extraordinary
writer of short, plain, furious German prose, and because the cheap quarto
pamphlet — a few folded sheets, unbound, printable overnight, priced like a
chicken — was an ideal vehicle for it. Presses in dozens of towns reprinted his
tracts without permission and without paying him, which is exactly why the
tracts travelled. Luther became, by a wide margin, the most published author
Europe had yet produced.

His opponents were slower to use the same tools, partly because the Church's
habit was to write in Latin for other clerics. Woodcut images did work that
literacy could not: a crude picture of the pope as a monster could be read by
anyone. Pamphlets were read aloud in taverns and marketplaces to listeners who
could not read themselves, which is worth remembering whenever print is
discussed as though it only reached the literate. The Reformation was not
caused by printing. But its speed, its geography, and its refusal to be
contained were properties of a communications system that had no chokepoint.

Authorities noticed. Licensing systems, pre-publication censorship, and indexes
of prohibited books followed across Catholic and Protestant territories alike,
and they worked imperfectly everywhere. In England the Crown handled the
problem by chartering the Stationers' Company and granting it a monopoly on
printing in exchange for policing its members — an arrangement that made
censorship into a property right, and from which, by a long and crooked path,
modern copyright eventually descends.

## Reading, and the People Who Did It

Over the following two centuries the habits around books changed as much as the
books. Silent reading, already established among the learned, spread as
punctuation and spacing and typography made it easier. Domestic libraries of a
few dozen volumes became plausible for merchants and country clergy. Scholars
describe a slow shift from intensive reading — a handful of texts returned to
for a lifetime, largely memorized — toward extensive reading, in which a person
passes through many books once. Both persisted, often in the same household,
the Bible read intensively and everything else consumed.

Printing also began to freeze languages. A printer choosing which regional
spelling to set, and which dialect a book would be marketed in, was making a
decision about what counted as the standard form of a language, and the
accumulation of such decisions hardened the vernaculars of the chancery towns
into national tongues. Scribal culture had tolerated enormous local variation
because every copy served a local reader. The press served a market, and
markets want uniformity.

The trades tell the same story from underneath. Scribes did not vanish overnight
— the professional copying of legal and administrative documents went on for
centuries — but the copying of books as a livelihood collapsed. In its place
came papermakers, type founders, ink makers, engravers, correctors, binders,
pedlars, and the travelling agents of the Frankfurt book fair, where publishers
swapped unsold sheets by the bale. The book became an object manufactured in
one place and consumed in another, and the whole apparatus of distribution that
now surrounds every medium had to be invented around it.

What print did not do was democratize immediately or evenly. Books remained
expensive relative to a labourer's wage for a long time, literacy rates climbed
slowly and unequally, and the great expansion of cheap reading matter waited on
machine papermaking and the steam press in the nineteenth century. The four
centuries in between were spent working out what a society does when copies are
cheap and identical and nobody can call them all back.
