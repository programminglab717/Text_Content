# The Attention Economy

In 1971 the economist Herbert Simon made an observation about information
systems that has outlived nearly everything else written about computers that
decade. Information, he pointed out, consumes something. What it consumes is
the attention of whoever receives it. So an abundance of information
necessarily produces a scarcity of attention, and the interesting design
question becomes how to allocate that attention well among the many things
competing to absorb it.

The phrase attention economy came later, but the economics were already
visible in Simon's formulation. Attention has the properties that make
something a genuine economic good: it is finite, it cannot be stockpiled, and
it is rival in the strongest sense — a minute given to one thing is not
available to anything else, ever. Unlike money it cannot be borrowed against
future earnings. Unlike land it cannot be improved. The total supply is
roughly the waking hours of the population, and it is not growing.

An entire sector of the economy is organised around buying this good cheaply
from the people who own it and reselling it at a markup.

## The Business Model Is Older Than the Technology

Benjamin Day founded the New York Sun in 1833 and priced it at a penny when
respectable papers cost six. He was not undercutting his competitors so much
as changing what he was selling. The six-cent papers were sold to subscribers
who paid what the paper cost to produce. The Sun was sold below cost to
anyone, on the street, and the revenue came from merchants who wanted access
to the resulting crowd. The product being sold was no longer the newspaper. It
was the reader.

Everything that follows is elaboration. Commercial broadcasting formalised the
arrangement by giving programming away entirely and constructing an elaborate
measurement apparatus — household diaries, then metering equipment, then
panels weighted to stand in for a national population — because advertisers
would not pay for an audience they could not count. The critic Dallas Smythe
argued in the 1970s that this made the audience itself the commodity the
industry produced, and that the working day of a broadcast viewer should be
understood as labour performed for advertisers. It sounded eccentric at the
time. It reads now as a fairly plain description of the accounts.

What the old arrangement could not do was verify itself. The line attributed
to the merchant John Wanamaker — that half his advertising budget was wasted
and he did not know which half — described a real structural condition.
Advertisers bought a probabilistic association between a message and a
population, and the link between that purchase and any subsequent sale was
mostly inference.

## What Measurement Changed

Digital distribution collapsed the measurement gap, and that collapse is the
hinge of the whole story. When every impression can be counted, every click
logged, and every subsequent action attributed to the impression that
preceded it, the advertiser's purchase changes from a rough bet to something
that looks like a transaction with a receipt. Prices reorganise around what
can be measured. Inventory gets auctioned in the milliseconds while a page
loads. And the publisher's incentive shifts from producing something good to
producing something whose engagement can be demonstrated.

The consequences run deeper than the familiar complaint about clickbait,
though that is real enough: headline variants tested against each other in
live traffic converge reliably on curiosity gaps and mild outrage, because
those are what people click, and what people click is what the system can
see. The deeper consequence is that optimisation targets became design
targets. A recommendation system told to maximise clicks learns to promote
misleading thumbnails. Retarget it at total watch time and it learns instead
to find the next thing that will hold someone, which is a better objective
and still not the same as serving them well. Any single measurable proxy,
pushed hard enough, comes apart from the thing it was meant to proxy for.

Precise measurement also dissolved a cross-subsidy that had quietly funded a
great deal of expensive work. A newspaper's classified section, its automotive
listings and its department store pages were profitable, and those profits
paid for a statehouse bureau that no advertiser had ever asked for. Once
advertisers could buy the specific people they wanted — someone who had just
searched for a used car, wherever that person happened to be — they stopped
paying for proximity to a car section and the bundle came apart. The revenue
did not so much decline as relocate, away from institutions that produced
journalism as a by-product of selling attention and toward intermediaries that
produce nothing except the match. Attention that was once purchased in
neighbourhoods is now purchased one person at a time, and the institutions
that lived off the neighbourhood rate have no equivalent to sell.

The interface techniques that followed are mostly borrowed from older
behavioural research. Rewards delivered on an unpredictable schedule sustain a
behaviour far longer than rewards delivered reliably, which is the principle
behind a slot machine and behind a feed that refreshes to show an
indeterminate number of new items. Removing a natural stopping point removes
the moment at which a person would otherwise decide whether to continue;
infinite scroll, invented as a small convenience by a designer who has spent
the years since publicly regretting it, does exactly that. Autoplay makes
continuing the default and quitting the action requiring effort. Notifications
manufacture the interruption that reopens the session. Streaks and counters
convert use into an obligation with a cost for lapsing.

None of this required anyone to be malicious. It required a metric, a testing
apparatus, and enough traffic to detect a one percent improvement. The
mechanism is closer to selection than to design: thousands of variants are
tried, the ones that retain people survive, and after enough iterations the
product has features nobody specifically intended.

## The Numbers Are Softer Than They Look

The irony of the measured economy is how much of the measurement is
unreliable. The industry standard for a display advertisement counting as seen
is minimal — roughly half its pixels in view for about a second, which is well
below the threshold at which a human would notice it. A substantial portion of
recorded traffic has never been human at all; organised fraud operations have
run server farms that simulated browsing sessions and collected payment for
advertisements shown to nothing. Attribution models routinely credit a
conversion to the last advertisement a buyer happened to encounter, which
systematically overvalues advertising shown to people who had already decided.

When advertisers have run genuine controlled experiments rather than reading
dashboards, the results have been repeatedly deflating. A large online
marketplace found that paid search advertising on its own brand name produced
almost no incremental sales, because the people clicking those advertisements
were customers who would have typed the address anyway. A multinational
consumer goods company cut an enormous sum from its digital budget over a
single year, concentrated on inventory it could not verify, and reported no
detectable effect on revenue. The attention that changes hands in these
markets is partly real and partly a shared accounting fiction that everyone in
the chain has an interest in maintaining.

## What the Scarcity Actually Costs

The strongest argument against treating attention as ordinary inventory is
that the seller cannot really consent in the way markets assume. You agree to
a price for your labour knowing roughly what you are giving up. You do not
agree to the fourth hour of a feed; you arrive at it, having intended to spend
four minutes, by a route designed to make the intention hard to hold. The
transaction is not exactly coerced, but it is not exactly chosen either, and
the standard economic defence — revealed preference, people did the thing so
they must have wanted it — cannot distinguish between wanting something and
being unable to stop.

The supply side has its own harsh arithmetic. Because attention is finite and
the cost of publishing has fallen to nothing, the number of people competing
for it has grown without limit while the pool has not, and the distribution of
returns is ferociously unequal — a small number of entrants absorb most of the
available attention and the long remainder share a residue that will not
support anyone. Producers respond the way sellers in any glutted market
respond: by increasing volume, by chasing whatever format the distribution
system currently favours, and by accepting terms from intermediaries they
cannot negotiate with. The characteristic complaint of working creators — that
the platform changed the algorithm and their livelihood halved overnight — is
not a grievance about a company's manners. It describes a market where the
buyer sets the terms because the raw good is abundant and the channel to
market is owned.

There is also a compositional problem. Individually these systems are trading
in small quantities of time. Collectively they are competing for the same
fixed pool, and against everything else in it. A streaming executive once
remarked that his company's real competitor was sleep, which was meant as a
boast about ambition and is better read as a description of where the marginal
hour comes from. When the pool is fixed and the number of well-capitalised
bidders grows, the bidding gets more aggressive regardless of anyone's
intentions.

Counter-pressures exist and are more mixed than their advocates suggest.
Subscription models genuinely change the incentive — a publication paid by
readers must satisfy them over a year rather than capture them for a session —
but they also price out the people least able to pay and tend toward serving
an audience whose existing views are known. Ad blockers shift costs rather
than eliminating them. Screen-time dashboards report consumption without
altering the thing being consumed, in the way a calorie counter on a vending
machine changes less than one hopes. Regulation has moved against the most
mechanical techniques — default-on autoplay for minors, designs that make
cancellation deliberately laborious — which is useful and addresses a small
fraction of the surface.

The most durable interventions turn out to be crude and structural: leaving
the device in another room, disabling the notification channel rather than the
individual notification, using tools that do not have a feed. This is not a
moral failing on the part of people who cannot manage it through willpower.
An individual is bringing attention and intention to a contest against systems
that have run millions of experiments on how to divert both. Expecting the
individual to win that on discipline alone misunderstands what is on the other
side of the table.
