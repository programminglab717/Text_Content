# The Attention Economy

In 1971 the economist Herbert Simon made a remark about information systems
that has outlived nearly everything else written about computers that decade.
Information, he pointed out, consumes something, and what it consumes is the
attention of whoever receives it. An abundance of information therefore
produces a scarcity of attention as a matter of arithmetic, not sentiment, and
the real design problem becomes how to allocate that attention sensibly among
the many things competing to absorb it.

Simon was describing office automation. The phrase attention economy came
later, popularised in the 1990s by writers who argued that as material goods
grew cheap and information grew free, the genuinely scarce resource — the one
that would organise status, income and power — was the regard of other people.
That sounded like futurism at the time. It has since become a fairly plain
description of how several of the largest companies on earth make their money.

## A Good With Unusual Properties

Attention qualifies as an economic good in the strict sense, and its
properties are unforgiving. It is finite, bounded by waking hours and by how
long a person can concentrate within them. It cannot be stored: an unwatched
hour is not banked for later, it simply expires. It is rival in the strongest
available sense, since a minute spent on one thing is unavailable to every
other thing forever. It cannot be borrowed against future earnings the way
money can, and unlike land it cannot be improved or reclaimed. The aggregate
supply is roughly the waking hours of the population, and that quantity grows
only as fast as the population does.

There is a further asymmetry that markets handle badly. Giving attention is
expensive for the person giving it; taking it is nearly free for whoever
takes. Sending a notification costs the sender nothing measurable. Receiving
one costs the recipient not only the seconds spent reading it but the longer
tail of re-establishing whatever they were doing before — research on
interrupted knowledge work has consistently found that recovery takes
substantially longer than the interruption itself, and that people compensate
for interruptions by working faster under more strain rather than by finishing
later. The costs land almost entirely on one side of a transaction the other
side controls.

The critic Dallas Smythe argued in the 1970s that broadcast audiences should
be understood as the actual commodity being produced, and that time spent
watching was a form of unwaged labour performed for advertisers. It read as
provocation then. It reads now as a slightly flat description of the accounts,
with one revision: what is sold today is not the audience as a bulk quantity
but the probability that a specific person will still be attending in ten
seconds.

## When Retention Became the Objective

The consequential change was not that attention started being sold — it had
been sold since the first newspaper took an advertisement — but that it
started being sold against a metric computed in real time. Once every
impression, scroll, pause and resumption can be logged and attributed, the
seller's problem becomes a well-defined optimisation: choose the arrangement
of content that maximises the measured quantity.

That sounds harmless and is not, because the measured quantity is always a
proxy for something nobody can measure directly. A system told to maximise
clicks learns to promote misleading thumbnails, since a click is registered
before disappointment can be. Point it instead at cumulative watch time, a
genuine improvement, and it learns to find whatever will hold a particular
person next — which is better, and still not the same as serving them well,
because the objective cannot distinguish an hour someone is glad to have spent
from an hour they could not put down. Push any single proxy hard enough and it
comes apart from the thing it was standing in for. The systems doing this are
not reasoning about people; they are running enormous numbers of small
comparisons and retaining what performs.

The design consequences followed from the selection process rather than from
anyone's intentions. Rewards delivered on an unpredictable schedule sustain a
behaviour far longer than reliable ones, which is the mechanism behind a slot
machine and behind a feed that refreshes to reveal an indeterminate number of
new items. Removing a natural endpoint removes the moment at which a person
would have decided whether to continue; infinite scroll, introduced as a minor
convenience by a designer who has spent the years since publicly regretting
it, does precisely that. Autoplay makes continuation the default and stopping
the action that requires effort. Notifications manufacture the interruption
that reopens a session. Streaks and counters convert use into an obligation
with a penalty for lapsing. None of this required malice. It required a
metric, a testing apparatus, and enough traffic to detect a one percent
improvement.

## What Engagement Does Not Capture

The measurement that drives all this is looser than its precision suggests. A
display advertisement is conventionally counted as seen when about half its
pixels have been in the viewport for roughly a second — a threshold well below
what a person would need to register that anything was there. Session length
counts a tab left open while someone makes lunch. Dwell time cannot tell
absorption from confusion. A video counted as watched may have played to an
empty room.

Engagement, the industry's master construct, is an aggregate of these proxies,
and it systematically confuses several different states. Attention given
gladly and attention given compulsively produce identical logs. So do
attention given to something excellent and attention given to something
infuriating; the argument in a comment thread and the essay that provoked it
score the same. Whatever genuinely distinguishes these — whether the time was
well spent, whether the person would choose it again, whether they felt better
or worse afterwards — is expensive to collect, arrives slowly, and cannot be
fed into an automated loop that iterates hourly. So the fast proxy wins by
default, and the slow signal, which is the one that matters commercially over
years, gets noticed only when audiences drift away for reasons no dashboard
recorded.

## The Glut on the Supply Side

Because publishing costs nothing and attention is fixed, the number of people
competing for it grows without bound while the pool does not. The resulting
distribution is ferociously unequal in the way such contests always are: a
small fraction of entrants absorb most of what is available, and the long
remainder divide a residue that will not support anybody. Producers respond
the way sellers in any glutted market respond — by raising volume, by chasing
whatever format the distribution system currently rewards, and by accepting
terms from intermediaries they have no standing to negotiate.

The characteristic complaint of working creators, that a ranking change halved
their income overnight, is not really a grievance about corporate manners. It
describes a market in which the raw good is abundant, the route to buyers is
owned by a third party, and the terms are set by whoever controls the route.
The same structure explains why so much of what is produced converges on
similar lengths, similar openings and similar rhythms. Those are not
aesthetic choices. They are adaptations to a distribution system that
measures whether people stay in the first few seconds.

## A Fixed Pool and Many Bidders

The most awkward argument against treating attention as ordinary inventory is
that the seller cannot consent in the way markets assume. Someone agrees to a
wage knowing roughly what they are giving up. Nobody agrees to the fourth hour
of a feed; they arrive at it having intended to spend four minutes, by a route
built specifically to make the original intention hard to keep. The standard
defence — revealed preference, they did it so they must have wanted it —
cannot distinguish wanting something from being unable to stop.

The scarcity also binds collectively, and not only for individuals. Public
attention behaves like a single narrow channel: only a few problems can occupy
it at once, and the political scientist Anthony Downs described the
consequence decades ago as an issue-attention cycle, in which a problem is
discovered with alarm, briefly dominates, then recedes as the public learns
what solving it would cost — with the problem itself unchanged. Anything that
increases the number of well-funded bidders for that channel makes every
issue's tenure in it shorter.

At the individual level the same fixed-pool logic shows up in the boast, made
by a streaming executive, that his real competitor was sleep. Read as
ambition it is fatuous. Read as accounting it is accurate, because once the
easy hours are allocated the marginal hour has to come from somewhere, and the
things it comes out of — sleep, unstructured time, conversation, boredom —
have no revenue attached and no one bidding on their behalf.

Countermeasures are more mixed than their advocates admit. Subscriptions
genuinely change the incentive, since a publication paid by readers must
satisfy them across a year rather than capture them for a session, but they
also exclude people who cannot pay and drift toward serving an audience whose
preferences are already known. Screen-time dashboards report consumption
without altering what is consumed, in the way a calorie count on a vending
machine changes less than one hopes. Regulation has moved against the most
mechanical devices — autoplay defaults for minors, cancellation flows designed
to exhaust people — which is useful and touches a small part of the surface.

What actually works tends to be crude and structural: the device in another
room, the notification channel disabled at the source rather than dismissed
one at a time, tools that simply have no feed. That this requires blunt
instruments is not a failure of character. A person brings ordinary intention
to a contest against systems that have run millions of experiments on how to
divert it, and expecting them to win on discipline alone mistakes what is
sitting across the table.
