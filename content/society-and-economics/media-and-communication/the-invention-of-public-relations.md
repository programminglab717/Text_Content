# The Invention of Public Relations

In October 1906 a Pennsylvania Railroad train derailed on a trestle near
Atlantic City and dozens of passengers drowned. The railroad's standing policy
in such situations, like that of most large American corporations at the time,
was to say nothing, deny access, and wait. A young former newspaperman named
Ivy Lee, newly retained by the company, persuaded its executives to do the
opposite. He chartered a train, carried reporters to the wreck site, and gave
them the facts the railroad had. Coverage was extensive and, on the whole,
less hostile than the silence would have produced. Lee had discovered
something that still organises the business: in a contest over what happened,
the party that supplies the usable account first usually defines it.

Shortly afterwards he circulated a one-page statement to editors announcing
that his office was not a secret press bureau, that all its work was done in
the open, that it would supply news rather than advertising, and that it would
identify its clients and provide further detail on request. The Declaration of
Principles is routinely cited as the founding document of the field. It is
also the first instance of a manoeuvre the field has repeated ever since:
announcing its own transparency as a form of persuasion.

## From Press Agentry to Counsel

What Lee was distinguishing himself from was press agentry, the
nineteenth-century trade of planting items in newspapers on behalf of
theatres, patent medicines and travelling shows. P. T. Barnum had worked the
form to exhaustion, manufacturing controversies about his own exhibits on the
correct theory that an argument in print sold more tickets than a paid
advertisement. Press agents were paid for column inches and were not fussy
about how they got them.

The reframing from agent to counsel mattered because it changed who the client
was and what the work touched. A counsel advised on conduct, not only on
announcements, on the premise that a company's reputation was downstream of
what it actually did. Lee's own career demonstrated how far that premise could
be stretched. After the Colorado mine strike of 1914 ended with the deaths of
strikers and their families at Ludlow, he was hired to repair the Rockefeller
name. Part of his advice was genuine counsel — he urged the elder Rockefeller's
son to go to Colorado, meet the miners, eat with them and listen, which was
unprecedented and which worked. Part of it was a bulletin campaign whose
handling of casualty figures earned him the nickname Poison Ivy from union
organisers and a permanent place in every critical history of the industry.

## Bernays and the Third Party

Edward Bernays, a nephew of Sigmund Freud who had worked on the American
government's wartime information committee, supplied the field with its theory
and most of its enduring techniques. His books of the 1920s argued that modern
societies are too complex for citizens to form opinions from direct
experience, that opinion is therefore shaped by an unseen professional class,
and that this was not a scandal but a structural necessity of democracy at
scale. He used the word propaganda for what he did until the word became
unusable, and then he used engineering of consent.

His central operational insight was that an interested party should never make
its own case. To sell more bacon, he did not advertise bacon; he had a
physician survey colleagues on whether a substantial breakfast was healthier
than a light one, collected a large number of agreeable replies, and let the
resulting news story about medical opinion do the work. To attach cigarettes
to women's emancipation, he arranged for debutantes to light what publicists
briefed to reporters as torches of freedom while walking in a New York Easter
parade, generating a national argument about women smoking in public in which
the tobacco company never appeared. Decades later, working for a fruit company
whose Central American holdings were threatened by land reform, he ran a
sustained campaign framing a Guatemalan government as a communist beachhead,
and did it largely through sponsored press tours and material that reached
editors already shaped into copy.

The pattern is constant: locate a third party with independent credibility,
give it a reason to speak, and let the client's interest travel inside
somebody else's mouth. Nearly every modern technique — the funded institute,
the survey with a press release attached, the influencer gifting programme,
the ostensibly grassroots coalition — is a variation on it.

## The Machinery of the Information Subsidy

Inside the corporation, the function was institutionalised by executives like
Arthur Page at the telephone company, who insisted that public relations
belonged in the room where decisions were made rather than at the end of the
process. That gave the field its respectable self-image and its most
defensible claim: that an organisation which has to explain itself publicly
behaves better than one that does not.

The everyday work, though, is supply. Scholars describe it as an information
subsidy — reducing the cost to a journalist of producing a story that serves
the source. A press release is a subsidy. So is a briefing document, a
pre-cleared interview, an embargoed report released to selected outlets with
enough lead time to be written up properly, an exclusive traded for favourable
placement, a set of broadcast-quality clips supplied free to television
stations, and an event staged for no purpose except to be covered — what
Daniel Boorstin named the pseudo-event, an occurrence that exists because
someone scheduled it and whose only claim on attention is that it is
happening.

The subsidy works because newsrooms are resource-constrained in exactly the
way the subsidy relieves. As reporting staffs contracted over the past several
decades while communications departments grew, the ratio tipped: in the United
States there are now several public relations practitioners for every working
journalist, and the gap continues to widen. Research into British national
newspapers in the 2000s, most publicly summarised in Nick Davies's account of
the trade, found that a large majority of stories carried material derived
wholly or partly from public relations sources or wire copy, and that only a
small fraction showed evidence of independent checking. The word coined for
the result, churnalism, describes a newsroom that processes supplied material
rather than gathering its own.

## Crisis, and the Difference Between Message and Conduct

The discipline's own textbook case is the 1982 poisoning of Tylenol capsules
in the Chicago area, where Johnson & Johnson pulled its product nationally at
enormous cost before anyone required it to, communicated continuously and
without hedging, and returned to market with tamper-evident packaging. The
company's reputation survived because the decision was defensible before it
was explained. The counter-example is usually the Exxon Valdez grounding in
1989, where a slow and distant corporate response — a chairman who did not
appear at the scene for a long time, statements that emphasised legal position
— turned a shipping accident into a lasting symbol.

The gap between those two cases is the honest core of the field and the reason
practitioners insist the work is not spin. It is also the part clients most
often decline to buy. The louder history of the profession is the one in which
messaging substitutes for conduct. The tobacco industry's response to the
first solid epidemiology linking smoking to cancer was designed by a large
agency: a full-page statement published in hundreds of newspapers in 1954,
promising to fund disinterested research through a new industry committee, a
body that went on to sponsor work for decades while the industry's own
scientists documented harms internally. The strategy did not claim cigarettes
were safe. It claimed the question was open, which was cheaper and lasted
longer.

The same repertoire recurs. Front organisations with civic-sounding names
whose funding is not disclosed. Astroturf campaigns that simulate spontaneous
citizen pressure, a term popularised by a US senator who complained that he
could tell the difference between real grass and the artificial kind arriving
in his mail. And, at the outer edge, testimony to a congressional caucus in
1990 by a young Kuwaiti woman describing atrocities she said she had
witnessed, arranged by an agency working for a group funded by the Kuwaiti
government, whose identity as a member of the ruling family was not disclosed
at the time.

## What Restrains It

Not much, formally. Public relations is not a licensed profession; nobody can
be struck off. The professional associations maintain codes calling for
honesty, disclosure of who is paying, and avoidance of conflicts, and they can
expel members, which mostly means the members resign first. Regulation reaches
only specific corners: rules requiring paid endorsements to be disclosed,
requirements that broadcasters identify sponsored material, securities law
governing what a listed company may say about its own prospects, lobbying
registers that capture some but not all influence work.

The stronger constraint has always been an adversary with time and budget — a
reporter who checks the funding of the institute, a regulator who subpoenas
the internal file, a researcher who compares what a company says publicly with
what its own scientists wrote. Which is an uncomfortable dependency, because
the same economics that expanded the supply side of this relationship have
been shrinking the side that checks it. Lee's insight about who defines the
account holds either way. It just matters a great deal more when nobody is
positioned to offer a second one.
