# The Rise of Radio

The first radio receiver most people ever touched cost about as much as a good
meal and had no batteries in it. A crystal set was a coil of wire wound on an
oatmeal carton, a chip of galena held in a clip, a fine wire — the cat's
whisker — that you poked around the crystal's surface until you found a spot
that rectified, and a pair of headphones. The signal itself supplied the power.
A boy in Iowa in 1923 could string an aerial along the barn roof, hunt for the
sensitive spot with the patience of someone tuning a violin, and hear a dance
band playing in Chicago that evening. Nothing in human experience had prepared
anyone for that, and the accounts written at the time have a quality of
astonishment that later media never quite provoked.

Getting there took about thirty-five years and a series of inventions that were
not obviously aimed at the same destination. Heinrich Hertz generated and
detected electromagnetic waves in his laboratory in the late 1880s, confirming
Maxwell's mathematics and stating, when asked about applications, that he saw
none. Guglielmo Marconi, who was less interested in physics than in commerce,
spent the following decade turning the phenomenon into a business — longer
aerials, better grounds, tuned circuits, and eventually a signal that crossed
the Atlantic. But what Marconi sold was wireless telegraphy: dots and dashes,
point to point, a replacement for cable where cable could not go. Ships talked
to shore stations. Navies talked to fleets. The apparatus was a spark-gap
transmitter, which is essentially a controlled lightning bolt, and it splattered
energy across the whole spectrum. You could not put a voice on it.

## From Sparks to Voices

Voice required a smooth continuous wave that could be varied in amplitude by a
microphone, and three routes to one were tried. Reginald Fessenden had a
high-frequency alternator built — a rotating machine generating radio frequency
directly — and used it for early demonstrations of speech and music sent over
water. Arc transmitters did something similar by a different path. But the
device that settled the matter was small, fragile, and glass. Lee de Forest put
a third electrode into a vacuum tube diode in 1906, producing the triode, in
which a tiny voltage on the grid controls a large current through the tube. De
Forest did not fully understand what he had. Others did. A triode amplifies,
and with feedback it oscillates, which means the same component can generate a
clean carrier wave at the transmitter and pull a faint signal up to audibility
at the receiver.

Edwin Armstrong worked out the feedback circuit as a student and spent much of
his life in court over it, first against de Forest and later against the
corporation that had grown up around the technology. He also invented the
superheterodyne receiver, which converts any incoming station down to a fixed
intermediate frequency where a single well-designed amplifier can do the work —
the architecture inside nearly every radio built since. Decades later he
developed wideband frequency modulation and demonstrated that static, the
plague of AM listening, was largely defeatable. The industry he had helped
create resisted FM for years because it threatened the value of existing
stations and receivers. Armstrong died by his own hand in 1954, still
litigating.

Amateurs were pushing from below the whole time. Thousands of American hobbyists
built their own stations, and the government, irritated by interference with
naval traffic and prodded by the Titanic disaster, passed a licensing act in
1912 that banished them to wavelengths shorter than two hundred meters —
territory thought to be worthless. It was not. Short waves refract off the
ionosphere and return to earth hundreds or thousands of miles away, and the
amateurs discovered this by doing it, which is why international shortwave
broadcasting exists at all.

## Who Pays for the Air

The First World War shut down civilian wireless and put the patents into a
government-brokered pool, and at the end of it the Navy pressed for an American
company that would keep the technology out of British hands. The Radio
Corporation of America was assembled in 1919 out of cross-licenses among the
major electrical and telephone manufacturers, which meant that when broadcasting
arrived it arrived inside a structure already designed to allocate a market.

Broadcasting itself began almost as an accident of manufacturing. A Westinghouse
engineer's experimental station in Pittsburgh had drawn a local following, and
the company recognized that regular programming would sell receiving sets. KDKA
carried the presidential election returns in November 1920 to an audience of
perhaps a few thousand, an event usually given as the beginning, though several
other stations have reasonable claims. What followed was a genuine mania.
Within two years hundreds of stations were on the air, licensed to newspapers,
department stores, universities, churches, and battery manufacturers, nearly all
of them operating at a loss for promotional reasons. Receiver sales went
vertical. And everyone confronted the same question: broadcasting has no
turnstile. You cannot charge the listener, because you cannot tell who is
listening or stop anyone who is not paying.

Different countries answered differently, and the answers determined what radio
became. Britain chartered a public corporation funded by a licence fee on
receivers, run under John Reith's conviction that broadcasting should inform,
educate, and entertain in roughly that order of dignity, and kept advertising
off the air entirely. Most of Europe followed some version of that. The United
States tried the licence fee idea briefly and abandoned it. Herbert Hoover, as
Commerce Secretary, presided over conferences that denounced the notion of
advertising chatter polluting the ether. Then in 1922 the telephone company's
New York station began selling blocks of airtime to anyone who wanted to talk,
starting with a Long Island real estate developer promoting apartments, and the
question was effectively settled by cash flow.

The other unsolved problem was physical. Radio spectrum is a commons that
degrades when crowded, and by the mid-1920s stations were jumping frequencies,
overlapping, and drowning each other out. The Radio Act of 1927 created a
federal commission with power to license, assign frequencies, and refuse
renewal, using a standard borrowed from utility law: public interest,
convenience, and necessity. That phrase did two things at once. It rationalized
the band, and it established that American broadcasters held a revocable
privilege rather than a property right — a lever that shaped programming
obligations for the next sixty years.

## The Network and the Living Room

Scarcity of good frequencies plus the expense of programming produced
networking. Wire the stations together with leased telephone lines, originate a
program once in New York, and feed it to affiliates everywhere. NBC began in
1926 with two chains; CBS followed under William Paley, who understood before
most of his competitors that a network's product was not programming but
audience, and that you could give affiliates shows for free if you kept the
advertising minutes. Sponsors, working through advertising agencies, did not
merely buy time — they owned and produced the shows outright, which is why the
serialized daytime dramas underwritten by soap manufacturers acquired a name
that has outlived the arrangement entirely.

What radio did to domestic life is harder to measure than what it did to
markets, but people at the time were clear that something had changed. A voice
in a living room is intimate in a way that print is not; it arrives in real
time, it is heard simultaneously by strangers in every direction, and it has a
grain and a warmth that a newspaper column cannot carry. Franklin Roosevelt
understood this immediately, speaking in a conversational register directly past
the overwhelmingly hostile newspaper publishers to people sitting at their own
kitchen tables. So did less benign figures — the broadcast priest whose weekly
audience of millions drifted from populist economics to open antisemitism, and
the German state that subsidized a cheap receiver designed to pick up domestic
stations well and foreign ones poorly.

The medium also got a founding myth it did not deserve. When Orson Welles
dramatized an invasion from Mars on Halloween eve in 1938 in the form of
simulated news bulletins, some listeners were certainly alarmed; a nationwide
panic, however, was mostly manufactured afterward by newspapers that were
delighted to run stories about their competitor's recklessness. The episode
survives as the standard illustration of media power largely because it was
told that way.

Radio's later life is a case study in a medium surviving its own obsolescence.
Television took the evening family audience, the big variety shows, the drama
anthologies, and most of the national advertising within about a decade. What
was left turned out to be durable: the transistor made receivers pocket-sized
and cheap, the car made them a default companion for a commute, and stations
rebuilt around recorded music, tight format clocks, local news, and personality.
The thing that could not be carried anywhere and cost a month's wages in 1922
became the cheapest and most portable medium there is, and it is still the one
that reaches people whose electricity and literacy cannot be assumed.
