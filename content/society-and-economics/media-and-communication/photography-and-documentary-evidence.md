# Photography and the Problem of Documentary Evidence

On a July afternoon in 1863, a few days after the fighting at Gettysburg
ended, Alexander Gardner and his assistants photographed a dead Confederate
soldier lying in a rocky nook among the boulders on the southern end of the
battlefield. A rifle leaned against the stones beside him. Gardner published
the picture as "Home of a Rebel Sharpshooter," with a caption imagining the
boy's slow death and the mother who would never know where he fell. It is one
of the most reproduced images of the American Civil War.

More than a century later the historian William Frassanito, comparing
Gardner's plates against the terrain and against each other, established that
the same corpse appears in another photograph taken some forty yards away, in
open ground, in different clothing arrangement and without the rifle. The body
had been carried to the picturesque spot and posed. The rifle was a prop the
crew travelled with; it was not a sharpshooter's weapon. The dead man was
almost certainly not a sharpshooter at all.

Nothing about the photograph is false in the narrow sense. A real man really
died at Gettysburg and this is really his body. What the plate cannot tell
you, and what no plate has ever been able to tell you, is everything that
happened in the ten minutes before the shutter opened.

## The Evidentiary Claim and Where It Comes From

Photography arrived with a metaphysical advantage over drawing. A sketch
passes through a person; a photograph, it seemed, was made by the light
itself, an automatic transcription in which human judgement had no role. That
intuition is not wrong so much as radically incomplete, and courts, editors
and scientists have spent the intervening two centuries working out exactly
which part of it to trust.

What a photograph actually testifies to is narrow and strong: at a particular
instant, light in a particular pattern arrived at a particular surface. That
is genuinely more than a drawing offers. It is why a photograph of a bruise
matters in a courtroom and why satellite imagery of a construction site
matters in an arms-control dispute. But the frame is a choice, the moment is a
choice, the lens compresses or exaggerates distance, the exposure decides what
is visible in shadow, and the caption — which no camera produces — supplies
the who, where, when and why on which almost all the meaning depends.

Roger Fenton, photographing the Crimea in 1855 with a wagon full of wet plates
that had to be coated, exposed and developed within minutes, made two frames
of a shell-scarred ravine. In one the cannonballs lie in the gullies beside
the road; in the other they are scattered across the road itself. Only the
second was published, under a borrowed line from the psalms, as "The Valley of
the Shadow of Death." Which came first has been argued over for decades and
investigated in careful detail by the filmmaker Errol Morris, whose reading of
the displaced rocks on the slope suggests the road was cleared first and
loaded afterwards. The argument matters less than what it exposes: to settle a
question about a photograph, everyone involved had to leave the photograph and
reason about physical evidence, provenance and the photographer's incentives.

## The Reformers, and the Argument From Discomfort

The documentary tradition proper begins with people who wanted pictures to do
something. Jacob Riis carried magnesium flash powder into New York tenements
in the 1880s, igniting it in rooms where the residents had not been asked and
could not have consented meaningfully, producing images of lodging houses and
alley sleepers that startled comfortable readers precisely because the flash
made visible what darkness had kept private. The photographs helped drive
tenement reform. They also fixed their subjects permanently as specimens.

Lewis Hine worked the opposite way. Employed by a committee campaigning
against child labour, he talked his way into mills and canneries, photographed
the children at their machines, and then — knowing that a picture alone could
be dismissed as unrepresentative — wrote down names, ages, hours and wages,
sometimes measuring a child's height against the buttons on his own coat so he
could later testify to the scale. His captions are the reason the images
functioned as evidence rather than sentiment.

The Farm Security Administration photographic unit of the 1930s produced the
canonical American documentary archive under Roy Stryker, who issued shooting
scripts telling his photographers what themes to cover, and who edited by
punching holes through the negatives he rejected. Tens of thousands of images
in the surviving file carry that black hole, a physical record of the
selection that made the collection. Dorothea Lange's portrait of a migrant
mother at a pea-pickers' camp in California was the last of several frames
made as Lange moved closer; a thumb intruding at the edge of the negative was
later retouched out; and the woman herself, later identified as Florence Owens
Thompson, spent the rest of her life as an anonymous symbol of a decade she
had experienced rather differently than the caption suggested.

## Removal, Addition, and the Line Editors Draw

Deliberate falsification has its own long history, and for most of the
twentieth century it required a darkroom and patience. Soviet archives
provided the most systematic case: as officials were purged, they were
airbrushed out of group photographs, retouched from platforms and balconies
until the surviving prints showed a shrinking cast of leaders standing beside
progressively emptier space. The collector David King assembled a book of
before-and-after pairs that reads as a political history told entirely in
erasures.

In commercial publishing the line moved slowly and then all at once. A
magazine that digitally narrowed the gap between two pyramids in the early
1980s to fit a vertical cover was doing something that, before scanning, would
have been prohibitively difficult; the controversy that followed established
the principle that a news photograph's geometry is part of its content. The
rules that wire services and press photography associations now enforce are
therefore strict and somewhat arbitrary at the edges: you may adjust global
tone, contrast and colour balance as you would have in a darkroom; you may
crop; you may not add, remove or move anything within the frame. Photographers
have been fired for cloning a bit of smoke in a conflict image, and for
combining two frames of the same scene into a better-composed single picture,
even when the resulting image misrepresented nothing anyone could point to.
Picture contests have disqualified substantial fractions of their finalists
after examining raw files, usually not for inserted objects but for processing
so heavy that it manufactured a mood the scene did not have.

## Verification as a Working Craft

The practical response to all this has been to stop treating the image as
self-sufficient and to treat it as one artefact in a case file. The methods
are now reasonably well codified and largely available to anyone.

Provenance comes first: find the earliest instance of the file rather than the
one that reached you, since a reverse image search that turns up the same
frame three years earlier settles most claims immediately. Metadata embedded
by the camera may record time, exposure and coordinates, though it is
trivially editable and routinely stripped by platforms, so it is corroboration
rather than proof. Then the picture is interrogated against the world:
buildings, signage, road markings, vegetation and terrain profile matched
against satellite and street-level imagery to establish location; shadow
length and direction against the sun's known position to constrain the time of
day and season; weather records against visible cloud and ground conditions;
the language on a shopfront against the region claimed. A frame that survives
all of that is not merely plausible — it has been pinned to a place and an
hour by evidence external to itself.

This is the work that open-source investigators have made routine over the
past decade, and it is a return to Hine's insight rather than a departure from
it. The photograph is powerful because it captures something no witness's
memory can, and weak because it arrives stripped of the context that makes it
mean anything. Verification puts the context back.

The newer difficulty is not that convincing fakes can be generated cheaply,
though they can. It is the second-order effect legal scholars have named the
liar's dividend: once any image can be dismissed as fabricated, the person
caught on camera has a defence available that used not to exist. Which returns
the whole enterprise to where Gardner left it — the picture never carried the
proof by itself. Provenance did, and testimony did, and the willingness of
somebody to stand behind the caption and be checked.
