# Journalism Ethics and the Cases That Made Them

Almost every rule in a newsroom code of ethics is a scar. The codes read like
abstract principle — seek truth, minimise harm, act independently, be
accountable — but each clause was written by someone who had just watched a
specific disaster and wanted to make sure it had a name. Read them alongside
the history and they stop sounding like homily and start sounding like
maintenance instructions.

The first American code of any standing, the Canons of Journalism adopted by
newspaper editors in 1923, arrived at a moment when the trade was trying to
distinguish itself from the sensationalism of the preceding decades and from
the publicists who had begun to feed it copy. It asserted sincerity,
truthfulness, accuracy, impartiality, fair play, decency, and independence
from private interest. Within a few years one of the editors who signed it was
implicated in the Teapot Dome oil scandal, which tells you roughly how much
work a declaration does on its own.

## Objectivity Is a Method, Not a Mood

The most misunderstood idea in the whole field is objectivity. It was never a
claim that reporters have no opinions or that every story has two equal sides.
Walter Lippmann, writing shortly after the First World War, proposed it as a
response to precisely the opposite problem: journalists were saturated in
opinion, their own and other people's, and had no disciplined way of telling
what they actually knew. His answer was procedural. Borrow the habits of
evidence from the sciences — state your sources, describe your method,
distinguish observation from inference, be explicit about uncertainty — so
that another person working from the same material could check the result.
Objectivity in that sense is a property of the process, not of the
practitioner.

The degraded version, which treats fairness as the equal arraying of claims
without regard to their evidentiary standing, produces a characteristic
failure: a story about whether a thing is true becomes a story about the
dispute over whether it is true, and a reader learns only that people
disagree. Beat reporters who cover scientific or statistical subjects run into
this constantly, and the usual repair is to report the weight of evidence
explicitly rather than pretend that weight is not a fact about the world.

Verification is where the method lives in practice. Confirm with a second
source who has independent access to the information rather than a second
person repeating the same rumour. Go back to the original document rather than
the description of it. Read the quote back to the person who said it when the
stakes are high and the phrasing is delicate. Call the subject of the
allegation, in time for the answer to change the story, not after it is set.
None of this is glamorous. All of it is what separates a reported claim from a
repeated one.

## Sources, and the Cost of Anonymity

Granting a source anonymity is a transaction. The source gets protection from
consequences; the reader gives up the ability to weigh who is speaking and
why. Good practice treats that trade as expensive: used when the information
could not be obtained otherwise and matters enough to justify the loss, with
the reasons for the grant stated in the story, and with the newsroom — not
just the reporter — knowing who the person is. The abuses are familiar. An
anonymous official can attack a rival with no fingerprints. A government can
float a policy and disown it if it lands badly. A reporter can drift into a
comfortable relationship in which access is traded for a persistently
flattering frame.

The protection is also, in most jurisdictions, a promise made against
uncertain law. The United States Supreme Court declined in 1972 to find a
constitutional privilege exempting reporters from grand jury testimony, and
the shield laws passed by many states since then vary in strength and do not
bind federal courts. When the New York Times reporter Judith Miller spent
weeks in jail in 2005 rather than identify a confidential source, she was
demonstrating what the commitment costs when it is actually tested. A promise
of confidentiality should not be given by someone who has not thought about
that.

## Minimising Harm

The clause that generates the most argument inside newsrooms is the one about
harm, because it is the only major principle that pulls against publication.
Naming a sexual assault complainant. Photographing a body that a family has
not yet been told about. Identifying a juvenile. Publishing the home address
of someone the crowd is angry with. Reporting the method used in a suicide,
which decades of research links to subsequent imitation, and which most
guidelines now advise against describing in any detail.

The useful test is not whether publishing would upset someone — much
worthwhile journalism does — but whether the harm is proportionate to what the
public actually gains, and whether the same public gain could be had at lower
cost. Ordinary people caught up in news by accident are owed more care than
officials who sought power and the scrutiny attached to it. A grieving family
has no obligation to give an interview, and the ethical failure in a
death-knock is not the knock but the refusal to accept a no.

Undercover work sits on the same fault line. The Chicago Sun-Times bought and
operated a bar in the late 1970s, wired it, and documented a procession of
inspectors soliciting payoffs. It was compelling and its findings were real,
and the Pulitzer board declined to give it a prize, on the reasoning that
journalists who deceive to get a story weaken the standing from which they
condemn deception. Two decades later a network newsmagazine put producers
inside a supermarket chain with fabricated job applications; the company sued,
not for libel — the reporting held up — but for the deception used to get in,
and the resulting litigation did more to discourage the technique than any
code clause. The defensible version of undercover reporting has always
required a high bar: serious wrongdoing, no other route to proof, and full
disclosure to the audience of what was done and why.

## Independence, and the Wall

The commercial pressure on ethics is structural rather than dramatic. Very few
newsrooms are told what to write. What happens instead is that a subject
becomes unattractive because covering it costs a relationship. The clearest
illustration remains the Los Angeles Times's arrangement in 1999 to share the
revenue from a magazine issue about a new downtown arena with the arena's
owner — a deal the newsroom learned about after publication, and which
produced an internal revolt because it made every word in the issue
unreadable as journalism, regardless of whether any of it had been influenced.

Today the pressures include branded content that mimics editorial typography,
analytics that quietly reward whatever performs, events businesses that sell
sponsorships to the industries a paper also covers, and the fact that a large
share of digital advertising is placed by intermediaries who care about
audience attributes rather than editorial quality. The old physical wall
between business and editorial was always partly a fiction, but the fiction
had a function: it gave a reporter a place to stand when the answer had to be
no.

## Accountability Is the Part Everyone Skips

Fabrication scandals are instructive less for the fabrication than for how
long it went unnoticed. The Washington Post reporter who invented an
eight-year-old heroin addict in 1980 and won a Pulitzer for it had colleagues
who doubted the story and an editing chain that wanted it to be true. A New
Republic writer in the 1990s manufactured events, sources and organisations,
and built fake supporting material to survive fact-checking. A New York Times
reporter in 2003 filed datelined stories from places he had not visited,
lifting detail from other outlets' photographs and wire copy. In each case the
institution's own verification habits had loosened around a favoured writer,
and in each case the repair that mattered was not the firing but the published
account of how it happened.

That is what accountability means in the codes: correct promptly and
conspicuously, explain rather than bury, describe your sourcing when it is
unusual, and submit your own conduct to the scrutiny you apply to everyone
else. Many papers once employed an ombudsman or public editor to do this from
inside; most have abandoned the post, on the theory that social media now
performs the function. It does not, quite. Criticism from outside cannot open
an editor's inbox or ask a reporter to produce the notebook.

The unglamorous conclusion that working journalists tend to reach is that
ethics in the trade is rarely a confrontation with an obvious wrong. It is a
sequence of small decisions taken under deadline about how hard to push on a
detail that probably does not matter — the second call, the document you have
not actually seen, the quote that is better than what the person really said.
Codes are useful mostly because they make those decisions visible before the
scar forms.
