# How Propaganda Works

In 1622 Pope Gregory XV established a standing committee of cardinals to
supervise missionary activity in territories Rome considered spiritually
contested. He called it the Congregatio de Propaganda Fide, the congregation
for propagating the faith, and the word entered European languages as a piece
of administrative Latin — the name of an office, carrying no more moral weight
than "bureau" or "commission." Three centuries later it had become an
accusation you could not answer. Almost all of that change happened between
1914 and 1945, when industrial states discovered that the presses, railways,
cinemas and radio transmitters built to move goods and armies could move
opinion just as efficiently, and set about proving it at a scale nobody had
previously attempted.

The thing that makes propaganda a coherent object of study is not that it
lies. A great deal of it is factually accurate, and some of the most effective
campaigns in the historical record contain no verifiable falsehood at all. The
working definition used by most scholars — deliberate and systematic attempts
to shape perception and direct behaviour in ways that serve the communicator's
aims — puts the emphasis elsewhere: on intent, on organisation, and on whose
interests the message is built to serve. A newspaper editorial is persuasion.
A government ministry with a budget, a distribution network, a target audience
segmented by region and class, and a monthly assessment of whether the desired
behaviour is increasing, is something else.

## What the First Industrial Campaigns Looked Like

Britain's War Propaganda Bureau operated out of Wellington House in London
under Charles Masterman, and its most important innovation was concealment of
authorship. Rather than issue official pamphlets, it recruited well-known
novelists, historians and academics, printed their work through ordinary
commercial publishers, and circulated it through channels that looked entirely
civilian. Material aimed at American opinion arrived in the United States
carrying the scholarly apparatus of footnotes and appendices, not a government
crest.

The Bryce Report of 1915, a royal commission inquiry into German conduct in
occupied Belgium, shows both the power and the long-term cost of the method.
Some of what it described had happened. Much of it rested on uncorroborated
testimony, collected without cross-examination, from witnesses whose
depositions were later unavailable for review. The document did its immediate
work. Its slower effect was corrosive: when the atrocities of the following
war were reported, a generation that had learned to discount the last round of
horror stories was slow to believe them.

The American Committee on Public Information, created within days of the 1917
declaration of war and run by the journalist George Creel, solved the problem
of reach differently. Alongside posters, films and a daily bulletin of
official news, it fielded an army of volunteer speakers — the Four Minute Men
— who stood up in cinemas during reel changes and delivered a short, centrally
themed talk to a captive audience. Tens of thousands of them, in towns too
small to support a newspaper, produced something no printing operation could:
saturation delivered by a neighbour.

The recoil came in the 1920s, when participants published memoirs and
academics began treating the whole business as a subject. Harold Lasswell's
study of wartime technique, published in 1927, established the analytical
vocabulary still in use. The Bolsheviks had already drawn a distinction, taken
over from earlier Russian Marxist writing, between the propagandist who
presents many ideas to a few people and the agitator who presents one idea to
a crowd. Both functions were built into the party apparatus, which is why
Soviet institutions carried the compound name agitprop.

## The Device Catalogue, and Its Limits

The Institute for Propaganda Analysis, founded in New York in 1937 by
educators and social scientists worried about domestic demagogues and European
radio, produced the list that still anchors most school media-literacy
teaching. Its seven devices were meant as a pocket field guide.

Name-calling attaches a hostile label to a person or policy so the label does
the arguing. Glittering generalities do the reverse, wrapping a proposal in
words — freedom, security, reform — that everyone approves of and nobody can
define narrowly enough to test. Transfer borrows authority from a symbol
placed alongside the message: a flag, a cathedral, a laboratory coat.
Testimonial substitutes a trusted person's endorsement for evidence. Plain
folks establishes that the speaker is ordinary, and therefore safe. Bandwagon
asserts that the matter is already settled and the audience is joining, not
deciding. Card stacking selects and arranges true material so that only one
conclusion appears to be available.

The Institute dissolved in the early 1940s, partly for lack of money and
partly because teaching citizens to be suspicious of official messages became
awkward once the United States was at war. The list survived it, though
scholars have never been satisfied with it. Its weakness is that it catalogues
surfaces. It tells you what a message looks like, not what it does, and a
sufficiently skilled communicator can avoid every one of the seven and still
run a campaign. Card stacking is the exception, and it has aged best, because
selection is the operation propaganda cannot do without. Change nothing, invent
nothing, simply choose which true things to put in front of people and in what
order, and you have most of the effect with none of the exposure that comes
from being caught in a lie.

## Agitation, Integration, and the Question of Source

Jacques Ellul, writing in France in the 1960s, argued that the loud kind of
propaganda everyone can recognise is the less consequential kind. Agitation
propaganda is short-term and mobilising; it works on existing discontent,
demands action, and burns out. Integration propaganda is ambient and slow. It
does not ask for anything. It supplies the assumptions people reason from, the
sense of what is normal, the feeling of being at home in a set of
arrangements, and it is carried as much by entertainment, advertising and
schooling as by anything a ministry issues. Ellul's more provocative claim was
that educated people are the easier target, because they consume more
information, hold more opinions about matters they cannot personally verify,
and are confident that manipulation is something that happens to other people.

Analysts of influence operations use a second axis, classifying material by
how honestly it identifies its sponsor. White propaganda names its source — an
information film with a government seal, a clearly badged state broadcaster.
Black propaganda falsely claims to come from somewhere else, as with the
Second World War stations that purported to broadcast from inside the enemy's
own armed forces. Grey sits between: the source is unstated or untraceable,
and the material circulates as though it had no origin. Grey is where most
contemporary work sits, because the cost of laundering a message through
sympathetic intermediaries, front organisations and anonymous accounts has
collapsed while the credibility premium for seeming unsponsored has not.

## What Researchers Learned About Effects

Early theorists assumed something close to injection — that a message reached
a mass audience and took. The first generation of empirical work took that
apart. Voter studies in the 1940s found that campaign communication mostly
reinforced existing preferences, that few people converted, and that messages
reached most citizens second-hand through locally influential acquaintances
rather than directly. Experiments run on soldiers watching wartime orientation
films found they learned facts from them, shifted attitudes only modestly, and
changed their willingness to fight hardly at all.

That "limited effects" conclusion was itself too narrow, and later work
recovered the middle ground. Agenda-setting research found that media coverage
is poor at telling people what to think and very good at determining what they
think about. Framing research showed that selecting and emphasising particular
aspects of a situation — calling a payment a levy or a contribution, a
combatant a militant or a defender — shapes the conclusions available without
any false statement. Psychological work on repetition established that a claim
encountered several times is rated more plausible than one encountered once,
regardless of its truth, because familiarity is easily mistaken for
corroboration.

The methods scholars use follow from this. Content analysis codes large bodies
of material against a defined scheme so that emphasis can be measured rather
than asserted. Framing and discourse analysis read closely for metaphor,
agency and what is left in the passive voice. Archival work reconstructs the
producing organisation: budgets, instructions to editors, internal assessments
of whether anything worked. Network analysis maps coordination among accounts
and outlets, looking for synchronised timing and shared infrastructure rather
than judging content at all. Experiments test specific mechanisms, including
whether warning people about a technique in advance — inoculation, in the
tradition running back to William McGuire's work in the 1960s — reduces its
later effect. It generally does, modestly, and the effect fades.

The practical residue of all this is a short list of questions worth asking of
any message that arrives with unusual force. Who produced it, and who paid.
What behaviour would count as success for them. What is absent that a fair
account would include. Which emotion the item is engineered to produce, and
whether that emotion is doing work that evidence should be doing. Whether the
central claim is stated in a form that could be checked. None of this is a
personality trait or a form of cleverness. It is a habit, and like most
habits it is easiest to keep when you apply it first to material you already
agree with.
