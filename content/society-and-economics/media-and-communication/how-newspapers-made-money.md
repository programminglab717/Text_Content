# How Newspapers Made Money

Pick up a metropolitan daily from 1985 and weigh it. On a Sunday it might run
to three hundred pages and four pounds, and the overwhelming majority of that
mass is not journalism. It is supermarket circulars, department store spreads,
automotive sections, six or eight dense pages of classified type advertising
apartments and welders and used Chevrolets, plus the coupons and the real
estate listings and the personals. A reader paying seventy-five cents for that
object was not paying for the paper it was printed on, let alone for the
statehouse bureau. The transaction that actually funded the enterprise was
happening somewhere else entirely, and understanding where is the whole of the
newspaper business.

A newspaper sells two different things to two different sets of customers, and
the second sale is the one that matters. It sells news to readers, usually at a
price well below what the copy costs to print and deliver. It then sells the
attention of those readers to advertisers, at a price set by how many of them
there are and how desirable they look. Economists call this a two-sided market,
and it has an odd internal logic: cutting the cover price can increase profit,
because every additional reader is inventory you can resell. For most of the
twentieth century, circulation revenue at a typical American daily covered
something in the neighborhood of a fifth of total revenue and sometimes barely
covered the cost of the trucks. Advertising paid for everything else.

## How the Trade Got That Way

It was not always so. The colonial and early republican press in America was a
printer's sideline, subsidized by government printing contracts, cheap postal
rates for newspapers, and party patronage. Papers cost six cents, sold by
annual subscription to merchants and the politically engaged, printed shipping
manifests and commodity prices and long unsigned invective, and made no
pretense of neutrality because their sponsors were the point. In Britain the
constraint was a stamp duty, a tax on advertisements, and a tax on paper —
collectively nicknamed the taxes on knowledge — which kept the legitimate press
expensive and respectable and drove a defiant unstamped press underground.
Those taxes came off in stages in the middle of the nineteenth century, and
British circulations immediately exploded.

The American break came earlier and from a different direction. In the 1830s a
handful of New York publishers began selling papers for a penny on the street,
through newsboys, one copy at a time, filled with police court reports, crime,
human interest, and stunts rather than commodity prices. The penny papers were
not cheaper because their proprietors were generous. They were cheaper because
a much larger circulation was worth more to advertisers than a small elite one,
and because new cylinder presses had made it possible to print enough copies to
matter. Within two decades the model had spread to every large city, and the
character of the press changed with it: aimed at everyone, sold on the street
by its front page, and dependent on the sort of advertiser who wants volume.

Two more pieces arrived quickly. The telegraph made distant news available but
charged by the word, so competing papers formed cooperatives to share the cost
of a single correspondent's dispatch — which is where the wire services come
from, and why wire copy is written in a flat, shareable register that offends
nobody. And the department store arrived. Large retailers with enormous
inventories and thin margins needed to tell a whole city what was on sale this
week, and the newspaper was the only instrument that could do it. The
full-page store advertisement and the modern daily grew up together, each
dependent on the other.

The advertising dependence quietly rewrote the editorial product too. A partisan
sheet aimed at one faction has a ceiling on its circulation equal to the size of
that faction. A paper that wants every household in the county cannot afford to
insult half of them, and neither can the wire service selling the same dispatch
to a Democratic paper in Ohio and a Republican one in Maine. The professional
norms that hardened in the early twentieth century — attribution, balance, the
separation of news columns from the editorial page, the reporter's studied
absence from their own copy — were genuine intellectual commitments held by
people who believed in them. They were also, conveniently, the editorial posture
that maximizes the addressable market. Objectivity was an ethic with a business
case underneath it, which is part of why it took hold so completely in a
commercial press and so much less completely in the subsidized European party
papers of the same period.

## Fixed Costs and the One-Paper Town

The structural fact that shaped everything after that is the cost curve. The
first copy of a newspaper is ruinously expensive: reporters, editors, a
building, a press hall, a wire subscription, the accumulated work of a day. The
second copy costs newsprint, ink, and a marginal turn of the press. The
thousandth costs the same. That shape — enormous fixed cost, small and falling
marginal cost — produces a market that tends toward a single winner.

The mechanism is usually called the circulation spiral, and it is brutally
simple. The larger paper in a town offers advertisers more readers per dollar,
so it wins more advertising, so it can afford more pages and more reporters and
better comics, so it attracts more readers, so its advantage widens. The
smaller paper's costs do not fall as it shrinks; its fixed costs are nearly the
same. It bleeds until it folds or merges. Between the 1920s and the 1970s
nearly every American city that had supported four or five competing dailies
narrowed to one, or to a morning and an evening under joint ownership. This was
not primarily a story of chain owners buying up independents, though that
happened. It was arithmetic.

Within that consolidation there was a second, slower casualty. Afternoon papers
had once outnumbered morning ones, printed for a workforce that finished shifts
at four and read on the streetcar home. Suburbanization put those readers in
cars on congested highways where nobody reads anything, and the evening network
newscast got to the day's news first and free. Delivery trucks that once moved
through a compact city at midday now crawled through afternoon traffic to reach
subdivisions twenty miles out. Evening dailies died across the country through
the 1970s and 1980s, often by folding into their morning sibling, and the
morning-only metro became the standard shape of the business.

The cost side had its own pressures. Newsprint is a globally traded commodity
whose price swings violently, and it was typically the second-largest line in a
publisher's budget after payroll, which meant a bad year in pulp markets could
erase a good year in advertising. Production was also heavily unionized and
heavily skilled: hot-metal composition by linotype operators, stereotypers,
pressmen, mailers. The shift to photocomposition and offset printing in the
1960s and 1970s eliminated whole crafts, produced some of the bitterest strikes
in American and British labour history, and left publishers with cost
structures that looked, for about twenty years, extraordinarily comfortable.

The surviving monopoly daily in a mid-sized city was, by the late twentieth
century, one of the most profitable businesses in the country. Operating
margins of twenty to thirty percent were ordinary. The moat was physical:
anyone wanting to compete needed a press costing millions, a distribution
network of trucks and contract carriers, and a sales force with relationships
across every retail category in town. It is worth being precise about what that
protected. It protected the advertising franchise. The journalism was a
byproduct that the franchise could afford, and largely an excellent one,
because a monopoly with high margins and a professional culture can fund a
Baghdad bureau out of petty cash.

## The Bundle, and What Broke It

The product itself was a bundle, and that turns out to be the hinge of the
whole story. Inside one physical object, sold as a unit, were a dozen
unrelated services: yesterday's baseball scores, the television grid, the
comics, the crossword, the death notices, the grocery coupons, the classifieds,
the stock tables, the weather, and a small amount of original reporting about
government. Readers bought the bundle for whatever they personally wanted from
it. Advertisers paid for the aggregate. Nobody was asked to value the
statehouse coverage separately, which was fortunate, because comparatively few
people would have paid much for it.

Classified advertising was the most lucrative strand. Want ads, help wanted,
apartments, and used cars are close to pure profit — set in tiny type by the
customer's own words, requiring almost no production cost, priced per line, and
renewed weekly by people with a real need. In the good years classifieds could
approach a third of a metro daily's revenue. Australian publishers called the
category rivers of gold, which was not hyperbole.

The bundle came apart strand by strand, and it came apart from the profitable
end first. A free listings site does classifieds better than a newspaper can:
searchable, unlimited length, photographs, no deadline, and cheap or free.
Specialized job boards, car marketplaces, and apartment sites took the rest.
Stock tables, television listings, weather, and sports scores all became
commodities available instantly and free, so the incidental reasons to buy a
paper evaporated. Then display advertising migrated to platforms that could
target a specific person rather than a citywide readership, and could charge
for a measured click instead of an estimated impression. Each of these
departures was rational for the advertiser and invisible to the reader, and
together they removed the cross-subsidy that had paid for everything the paper
did that nobody would pay for directly.

What is left is an industry trying to make reader revenue do the work that
advertising did for a century and a half. Metered paywalls turned out to work
far better than the early consensus predicted, but they work unevenly. A
handful of national and global titles with distinctive reporting have built
large digital subscription businesses; a daily in a city of two hundred
thousand generally cannot, because its readers can get national news elsewhere
and there are not enough of them to support a newsroom on subscriptions alone.
The result is visible on any map of American local news: counties with no daily
at all, papers reduced to a single reporter, chains stripping assets, and a
patchwork of nonprofit newsrooms funded by foundations and members that
collectively replace a fraction of what was lost.

It is tempting to read this as a technology story, and partly it is. But the
underlying point is narrower and older. Journalism about city councils and
zoning boards and county sheriffs was never a product with a market of its own.
It was financed, for about a hundred and fifty years, by an accident — the fact
that the cheapest way to tell a city about a sale on mattresses happened to be
a machine that also employed reporters.
