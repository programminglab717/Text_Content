# What Advertising Actually Does

On the side of a brick building in almost any old American city, usually facing
a parking lot that used to be another building, there is a painted sign for
something that no longer exists. Flour, a tonic, a brand of plug tobacco, a
hotel. The paint has thinned to a ghost, and the product is gone, but the
lettering is still legible enough to read from the street eighty or a hundred
years later. Someone paid a wall-dog to climb a rig and spend a week putting it
there, and had a theory about why it was worth the money. That theory is the
interesting part, because a century of argument about advertising has still not
fully settled what the money buys.

There are three serious answers, and they are not mutually exclusive. The first
is that advertising informs. A person who does not know that a shop exists, or
what it charges, or that a product has been improved, cannot act on that
information, and searching costs time. On this account advertising lubricates
markets and makes them more competitive, and the evidence usually cited is
about prices: in categories where advertising is restricted by professional
rules or law, prices tend to be higher than where it is permitted. The second
answer is that advertising persuades — that it does not merely report a
preference but manufactures one, attaching feeling to an otherwise
undifferentiated commodity. The third is subtler. Advertising may work partly
as a signal. Mounting an expensive campaign is a way of burning money publicly,
and a firm selling something shoddy cannot afford to burn money on customers
who will never return. The extravagance itself is the message: we expect you to
come back.

## The Barrel and the Package

You can watch these forces get organized around a single historical shift. For
most of the nineteenth century a grocer sold crackers out of an open barrel,
soap cut from a long bar, flour scooped from a bin. The customer's trust was in
the grocer, who selected the goods and stood behind them. The manufacturer was
anonymous and interchangeable, and competed only on price to the wholesaler.

Sealed packaging changed the location of trust. Once a biscuit came in a
printed carton with a waxed paper liner and a name on it, the maker could
promise something directly to the eater and could be blamed directly if the
promise failed. The National Biscuit Company's campaign for a branded soda
cracker at the end of the 1890s is the usual textbook case, and the significance
is not the advertising but what the advertising made possible: a national
manufacturer could now build a relationship with millions of households over
the grocer's head, charge a premium for it, and defend that premium against a
cheaper identical product sitting beside it. Branded packaged goods and mass
advertising are the same phenomenon viewed from two directions. Soap, cereal,
cigarettes, soft drinks, and patent medicines — categories where the physical
product is cheap, replicable, and hard to distinguish by inspection — became and
remained the heaviest advertisers, which is exactly what the theory predicts.

The agency business grew up alongside it and acquired a peculiar shape. The
first agents were space brokers: they bought newspaper columns wholesale and
resold them to advertisers at a markup, and they worked for the publisher, not
the advertiser. Over time the relationship inverted — agencies began
representing the client, planning where the money should go, and eventually
writing the copy and making the pictures. But the compensation structure never
fully caught up. Agencies were paid a commission on the media they bought,
conventionally around fifteen percent, which meant the creative work was
nominally free and the incentive ran toward spending more. That arrangement
survived for the better part of a century and is a large part of why the
industry developed as it did.

## Reason Why, and the Revolt Against It

Two temperaments have alternated in the copywriting trade since the beginning.
The first insists that an advertisement is a salesman in print, and that its job
is to make a specific claim and give a reason to believe it. Claude Hopkins is
its patron saint. He tested everything he could with coupons, counted returns,
and built campaigns on what he called preemptive claims — describing some
ordinary step in a manufacturing process that every competitor also performed,
but that nobody had bothered to tell the public about. His famous demonstration
involved a brewery whose bottles were sterilized with live steam, a routine
practice he described in such vivid detail that the claim became the brand's
property. The technique is honest in the narrow sense and mildly deceptive in
the broad one, which is a fair summary of the tradition.

The second temperament holds that people are not persuaded by arguments about
soap, and that what an advertisement really does is make you feel something and
attach the feeling to a name. The creative revolution of the late 1950s and
1960s in New York is the high point of this view, and its central figure argued
that execution — wit, surprise, art direction, restraint — was itself the
message. The campaign that made the case was for a small, ugly, foreign car
sold to a country that wanted large chrome ones, and it succeeded by conceding
everything: admitting the car was homely, admitting it was slow, printing vast
white space with a tiny photograph, and trusting the reader to notice that only
a confident company talks that way. The rival rental car firm that announced it
was in second place and therefore tried harder was working the same trick. Both
are, on inspection, signalling arguments rather than informational ones.

Research arrived in between and never quite settled anything. Firms counted
readership and recall, ran split tests in different cities, and convened small
groups of consumers to talk about what a product meant to them. In the 1950s a
vogue for depth psychology produced motivational research, with its confident
pronouncements about what a convertible or a cake mix symbolized, and a
bestselling book that told the public it was being manipulated by people who
understood its unconscious better than it did. The panic peaked with subliminal
advertising — the claim that flashed messages in a cinema had increased popcorn
sales — which turned out to be a fabrication by the man who announced it, and
which is still cited as fact more often than the retraction is.

## The Measurement Problem

The oldest complaint in the business is a line usually attributed to the
department store magnate John Wanamaker: half the money spent on advertising is
wasted, and nobody knows which half. The complaint is precise about the real
difficulty. Advertising's effect has to be separated from everything else
moving at the same time — seasons, prices, distribution, competitors,
weather — and the most heavily advertised products are also the ones already
selling, so correlation is nearly useless.

Digital advertising promised to end this, and in an important sense it made it
worse. Clicks and conversions are countable, which meant attribution models
could assign credit to particular impressions and report precise returns, and
precision was mistaken for accuracy. The structural problem is selection:
targeting systems are designed to show advertisements to the people most likely
to buy, which are disproportionately the people who would have bought anyway.
When large advertisers have run genuine experiments — switching campaigns off
in randomly chosen regions and comparing — the measured effects have sometimes
been a small fraction of what the attribution dashboards reported, and in at
least one well-documented case involving branded search terms, close to nothing.
Advertising certainly works. Knowing how much, for a particular campaign, in a
way that survives a controlled test, remains genuinely hard.

Meanwhile the money reorganized the media it funded. An auction running in the
fraction of a second before a page loads decides which advertiser gets the
space, with the price set by competing bids, and the publisher's share of what
the advertiser spends passes through a chain of intermediaries taking margins
along the way. The same logic that built painted walls and sponsored radio
serials now builds recommendation systems, because the product being refined is
the same one it always was: the probability that a particular person is paying
attention at a particular moment.

What that arrangement does to a society is a separate question, argued mostly
between two positions. One holds that advertising is a subsidy — that nobody
would pay directly for most of the news, entertainment, and search infrastructure
they consume, and that the alternative to advertising is not a purer media
ecosystem but a much smaller and more expensive one. The other holds that
production creates the wants it then satisfies, that a system dedicated to
manufacturing dissatisfaction has real costs even when every individual
transaction is voluntary, and that attention taken without consent is a genuine
externality rather than a fee honestly paid.

Both are right about something, which is why the argument does not resolve. It
is worth noticing, though, that neither side disputes the basic mechanism. A
message put in front of enough people, often enough, in a form they do not
resent too much, changes what some of them buy — reliably enough that a
manufacturer will pay a man to spend a week on a scaffold painting a wall, and
reliably enough that the wall is still being read a century after the company
went under.
