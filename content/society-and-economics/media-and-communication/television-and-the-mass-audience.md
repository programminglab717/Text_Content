# Television and the Mass Audience

There is a particular kind of memory that belongs only to a few decades of the
twentieth century: walking down a residential street on a warm evening and
hearing the same laugh track coming out of every open window at the same
moment. Not similar programs. The same program, at the same second, in every
house on the block, and in most houses in the country. For roughly thirty-five
years that was an ordinary experience, and it has since become an impossible
one. The mass audience that television assembled was not a natural condition of
modern life. It was the product of a specific and fairly brief set of technical
and regulatory accidents, and when those accidents were corrected the audience
came apart.

The scarcity came first. Television needs far more bandwidth than radio, and
the very high frequency band allocated for it in the United States had room for
a limited number of channels, of which only a handful could be used in any one
city without interference. Regulators froze new station licences at the end of
the 1940s while they sorted out the mess, and by the time the freeze lifted and
the higher UHF band was opened, the VHF stations had a four-year head start,
better propagation, and sets that in many cases could not tune UHF at all. The
outcome was structural: three national networks, and in most markets three
viable stations. Britain arrived at an even tighter version — a single public
broadcaster until the mid-1950s, then one commercial competitor — and much of
Europe ran one or two state channels. Everywhere, the number of things a person
could watch at eight o'clock was small enough to count on one hand.

Adoption was the fastest of any household technology to that point. At the
start of the 1950s a television set was a neighborhood curiosity that people
crowded into a bar or a wealthier cousin's parlour to see. By the end of the
decade it was in the large majority of American homes, and the furniture had
been rearranged around it. Cinema attendance fell hard. Evening radio
programming collapsed. The pattern of the American evening — dinner, then a
block of hours facing the same direction — was set within about ten years and
held for two generations.

## The Audience as a Product

What the networks actually sold was not programming. It was delivered
attention, measured and priced by the thousand, and the measurement apparatus
mattered as much as the cameras. Ratings firms recruited panels of households,
first with paper diaries and mechanical recorders, later with meters wired to
the set and eventually with devices that asked each person in the room to press
a button identifying themselves. From these small samples, national audience
estimates were extrapolated, and from those estimates flowed the price of a
thirty-second spot, the renewal of a series, and the career of everyone
involved.

This arrangement produces a specific kind of content, and the industry was
candid about it. With three choices and a viewer who has already decided to
watch television, the winner is not the show most people love. It is the show
the fewest people will turn away from — a programming executive of the period
called it the least objectionable program, and meant it as description rather
than complaint. Offence, difficulty, ambiguity, and anything that drove a
particular demographic out of the room were expensive. The result was a
midcentury schedule of extraordinary competence and extraordinary caution.

The other design principle was flow. Raymond Williams, watching American
television for the first time in the 1970s, noticed that the real object was
not the individual program but the continuous, planned sequence — program into
advertisement into promotion into next program, with the seams deliberately
smoothed so that stopping required an act of will. Schedules were built to hand
audiences forward: a strong show at eight existed partly to deliver viewers to
a weak one at eight-thirty. Nobody was expected to choose a program. They were
expected to choose an evening.

One structural change is worth singling out. In the early years sponsors owned
their shows outright, inherited from radio practice, which is why a drama
anthology carried a manufacturer's name in its title. The quiz show scandals at
the end of the 1950s — rigged outcomes, coached contestants, congressional
hearings — gave the networks both a reason and a pretext to seize control of
their own schedules. Afterwards, advertisers bought spots scattered across the
lineup rather than programs, which spread their risk, spread their reach, and
concentrated editorial power in three corporate broadcast divisions. Nearly
everything people mean by the golden age of network television happened under
that arrangement.

## What Simultaneity Did

The interesting question is not what people watched but what it meant that
everyone watched it together. A shared broadcast creates common knowledge in
the technical sense: I saw it, and I know you saw it, and I know you know I saw
it. That is a powerful social substance. It gave a continent-sized country a
set of references — catchphrases, theme songs, faces — that crossed regions and
classes that otherwise had almost nothing in common. It made the Monday morning
conversation possible.

It also made a genuinely national politics possible, and a national grief. The
long weekend of coverage after the Kennedy assassination is usually described
as the moment television became the country's nervous system: the networks
dropped commercials, people did not leave their sets, and the funeral was
watched in something close to unison. The first moon landing was watched by a
substantial fraction of humanity at the same instant. Vietnam was the first war
whose footage arrived in living rooms in time to matter, and the civil rights
movement was strategically conceived in part around the certainty that
televised images of police dogs and fire hoses would be seen in northern homes
that would otherwise never have encountered them.

Claims about television's effects have often run further than the evidence
supports. The widely repeated story that radio listeners thought Nixon won the
first 1960 debate while television viewers thought Kennedy did rests on very
thin polling and has been picked apart for years, though it survives because it
is such a tidy illustration. The argument that heavy viewing cultivates an
exaggerated fear of crime is more substantial but contested. The broader thesis
that television hollowed out civic life — that the hours it absorbed came
directly out of clubs, unions, church suppers, and visiting — is a serious one,
supported by the timing of the decline in American associational membership and
by the correlation between when television arrived in a community and when its
civic participation started falling. Causation is hard here. But the claim
requires no exotic mechanism: an evening spent one way is an evening not spent
another way, and television took four hours a day.

## The Slow Unbundling

The mass audience began to erode almost as soon as it was complete, and it
eroded through devices rather than through any change in taste. The remote
control made abandoning a program nearly free, which killed the economics of
flow. The videocassette recorder detached programs from schedules and let
viewers skip advertisements, which is why broadcasters fought it in court.
Cable, which had begun in the 1940s as a way to get a decent picture into
mountain valleys, turned into something else when satellites made national
distribution cheap: a movie channel supported by subscription rather than
advertising, then an all-news channel, then an all-sports channel, then
hundreds more, each viable at an audience a network would have cancelled
instantly.

Cable channels also had a revenue structure the broadcasters lacked — carriage
fees from the cable operator plus advertising — which meant a channel could
survive by being indispensable to a small, defined group rather than tolerable
to everyone. That inverted the incentive that had governed the medium. It
became rational to make programs that some people loved and others detested,
which is the commercial precondition for the ambitious serialized drama that
arrived in the following decades. The audience fragmented, and the work got
better and narrower at the same time.

Streaming finished it. On-demand delivery abolishes the schedule, which was the
last shared thing: even viewers watching the same series are not watching it
together, and the release of a full season at once makes simultaneity a
scheduling choice rather than a fact of the technology. What survives of the
old condition is almost entirely live sport, which is the one product that
cannot be watched later without being ruined, and which is consequently now
priced as though it were the only thing on television. In a sense it is.

Looking back, the striking thing is how short the era was. A person born early
enough to remember a house without a set could live to watch their grandchildren
stream different programs on four devices in the same room. The mass audience
looked permanent while it lasted because everything about daily life was
organized around it. It was really an artifact of a spectrum shortage.
