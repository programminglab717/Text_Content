# The Invention of Zoning

In 1915 a forty-story office tower opened on lower Broadway in Manhattan,
rising straight from its lot lines with no setback anywhere on its face. The
Equitable Building was entirely legal. Nothing in New York law said a building
could not cover its whole parcel and climb as high as the steel frame would
carry it. The owners of the properties across the street, watching their
rentable daylight disappear behind an acre of masonry, discovered they had no
remedy worth pursuing — and concluded that the only way to get one was to
invent a new kind of law.

What they got, the following year, was the 1916 Zoning Resolution, generally
described as the first comprehensive zoning ordinance in the United States. It
did three things at once, and nearly every American zoning code since has done
the same three. It divided the city into districts and specified which
activities were permitted in each. It capped how much building a lot could
hold. And it required that above a certain height a tower step back from the
street by a fixed amount for each further increment of height, so a wedge of
sky stayed visible from the sidewalk. That last provision is why Manhattan
buildings from the 1920s and 1930s taper upward in stages like ziggurats. The
silhouette of that era came out of an arithmetic formula, not an aesthetic
movement.

## Before the Map, There Was the Lawsuit

Zoning replaced nuisance law, and the appeal of the replacement makes sense
only if you see what nuisance could not do. Under common law a landowner harmed
by a neighbor's activity — a tannery, a rendering plant, a livery stable, a
foundry throwing sparks onto wooden roofs — could sue, and ask a court to
enjoin the activity or pay for the damage. This worked, slowly, one dispute at
a time, and only after the harm had already happened. It told nobody in advance
what the parcel next door might become. A builder could not know whether the
row of houses he was financing would face a gasworks in three years. A judge
could not plan a city; he could only rule on the quarrel in front of him.

Zoning's promise was to move the decision forward in time and up in scale, to
declare on a map, before anyone built anything, which activities belonged
where. Justice George Sutherland reached for precisely that logic in 1926 when
the Supreme Court upheld zoning for the first time, observing that a nuisance
is often simply a right thing in the wrong place — a pig in the parlor rather
than the barnyard. The case, Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty, came out of a
Cleveland suburb that had zoned a company's acreage for housing and thereby cut
its value as industrial land. The Court held that sorting a municipality by use
was a legitimate exercise of the police power rather than a taking of property,
and the question of whether American cities would be zoned was effectively
closed.

The American story has an uglier parallel track running alongside it. Some of
the earliest use-district ordinances were aimed at particular people rather
than particular hazards: a California ordinance of the 1880s restricting where
laundries could operate was in practice a measure against Chinese laundrymen.
Several cities in the 1910s adopted openly racial zoning, assigning blocks to
white or Black occupancy. The Supreme Court struck those down in Buchanan v.
Warley in 1917 — on property-rights grounds rather than equality grounds — and
the sorting function migrated into instruments that did not have to name race
at all: minimum lot sizes, prohibitions on apartment buildings, restrictive
covenants written into deeds, and eventually federal mortgage underwriting
standards that treated mixed neighborhoods as poor collateral.

## The Model Statute and the Crown at the Top

Zoning spread across the country with unusual speed for an American legal
innovation, and the reason is bureaucratic rather than ideological. In the
1920s the federal Commerce Department, then under Herbert Hoover, published a
model statute — a standard zoning enabling act — that state legislatures could
adopt nearly verbatim to grant their municipalities the authority to zone. Most
of them did, often copying whole paragraphs. The model arrived with a structure
already built into it: a ladder of districts running from most protected to
least, with the detached single-family house on the top rung.

That hierarchy inverted how cities had historically worked. A nineteenth-century
street was routinely mixed by floor as well as by block — a shop below, rooms
above, a workshop in the rear yard. The new codes treated mixture itself as the
problem and separation as the cure, and because the ladder was one-directional,
the protected district admitted nothing below it. An apartment building was
excluded from a single-family district not because apartments are dangerous but
because the ordinance placed them lower on the ladder. Sutherland's own opinion
described apartment houses, in a residential district, as something close to a
parasite. A doctrine invented to keep slaughterhouses away from bedrooms was
soon being used to keep duplexes away from bungalows.

## The Numbers Do More Work Than the Map

People argue about the colors on a zoning map, but most of the binding
constraints are the numerical standards printed in the accompanying tables. A
parcel zoned for commercial use may still be impossible to develop
commercially. Floor area ratio sets total building floor area as a multiple of
lot area, so an FAR of 1.0 on a ten-thousand-square-foot lot permits ten
thousand square feet of building however you arrange it. Lot coverage limits
the footprint. Front, side, and rear setbacks push the building inward from
every edge. Minimum lot size sets the smallest parcel that may hold a house,
and therefore the maximum number of houses per acre no matter how small the
houses are. Height limits cap the stack. And parking minimums require a
specified number of spaces per dwelling unit or per thousand square feet of
floor space.

Parking requirements deserve particular attention because they are so often
decisive and so rarely debated. A surface parking space with its share of drive
aisle consumes something on the order of three hundred square feet. Requiring
two spaces per apartment can therefore demand more land for cars than for
people, and on a small urban parcel the requirement alone can make a project
geometrically impossible — the site cannot hold both the building the market
wants and the parking the code insists on. The economist Donald Shoup spent a
career pointing out that these numbers were mostly copied between cities
without being derived from anything, and that they constitute a substantial
hidden subsidy to driving, paid for in the price of everything built near the
parking.

## Other Countries Wrote Different Rules

None of this was inevitable, which becomes clear the moment you look at how
other wealthy countries regulate land. Japan zones at the national level, using
roughly a dozen standard categories that apply identically in every
municipality, and the categories are nested rather than exclusive: a zone that
permits light industry also permits shops and housing, and the most restrictive
residential zone still allows small stores and clinics. A category defines the
worst nuisance permitted, not a list of everything else forbidden. The practical
effect is that a corner shop, a small apartment building, and a house can share
a street without anyone needing a variance, and that national rules are hard for
a single neighborhood to capture.

Houston, famously, has no zoning ordinance, having rejected one by referendum
more than once. It is not an unregulated city, though. Private deed
restrictions, enforced by the municipality, do much of the work of use zoning in
its older neighborhoods, and until reforms beginning in the late 1990s the city
imposed minimum lot sizes that constrained density about as firmly as a zoning
map would have. When those minimums were relaxed, townhouses appeared on
subdivided lots across the inner city almost immediately — a fairly clean
natural experiment in what a single number in a code can hold back.

## The Reform Wave

For most of a century, zoning changes moved in one direction: down. Densities
were reduced, minimum lot sizes raised, apartment districts rezoned to
single-family. In the last decade that direction has begun to reverse in
places. Minneapolis rewrote its comprehensive plan to allow up to three units
on lots that had permitted one. Oregon required its larger cities to legalize
duplexes and, in the biggest of them, fourplexes. California created a statewide
right to split a single-family lot and add units. Auckland substantially upzoned
a large share of its residential land in a single consolidated plan.

The results so far suggest that legalization is necessary and not sufficient.
Where upzoning has been paired with workable standards — reasonable setbacks,
no parking mandate, a permitting path that does not require discretionary
approval — new units have appeared in meaningful numbers. Where a jurisdiction
technically legalized a fourplex but left a minimum lot size, two required
parking spaces, and a design review hearing in place, almost nothing was built,
and both sides of the argument claimed vindication. The map changed color. The
table of numbers underneath it did not.
