# How a Census Gets Taken

In the winter of 1086, teams of royal commissioners rode out across England
with a list of questions. How many hides of land does this manor hold? Who held
it before the Conquest and who holds it now? How many ploughs, how many villeins
and cottars and slaves, how much meadow and woodland and pasture, how many mills
and fishponds? What was it worth then, what is it worth now, and could it be
made to yield more? The answers were sworn to by juries of local men, cross-
checked against a second set of commissioners sent to territory they had no
interest in, and copied up into two volumes. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle recorded
the exercise with something close to disgust, complaining that the king's men
were so thorough that not an ox nor a cow nor a pig escaped their writing. It
was shameful to do, the chronicler added, and the king did not think it shameful
to do.

That reaction is the oldest and most consistent fact about census-taking. Being
counted has almost always meant being made legible to a power that wants
something, and the resistance to enumeration is not irrational paranoia but an
accurate reading of what enumeration was historically for.

## Counting for Extraction

The Roman census, from which the word descends, was a periodic assembly at which
male citizens declared themselves, their households, and their property to the
censors, who assigned them to a class. That classification determined their tax
liability, their place in the military levy, and the weight of their vote in the
centuriate assembly. It closed with a purification ritual, the lustrum, which is
how a five-year period came to be called by that name. The Chinese imperial
state ran household registers on an extraordinary scale from the Han dynasty
onward, recording households and individuals for the purposes of taxation,
corvée labour, and conscription, and producing totals in the tens of millions
that remain among the most impressive administrative achievements of the ancient
world. The Inca kept their own population accounts on knotted cord, organised
through a decimal hierarchy of officials responsible for ten households, a
hundred, a thousand, with each rank reporting upward.

What all of these have in common is direction of flow. The information goes up,
the demand comes down, and the person counted gets nothing in particular for
having been counted.

The suspicion ran deep enough to become religious. The Hebrew Bible tells of a
census ordered by David that is treated as a transgression and followed by
plague, and Jewish practice preserved a lasting reluctance to count people
directly — a room is checked for a quorum by reciting a verse with the right
number of words, or by counting "not one, not two," rather than pointing at
heads. Similar unease surfaces elsewhere. Counting a herd invites the evil eye;
counting children tempts fate. Underneath the superstition sits a practical
observation that peasants everywhere made independently: the man with the tally
sheet is followed sooner or later by the man with the tax demand or the
recruiting sergeant.

The modern census reverses part of that. When the framers of the United States
Constitution required an actual enumeration of the population every ten years,
they tied it not to taxation alone but to representation: seats in the House
would be apportioned among the states according to the count. This made the
census something citizens had a positive interest in — a place with more people
got more power — and it built in a permanent incentive for everyone to want
their own side counted fully, which turns out to be a better guarantee of
completeness than any amount of administrative zeal. It also built in an
equally permanent incentive to fight over the rules, which is why the American
census has been politically contentious more or less continuously since 1790.

## The Questions Behind the Questions

Every census rests on definitions that look technical and are not. The first is
residence. A de facto census counts people where they physically were on a
particular night; a de jure census counts them where they usually live. The
distinction matters enormously for university towns, military bases, resort
areas, and prisons. Counting incarcerated people at the prison rather than at
their home address shifts population, and therefore legislative seats and
funding, from the urban neighbourhoods most prisoners come from to the rural
counties where prisons are built — a quirk that produces real political
consequences and that several jurisdictions have recently legislated against.
Similar arguments attach to students counted at university, to citizens living
abroad, to people with two homes, and to anyone whose living arrangements do not
resolve into a single address.

The second is the category system. A census does not merely record race,
ethnicity, religion, language, caste, or disability; it fixes a list of
permissible answers, and the list is an act of government. Categories appear,
merge, split, and vanish between rounds, and people who lived their whole lives
without a label acquire one. Once a category exists and is counted, resources,
quotas, and grievances attach to it, and it becomes very difficult to remove.
Colonial administrations were especially consequential here, because their
enumerations hardened fluid local identities into fixed administrative ones that
outlasted the empires that invented them.

## Getting the Answers In

For most of the modern era, a census meant enumerators on foot. Districts were
carved up, temporary staff hired in enormous numbers for a few weeks, and
schedules filled out doorstep by doorstep in handwriting that the tabulators
afterwards had to decipher. This is why old census returns are such rich
documents for genealogists and social historians: an enumerator wrote down what
he was told, plus sometimes what he thought, and the result is a snapshot of a
household's composition, occupations, birthplaces, and infirmities in one line.

Processing was the bottleneck. The 1880 United States census took the better
part of a decade to tabulate by hand, which raised the real prospect that the
next one would not be finished before the one after it began. Herman Hollerith,
who had worked on that census, built a machine that read holes punched in cards
by means of spring-loaded pins dipping into cups of mercury to close a circuit,
advancing counters and sorting cards into bins. The 1890 count was tabulated in
a fraction of the previous time, and Hollerith's company eventually became part
of the firm that grew into IBM. Mechanised data processing was, in a real sense,
invented in order to count people.

Later came self-enumeration — mailing forms to households and asking them to
fill them in — which is far cheaper and produces better answers to sensitive
questions, at the cost of missing everyone who does not return the form.
Follow-up visits target the non-responders, and the cost per additional
household rises steeply as the remaining cases get harder. Internet response has
now displaced much of the paper, though it introduces its own problem: verifying
that a household submitting online is the household it claims to be.

## Who Gets Missed

No census counts everyone, and the failures are not random. Undercounts fall
disproportionately on young children, renters and recent movers, people in
irregular housing or sharing crowded accommodation, undocumented migrants, and
anyone with reason to avoid officials. Because those characteristics cluster
geographically, the errors cluster too, and a uniform-sounding national accuracy
figure conceals neighbourhoods missed by a tenth or more while affluent suburbs
are counted almost perfectly.

Statisticians measure this with post-enumeration surveys — an independent
recount of a sample of areas, matched case by case against the census returns to
estimate how many were missed and how many counted twice. What to do with the
result is the contentious part. Adjusting the published figures using
statistical estimation would make the totals more accurate but makes the numbers
partly a product of a model, and in the United States, where the count directly
determines political power, that proposal has been fought all the way to the
Supreme Court.

A newer difficulty has emerged from the richness of published tables. When a
statistical agency releases counts broken down by small geography and by several
characteristics at once, the cells get thin — one household of a given
composition on a given block — and it becomes possible to work backwards from
published aggregates, especially when they can be matched against commercial
databases, to reconstruct records about identifiable people. The United States
responded in its 2020 count by injecting calibrated statistical noise into the
published tables under a framework known as differential privacy, which bounds
mathematically how much any one person's participation can affect what is
released. Demographers, epidemiologists, and local officials objected loudly
that deliberately corrupted small-area figures damage exactly the uses the
census exists to serve. Both sides are right, which is what makes it a genuine
trade-off rather than a mistake.

Confidentiality is the other side of accuracy, because people answer honestly
only if they believe the answers cannot be used against them. Most countries now
seal individual records for decades — seventy-two years in the United States, a
century in Britain — and publish only aggregates. The protections are taken
seriously and have still been breached. During the Second World War the United
States Census Bureau supplied neighbourhood-level tabulations that assisted in
the internment of Japanese Americans, and historians working in the archives
later established that individual information was provided as well. The Dutch
civil registration system, admired before the war as the most complete in
Europe, gave the German occupation an accurate list of who was Jewish and where
they lived; the resistance mounted an armed raid on the Amsterdam registry
office in 1943 specifically to burn the records. Postwar Dutch discomfort with
population registration is one reason the Netherlands abandoned the traditional
door-to-door census altogether.

## The Census Without Enumerators

That abandonment pointed toward what several countries now do. The Nordic states
maintain continuously updated population registers, in which each resident has
an identity number linked to address, household, employment, education, and
income records. A census becomes a matter of extracting and linking files on a
reference date — no forms, no enumerators, no follow-up, at a small fraction of
the cost, and repeatable annually rather than once a decade.

The trade-offs are substantial. Register-based counting requires a society
comfortable with a comprehensive, permanently maintained state record of where
everyone lives, and it measures only what the registers happen to contain, which
excludes most questions about identity, belief, or how people actually organise
their households. Registers also drift from reality: people move without
notifying anyone, and the file says they are still at the old address. The
traditional census is slow, expensive, and increasingly hard to staff, but it
has one durable virtue. It goes and looks.
