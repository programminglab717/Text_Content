# What Public Space Is For

The plaza in front of the Seagram Building on Park Avenue is, by most design
standards, a cold place. Granite, two shallow pools, almost no planting, a
building set back behind it like a slab of bronze. It has no playground, no
kiosk, no shade structure. And yet when researchers pointed time-lapse cameras
at Manhattan's open spaces in the 1970s, the Seagram plaza turned out to be one
of the most heavily used in the city, while other plazas a few blocks away —
newer, more expensively landscaped, equipped with more features — sat empty all
day. The difference was not aesthetics. It was ledges. The Seagram plaza has
long, broad, waist-high parapets running along its edges at exactly the height
of a chair seat, and people sit on them by the hundred.

William H. Whyte, who ran that study, kept arriving at findings of the same
unglamorous kind. People sit where there is something to sit on. They sit in
the sun in cold weather and the shade in warm weather. They stand talking in
the middle of the pedestrian flow rather than off to the side, blocking the very
traffic they are part of. Food attracts people, and so does water they are
allowed to touch. The single best predictor of whether people would use a space
was whether other people were already using it. Whyte's conclusion, which sounds
banal until you count how many built plazas ignore it, was that what attracts
people most in a city is other people.

## The Old Argument About Squares

Public space is not a modern invention needing justification. The Greek agora
was simultaneously a market, a law court, a gossip exchange, and the place where
citizens assembled to decide things; the Roman forum served the same braided
function. Medieval European towns grew around market squares whose use was
governed by charter — who could trade there, on what days, under whose weights
and measures. What those spaces had in common was not beauty or leisure. They
were where the business of a settlement physically happened, and being present
in them was close to a condition of membership.

The modern public park comes from somewhere else entirely. It was a nineteenth
century public health and public order project, conceived for industrial cities
whose working populations lived in conditions that alarmed the people who ran
them. Birkenhead Park outside Liverpool, opened in the 1840s, was among the
first parks built with public money for general use rather than opened to
visitors by an aristocrat's permission. Frederick Law Olmsted visited it and
carried its lessons back to New York, where the Greensward plan he made with
Calvert Vaux for Central Park treated the park as a deliberate machine: sunken
transverse roads so cross-town traffic would not disturb the pastoral illusion,
sequences of meadow and wood intended to be experienced in motion, a bridle
path, a lake. Olmsted was explicit that he wanted classes who would never
otherwise meet to share ground. Whether that worked is arguable. That it was the
point is not.

Then came the twentieth century, which did something strange to the square. The
modernist tower set in a plaza replaced the street wall, and the plaza it
produced was often a residual — leftover ground at the base of a building rather
than a room with walls. New York eventually made this an explicit bargain:
zoning rules from the early 1960s granted developers additional floor area in
exchange for providing plazas at street level, which is why so much of midtown
sits behind an apron of granite. Hundreds of these spaces were built. A great
many of them were useless, and it was precisely that failure that prompted the
city to fund Whyte's research and then to rewrite its rules to require seating,
trees, and sunlight rather than merely open air.

## Space That Looks Public

The same period produced a more consequential substitute. Victor Gruen, an
Austrian émigré architect, designed the fully enclosed, climate-controlled
shopping mall in suburban Minnesota in the 1950s, and he did it with civic
ambitions. He imagined the mall as the town center a subdivision otherwise
lacked, with medical offices, schools, apartments, and public meeting rooms
around the retail. Developers built the retail and skipped the rest. By the end
of his life Gruen was saying bluntly that he refused to pay alimony for the
bastard offspring of his idea.

What the mall proved is that a space can perform publicness while remaining
private property. It has a floor, a roof, benches, a fountain, strangers, and
the general feeling of a street. It also has a leaseholder who may exclude you.
American courts sorted this out in a way that is instructive: the federal
constitutional right to free speech was held not to reach into a privately owned
mall, but individual states were permitted to grant broader rights under their
own constitutions, and some did. The upshot is that whether you may gather
signatures outside a department store depends on which state the store is in.

The private management of ostensibly public ground has become subtler since.
Business improvement districts, funded by a levy on commercial property owners,
now sweep, plant, program, and patrol many downtown streets, generally doing it
better than the municipality did. They also have interests. The bench with a
central armrest, the sloped ledge, the sprinkler that runs at three in the
morning, the studs set into a sheltered doorway — this vocabulary of hostile
architecture is not designed against the general public but against a specific
minority of it, and it works by making the space slightly worse for everyone in
order to make it unusable for someone. A ledge you cannot sleep on is also a
ledge you cannot lie on to read.

## Why Friction Is the Feature

The usual defence of public space is that it is pleasant, and it often is. But
the more serious argument is about obligation. A genuinely public space is one
where you cannot choose your company. You share a bus shelter, a swimming pool,
a library reading room, a queue at a market stall with people whose politics,
class, language, and personal hygiene you did not select. The sociologist Lyn
Lofland described the city as a world of strangers in which people learn to
manage co-presence with others they will never know — reading cues, keeping
distance, granting the small ritual courtesies that let a crowded pavement work.
That competence is learned by practice, and it is only available where practice
is unavoidable.

Erosion of that competence is what worries people about the substitution of
private, filtered, curated environments for open ones. The gated subdivision,
the members' club, the car, the food court with a security contract — none is
sinister on its own, and each offers real goods, chiefly predictability. What
they remove in aggregate is the daily low-stakes rehearsal of dealing with
people who are not like you. A society can function with very little of it, but
it becomes a society in which the presence of unfamiliar people registers
primarily as a threat.

Assembly is the sharpest version of the same point. A right to gather and be
counted requires ground to gather on, and the ground has to be somewhere that
matters — outside a parliament, in front of a ministry, in the square the whole
city recognises. The political protests of the last few decades have tended to
attach themselves to specific pieces of pavement for exactly this reason: a
square in Cairo, a park in central Istanbul, the roads through Hong Kong's
financial district. Governments understand this as well as protesters do, which
is why the management of protest is so often a management of geography —
permits tied to particular routes, fences installed around lawns, a plaza
declared a transport interchange and therefore subject to different rules. You
can restrict speech very effectively without ever mentioning speech, simply by
controlling where standing still is allowed.

There is a smaller and more practical case too. Public space is the only
infrastructure that scales down to people with nothing. A park bench is the
cheapest possible amenity and one of the few available equally to a banker and
to someone sleeping rough. Public libraries have quietly become the most
important indoor public space in many cities, for reasons that have little to do
with books: they are warm, free, staffed, and require no purchase to enter. That
combination is now rare enough that libraries absorb social functions they were
never funded for.

Children are the group whose use of public space has changed fastest and been
noticed least. Within living memory, the ordinary range of an eight-year-old in
a European or North American town extended for a mile or more without adult
supervision — to a park, a shop, a friend's house, along the backs of gardens
and down alleys. That range has contracted sharply, driven less by any rise in
danger from strangers than by traffic volumes, by the reorganisation of streets
around moving and storing cars, and by a shift in what counts as responsible
parenting. The consequence is that a great deal of childhood has moved indoors
or into scheduled, supervised, fee-charging settings. Play streets, home zones,
and school-run closures are attempts to get some of it back by reclaiming
carriageway, and they work when they are permanent enough that children believe
in them.

## The Maintenance Problem

Public space is cheap to build and expensive to keep, and the expense is mostly
people. A plaza needs someone to empty the bins, fix the drinking fountain,
replant in spring, unlock the toilets, and be visibly around. Jane Jacobs'
observation about eyes on the street was not a call for surveillance; it was an
argument that the ordinary presence of shopkeepers, residents, and passers-by
with a stake in a place does the work that cameras and patrols do badly. Remove
the shops, empty the ground floors, route everyone through a parking structure,
and the street is unsafe in a way that no amount of lighting fixes.

Which is why the most reliable predictor of a good public space is not its
budget or its designer but whether anyone in particular is responsible for it
on a Tuesday afternoon. Spaces with an identifiable steward — a market trader,
a caretaker, a neighbourhood association with keys, a café whose tables spill
out — tend to stay usable across decades. Spaces belonging to everyone in
general and no one in particular tend to be inspected, found wanting, redesigned
at considerable cost, and then fail again for the same reason as before. The
ledge people sat on is removed in the renovation, and nobody records why the
place stopped working.
