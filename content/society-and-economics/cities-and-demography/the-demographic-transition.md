# The Demographic Transition

Sweden has been counting its own births and deaths without interruption since
1749, longer than any other country. The clergy already kept parish registers of
baptisms, marriages, and burials; what the crown added was the requirement to
tabulate and forward them, so that for two and a half centuries there exists an
unbroken national series of how many Swedes arrived and how many left. Plot the
two lines and the shape of the modern world falls out of them. For the first
century the curves run close together and both jump around violently — a famine
year, an epidemic year, a war year, each producing a spike of deaths and a dip
of births nine months later. Then, somewhere in the early nineteenth century,
the death line begins to sink and stops spiking. The birth line stays up. The
gap between them, which is population growth, opens like a pair of scissors and
stays open for roughly a hundred years. Then the birth line comes down to meet
the death line, and the scissors close.

That figure — two curves, a gap, a closure — is the demographic transition. The
name was attached to it in the middle of the twentieth century by American
demographers, Warren Thompson and later Frank Notestein among them, who noticed
that country after country was tracing the same path with different timing. It
has since become the single most reliable generalisation in social science, and
also one of the most frequently misunderstood, because people read it as a law
when it is really a description of something that has happened repeatedly for
reasons that were not always the same.

## The Equilibrium That Came First

The pre-transitional regime is usually described as high birth rates balanced by
high death rates, which is true but makes it sound passive. It was not. A
population growing at even one percent a year doubles in seventy years, and
pre-industrial economies could not absorb that, so something had to hold
fertility below its biological maximum. In much of western Europe the brake was
marriage. Couples did not typically marry until they could support a household
of their own, which meant waiting for a farm tenancy or a place in a craft, so
brides were commonly in their mid-twenties and a substantial minority of people
never married at all. Since childbearing outside marriage was rare and heavily
sanctioned, the age at the altar functioned as a fertility control valve that
responded to economic conditions — marriages fell in bad harvest years and rose
in good ones. Other societies solved the same problem differently, with
prolonged breastfeeding, postpartum abstinence taboos, infanticide, or the
dispatch of surplus sons into monasteries and armies.

Mortality, meanwhile, was not merely high but unstable. Life expectancy at birth
of around thirty does not mean people dropped dead at thirty; it means that so
many died in the first five years that the average was dragged down. Someone who
reached adulthood had a reasonable chance of seeing sixty. What made the system
precarious was the crisis year — the harvest failure that emptied a village, the
plague that took a third of a town in a season. Populations spent much of their
time recovering from these shocks rather than growing.

## Why Death Retreated

The first half of the transition is the decline of mortality, and the striking
thing about it is how little of it was medicine. The great falls in death rates
in nineteenth century Europe happened before antibiotics, before most vaccines,
and largely before doctors could do much for infectious disease beyond diagnose
it. Thomas McKeown argued in the 1970s that improving nutrition did nearly all
the work: better fed bodies survive infections that kill malnourished ones.
Later scholars pushed back hard, and the balance of evidence now credits public
works more heavily — piped clean water, sewers that discharged somewhere other
than the water intake, drainage, street cleaning, quarantine, the pasteurisation
and safer transport of milk, and the slow professionalisation of midwifery. What
these measures share is that they cut the transmission of diarrhoeal and
respiratory disease among infants and small children, which is where most of the
deaths were concentrated. Smallpox vaccination, unusually, was a genuinely
medical intervention with a large early effect.

Whatever the mix, the crucial feature of mortality decline is that it required
no decision by ordinary families. Nobody has to be persuaded to survive. Clean
water arrives, and child deaths fall whether or not anyone understands why. This
asymmetry explains the timing of the whole transition: the top line drops
promptly and involuntarily, while the bottom line waits on millions of private
decisions that take a generation or more to change.

## Why Births Followed

The fertility half is the harder and more contested one. The mechanical account
runs like this: when nearly all your children survive, the number of births
needed to end up with two or three grown sons and daughters falls sharply, and
families that continue having six find themselves supporting six. Add
urbanisation, which converts a child from a pair of hands on a farm into a
dependent in a rented room; add compulsory schooling and restrictions on child
labour, which delay the age at which a child produces anything; add rising
returns to educating each child, which makes fewer, better-schooled children an
attractive substitute for many; add women's literacy and paid employment, which
raises the cost of a pregnancy in forgone income and shifts bargaining power
within households. Somewhere in that list, most of the fertility decline is
usually found.

But the timing does not always cooperate. France began reducing its fertility in
the late eighteenth century, before it industrialised, before its mortality had
fallen much, and before anyone had modern contraception — apparently through
withdrawal, later marriage, and simple intent. A large comparative project at
Princeton in the mid-twentieth century examined fertility decline across
hundreds of European provinces and found that it spread in patterns that
followed language and cultural region more closely than they followed income,
urbanisation, or literacy. Neighbouring districts at very different levels of
development turned at the same moment if they spoke the same dialect; districts
at identical development levels across a linguistic boundary did not. That
result shifted the debate. Alongside the economics there is something like
diffusion — the spread of the idea that the size of a family is a thing one
decides rather than a thing that happens, and of the practical knowledge for
acting on it.

## Timing, Speed, and the Long Tail

Europe took most of two centuries to complete the passage. Later transitions
have been dramatically faster, because the countries making them could import
the mortality technology whole — vaccines, antibiotics, oral rehydration,
insecticide-treated nets — rather than inventing it, and because contraception
and the norms around it also arrived ready-made. South Korea, Thailand, and Iran
each compressed into a single generation a fertility decline that took Britain
eighty years. Iran's is the extreme case: a very high birth rate collapsed to
around replacement within roughly fifteen years, aided by a state family
planning programme that reversed an earlier pronatalist policy and by a rapid
rise in girls' schooling.

It is worth being careful about what the headline number measures. The total
fertility rate is not a count of children any real woman had; it is a synthetic
figure, built by taking the birth rates observed at each age in a single year
and asking what a hypothetical woman would end up with if she lived through all
of them. That construction makes it responsive and comparable, but it also makes
it sensitive to timing. If a generation collectively postpones childbearing by
three years, the measured rate sags during the postponement and then partially
recovers, even though completed family size may barely move. A good deal of
alarmed commentary about collapsing birth rates in particular years is reading
this tempo distortion as though it were a change in how many children people
will eventually have. The distortion is real, but it is not the whole story —
where postponement has run for decades, completed family size has fallen too.

Two consequences follow from speed, and they are opposite in sign. The first is
the demographic dividend. For a few decades after fertility falls, a country has
an unusually favourable age structure: the large cohorts born before the decline
are of working age, the cohorts behind them are small, and the elderly
population has not yet grown. Dependants are few relative to workers, household
savings rise, and if the economy can employ those workers the growth effect is
substantial. Much of East Asia's postwar acceleration sits inside that window.
The window closes on schedule, because the large working cohorts are the ones
who will later be old.

The second is momentum. A population does not stop growing when fertility
reaches replacement level; it keeps growing for decades, because the women who
will have those two children each are themselves an unusually numerous
generation. A country can be at two children per woman and still add tens of
millions of people, simply because there are more potential mothers than there
used to be. Roughly speaking, most of the population growth the world will see
this century is already determined by the age structure now alive.

Sub-Saharan Africa is where the model's predictive weakness shows most clearly
at the other end. Mortality there has fallen substantially, as the theory
expects, but fertility has come down more slowly and less uniformly than
comparable income and schooling levels elsewhere would have implied, and in some
countries it stalled for a decade at a level well above what the sequence
predicted before resuming. Explanations offered include the structure of
landholding and of marriage, the relative weakness of the shift out of
agriculture, the value placed on large kin networks, and the patchiness of girls'
secondary schooling. The practical upshot is that the continent's share of world
population is rising steeply, and that the projections people quote for 2100 are
mostly projections about how fast African fertility declines from here.

The model's weakest point is the end. The original formulation assumed the birth
line would settle at the death line and stay there, producing a stable
population. Instead, in country after country, fertility has passed through
replacement and kept falling — to one and a half children per woman across much
of Europe, well below that in parts of East Asia and southern Europe. Nothing in
the theory anticipated this, and no country has yet found a reliable way to push
the number back up. Whatever explains it — housing costs, the difficulty of
combining work with care, the lengthening of education, a shift in what people
expect a life to contain — it means the transition's final stage is not a plateau
but an open question, and the societies furthest through it are the ones
improvising an answer.
