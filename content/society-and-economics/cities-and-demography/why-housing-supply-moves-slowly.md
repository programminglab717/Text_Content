# Why Housing Supply Moves Slowly

The apartment building opening this spring was drawn on paper five or six years
ago, when interest rates were different, steel cost something else, and the
neighborhood had a different set of rents. Its developer made a bet on
conditions that no longer exist and cannot now be unmade. This is the first
thing to understand about housing supply: it is the output of a pipeline with a
very long throat, and what comes out the end was ordered under circumstances
that have since expired.

Nearly every confusing thing about housing markets follows from two physical
facts. Houses take years to produce, and once produced they last for a century
or more. Almost everything else — the boom-bust cycle in construction, the
asymmetry between expensive cities and cheap ones, the way a building boom seems
to arrive exactly when it is least needed — is downstream of those two.

## A Stock, Not a Flow

In a country with a mature housing stock, a normal year's construction adds
something in the neighborhood of one percent to the total number of homes.
Demolitions and losses subtract a fraction of that. So even a construction boom
of historic proportions changes the stock slowly: doubling production for a
decade might raise the total supply by a tenth.

This sounds like an argument that building is futile. It is not, because prices
are not set by the average unit. They are set at the margin, by the household
that would otherwise be the last one accommodated. A metropolitan area short a
few tens of thousands of homes relative to the households that want to live
there does not experience a shortfall evenly distributed as mild inconvenience.
It experiences it as competitive bidding at every rung of the ladder, with the
losers doubling up, commuting from farther out, or leaving. Adding a small
number of units relative to the stock can be a large number relative to that
margin. The same arithmetic runs in reverse and explains why a modest oversupply
produces concessions, free months, and falling rents that look wildly out of
proportion to the number of empty units.

## The Pipeline

Follow a single mid-rise apartment building from idea to occupancy and the lag
stops being abstract. A developer options a site — paying for the right to buy
it later, conditional on approvals — and hires architects to produce enough
design to file. Entitlement follows: the discretionary approvals, environmental
review, hearings, and appeals that determine whether the project may exist in
the form proposed. In a permissive jurisdiction this takes months. In a
restrictive one it takes years, and may end in nothing, which is why the option
is written the way it is.

Only then does construction financing close, which requires a lender to believe
a rent forecast three years out. Sitework, foundation, structure, envelope,
fit-out: two to three years for a building of any size, longer if a crane must
be shared or a transformer is back-ordered. Lease-up follows. From the first
sketch to the first tenant, five years is ordinary and eight is unremarkable.

The consequence is that supply responds to yesterday's prices. Developers commit
capital when rents are already high, because that is when the numbers work and
when lenders are willing. The buildings arrive after the peak, sometimes into a
market that has already cooled, which is how a city can suffer a severe shortage
and a glut of new luxury units within the same decade without any contradiction.
The pipeline also means that policy changes — a new zoning allowance, a tax
abatement, an interest rate cut — produce visible buildings years after the
politicians who enacted them have moved on, and are routinely credited to
whoever happens to be in office when the ribbon is cut.

## Durability, and the Kink

Housing is exceptionally durable, and durability makes supply asymmetric.
Building more is possible when demand rises. Building less is not an option when
demand falls, because the existing houses do not evaporate; they simply sit
there getting cheaper.

This produces two entirely different kinds of housing market. In a growing
metropolitan area, prices are held near the cost of construction only if
building is easy. Where building is hard, prices rise far above construction
cost and stay there, because the constraint is not the ability to pour concrete
but the ability to obtain permission. In a shrinking area, prices fall toward
whatever someone will pay to occupy a structure that already exists, which can
be far below what it would cost to build the same house new. Nobody builds in
such a place — not because construction is banned but because it is irrational
to spend two hundred thousand dollars producing something that would sell for
sixty. The cheap housing of declining cities is not an achievement of policy. It
is the residue of a population that left and a building stock that could not
follow.

The same durability explains why a house's price is really two prices welded
together. The structure depreciates, slowly, and could in principle be
reproduced at known cost. The land does not depreciate and cannot be reproduced
at all. In a cheap market, most of what you buy is the structure. In an
expensive one, most of what you buy is the ground beneath it, and the difference
between the two markets is mostly a difference in what the ground is permitted
to hold.

## Filtering, and the Chain of Moves

New housing is almost always expensive housing. Materials, labor, land, and
financing cost what they cost, and the resulting unit sells or rents near the
top of the local market. From this, people reasonably conclude that new
construction cannot help anyone but the affluent.

The mechanism that complicates that conclusion is filtering. Housing ages out of
the top of the market. A building that was luxurious in 1975 is ordinary now and
will be cheap in thirty years, not because anyone planned it but because
fashions, layouts, and systems date, and because maintenance is deferred. Most
of the genuinely affordable unsubsidized housing in any American city is simply
old housing that used to be expensive. Filtering runs slowly and it runs in
reverse in very hot markets, where old buildings are renovated back up the
ladder rather than allowed to drift down it.

There is also a shorter-run version, which researchers have traced by following
who moves into a new building and then who moves into the homes those people
vacated. The chains reach further down the income distribution than the price of
the new building suggests, because each move frees a unit for someone else. The
chains are not infinite and they leak — some vacated units are held empty, some
are converted, some movers came from out of town — but they are real and
measurable, and they are why the price of a new building is a poor guide to who
ultimately benefits from it.

## Why Nothing Gets Cheaper to Build

Most industries produce more output per worker-hour every decade.
Construction, stubbornly, does not — measured productivity in homebuilding has
been roughly flat or worse over long stretches, while manufacturing has
transformed itself several times over. The reasons are structural. Every site is
different. The product is enormous, cannot be shipped economically once
assembled, and must satisfy a code that varies by jurisdiction. The workforce is
assembled per project from specialized subcontractors who each bear their own
scheduling risk. Weather interrupts. The financing is short-term and expensive,
so schedule delays compound into real money.

Factory production keeps being proposed as the escape and keeps disappointing.
It works, technically — the post-war American tract house, built with
prefabricated components and crews trained on a single repeated task, is a real
example of construction learning to behave like manufacturing — but factories
need continuous volume to amortize their tooling, and housing demand is
violently cyclical and intensely local. Manufactured homes, built to a national
standard and shipped complete, genuinely are cheaper per square foot than
site-built housing, and they occupy a small slice of the market anyway, partly
because of financing that treats them as depreciating chattel rather than
appreciating real estate, and partly because most places have quietly made it
difficult to put one anywhere desirable.

## The Missing Middle

Between the detached house and the elevator building sits a set of forms that
used to be built everywhere and now are built almost nowhere: the duplex, the
triple-decker, the courtyard walk-up, the small apartment house over shops.
These are not unfashionable. They are structurally advantaged — wood frame,
modest foundations, no elevator, no structured parking — and they are what a
lot of people would choose at a price they could afford.

They vanished for reasons that compound. Most residential land does not permit
them. Building codes impose step changes at certain thresholds, so a building
that crosses from three stories to four, or from one stairway to two, picks up
costs that a small project cannot carry. Lenders and institutional investors
prefer deals large enough to justify their underwriting overhead, which pushes
capital toward two-hundred-unit buildings and away from twelve-unit ones. The
small builders who once put up a few of these a year, financed by a local bank
that knew them, largely stopped existing after the last financial crisis. A
sector cannot be reassembled by legalization alone once the firms, the crews,
and the credit relationships that constituted it have dispersed.
