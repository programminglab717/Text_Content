# How the Suburbs Were Built

On the potato fields of central Long Island after the Second World War, a
construction crew moved down a street doing one thing. Behind them came another
crew doing the next thing, and behind that another. The Levitt organisation had
broken house-building into more than two dozen discrete operations and assigned
each to a specialised team that did nothing else all day: pour the slab, stack
the lumber at measured intervals, frame, sheathe, shingle, hang the cabinets,
paint the white coat, paint the trim. There were no basements, because digging
them was slow. Lumber arrived precut from the company's own yard. Appliances
came from company-owned suppliers. At peak the operation finished dozens of
houses a day, and the buyers — veterans, mostly, with a mortgage guaranteed by
the federal government — could move in with almost nothing down.

Levittown is the image everyone has of suburbanisation, and it is misleading in
one important way. The houses were the visible part. What actually built the
postwar suburb was a set of decisions about finance, roads, and law, most of
them made in offices, and almost none of them announced as a plan to reshape
where people live.

## Before the Car

Suburbs are older than the automobile and originally had nothing to do with it.
Nineteenth century railway companies built lines outward from city centres and
discovered that the land they passed through became valuable the moment it was
served, so many of them went into the property business themselves. The result
was a string of settlements beaded along a track, each clustered tightly around
its station because everyone had to walk there. Beyond a comfortable walking
radius the land stayed in farms.

Streetcars did the same thing at finer grain, and with the same business model.
Operators in dozens of cities were simultaneously transit companies and land
developers, running lines out past the built edge into holdings they already
owned. The streetcar suburb that resulted had a characteristic shape: fingers of
housing extending along the lines, dense enough to support frequent service,
with a commercial strip at the stops and open land in the gaps between fingers.
Houses sat close together on narrow lots, because the lot had to be within a few
minutes of the rails.

The automobile's real effect was not to create suburbs. It was to make the land
between the fingers developable. Once a household could reach anywhere by car,
the constraint of proximity to a line vanished, the gaps filled in, and the
edge could expand in every direction at once and at much lower density. Every
later feature of the pattern follows from that release.

## The Mortgage and the Highway

Before the 1930s, buying a house in the United States generally meant finding
half the price in cash and taking a loan of five or ten years that paid interest
only, with the principal falling due at the end as a lump sum that had to be
refinanced. In a downturn, when lenders would not renew, borrowers with good
income and good intentions lost their homes anyway. The foreclosure wave of the
Depression discredited the whole arrangement.

What replaced it was the long-term, self-amortising, government-insured mortgage
— twenty, then twenty-five, then thirty years, with a small down payment and a
level monthly payment that retired the debt. Federal insurance took the default
risk off the lender, which meant lenders would make loans they previously would
not, at rates they previously would not. The monthly cost of owning fell below
the cost of renting for a very large slice of the population, and the veterans'
loan programmes after the war extended that to millions more with essentially no
deposit at all. Nothing in the twentieth century did more to move households out
of cities.

The insurance came with underwriting rules, and the rules had geography. Federal
manuals graded neighbourhoods for lending risk and rated them down for age,
mixed use, proximity to industry, and — explicitly — for the presence or nearby
presence of Black residents, who were described as an adverse influence on
property values. Maps were drawn with the worst-rated areas outlined in red.
The effect was doubly powerful: capital was withheld from older mixed
neighbourhoods, which then decayed as predicted, and it was channelled toward
new, uniform, racially exclusive construction at the periphery. Private racial
covenants written into deeds did the rest until the courts made them judicially
unenforceable in the late 1940s, after which the same exclusion continued
through steering, blockbusting, and the everyday discretion of agents and
lenders. The suburb was not incidentally segregated. Segregation was written
into its financing.

Tax law reinforced the tilt. Mortgage interest and local property taxes were
deductible from income while rent was not, and the value a household got from
living in a house it owned was never treated as income at all, which meant the
tax system quietly subsidised owner-occupancy relative to renting and subsidised
larger, more expensive houses most of all. None of it was designed as a
settlement policy. It behaved like one.

Then the roads. The American interstate programme authorised in 1956 committed
federal money to tens of thousands of miles of limited-access highway on terms
that no state could refuse — the federal government covering the overwhelming
share of construction costs, with the states paying a small remainder. That
funding formula is more consequential than the mileage. A state choosing between
a highway paid for almost entirely from Washington and a transit system paid for
almost entirely from its own revenue was not really choosing. Routed through
cities as well as between them, and combined with urban renewal clearances,
those highways demolished substantial parts of older neighbourhoods —
disproportionately poor and Black ones, which had the least ability to object —
while simultaneously opening farmland thirty miles out to commuters. The same
project pushed at both ends.

## Zoning Gave It Shape

Finance determined who could buy and where capital would flow. Zoning
determined what could be built, and it is why suburbs in wildly different
regions look so similar.

American courts upheld comprehensive zoning in the mid-1920s, and municipalities
adopted it rapidly. The characteristic suburban ordinance separates uses
absolutely — housing here, shops there, workplaces somewhere else entirely —
sets a minimum lot size and a minimum house size, requires deep setbacks from
the street and the side lot lines, caps the number of dwellings per acre, and
requires each building to supply a fixed number of off-street parking spaces.
Each rule is defensible on its own terms. Together they make it illegal to build
anything resembling a traditional town: no corner shop within walking distance
of houses, no flat above the shop, no duplex on the corner lot, no building
close enough to the pavement to make the pavement feel like a room.

Minimum lot sizes and minimum house sizes also function as a price floor. If the
smallest legal dwelling on the smallest legal parcel costs a certain amount,
nobody poorer than that can live in the jurisdiction, and no fair-housing law
touches the mechanism because it never mentions anyone by name. Where
municipalities are small and numerous and fund their schools from local property
tax, the incentive to zone this way is overwhelming: every low-value household
admitted costs more in services than it contributes. Fragmented local government
turns exclusion into ordinary fiscal prudence.

Then there is the street network. Older grids distribute traffic across many
parallel routes; the postwar subdivision replaced them with a hierarchy of
cul-de-sacs feeding collectors feeding arterials. This makes the residential
street quiet, which was the point. It also means that every trip, however short,
must use an arterial, concentrating all movement onto a few congested roads,
making distances by road far longer than distances as the crow flies, and
making walking to a destination a quarter mile away a journey of a mile and a
half along a road with no footway.

## It Did Not Stop at the Bedroom

The assumption behind early suburbanisation was that jobs stayed downtown and
people commuted in. By the 1970s that was breaking down. Offices, warehouses,
research campuses, and eventually corporate headquarters followed the workforce
outward, drawn by cheap land, easy parking, and highway interchanges. Clusters
of office space appeared at freeway junctions with more floor area than many
downtowns, which the journalist Joel Garreau named edge cities. Commuting
patterns scrambled: suburb-to-suburb trips became the dominant flow in most
large metropolitan areas, and these are precisely the trips that transit serves
worst, because they run from everywhere to everywhere rather than from many
places to one.

The pattern is not uniquely American, though its American version is the most
extreme. Britain's interwar ribbon development along arterial roads produced
millions of semi-detached houses before green belts and postwar planning law
constrained the edge and pushed growth into new towns instead. France built its
peripheral housing as public housing, in slabs and towers, inverting the
American social geography so that the poor live at the edge and the wealthy in
the centre. Across much of Latin America the periphery grew through self-built
housing on informally occupied land, which regularised itself over decades as
residents added floors and eventually obtained services and title. The common
element everywhere is that the edge is where land is cheap and existing
residents are few enough not to object.

## What Comes Next Is Slower Than the Building Was

The first-ring suburbs built in the 1950s are now old, and many of them have
inherited the trajectory their planners assumed would belong to the city:
declining tax base, aging infrastructure, and rising poverty. In several
countries more poor people now live in suburbs than in central cities, without
the concentrated social services that cities built up over a century, and
without transit for people who cannot afford a car.

Retrofitting is underway and is difficult. Dead shopping malls become housing
and town squares; accessory dwellings appear in back gardens; cities have begun
repealing exclusive single-family zoning and abolishing parking minimums. All of
it runs against the durability of what was built. Houses get renovated and
replaced routinely, and the lot lines, street rights-of-way, drainage easements,
and utility runs beneath them almost never change. A subdivision platted in 1954
will still be shaping how people move in 2154, because the pavement is a
property boundary and property boundaries outlast nearly everything laid on top
of them.
