# Building Around the Station

Stand at the exit of a suburban rail station at eight in the morning and you
can usually tell within thirty seconds whether the place was designed around the
trains or merely next to them. If people are walking off in four directions on
sidewalks that lead somewhere, the station is part of a neighborhood. If nearly
everyone crosses a parking lot to a car, or waits on a shoulder for a shuttle,
the station is a mouth attached to a road network that happens to have a
platform bolted onto it. The train service may be identical in both cases. What
differs is the eighty acres around the platform, and that is the whole subject.

Transit-oriented development is the deliberate practice of arranging those
acres so that walking to the train is the obvious choice rather than an act of
determination. The term is relatively recent — the architect and planner Peter
Calthorpe did more than anyone to popularize it in the late 1980s and early
1990s — but the practice is old enough to have built large parts of most cities
that have rail at all.

## The Walkshed Is Smaller Than the Circle

Planners routinely draw a half-mile circle around a station and call it the
station area. A half mile is roughly a ten-minute walk for an unhurried adult,
and willingness to walk falls off steeply past that, faster for a bus stop than
for a rail station and faster in bad weather or at night.

The circle is a fiction, though, because people walk on the street network, not
through buildings. What matters is the area actually reachable in ten minutes on
foot, and that shape is almost never a circle. A six-lane arterial with signals
a quarter mile apart may cut the reachable area in half by itself. A rail line
approached from only one side, a river with no pedestrian crossing, a
superblock, a cul-de-sac subdivision whose streets connect to nothing but a
collector road — each of these can shrink a nominal half-mile catchment to a
fraction of its drawn size. The single most consequential thing a city can do
for a station area is often not rezoning at all but cutting new pedestrian
connections through whatever is blocking the shed: a path, a bridge, a signalized
crossing, an opening in a wall.

Once you start measuring sheds this way, the failure mode of a great many
American stations becomes obvious. The land closest to the platform, the most
valuable real estate in the catchment by a wide margin, is frequently occupied
by surface parking. That parking is not merely a missed opportunity; it is an
active barrier, because a person housed on the far side of it must cross it on
foot to reach the train.

## The Railroads Were Developers First

The historical version of this idea was never described as policy because it
was simply how the business worked. Nineteenth and early twentieth century
streetcar and interurban companies in North America frequently made more money
selling land than selling rides. A company would buy farmland cheaply, extend a
line to it, subdivide it into lots, and capture the difference in value that its
own service had created. The compact, gridded, porch-fronted neighborhoods that
ring the cores of many older American cities are the physical residue of that
business model, and their walkability is not nostalgia — it is the geometry a
developer chose when the customers arrived on foot.

Japan built the most durable version of the same idea. Private railway
companies there were founded as, and remain, integrated property businesses.
Ichizo Kobayashi, running what became the Hankyu group in the Osaka region,
built a line out toward undeveloped land, sold houses along it to a new salaried
middle class, put a department store at the city-side terminal so commuters
would shop on the way home, and placed attractions at the far end — most
famously the Takarazuka theatrical revue — to generate ridership in the
off-peak, reverse-peak direction. Nearly every element of that arrangement is
still standard practice for Japanese private railways, which is a large part of
why some of them operate without subsidy.

Hong Kong formalized the model into an explicit financial machine. The transit
operator there is granted development rights over the land above and around new
stations, develops it in partnership with private builders, and uses the
proceeds to fund the railway itself. Rail plus property, as the arrangement is
known, works because the value a new line creates in surrounding land is
enormous and would otherwise accrue entirely to whoever happened to own that
land already. Capturing some of it is not a subsidy to transit. It is the
transit paying for itself out of what it makes.

## Both Ends of the Trip

A common and expensive mistake is to treat density near the origin as the whole
problem. A transit trip requires a walkable origin and a walkable destination.
Ten thousand apartments around a suburban station generate little ridership if
the employment they serve is spread across office parks reachable only by car
from a different station.

The regional plans that worked best were explicit about both ends. Copenhagen's
post-war Finger Plan channelled metropolitan growth along a handful of rail
corridors radiating from the core, with green wedges kept between them, so that
both the housing on the fingers and the jobs in the palm sat on the network.
Stockholm built new towns along its metro extensions, with a commercial center
and apartment blocks directly at each station and lower density trailing away
from it — a deliberate, legible gradient rather than uniform sprawl.

The clearest American example is a corridor in Arlington County, Virginia. When
a Washington metro line was planned through the county, the obvious cheap
alignment ran in the median of an interstate. The county pushed instead for the
line to be tunnelled beneath an existing commercial street, with several closely
spaced stations, and then rezoned the blocks around each one for high density
tapering sharply down to the existing single-family streets a few blocks back.
Decades of office and apartment construction followed, concentrated within
walking distance of the platforms, while the neighborhoods behind them were
largely untouched. The counterfactual is visible a few miles away, where similar
lines run in highway medians and the station areas are mostly parking.

## Why So Many Stations End Up Surrounded by Asphalt

The forces producing the parking crater are mundane. Park-and-ride ridership is
real, immediate, and easy to forecast; walk-up ridership from housing that does
not exist yet is speculative and arrives over decades. Transit agencies are
frequently graded on ridership next year. Parking minimums in the local zoning
code apply to buildings near stations exactly as they apply everywhere else,
so an apartment building at the platform must provide as many spaces as one
three miles away. Arterial roads built to move traffic at forty-five miles an
hour make the surrounding blocks unpleasant to walk regardless of what is built
on them. And station sites are often chosen for engineering convenience — a
vacant parcel, a wide right-of-way, a cheap acquisition — rather than for what
is already around them.

The useful distinction is between transit-oriented and merely transit-adjacent
development. A tower at a station with two structured parking spaces per unit,
fronted by a driveway, is transit-adjacent. Its residents will mostly drive,
because the building has been built to make driving easy and the surroundings
offer nothing to walk to. Orientation is not proximity. It is what the building
faces, what the ground floor does, where the front door is, and whether the
parking is a requirement or an option.

## Frequency Is Part of the Land Use

None of the geometry matters if the service is bad. A station with a train every
ten minutes supports a fundamentally different way of living than one with a
train every forty, because the ten-minute service can be used without
consulting a timetable. Walk-up frequency is the threshold at which transit
stops being an appointment and starts being an amenity, and it is the point at
which households begin to shed cars — which in turn is what makes low-parking
buildings financeable.

This is also why the mode matters less than it is usually made to. Curitiba, in
Brazil, built its reputation on buses: dedicated center-running lanes, prepaid
boarding at enclosed stations, and a street hierarchy explicitly designed so
that the highest-density zoning followed the busways. Jaime Lerner, the
architect-mayor most associated with the system, made the land use and the
transit a single decision rather than two, which is what distinguished it from
the many bus corridors elsewhere that carry high ridership through neighborhoods
zoned for warehouses.

The unresolved difficulty is that all of this works, and works visibly, and
therefore raises land values around the stations it serves. A corridor that
becomes genuinely convenient becomes desirable, and the households who most
depend on cheap transit access are frequently the least able to outbid anyone
for the privilege of staying. Cities that have taken this seriously tend to
acquire land near planned stations early, while it is still cheap and before
the alignment is announced, and to write affordability requirements into the
upzoning itself rather than negotiating them building by building afterward.
Doing it later costs more and yields less, because by then the city is buying
back the value its own tracks created.
