# Reading a Street for Heat

On a clear July afternoon, point an infrared thermometer at a bare asphalt
parking lot and it will read something absurd — fifty or sixty degrees Celsius,
far above the air temperature a weather station a mile away is reporting.
Point it at the grass beside the lot and it may read close to air temperature.
Point it at the underside of a tree canopy and it reads lower still. The air
above all three is roughly the same. The difference is in what the surfaces are
doing with sunlight, and that difference, not the number on the forecast, is
most of what determines whether a street is survivable in a heat wave.

Learning to read a street for heat is a matter of looking at four things: where
the sun lands, what it lands on, how much sky each point can see, and where the
air can go. Everything else — canopy targets, cool roof ordinances, misting
fans — is downstream of those.

## The Thermometer Is Measuring the Wrong Thing

Air temperature is convenient because it is cheap to measure and easy to
broadcast, and it is a poor proxy for heat stress on a human body. A person
standing outdoors exchanges heat four ways: conduction through contact,
convection with moving air, evaporation of sweat, and radiation. On a sunny day
radiation dominates, and the relevant quantity is the mean radiant temperature —
the weighted average temperature of everything in the person's field of view,
including the sun, the sky, the pavement, and the wall across the street.

Two people in the same block of air, one in direct sun over asphalt and one
under a dense tree, can be experiencing mean radiant temperatures ten or fifteen
degrees apart. The shaded one is receiving almost no direct shortwave radiation,
is looking at a canopy near air temperature rather than a griddle, and is losing
heat upward to whatever patch of sky remains visible. The air thermometer cannot
tell them apart. Instruments that try to account for this, like the black globe
thermometer used to derive wet bulb globe temperature for athletic and military
heat protocols, exist precisely because the shaded and unshaded versions of the
same afternoon are different events physiologically.

Humidity sets the ceiling on the whole system, because evaporation is the body's
last resort. When the wet-bulb temperature climbs, sweat stops carrying heat
away efficiently, and past a certain point no amount of shade or wind helps a
person who cannot get somewhere cooler. This is why the same air temperature is
merely uncomfortable in a dry climate and dangerous in a humid one, and why
evaporative strategies that work beautifully in the desert — misters, fountains,
swamp coolers — are close to useless on a humid coast.

## What the Surfaces Do After Dark

Dark pavement absorbs the large majority of the sunlight hitting it. Most of
that energy goes into the material itself, because asphalt and concrete are
dense and conduct heat reasonably well, so they store warmth deep in the slab
through the afternoon. The stored heat comes back out as longwave radiation
after sunset, slowly, over hours.

Vegetated ground behaves entirely differently for a reason that has nothing to
do with color. Plants and moist soil spend a large fraction of absorbed energy
evaporating water rather than heating themselves, converting sunlight into
latent heat that leaves with the water vapor. A lawn that has been watered stays
near air temperature all afternoon and has very little stored heat to release at
night. A lawn in drought, browned off and with its stomata shut, loses that
advantage almost entirely — which is a limitation worth remembering, because
heat waves and droughts arrive together.

The third variable is geometry. Sky view factor is the fraction of the
hemisphere above a given point that is actually open sky rather than building or
canopy. A point in the middle of a field has a sky view factor near one and can
radiate freely to space at night, which is why open ground cools fast after
sunset. A point at the bottom of a narrow street canyon between tall buildings
has a low sky view factor: heat radiated from the pavement hits a facade instead
of escaping, gets partly re-radiated back down, and the canyon cools slowly. The
same geometry that shades the street at noon traps it at midnight.

That nighttime behavior is the part that kills people. Human physiology tolerates
a hot day reasonably well if the night provides relief; core temperature comes
back down, sleep is possible, and the cardiovascular strain resets. When
overnight minimums stay high for several days running, the recovery never
happens, and excess deaths climb through the back half of a heat wave rather
than on its hottest afternoon. Heat mortality is concentrated among the elderly,
the isolated, people with cardiovascular and respiratory conditions, and people
in top-floor apartments under uninsulated roofs without air conditioning. The
social autopsy of the 1995 Chicago heat wave made the uncomfortable point that
neighborhoods with similar poverty rates had very different death rates,
depending on whether people had reason to leave their apartments and someone to
check on them.

## A Tree Is a Shade Structure That Also Sweats

Street trees do two distinct jobs, and conflating them leads to bad decisions.

The first is interception. A dense crown blocks most of the direct beam before
it reaches the pavement, which means the pavement never heats, which means there
is no stored heat to re-radiate at the person walking underneath. Shade from a
building awning or a shade sail does this part just as well. The second job is
transpiration: water drawn up through the roots and released through the leaves,
carrying latent heat away. A large tree in leaf on a hot day moves a remarkable
quantity of water this way, and the effect is measurable in the air immediately
beneath and downwind of the crown.

The practical consequences are specific. Shade delivered over pavement is worth
far more than the same shade over grass, because it prevents a much larger
radiant load. A row of small ornamental trees with sparse crowns, planted
because they were cheap and would not interfere with the power lines, delivers a
small fraction of the benefit of a single large-canopy species allowed to mature
over the sidewalk. Canopy measured as percentage of citywide land cover is a
weak metric, since a city can hit an impressive number entirely in parks and
wealthy subdivisions while the bus stops and the commercial strips where people
actually stand in the sun remain bare. What matters is shade where people are,
at the hours they are there, which for a west-facing bus stop means late
afternoon and argues for a tree placed on the southwest side rather than wherever
the utility conflicts were easiest.

## Where the Canopy Isn't

Map tree canopy against almost any measure of income in almost any city and the
correlation is stark enough to be visible without statistics. The bare parts are
the industrial edges, the arterial commercial strips, the dense blocks of older
rental housing, and the public housing built with lawns rather than trees. In
American cities, canopy maps also track the federal mortgage risk maps drawn in
the 1930s with uncomfortable fidelity: neighborhoods graded hazardous then are
measurably hotter now, decades after the grading itself was outlawed.

The causes are cumulative rather than conspiratorial. Trees are an asset that
takes forty years to mature, so present-day canopy reflects who had the money,
the tenure security, and the political leverage to plant and protect trees two
generations ago. Renters do not plant trees. Commercial strips widened for
traffic lost their planting strips. Industrial zoning never required any. And
where maintenance budgets are thin, removals outpace plantings, which compounds
quietly year over year.

Planting campaigns aimed at closing the gap frequently underperform, and the
reasons are worth knowing before launching one. Residents are sometimes asked to
consent to a tree in front of a house they rent, and decline, because they will
be responsible for the leaves, the sidewalk, and the branch that comes down in a
storm. A neighborhood that has watched the city plant trees before and then
abandon them has a rational basis for skepticism. Programs that work tend to
handle the first several years of watering themselves, put the species choice in
front of residents rather than behind them, and treat pruning and sidewalk repair
as part of the offer.

## Keeping a Street Tree Alive Long Enough to Matter

A street tree planted into a small cut in the sidewalk, surrounded by compacted
structural fill designed to hold up pavement, is planted into a container. Roots
need oxygen and water, compacted subgrade supplies neither, and the crown can
only grow as large as the root volume supports. This is why so many street trees
stall at a size that provides no useful shade and then die young, and why
replacing them on the same schedule produces a permanent population of small
trees.

The fixes are engineering rather than horticulture:

- Give each tree a usable soil volume, roughly on the order of one to two cubic
  feet of soil per square foot of the canopy you eventually want.
- Where sidewalk must sit above that soil, use structural soil or a suspended
  pavement system that carries the paving load on a frame rather than by
  compacting the root zone.
- Connect adjacent pits into a continuous trench so several trees share one
  volume of soil.
- Water deliberately for the first three to five summers, which is when nearly
  all establishment failures happen.

One mature tree is worth many saplings, which makes protecting existing large
trees during construction the cheapest cooling available and root-zone damage
during utility work the most common way cities lose them.

## Roofs, Pavements, and the Person on the Sidewalk

Reflective roofs are the least ambiguous intervention. A light-colored membrane
reflects the majority of incoming sunlight, the roof runs far cooler, the top
floor beneath it becomes habitable, cooling loads drop, and the reflected energy
leaves upward without passing any pedestrians. The benefit is immediate and the
cost, on a roof being replaced anyway, is close to nothing.

Reflective pavement is more complicated, and the complication is instructive.
Lightening a street does lower its surface temperature and reduce the heat it
stores. But the sunlight it reflects does not vanish — a good deal of it ends up
striking people standing on the street, so measurements in trial installations
have sometimes found that pedestrian radiant load goes up even as the pavement
itself reads cooler. Cool pavement is a reasonable tool for a parking lot nobody
stands in, and a poor substitute for shade on a sidewalk. Which is the general
lesson of the whole subject: an intervention that improves a number measured at
the surface, or at the weather station, may be doing nothing at all for the
body walking past.
