# The Arithmetic of an Aging Population

Some years ago Japanese manufacturers noticed that their sales of adult
incontinence pads had overtaken their sales of infant diapers. It was a
throwaway business story at the time, the kind of statistic that gets passed
around for its novelty. It is also about as clean a summary of a structural
change as you will find. The same factories, the same shelves, the same
absorbent materials, and a customer base that had quietly inverted.

Aging societies get discussed almost entirely in the language of crisis —
pension time bombs, silver tsunamis, generational theft. Very little of that
language helps, partly because it assumes something has gone wrong. Nothing
has. A population becomes old by succeeding at the two things every society has
tried to do: keeping people alive and letting them have the number of children
they want. The difficulties are real, but they are the difficulties of an
outcome, not of a failure, and they have a specific shape that is worth
understanding before reaching for remedies.

## It Is Mostly Not About Longevity

The first counterintuitive fact is that populations age from the bottom, not the
top. People assume that societies grow old because individuals live longer, and
longer lives do contribute. But the dominant mechanism is fertility decline.

Think of a population as a stack of birth cohorts. If each cohort is a fifth
larger than the one before it, the stack is a pyramid with a wide base, and it
will look young no matter how long its members live, because there are simply
far more recent arrivals than old ones. Now hold mortality fixed and shrink each
successive cohort instead. The base narrows, the pyramid becomes a column, and
in time the column becomes top-heavy — not because anyone gained years at the
end of life, but because the young ranks were never filled. A country whose
women average one and a half children will see each generation come in at
roughly three-quarters the size of the one before, and after two or three such
steps the shape of the population is transformed.

Demographers mark the passage with conventional thresholds — a society is
commonly called aging once about seven percent of its people are over
sixty-five, aged at fourteen, and super-aged at around twenty-one. What matters
as much as the level is the speed of the passage, because speed determines how
much time institutions have to adapt. France took well over a century to move
from the first threshold to the second, spreading the adjustment across many
governments and several rewrites of its welfare state. Japan covered the same
ground in roughly a quarter of that time, and several countries now aging are
moving faster still, which means arriving at an old age structure while still at
a middle income and before the pension and care systems have been fully built.

Longevity gains matter, but they are modest by comparison and their effect is
slower. Adding three years to life expectancy at sixty-five adds three years of
claims on a pension system. Cutting the birth rate by a third permanently
reduces the number of people who will ever pay into it. This is why pronatalist
policies attract so much political attention despite their poor record: the
lever that matters is at the wrong end of a seventy-year pipeline, and pulling
it today changes the worker-to-retiree ratio in the 2060s, not now.

## What the Dependency Ratio Does and Doesn't Say

The standard summary measure divides the population outside conventional working
ages by the population inside them. It is a crude instrument. Taken literally,
the total dependency ratio of an aging rich country is not much worse than that
of a country full of children — you are simply supporting different dependants.
What changes is who pays and for what.

Children are supported overwhelmingly by their own parents, in the household,
out of current income, with the state contributing schooling. Older people are
supported largely through collective mechanisms: public pensions, publicly
financed healthcare, subsidised long-term care. Shifting dependency from the
young to the old therefore shifts a large volume of transfers from inside
families to inside budgets, where it becomes visible, contested, and subject to
election results. The resources consumed may be comparable. The politics are
not remotely comparable.

There is a political consequence embedded in the arithmetic. As a population
ages, the median voter ages with it, and older voters turn out more reliably
than younger ones. The result is an electorate whose centre of gravity sits
among people who own housing, draw pensions, use health services heavily, and
have no children in school. Spending patterns in aging democracies tend to
reflect this: pensions and healthcare are defended fiercely and indexed
generously, while childcare, school capital budgets, and new housing supply
compete for what is left. None of this requires anyone to behave selfishly. It
follows from who shows up.

The ratio also flatters itself by using fixed age brackets. Sixty-five was
adopted as a pension age in an era when a manual worker reaching it was often
physically finished, and it has survived decades of improvement in health. A
more honest version counts people by remaining life expectancy rather than
years lived — treating those with fewer than fifteen years ahead of them as
"old" — and by that measure populations have aged considerably less than the
conventional figures suggest. Whether that is a comfort depends on whether
institutions adjust, and institutions defined by a number in a statute
generally do not adjust on their own.

## Pensions Are a Claim on Future Output

Public pension systems in most countries are pay-as-you-go: the contributions of
today's workers are transferred more or less directly to today's retirees. There
is no vault. The system is a promise that a future generation will do the same
for you, and its solvency depends on the size and productivity of that future
generation.

People often propose funding as the escape — save and invest instead of
promising. This helps with the government's accounts but does less than expected
for the underlying problem, because the problem is not financial. A retiree in
2050 will eat food grown in 2050, occupy housing maintained in 2050, and be
cared for by someone working in 2050. Those goods and services must be produced
that year by the people alive that year. A funded pension is a stock of claims —
shares, bonds, property — that retirees must sell to somebody in order to eat,
and the buyers are the working generation. If that generation is small, the
assets fetch less. Funding changes the mechanism by which output is transferred
from workers to retirees; it does not change the fact of the transfer.

That leaves a short and unglamorous list of genuine levers. Work longer, which
raises the number of producing years per life and is the single most effective
adjustment. Raise participation among people who are able and willing to work
but currently are not, which in most countries means women constrained by
childcare and older workers pushed out by age discrimination and rigid pay
scales. Raise productivity, so each worker supports more. Contribute more or
receive less, which is arithmetic dressed as policy. Or bring in workers from
elsewhere.

## Migration Helps, But Not the Way People Think

Immigration is the fastest-acting lever, because migrants arrive already grown
and disproportionately at working age, at no cost to the receiving country for
their upbringing. It is genuinely useful. It also cannot hold an age structure
constant, for a reason that a United Nations study made vivid around the turn of
the century: to keep the ratio of workers to retirees fixed in an aging country,
you would need inflows so large that within a few decades the migrants and their
descendants would constitute the majority of the population. Migrants also get
old. Sustaining the ratio requires not one wave but an accelerating series of
them.

What immigration realistically does is buy time and smooth edges — filling
specific shortages, particularly in care and health work, and slowing the
decline of the labour force while other adjustments are made. Treating it as a
complete solution overpromises, and the overpromise tends to discredit the real
and considerable benefits.

## The Binding Constraint Is Hands, Not Money

The part of the problem least amenable to clever financing is care. Helping
someone with dementia wash and eat takes roughly as long now as it did fifty
years ago, and unlike manufacturing it resists mechanisation almost completely.
Costs in such work rise relative to everything else even when nothing gets worse
— wages must track the general economy to attract staff, while output per hour
stays flat. Care therefore gets steadily more expensive in real terms
indefinitely, and the constraint that bites first is not budgets but the number
of people willing to do the job at the wage on offer.

Whether this becomes unmanageable depends on a question nobody has settled: as
lives lengthen, do the years of serious disability at the end lengthen with them
or get compressed into a shorter final stretch? The optimistic view is that
improvements in cardiovascular health and the delay of dementia push frailty
back faster than death, shortening the dependent period. The pessimistic view is
that medicine has become better at preventing death than at preventing decline,
so we are extending the frail years. The evidence is mixed and differs by
country and by condition, and the difference between the two scenarios is worth
more to public finances than any plausible pension reform.

## Shrinking Is Not Spread Evenly

Aging arrives first and hardest in places young people leave. Rural Japan, inland
Spain, the Italian Apennines, the small towns of eastern Germany and the
American Great Plains have age structures far more extreme than their national
figures, because out-migration removes people at exactly the ages when they
would otherwise have children. The loss compounds: fewer young adults means
fewer births means a smaller school cohort means a school closure means another
reason for the remaining young families to go.

This creates a physical problem that gets less attention than the fiscal one.
Water mains, roads, schools, clinics, and sewer networks were sized for a
population that is not coming back. Fixed costs are spread over a shrinking
number of ratepayers, and the per-household cost of keeping a village connected
rises even as the households get poorer and older. Japan has accumulated
millions of abandoned houses, enough to have a word for them, and some
municipalities have begun the politically brutal work of deliberate
consolidation — concentrating services in a compact core and quietly withdrawing
from the periphery. It is the opposite of every planning skill built up during a
century of growth, and almost nobody has institutions designed to do it well.
