# What Rhyme Actually Does

A child who cannot yet read will finish a nursery rhyme for you. Stop halfway
through the second line and the ending arrives anyway, supplied by a mouth that
has never heard the phrase "rhyme scheme." Something in the sound made a promise,
and the promise was specific enough that only a handful of words could keep it.

That is the plainest thing rhyme does, and it is easy to underrate because it
looks like a parlor trick. It is not. Before writing was cheap, a poem had to
survive inside human heads, and heads leak. Rhyme plugs the leak. Paired with
meter, it works something like an error-detecting code: if a reciter drops a
word, the line will not scan; if he substitutes the wrong word at the end, the
sound will not chime, and both the reciter and his audience will know it
immediately. The constraint narrows the field of possible reconstructions to
something a memory can manage. This is why proverbs rhyme, why legal maxims and
weather lore and the number of days in September all come packaged in couplets,
and why advertising jingles outlive the products they sold.

## What Counts as a Rhyme

In English the rule is narrower than most people assume. A rhyme requires that
everything from the stressed vowel onward match, and that the consonant sound
immediately before that vowel differ. *Late* and *fate* qualify. *Late* and
*late* do not, because identity is not relation. *Fate* and *fake* do not,
because the match has to run to the end of the word, not merely start there.

Rhymes are classified by where the stress falls. Masculine rhyme lands on a final
stressed syllable: *night* and *light*, *hand* and *command*. Feminine rhyme
carries an unstressed syllable after the match: *motion* and *ocean*, *weather*
and *feather*. Triple rhyme — *fortunate* and *importunate* — is so conspicuous
in English that it nearly always reads as comic, whether or not the poet wanted
it to. Eye rhyme pairs words that look alike and sound different, *love* and
*move*, though a number of the eye rhymes in older poetry were true rhymes when
they were written and have simply drifted apart since.

English is, by the standards of its neighbors, a rhyme-poor language. It shed
most of its inflectional endings, so words do not arrive with a stock of shared
terminations attached. It also assembled its vocabulary from Germanic, French,
and Latin sources with incompatible stress habits, which leaves polysyllables
ending in a scatter of unstressed syllables that refuse to pair. Italian has the
opposite problem, which is to say no problem: a small set of vowel endings and
regular verb conjugations mean that rhymes are everywhere and cost nothing. Dante
sustains an interlocking three-rhyme chain across the whole *Commedia* without
visible strain. English translators attempting the same scheme either give up on
it, wreck the sense to preserve it, or quietly loosen it into near-rhyme. French
prosody goes further still and prizes *rime riche*, where the consonant before
the vowel matches too — precisely the thing English forbids as cheating.

## An Imported Technology

Rhyme is not native to the European poetic tradition. Greek and Latin verse was
quantitative, built on patterns of long and short syllables, and it treated
matching line endings as an occasional rhetorical ornament rather than a
structural principle. Classical poets could have rhymed systematically. They
chose not to.

The habit came in from elsewhere and from below. Late Latin Christian hymnody,
written to be sung by congregations rather than scanned by grammarians, shifted
from quantity to stress and picked up rhyme as it went; by the time of the great
medieval sequences, rhyme was doing structural work in Latin verse. Arabic poetry
had meanwhile developed a formidable rhyming practice, the classical *qasida*
holding a single rhyme sound across every line of a long poem, a feat no European
form has ever seriously attempted. Contact across Iberia and Sicily, the
troubadours of Provence, and the vernacular lyrics that spread north from them
made rhyme the default assumption of European verse. By Chaucer, English poetry
is a rhyming art and the alternative has been forgotten.

Then it was remembered. Surrey used unrhymed iambic pentameter to translate
Virgil, the Elizabethan theater found that blank verse let a speech run on
without the tick of a closing chime, and Milton, prefacing *Paradise Lost*,
denounced rhyme outright as the invention of a barbarous age, used to dress up
wretched matter and lame meter. Coming from a poet who had written some of the
finest rhymed lyrics in the language, this was less an argument than a
declaration of scale. Rhyme closes things. An epic is not supposed to close every
ten syllables.

## An Argument Made Out of Sound

The deeper function is semantic. A rhyme asserts that two words belong together,
and the ear accepts the assertion before the mind has a chance to check it. The
stock of available English rhymes is therefore a stock of received arguments:
*womb* and *tomb*, *breath* and *death*, *love* and *above*, *fire* and *desire*.
Each pair carries a proposition about how the world is arranged. A competent poet
knows this inventory and decides, line by line, whether to lean on a familiar
pairing or to refuse it — and refusing it is itself a move, since the reader's ear
has already guessed and been disappointed.

Pope built a whole style on the persuasive force of the closed couplet. Two
lines, a balanced antithesis, and a rhyme that snaps the clasp: the sound behaves
like the conclusion of a syllogism, and the argument feels more inevitable than
its content warrants. Byron worked the same mechanism in reverse. The comic rhyme
in *Don Juan* is a deflation device — he sets up a line of high sentiment about
the lords of ladies intellectual and then brings it down by asking whether the
ladies have not hen-pecked you all. The rhyme is the joke. Nothing in the
sentiment required puncturing; the sound did it.

Rhyme also governs a poem's timing. Where the rhymes fall determines when a
reader feels arrival. A ballad that rhymes only its second and fourth lines
leaves the odd lines free to wander, which is why ballads can carry narrative
without sounding like a series of verdicts. Terza rima leans forward, because the
unrhymed middle of each tercet is a debt the next tercet must pay, and the form
cannot stop until the poet deliberately breaks the chain. Ottava rima puts six
alternating lines in front of a final couplet, which is an invitation to end on a
punchline, and that is exactly what English poets have used it for. The
Shakespearean sonnet's closing couplet arrives after twelve lines of alternating
rhyme with an audible click, and the whole difficulty of writing one is that the
click will happen whether or not the poet has actually earned a conclusion.

## Rhyme Turned Against Itself

Once a device becomes an expectation, breaking it becomes expressive. Dickinson
rhymes *Room* with *Storm* — the consonants hold, the vowel slips — and the
result is a chord that will not resolve, which suits a poem in which a dying
woman is interrupted by a fly. Wilfred Owen, in a poem whose speaker escapes a
battle into a tunnel and meets the man he killed, built the whole thing on
pararhyme: *escaped* and *scooped*, *groined* and *groaned*. The consonant frame
is identical and the vowel is wrong every time. The effect is a rhyme scheme that
fails, systematically, to console. You cannot hear it and mistake the poem for a
resolution.

Slant rhyme is now the common currency. It gives a poet most of rhyme's binding
power without the false certainty of a perfect match, and it hugely expands the
available vocabulary, which matters in a language as rhyme-poor as English. The
free verse of the last century did not abolish sonic patterning so much as move
it off the line ending and into the interior of the line, where assonance and
consonance do the same work of tying a passage together without announcing
themselves.

Meanwhile the most technically demanding rhyming in English is happening in
recorded music. Hip-hop rebuilt rhyme at a density that written verse had never
sustained: multisyllabic chains, rhymes that begin mid-line and land off the
beat, whole bars matched vowel for vowel while the consonants slide. It treats
rhyme as a rhythmic instrument rather than as punctuation, which is closer to
what Arabic and Welsh prosody have always done than to anything in the English
literary tradition.

What holds across all of it is prediction. The ear runs ahead of the sense,
guessing at the word that has to come next, and rhyme is the mechanism that makes
guessing possible. A poem can satisfy the guess, delay it, or violate it, and the
emotional weather of the poem is partly made of which of those three it chooses,
line after line. The child finishing the nursery rhyme has already learned the
whole system. Everything after that is refinement.
