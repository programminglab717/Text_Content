# The Elegy and Its Consolations

In August 1637 a ship went down in the Irish Sea and a young Cambridge man named
Edward King drowned. His college assembled a memorial volume, as colleges did,
and among the Latin and Greek contributions was an English poem by a former
classmate who had not known King especially well. That poem, *Lycidas*, spends a
great deal of its length on shepherds, considerably more on the corruption of the
English clergy, and remarkably little on Edward King. It is also one of the most
consequential poems in the language, and the argument it started about what a
poem of mourning owes to the dead has never entirely been settled.

The word itself is older than the sorrow attached to it. In Greek, *elegeia*
named a meter, not a mood: the elegiac couplet, a line of dactylic hexameter
followed by a shorter line usually described as a pentameter, the pairing giving
the verse a falling, half-checked motion. Poets used it for whatever they liked.
Tyrtaeus wrote martial exhortations in it, Theognis wrote political and
aristocratic advice, Mimnermus wrote about the brevity of youth and pleasure. The
Roman poets who took it up — Propertius, Tibullus, the young Ovid — made it the
vehicle of erotic complaint, so that for several centuries an elegy was, more
often than not, a poem about a difficult love affair. What survives of that
tradition in the modern sense of the word is only the tone: something rueful,
backward-looking, addressed to what is no longer available. The clearest
exception is Catullus writing over his brother's grave after a long journey to
reach it, ending with *ave atque vale*, hail and farewell, which is grief with no
ornament on it at all.

## The Pastoral Machine

The conventions that govern the English elegy came from a different Greek source:
the pastoral lament. Theocritus wrote of a herdsman dying while the countryside
looked on. A poem on the death of Adonis became a model for how nature should
behave in the presence of a death, and a lament for the poet Bion established the
practice of mourning a poet in the same terms he himself had used. Virgil took
the form into Latin, and the Renaissance took it from Virgil, and by the
seventeenth century a fully specified apparatus was available to anyone who
needed to write about a death in public.

The parts are recognizable once you know to look. The poem opens by invoking the
muses and apologizing for the poet's unreadiness. It reproaches the guardian
powers for their absence — Milton asks where the nymphs were when the remorseless
deep closed over the head of their loved Lycidas, and then, in a move typical of
the form's self-awareness, immediately admits that the nymphs could not have done
anything. Nature mourns: flowers droop, the flocks stop grazing, the woods go
silent. A procession of mourners files past, often allegorical, often carrying
opinions the poet holds. There is a catalogue of flowers strewn on a grave that
may not exist, since the drowned body has not been recovered. And then the turn,
the moment the poem stops grieving and reverses its direction: the dead man is
not dead but risen, translated to another element, and the mourners are told to
weep no more. *Lycidas* ends with its speaker rising, shaking out his cloak, and
going off toward fresh woods and pastures new — the consolation completed and the
living resuming their business.

## Johnson's Objection

A century and a bit later Samuel Johnson looked at all this machinery and
refused it. His verdict on *Lycidas* was that its diction was harsh, its rhymes
uncertain, and its numbers unpleasing, but the charge that stuck was about
sincerity: where there is leisure for fiction, he wrote, there is little grief.
Shepherds, flowers, and sea nymphs are not what a man reaches for when he has
actually lost someone. The elaboration is evidence of composure, and composure is
evidence that the loss was not deeply felt.

The objection is powerful and it is also, probably, wrong about how mourning
works. Grief does not generate its own forms. People in the acute stage of loss
reliably fall back on inherited procedure — the wake, the recitation, the
specific food, the order of speakers at a funeral — precisely because they have
nothing of their own to bring. The conventions of the pastoral elegy are a
container, and the poem's job is not to sound spontaneous but to carry something
that cannot be carried unassisted. Johnson's objection does, however, identify a
real risk, and the history of the form after him is in large part a history of
poets trying to answer him.

## Gray's Enlargement

The most influential answer arrived in 1751 and changed the subject entirely.
Gray's *Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard* mourns nobody in particular. It
takes up a position at dusk in a village graveyard and contemplates the people
buried under the unlettered stones: farmers, laborers, women whose names the poem
does not know, the whole population of the unrecorded. Its famous lines work by
withholding the consolation that the pastoral elegy had been built to supply. The
paths of glory lead but to the grave. Many a flower is born to blush unseen and
waste its sweetness on the desert air. There is no apotheosis here, no
translation into another element — only the observation that the ambitious and
the obscure arrive at the same place, and that a great deal of human capacity is
simply never used.

This was an enormous move. The elegy had been an aristocratic instrument for
commemorating people whose deaths mattered to the state or the university. Gray
pointed it at everyone else. Nearly every elegy since that mourns an ordinary
person rather than an eminent one, and every war memorial that lists the private
soldiers by name, is downstream of that churchyard.

## The Long Grief and the Missing Body

Tennyson's *In Memoriam A. H. H.* did something the form had not previously
attempted: it stayed. Arthur Hallam died suddenly in 1833, and Tennyson kept
writing short lyrics about it for seventeen years before publishing them as a
sequence of more than a hundred and thirty sections. The effect is not a monument
but a record of duration — the way grief recurs, alters, becomes livable, and
then ambushes the mourner again on an anniversary. The stanza he used, four lines
of tetrameter rhyming so that the outer pair encloses the inner pair, turns back
on itself at the end of each unit instead of pushing forward. It is a shape that
cannot make progress, and over a hundred and thirty sections that is the point.

*In Memoriam* also let the doubt in. Tennyson was writing while the new geology
was making the earth unimaginably old and indifferent, and the poem's grief for
one man keeps sliding into a larger fear that nothing is conserved and no
individual life is intended. The consolation it eventually reaches is hard-won
and provisional in a way no pastoral elegy's ever was, and the line everyone
quotes about it being better to have loved and lost is a fragment of a much less
comfortable argument.

Whitman, mourning Lincoln in 1865, had a different problem: a public death, a
body traveling across the country by train, and an entire nation's mourning to
absorb. He solved it by refusing to name the dead man at all, building the poem
instead out of three recurring images — a sprig of lilac, a star fallen in the
west, and a hermit thrush singing in a swamp — and letting them carry what direct
statement could not. The result is an elegy with no biography in it, which is
perhaps the only way to write about a death that belongs to millions of people at
once.

## The Refusal

The twentieth century largely stopped accepting consolation. Hardy, whose wife
Emma died in 1912 after years of estrangement, wrote a sequence about her that is
full of remorse rather than praise — a mourner going back to places they had been
together, hearing her voice and knowing it is the wind, recognizing far too late
what he had. Nothing is redeemed. Auden, writing about Yeats in 1939, declined
the apotheosis in favor of something colder and stranger: the dead poet becomes
his admirers, dispersed into the people who read him, and poetry itself is said
to make nothing happen. Owen, before either of them, had written war elegies that
treat the traditional comforts as obscene, since the machinery of pastoral
consolation cannot be applied to men killed industrially without insulting them.

That refusal is now close to a convention in its own right. The contemporary
elegy tends to distrust the turn, to stop before the uplift, to include the
undignified details — the hospital parking lot, the argument that was never
resolved, the irritation felt at a deathbed. Douglas Dunn's sequence for his
wife, written in the 1980s, gets much of its force from domestic particulars
rather than from any attempt at transcendence. The elegies written during the
AIDS epidemic often carried an added charge of anger, because the deaths were
both premature and, in the mourners' view, permitted.

What has not changed is the underlying transaction. Somebody is gone, and
somebody else has to say so out loud, in public, in a form that other people
recognize. The eulogy, the obituary, the online memorial page with its accumulated
comments — all of them still reproduce the old sequence: the statement of loss,
the praise, the complaint against the powers that allowed it, and then, usually,
the attempt to end on something bearable.
