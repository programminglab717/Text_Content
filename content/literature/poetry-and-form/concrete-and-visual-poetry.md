# The Poem You Look At

In the 1633 edition of George Herbert's *The Temple*, a poem called "Easter
Wings" is printed turned on its side. The reader has to rotate the book. Once
rotated, the two stanzas resolve into a pair of wings, each one wide at the
opening, narrowing to a single short line at the waist, then opening out again —
and the narrowing happens exactly where the poem describes falling, thinning,
becoming poor, while the widening happens where it describes rising. The shape is
not an illustration attached to the poem. It is the poem's argument, executed in
the width of the type.

Herbert was not doing anything unprecedented, which is the first thing to
establish about visual poetry: the tradition is very old, it keeps being
forgotten, and it keeps being rediscovered as though it were new.

## Older Than Any Movement

Around the third century BC, Greek poets were writing *technopaegnia* — poems
whose line lengths produced a recognizable silhouette. Simmias of Rhodes left an
egg, an axe, and a pair of wings, and the egg is additionally a puzzle, meant to
be read not straight down but by alternating from the outside inward. Roman
poetry of the fourth century AD produced something more elaborate still: the grid
poems of Optatian, laid out as a perfect square of letters, with certain letters
picked out so that they trace a figure across the block and simultaneously spell
a second, shorter poem. The base text has to make sense, the highlighted letters
have to make sense, and the figure has to look like something. It is closer to
constructing a crossword than to writing verse.

The Carolingian monk Hrabanus Maurus took the form into the ninth century with a
book of figure poems in honor of the cross, each a dense lattice of letters with
crosses, figures, and geometric shapes drawn out in colored ink across the grid.
These were enormously laborious to copy, and the labor was part of the devotion.
A scribe reproducing one had to preserve every letter in its exact cell or the
picture would collapse.

Then a long quiet, and an occasional joke. Lewis Carroll set the mouse's tale in
*Alice* as a tapering tail of shrinking type, which is a pun made out of
typography — a thing print can do and speech cannot.

## The Page Becomes a Field

The decisive modern break came in 1897, when Mallarmé published a poem whose
title says that a throw of the dice will never abolish chance. It uses several
type sizes and weights, scatters phrases across the opening as though across a
sky, and treats the double-page spread rather than the line as its basic unit.
White space is not the absence of poem; it is a measure of silence, and the
distances between fragments are as composed as the fragments. Mallarmé understood
what he had done as bringing poetry closer to music — a score with rests in it —
and nearly everything visual in twentieth-century poetry descends from that
decision to stop treating the page as a neutral container.

Apollinaire made the idea popular and charming. His *Calligrammes* arrange lines
into rain falling in slanted streaks, into a fountain, a watch, a horse, the
Eiffel Tower. They are easy to enjoy and easy to underrate, because the shapes
mostly illustrate content rather than complicate it. The Italian Futurists went
in the other direction, tearing up the conventions of typesetting entirely:
Marinetti's *parole in libertà* threw words across the page in a mix of sizes and
faces meant to register the noise of artillery and machinery, and his printers
had to be persuaded to violate every rule of their craft to set it. Dada and the
Russian avant-garde produced their own versions, often on cheap paper with hand
lettering and rubber stamps, the roughness itself a statement.

## Concrete Poetry Proper

In the early 1950s the idea acquired a name and, briefly, a program. Eugen
Gomringer, born in Bolivia and working in Switzerland in the orbit of the
concrete art movement — where "concrete" meant art made of its own materials
rather than a representation of anything — began publishing what he called
constellations: very short poems, a handful of words arranged on a field, meant
to be taken in whole rather than read through. His best-known piece sets the
Spanish word for silence in a small rectangular block with one cell left empty.
The silence in the poem is the gap, not the word, and the word is repeated only
in order to build a frame around the place where it is absent.

At almost the same moment, without contact at first, three poets in São Paulo —
Augusto de Campos, his brother Haroldo, and Décio Pignatari — were doing
something similar under the name Noigandres, taken from an obscure word in
Pound's *Cantos*. They were more theoretical and more aggressive than Gomringer.
Their manifesto, issued in the late 1950s, called for a poetry that was
verbivocovisual, a term lifted from *Finnegans Wake*, meaning that the verbal,
the sonic, and the visual dimensions of a word were to be used at once and with
equal weight. They wanted poems that worked like diagrams, road signs, and
advertisements, in a country then rapidly industrializing and full of both.

Pignatari's most-reproduced poem takes the slogan *beba coca cola* — drink coca
cola — and runs it through a short series of rearrangements, letters dropping and
recombining, until the final line reads *cloaca*, sewer. It takes about four
seconds to read and it is unimprovable: the critique is not stated, it is
performed by the letters of the brand name themselves, which turn out to contain
the word all along. Mary Ellen Solt, working in the United States, built a
forsythia out of the letters of its own name, branches rising from a baseline of
the word.

The instrument that made much of this work possible was the typewriter. Every
character occupies the same width, which turns the page into a grid the poet can
count in. A typewriter poem can be positioned exactly and reproduced exactly,
without a compositor, and a whole generation's visual poetry therefore shares a
family resemblance: monospaced, rectangular, built by overstriking and careful
spacing. The Benedictine monk and poet dom sylvester houédard made hundreds of
dense abstract compositions this way, single sheets he called typestracts, using
the machine as a drawing tool and the ribbon's two colors as a palette.

Ian Hamilton Finlay started from concrete poetry and then walked the poem off the
page entirely. In a garden in the Pentland Hills south of Edinburgh he installed
words on stone, on wood, on boats, on bridges — a single phrase cut into a slab
set among trees, a line of verse on a sundial, inscriptions that argue with the
landscape and with each other. Visiting it is the only way to read it, and the
reading takes an afternoon and involves weather.

## What the Shape Costs

The obvious objection to visual poetry is that it is a picture with words in it,
and that the shape usually tells you nothing the words had not already said. That
criticism lands on a large fraction of the work, including most of what gets
posted today as shaped verse. A poem in the outline of a heart, about love, has
used its most expensive resource to repeat itself.

The stronger pieces do something the line cannot. They make the arrangement carry
information the language does not contain — an absence, a rate, a collapse, a
simultaneity. And they trade one kind of time for another. Ordinary verse is a
temporal art: it arrives in sequence, and the poet controls pace through line
breaks and syntax. A concrete poem is apprehended more or less at once, the way a
sign is, and then examined at the reader's own speed in whatever order the eye
chooses. Sequence is surrendered; simultaneity is gained. Whether that is a good
bargain depends entirely on what the poem needs.

It also changes the politics of translation. The Brazilian and Swiss poets were
deliberately internationalist, and a poem consisting of one word and a shape can
cross a language border almost intact — which was part of the appeal in the
postwar decades, and also part of what makes the work feel thin to readers who
want a poem to be made of sentences.

The impulse never died down. Erasure poetry treats an existing printed page as
raw material, blacking out or painting over most of it so that a new poem emerges
from what remains: Ronald Johnson carved one out of *Paradise Lost*, and Tom
Phillips spent decades painting over a forgotten Victorian novel page by page,
leaving small islands of text connected by hand-drawn threads. Susan Howe
arranges fragments of archival documents at angles across the page so the reader
confronts the physical damage and disorder of the record itself. Anne Carson
published an elegy for her brother as a box containing a facsimile of the
scrapbook she made, folded accordion-style, complete with the stains. Screens
added motion, and kinetic poems now do things print never could — words that
decay, rearrange themselves, or respond to a cursor.

What all of it assumes is the thing Herbert assumed when he asked a reader to
turn the book sideways: that the poem is not an invisible thing temporarily
lodged in ink, but an object with dimensions, and that its dimensions are
available to be written with.
