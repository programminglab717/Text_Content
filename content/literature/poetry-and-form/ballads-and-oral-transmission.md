# Ballads and Oral Transmission

A collector in the Appalachian hills in 1916 writes down a song from a woman who
learned it from her mother, who learned it from hers. The names in it are local.
The story is about a young man dying for love of a hard-hearted girl, and the
last stanza has a rose and a briar growing out of two graves and tying
themselves in a knot. The same song, with different names and the same rose and
briar, had been written down in Scotland long before, and Samuel Pepys recorded
hearing a version of it in London in the 1660s and finding it charming. Nobody
carried a manuscript across the Atlantic. The song traveled in people's heads,
and it arrived intact enough to be recognized and altered enough to belong where
it landed.

That combination — durable and unstable at once — is the central fact about
traditional ballads, and most of what is interesting about them follows from it.

## The Shape

The classic English and Scottish ballad stanza is four lines, the first and
third carrying four stresses, the second and fourth carrying three, rhyming only
on the short lines. It is the same measure hymnbooks call common meter, which is
why ballad tunes and hymn tunes are endlessly interchangeable and why almost any
ballad text can be sung to almost any ballad tune of the right shape. Singers
know this and use it; a song learned without its tune gets fitted with a
borrowed one.

The form is built for memory. The short rhyming lines give the ear a landing
point every eight beats. Stress-counting rather than syllable-counting means
extra unstressed syllables can be crammed in or dropped without damaging
anything, so a stanza survives having a word forgotten or a name substituted.
Many ballads also carry a refrain, sometimes interleaved between the narrative
lines so that every other line is the same nonsense or near-nonsense phrase —
the two sisters drowning to the repeated sound of a place name, the cruel mother
burying her children among fine flowers in the valley. Refrains let a group join
in, and they buy the singer a half-line of thinking time before the next piece
of story.

## Leaping and Lingering

Ballads narrate strangely, and the strangeness is consistent enough to be a
technique. Scholars have called it leaping and lingering. The song skips whole
stretches of a story without transition — years pass between stanzas, a journey
is a single line, a motive is never given — and then stops dead and dwells on
one scene, repeating a question three times with a single word changed.

That repetition with substitution is the form's signature device. A mother asks
her son where he has been and what he ate and who gave it to him, each in its
own stanza, each stanza identical except for the substituted phrase, and the
poison in the story emerges through accumulation rather than statement. It
works as suspense, as memory aid, and as a way of letting a listener who missed
a line catch up.

What ballads almost never do is explain. The narrator has no opinions and no
access to anyone's interior. Characters are given by action and by fixed
epithets — a milk-white steed, a berry-brown maid, a lily-white hand — and these
epithets recur across dozens of unrelated songs, part of a shared stock of
phrases any singer can reach for. Whole stanzas travel the same way. There is a
stock stanza about riding a mile, a mile and a mile, and a stock stanza about
someone hearing a sound and knowing at once what it means, and they show up
wherever they fit. A ballad is assembled partly out of prefabricated parts, and
the parts are the property of the tradition rather than of any song.

The subject matter is narrow and dark. A surprising share of the traditional
canon concerns killing within a family or a courtship: a brother murdering a
sister, a mother her infants, a wife her husband, a man the woman who has
refused him. Another large group deals with the supernatural taken entirely for
granted — talking birds, the dead returning to complain that their graves are
kept wet with weeping, a knight taken by the queen of Elfland. A third records
the border raiding between Scotland and England with a flat unsentimental
attention to who took whose cattle and who was hanged for it. Comic ballads
exist and travel well, but the songs that survived longest and spread furthest
are mostly about people doing irreversible things to each other and then living
in the aftermath.

## What Happens on the Way

Track a single ballad across a few hundred collected versions and the
characteristic kinds of drift become visible.

Names and places localize. A Scottish laird's hall becomes a plantation house; a
river with a Scots name becomes the nearest actual creek. Supernatural elements
get rationalized away — a revenant lover who in the oldest Scottish texts is
plainly a demon, with cloven hooves and a ship that sinks into hell, becomes in
American tradition a man who has simply come back to persuade a married woman to
leave her husband and her baby, and the song loses its devil and keeps its
grief. Stanzas migrate between ballads with similar situations, a process
collectors call contamination, though the word implies a purity the tradition
never had.

Some material wears away entirely. A song can lose the stanza that explained
the murder and be sung for generations as a sequence of vivid consequences with
no cause, and singers do not appear to be troubled by this. What survives
reliably is the concrete: the knife, the ring, the rose and briar, the three
questions. What goes first is transition, explanation, and anything abstract.
This is a selection pressure, not an aesthetic choice, and it accounts for a
great deal of the flat, hard brilliance people admire in ballads.

Print complicated everything. From the sixteenth century onward, broadside
ballads were printed on cheap single sheets and sold in the street, often with
no tune given beyond a note that it went to some already-known air. Broadsides
took songs out of oral tradition, fixed them, sometimes rewrote them badly, and
put them back into circulation. Singers learned from sheets and then sang the
sheets into variation again. The clean opposition between the oral folk and the
commercial press does not survive contact with the evidence.

## The Collectors

Most of what we have was gathered by people with theories. Francis James Child,
a Harvard professor, spent decades assembling *The English and Scottish Popular
Ballads*, printing several hundred ballads with all the variant texts he could
find. He worked from manuscripts and printed sources rather than fieldwork, and
he did not print the tunes, which left a gap that Bertrand Bronson later spent
much of a career filling. Child's numbering is still how these songs are
identified. One of his richest sources was a Scotswoman, Anna Gordon Brown of
Falkland, whose versions were taken down in the late eighteenth century and
whose repertoire has been argued over ever since.

Cecil Sharp came to the southern Appalachians during the First World War with
Maud Karpeles and found older British ballads alive there in quantity. He was
also looking for something specific — an uncorrupted English peasantry — and
his selection reflects it; he passed over material that did not fit. Later
collectors with recording equipment, the Lomaxes among them, captured what
transcription cannot: the actual tunes, the ornamentation, the free rhythm of
unaccompanied solo singing that no notation represents well. The recordings of
singers like Texas Gladden, Almeda Riddle, and the Scottish traveller singers
Jeannie Robertson and Belle Stewart are documents of a performance practice, not
just of texts.

Commercial recording arrived at almost the same moment. Record companies sending
engineers into the American South in the 1920s captured ballad singers alongside
everything else they could sell, and a song that had traveled orally for three
hundred years could now be bought on a disc and learned note for note. That
fixed some versions and killed others. The folk revival a generation later
worked largely from those recordings and from the printed collections, so the
ballads most people know today reached them through two layers of selection —
what a collector chose to write down, and what an A&R man thought would sell.
The tradition did not end so much as change its medium, and it kept doing what
it had always done: a song learned off a record gets sung wrong, and the wrong
version gets learned by someone else.

## Memory or Recomposition

The hardest question is what a singer is actually doing. Milman Parry and Albert
Lord, working with illiterate epic singers in the Balkans in the 1930s,
demonstrated that a performer could produce thousands of lines without
memorizing them, building each performance in real time out of formulaic phrases
and recurring scenes. The same singer's "same" song came out at different
lengths on different days and was, in a real sense, a new composition each time.

Whether ballad singers work this way has been argued for decades. Some scholars
found oral-formulaic recomposition in the great Scottish repertoires; others
pointed out that most recorded ballad singers say plainly that they are trying
to sing it the way they learned it, and that recordings of the same singer years
apart are often nearly identical. The likeliest answer is that ballads sit
between the two poles: shorter than epic, tune-bound, and aimed at reproduction,
but built from enough shared formula that a lapse can be repaired on the fly
without anyone noticing. Variation then accumulates slowly, across transmissions
rather than within performances.

That also settles an old quarrel. Nineteenth-century scholars argued that
ballads were composed communally, by the dancing throng, which later critics
correctly pointed out is not how songs get written. Someone wrote each one. But
the version we have of any given ballad was made by the tradition — by hundreds
of singers forgetting and improving and misunderstanding in the same direction
over centuries. Individual composition, communal re-creation. The result is
authorless in the only sense that matters: there is no text to be faithful to,
only a field of versions, and the song is the family rather than any member of
it.
