# How the Sonnet Became a World Form

Sometime in the first half of the thirteenth century, at the court of Frederick
II in Sicily, a notary named Giacomo da Lentini wrote fourteen lines and
arranged them in a way nobody had arranged them before. The court at Palermo was
a peculiar place — Norman, Arab, Greek and Latin traditions in the same rooms,
lawyers and bureaucrats writing love poetry in the vernacular between official
duties — and the form that came out of it has outlived everything else the court
produced. The word they used was *sonetto*, a little sound or little song,
which is already a joke of scale: the smallest serious poem, named as though it
were a scrap.

What Giacomo arranged was an eight-line unit followed by a six-line unit. The
octave rhymed in alternating pairs; the sestet took new sounds and could be
turned several ways. Scholars have suggested the octave grew out of the Sicilian
*strambotto*, a folk stanza, with the sestet added on, though the evidence is
thin and the argument has never settled. What matters is the proportion.
Eight and six do not divide evenly. The poem tips forward, and the hinge
between the two parts became the form's engine.

## The Turn

Everything that has happened to the sonnet since is a negotiation with that
hinge. The Italians called it the *volta*, the turn: the point where the poem
stops doing what it has been doing and does something else. It might reverse an
argument, or answer a question the octave asked, or narrow from a general claim
to a single instance, or simply change weather. The mechanism is structural
rather than thematic, which is why it survives translation into languages whose
rhyme resources look nothing like Italian.

A sonnet is therefore a machine for changing your mind in public, on a schedule.
You get roughly eight lines to establish a position with enough force that
abandoning it costs something, and roughly six to abandon or complicate it. The
tightness is the discipline. A poet who takes twelve lines to set up cannot
turn; a poet who turns in line four has written something else. Within that
constraint, the range is astonishing, and most of the form's history is poets
discovering that it will carry cargo nobody expected.

## Petrarch, and Two Centuries of Imitation

Dante used sonnets in the *Vita Nuova*, embedding them in prose commentary and
using them to argue about love as a philosophical matter. But it was Petrarch,
assembling and reassembling his *Canzoniere* over decades, who fixed the form's
associations for the rest of Europe. Three hundred and sixty-six poems, mostly
sonnets, circling a woman called Laura who is loved, unavailable, and eventually
dead — and around that situation a whole apparatus of paradox and self-division:
freezing in fire, at war with oneself, wanting what destroys.

The imitation that followed was industrial. Spanish poets took it up through
Boscán and Garcilaso; the French Pléiade through Du Bellay and Ronsard;
Portuguese through Camões. By the time it reached England in the 1520s and 30s
with Thomas Wyatt, the Petrarchan situation had hardened into a set of moves
that could be performed with varying degrees of sincerity. Wyatt translated
Petrarch and also bent him; his version of the poem about a white doe turns into
something with a collar reading *Noli me tangere*, *for Caesar's I am*, and the
danger in that line has nothing to do with courtly love and everything to do
with being a man at Henry VIII's court. Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, working at
the same time, rearranged the machinery.

## The English Rewiring

Surrey's change looks technical and is not. English is rhyme-poor compared with
Italian, which inflects its verbs and nouns into vast families of matching
endings. A Petrarchan octave asks for four *a* rhymes and four *b* rhymes —
trivial in Italian, a serious constraint in English, where the fourth rhyme for
any given sound is usually worse than the third. Surrey's solution was three
independent quatrains and a closing couplet: seven rhyme sounds instead of
five, each needing only a partner, not a family.

The structural consequence was larger than the phonetic one. Three quatrains
create three chances to turn rather than one, and the final couplet arrives with
enormous leverage — two lines that can confirm, undercut, or ironize everything
above them. Shakespeare used that leverage constantly, and also learned to hold
the old Italian break at line eight anyway, so that his sonnets often run on two
structures at once: a Petrarchan turn buried in a quatrain-and-couplet frame.
The poems published in 1609 are among the most technically restless in the
language partly because he refused to choose between the two shapes.

The 1590s in England were a sonnet mania. Sidney's *Astrophil and Stella*,
Spenser's *Amoretti* with its interlocking rhymes that chain each quatrain to
the next, Daniel's *Delia*, Drayton's *Idea* — sequences arrived in a rush and
then the fashion collapsed almost as quickly. Out of that decade came a second form nested inside the first: the sequence.
A sonnet sequence is not a long poem chopped into fourteen-line pieces. It is a
series of closed units with gaps between them, and the gaps do most of the
narrative work. Time passes between sonnets, moods reverse without explanation,
the beloved does something offstage that we infer from the speaker's change of
tone. Readers assemble a story from evidence rather than being told one, which
is why sequences sustain rereading long after their situations have gone stale.
The device has proved portable: nearly every later revival of the sonnet has
come with a revival of the sequence attached.

What kept the form alive
afterwards was its migration off the subject of love entirely. Donne pointed it
at God and used its compression for violence, opening with an imperative and
stacking stresses until the line buckles. Milton pointed it at politics,
writing occasional sonnets on massacres and on his own blindness, and in doing
so did something quietly radical: he ran his syntax straight through the
octave-sestet break, so that the turn happens mid-line, against the rhyme rather
than with it. The Miltonic sonnet is a single sentence that ignores its own
joints.

## Silence, and Return

Then it nearly vanished. For most of the Restoration and the eighteenth century
the sonnet was regarded as a quaint, slightly embarrassing antique; the age
preferred the heroic couplet, which is built for wit and argument and has no
interest in a fourteen-line container. The revival came late in the century,
and it came substantially through Charlotte Smith, whose *Elegiac Sonnets* went
through edition after edition and made the form available again as a vehicle for
private feeling and landscape rather than courtship.

Wordsworth picked it up from there and wrote hundreds, including one that is
explicitly a defense of working inside a small room, and another instructing
critics not to sneer at a shape that had served Shakespeare, Petrarch, Tasso,
Camões, Dante and Milton. Keats used both the Italian and English patterns and
was frustrated by both. Shelley wrote *Ozymandias* in a rhyme scheme that
belongs to neither, which nobody minds, because the poem turns exactly where it
should.

The Victorians made the sequence carry weight it had not carried before.
Elizabeth Barrett Browning published her love sonnets under a title pretending
they were translations from Portuguese, a thin disguise that let a living woman
write about her own courtship without standing fully in the open. Christina
Rossetti went further and wrote from inside the Petrarchan situation's blind
spot, giving the sequence to the woman who in the Italian tradition is looked at
and never speaks. Dante Gabriel Rossetti assembled *The House of Life* over
years, a hundred-odd sonnets built to be dense, pictorial, and slow. In each
case the fourteen-line unit is doing something it was not designed for, and
holding.

## Breaking It On Purpose

By the later nineteenth century poets had begun treating the form's rules as
material rather than law. Meredith wrote *Modern Love* in fifty sixteen-line
poems — four quatrains, no couplet, no room to turn comfortably — which is
formally a statement about a marriage with no exit. Hopkins invented the curtal
sonnet, a scaled-down version in roughly three-quarters proportion, keeping the
ratio while shrinking the whole. His late sonnets of despair take the
compression and use it as a pressure vessel.

The twentieth century did not abandon the sonnet; it multiplied it. Rilke's
*Sonnets to Orpheus* arrived in a burst of composition; Neruda wrote a hundred
love sonnets without rhyming them; Millay used the form with a directness that
got her dismissed and then recovered; Frost wrote one that is a single
sentence and another that turns an argument about design into genuine horror.
Gwendolyn Brooks put a sonnet sequence in the mouths of Black soldiers in the
Second World War, letting the form's decorum grind against what it is asked to
say. Lowell wrote unrhymed fourteen-line poems by the hundred. Ted Berrigan
built sonnets out of cut-up repeated lines.

More recently the form has become a site for argument about who gets to inherit
it. Wanda Coleman's American Sonnets took the shape and roughened it
deliberately; Terrance Hayes wrote a book of poems all bearing the same title,
each one fourteen lines, each one a box he describes as both a room and a
trap. That double reading — shelter and confinement — is available because the
form is genuinely small and genuinely fixed. Eight hundred years after a Sicilian
notary settled on fourteen lines, the useful thing about the sonnet is still
that there is not enough space in it, and that running out of room forces the
turn.
