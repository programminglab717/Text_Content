# Imagery and Metaphor

Ask a class to find the imagery in a poem and you will get a page with every
adjective underlined. Blue, cold, jagged, luminous. The exercise teaches that
imagery is decoration — the coat of paint applied after the sentence is built —
and it is close to the opposite of what the word means. An image is not a pretty
modifier. It is a particular thing, presented rather than explained, doing work
that the abstract statement it replaces could not do.

Consider the difference between writing that the morning was cold and writing
that the dog's water had a lid of ice on it. The second is not a more decorated
version of the first. It is a different kind of claim: it puts the reader outside
in the yard, it carries a household and an animal along with the temperature, and
it leaves the judgment — *cold* — for the reader to make. That handover is the
whole mechanism. The image supplies the evidence and declines to supply the
verdict.

## What Counts as an Image

The Imagists, working in London in the years before the First World War, built a
short-lived movement on exactly this point, and their most durable contribution
was a definition. Pound described an image as something that presents an
intellectual and emotional complex in an instant of time. That is abstract
phrasing for a concrete demand: stop explaining, and put the thing in front of
the reader entire. The specimen everyone cites is two of his own lines about
faces in a crowded metro station and petals on a wet black bough. The poem
contains no verb, no assertion, and no comparison word. Two pictures are set side
by side and the reader performs the connection, which takes perhaps half a
second and cannot be undone once it has happened. What survives of Imagism is not
the manifesto, abandoned quickly even by its authors, but the assumption it left
behind: that a poem can consist of presented particulars and nothing else, and
that a reader will supply the rest.

Williams pushed this further with a wheelbarrow, rain, and chickens, prefaced by
the claim that so much depends on them. The poem refuses to say what depends, or
why. Generations of readers have tried to make the wheelbarrow a symbol of rural
labor or the poet's aesthetic, and the poem holds out against all of it. The red
is just red. The point is that attention itself has consequences, and the poem
enacts that rather than arguing it.

Two practical corrections follow. First, imagery is not only visual, though the
etymology pushes everyone that way. The crunch of gravel, the smell of a hot
photocopier, the particular ache of a hand that has been holding a heavy bag,
the sensation of a floor that is not quite level — these are images, and the
non-visual ones often land harder because readers have fewer ready-made pictures
to file them under. Second, specificity is not a stylistic preference but a
functional requirement. *Bird* is a category. *Grackle* is a thing, with a walk
and a voice and a place it is likely to be standing. The named particular
activates knowledge the general term leaves dormant.

## Tenor, Vehicle, and the Distance Between Them

Metaphor is the operation of describing one thing in terms of another. I. A.
Richards supplied the terms most often used for its parts: the *tenor* is the
subject actually under discussion, the *vehicle* is what it is being described
as, and the shared properties that make the transfer work are the *ground*.
Grief is the tenor, a stone in the chest is the vehicle, and weight and hardness
and the sense of an object lodged where it does not belong are the ground.

Two things about this arrangement govern whether a metaphor succeeds. The first
is distance. If tenor and vehicle are too close, nothing happens — comparing a
lake to a pond is a classification, not a metaphor. If they are too far apart,
the reader cannot find the ground and the line reads as noise. The interesting
zone is the middle, where the connection is not obvious but becomes undeniable
once made, and the reader has to do a small amount of work to get there. That
work is where the pleasure lives.

The second is direction. Metaphors are asymmetric in a way similes are not.
Calling a surgeon a butcher is an accusation; calling a butcher a surgeon is a
compliment. The vehicle lends its properties to the tenor and the traffic runs
one way only. This is also why the choice of vehicle carries an argument: to
describe a city as an organism is to commit to talking about its health, its
growth, and eventually its death, and to describe the same city as a machine is
to commit to talking about efficiency, parts, and repair. The metaphor is not
illustrating a position already held. It is generating one.

That generative quality is clearest with metaphors that are so common nobody
notices them. English talks about argument as combat — positions are attacked and
defended, claims are indefensible, criticisms are shot down, someone wins. The
vocabulary is consistent enough to constitute a system, and it makes certain
moves in a conversation thinkable and others nearly unsayable. There is no
ordinary English for treating a disagreement as a dance or a joint construction
project, which is a limit on thought imposed by a figure of speech nobody chose.

Simile does the same work with a different logical claim. *Like* and *as* concede
that the identification is partial, and that concession buys room: a simile can
be extended, qualified, and wandered away from in a manner that a metaphor
cannot. The Homeric simile exploits this shamelessly, leaving the battlefield for
six or ten lines to follow a lion into a sheepfold or a farmer irrigating a
terrace, then returning to the spear it was ostensibly explaining. The
comparison has become a window into an entire second world, the peacetime one,
and the poem's meaning is partly made of the contrast between the two.

## Dead Metaphor, Mixed Metaphor, Sediment

Most metaphors die. The leg of a table, the mouth of a river, the head of a
department, the heart of the problem, grasping an idea, falling for someone,
running out of time — every one of these was a live transfer once and is now
simply what the thing is called. Language is sedimented metaphor, layer upon
layer, and the sediment is so deep that abstract thought would be impossible
without it. A poet's routine trick is to excavate: use a dead metaphor in a
context that reanimates its body, so that a reader who has said *the foot of the
mountain* a thousand times suddenly notices the foot.

Mixed metaphor fails for a reason worth stating precisely. It is not that mixing
is ugly; it is that a vehicle brings its own internal logic, and combining two
vehicles produces a scene that cannot be pictured or reasoned about. The moment a
writer sets the ship of state sailing, the ship's rules apply — it can founder,
be steered, take on water — and asking it to also put down roots breaks the
machinery that was doing the explaining.

## The Neighbors

Metaphor has relatives that work by association rather than resemblance.
Metonymy substitutes something adjacent: the crown for the monarchy, the White
House for an administration, a good table for good food. Synecdoche substitutes a
part for a whole or a whole for a part — hired hands, wheels for a car, boots on
the ground. The distinction matters because the two operations pull in opposite
directions. Metaphor jumps across categories on the strength of similarity;
metonymy stays put and slides sideways along a chain of real connection. Roman
Jakobson built a general theory on this pair, arguing that poetry tends toward
the metaphoric pole and realist narrative toward the metonymic one, which is a
rough claim but a useful lens: a realist novelist describes a character through
his possessions and gestures, while a lyric poet describes him as weather.

Old English and Old Norse verse compressed both operations into compound nouns.
The sea is the whale-road, the body is the bone-house, blood is battle-sweat.
These kennings are metaphors packed to the density of single words, and a
listener had to unpack each one in real time, which is part of why the verse
rewards being heard slowly.

## Images That Hold a Poem Together

Beyond the local figure, images do structural work. The metaphysical conceit is
the extreme case: a single comparison pursued past the point where an ordinary
metaphor would exhaust itself, as in Donne's twin compasses, where two lovers
separated by travel become the two legs of a drawing instrument, one fixed and
leaning after the other as it goes around. The conceit is showing off, and it
knows it, but it also converts an emotional claim into something a reader can
verify geometrically.

More common and more quietly effective is recurrence. An image arrives early in a
poem doing literal work, disappears, and returns at the end carrying everything
that happened in between. The second appearance is not a repetition; it is the
same object with a different charge, and the difference measures the distance the
poem has traveled. T. S. Eliot's term for the technique of finding an external
scene or chain of events that will reliably produce a particular emotion in a
reader — the objective correlative — names the ambition behind it. The poet does
not tell you what to feel and does not describe feeling it. The poet builds the
situation that causes the feeling, and then stands back.

The test for whether an image is working is unsentimental. Delete it. If the poem
loses only ornament, it was ornament. If the poem loses an argument, a shift in
time, a change in the temperature between two people, or the reason the last line
lands, then it was load-bearing, and it was never a description in the first
place.
