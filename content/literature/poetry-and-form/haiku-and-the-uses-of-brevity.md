# Haiku and the Uses of Brevity

The rule most English speakers learned in school is that a haiku has seventeen
syllables arranged five, seven, five. The rule is not wrong so much as
mistranslated, and the mistranslation has done real damage — it has produced
several generations of poems that are the right length and the wrong shape,
like a house built to correct dimensions with no door.

Japanese does not count syllables. It counts *on*, sound units closer to what
linguists call morae, and they are shorter than English syllables. A long vowel
counts twice. The syllabic *n* counts on its own. The word *haiku* is three
*on*, and the word *Nippon* is four. Seventeen *on* is a smaller container than
seventeen English syllables — most estimates put the equivalent information
load at something closer to ten or twelve English syllables. Which means that
a dutiful five-seven-five in English is not a faithful copy of the form. It is
roughly half again too long, and the extra room gets filled with the exact
material the form exists to exclude: articles, connectives, explanation.

## The Cut

What actually organizes a haiku is not the count but the break. Japanese poets
use *kireji*, cutting words — *ya*, *kana*, *keri* and others — which have
almost no lexical meaning and function as punctuation with an emotional
temperature. A *kireji* divides the poem into two unequal parts and holds them
apart. English has no equivalent, so translators and English-language poets use
a dash, a colon, or simply a line break and a change of subject.

The two parts are not a statement and its elaboration. They are two perceptions
set side by side, with the relation between them left unstated. That gap is the
poem. In Bashō's best-known hokku — the old pond, the frog jumping in, the
sound of water — the cut falls after the first phrase, and what follows is not
an explanation of the pond but a separate event that happens to occur at it.
Nothing is compared to anything. No conclusion is offered. The reader supplies
the connection, and because the reader supplies it, it lands as discovery rather
than as instruction.

This is why haiku resists metaphor of the explanatory kind. A simile tells you
how to relate the two halves; the form's whole mechanism depends on your not
being told. Poems that fail in this tradition usually fail by closing the gap —
by adding the line that makes the point, which is invariably the line that kills
it.

## The Season

The other traditional requirement is the *kigo*, a season word. This is not
decoration and not simply a way of establishing when the poem happens. Japanese
tradition accumulated enormous almanacs, *saijiki*, cataloguing thousands of
these words and their associations: not only obvious ones like snow or cherry
blossom, but frogs, certain insects, particular foods, specific festivals,
kinds of haze, the first bath of the year. Each carries a bundle of
associations that centuries of prior poems have deposited on it.

A season word is therefore a compression device. Naming one imports a whole
emotional register at no cost in syllables — which is precisely what a
seventeen-*on* poem needs, and precisely what a foreign reader lacks access to.
When English-language poets adopted the form they inherited the slot without the
almanac. Some have tried to build regional equivalents; most have settled for
concrete seasonal detail doing similar work more weakly.

## From Linked Verse to Standalone Poem

The form did not begin as a form. It began as an opening move. *Renga*, and
later the more playful *haikai no renga*, were collaborative linked-verse
sessions in which poets alternated stanzas of three lines and two, each link
connecting to the one immediately before it and departing from the one before
that. The opening stanza, the *hokku*, carried special obligations: it set the
season, acknowledged the place and the host, and had to be complete enough to
stand while still inviting continuation. Writing a good one was a compliment and
a display of skill.

Bashō, in the seventeenth century, raised the *hokku* into something with the
gravity of a whole poem. His travel book of the narrow road to the deep north
alternates prose and verse — the hybrid called *haibun* — and the verses in it
depend on the prose around them and also exceed it. Buson, a generation or so
later and a working painter, brought a pictorial eye and a taste for color.
Issa, later still, wrote with a plainness that can be mistaken for artlessness
and was not; his poems about insects, about poverty, and about the deaths of his
children are among the least decorated in the tradition.

The separation became official only in the late nineteenth century, when Masaoka
Shiki, a critic and poet working in the middle of Japan's rapid modernization,
argued that the opening verse should be read and written as an independent
poem and gave it the name haiku. He also pushed a doctrine of sketching from
life, which nudged the form away from literary allusion and toward direct
observation. Much of what Western readers take to be timelessly Japanese about
haiku is in fact a reform barely more than a century old.

## Haiku in English

The form arrived in English twice. The first time was through the Imagists
around 1912, who read what translations existed and took from them a principle
rather than a shape. Pound's two-line metro poem is the clearest result: he
described writing a much longer version, destroying it, writing a shorter one,
and arriving at the final form only after a long delay. Faces in a crowd,
petals on a wet black bough, and nothing joining them but a semicolon — the
Japanese cut, in English, without the syllable count.

The second arrival came after the Second World War, largely through R. H.
Blyth's multi-volume translations, which reached the Beats and through them a
wide readership. Kerouac wrote a great many short poems he called American
haiku and argued explicitly that the syllable count should be dropped, since it
measured nothing in English. Most serious English-language haiku practice since
has agreed: three lines, short-long-short, usually under fourteen syllables,
with the cut, a concrete image, and no editorializing.

The tradition has produced its own small classics — poems built on a single
observation turned by one word, often with the turn happening in the last line
and consisting of nothing more than a shift in scale. It has also produced an
enormous quantity of pleasant nature description, which is the form's
characteristic failure mode: a haiku that contains only one perception is a
photograph, not a poem.

## What Shortness Buys

Brevity is not a Japanese invention and its advantages are general. The Greek
Anthology preserves hundreds of epigrams two and four lines long, including
grave inscriptions whose economy is the source of their force. Martial built a
career on the closing sting of a short poem. The fragments of Sappho are read
partly for what the damage has removed, which is an accident that has taught
readers a real lesson: a text with gaps recruits its reader.

That is the mechanism. A short poem cannot contain its own interpretation, so
the interpretation has to happen in the reader, who is then invested in it. It
also cannot hide. In a long poem a weak line is carried by its neighbors; in a
seventeen-*on* poem every word bears load, and a single slack adjective sinks
it. Williams's sixteen words about a wheelbarrow survive because there is
nothing in them to remove, and because the poem's opening claim — that so much
depends on this — is never cashed out, so the reader spends decades deciding
what depends and why.

The discipline transfers even to writers who never attempt the form itself.
Learning to place a cut, to trust juxtaposition, and to leave the connective
tissue out is a general skill, and the quickest way to acquire it is to spend a
while trying to say something true in eleven syllables and finding out how many
of your habits are padding.
