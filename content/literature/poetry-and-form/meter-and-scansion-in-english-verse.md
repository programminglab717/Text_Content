# Meter and Scansion in English Verse

Say this aloud: *The curfew tolls the knell of parting day.* Now say it the way
you would say it to someone standing next to you at a bus stop, with no thought
of poetry at all. The two readings are not identical, but they are close, and
the closeness is the point. Gray's line is metrical, but it does not force you
to chant. What scansion tries to describe is the narrow gap between how the line
is built and how a person actually says it — a gap that good poets keep open on
purpose, because that is where most of the expressive work happens.

English meter is usually accentual-syllabic. Two things are being counted at
once: the number of syllables in the line, and the arrangement of stressed and
unstressed syllables within it. A line of iambic pentameter nominally has ten
syllables in five alternating pairs, weak-strong, weak-strong. That nominal
pattern is a template, not a transcript. Almost no interesting line of verse
matches it exactly, and lines that do match it exactly tend to sound wooden.
Pope made the joke himself in *An Essay on Criticism* — "And ten low words oft
creep in one dull line" — a line that is perfectly regular and deliberately
dreary, so that the reader feels the point rather than merely agreeing with it.

## Stress Is Relative

The single most useful thing to know before scanning anything is that stress in
English is not a binary property of a word sitting in a dictionary. It is a
comparison between neighbors. In *the knell of parting day*, the word *of*
carries less force than *knell* on one side and *part-* on the other, so it
reads as unstressed. Put the same *of* between two genuinely weak syllables and
it can carry the beat perfectly well. Metrists call this promotion and demotion:
a weak syllable flanked by weaker ones gets promoted into the strong position,
and a heavy monosyllable wedged between two heavier ones gets demoted out of it.

This is why arguments about scansion so often go in circles when people treat it
as a matter of right answers. Two experienced readers can mark the same
pentameter differently and both be defensible, because they are hearing slightly
different weightings of the same relative contrasts. What they will rarely
disagree about is the underlying template. You can feel the five-beat frame
under a line even when the surface refuses to sit neatly on it — and a poet can
rely on that, throwing the surface off the frame for a syllable or two, trusting
that the reader's ear will hold the frame steady in the background.

## The Feet, and What They Actually Do

The traditional vocabulary is borrowed, somewhat awkwardly, from Greek and
Latin. Classical meters counted syllable length — long and short vowels,
measured in duration — not stress, so the transfer is a translation rather than
an inheritance. Still, the names are convenient shorthand. The iamb is
weak-strong (*destroy*). The trochee is strong-weak (*garden*). The anapest
piles two weak syllables before a strong one (*in the night*); the dactyl
reverses it (*merrily*). The spondee is two strong syllables together, the
pyrrhic two weak ones. Coleridge wrote a small demonstration poem for his son in
which each foot is described in its own rhythm, which remains the fastest way to
get the four main ones into your ear.

Line length gets a second Greek term stacked on top: dimeter, trimeter,
tetrameter, pentameter, hexameter, for two through six feet. Most English verse
before the twentieth century lives in two of these. Iambic pentameter carries
the long poem, the dramatic speech, and nearly all blank verse and sonnets;
iambic tetrameter, often paired with trimeter, carries song, ballad, hymn, and
light verse. The difference is not merely length. A pentameter line has an odd
number of feet and resists splitting cleanly in half, which is why it tolerates
long, clause-heavy sentences without falling into sing-song. Tetrameter divides
neatly into two and two, and the ear keeps wanting to rock.

## Substitution

The real grammar of English meter is the art of departing from the pattern in
controlled ways. The commonest departure is the trochaic first foot: a line
begins with a strong syllable where a weak one is expected, then falls back into
step. Shakespeare does it constantly, and so does almost everyone writing
pentameter after him, because it gives the line a shove off the starting block
without disturbing anything downstream.

Substitution in the middle of a line carries more weight. When Donne opens a
holy sonnet with *Batter my heart, three-personed God*, the stack of heavy
syllables at the start jams the line up against itself; the violence of the
prosody is the argument. Tennyson's *Break, break, break* does something similar
with three consecutive stresses against a nominally light meter, and the effect
is the sound of a thing hitting something and stopping. The most extreme example
in the language may be Lear's *Never, never, never, never, never* — five
trochees in a row where the play's meter expects five iambs, a complete
inversion of the line that reads as the collapse of ordinary speech under grief.

Two other tools sit alongside substitution. The caesura is the pause inside the
line, usually created by syntax or punctuation rather than by meter, and moving
it around from line to line is the chief means of keeping blank verse from
droning. Enjambment runs the sentence past the line ending, so that the metrical
unit and the grammatical unit pull against each other; the reader arrives at the
end of a line, is held for a fraction of a second by the line break, and then
discovers that the sentence has somewhere else to go. Milton built *Paradise
Lost* out of exactly this tension, long periodic sentences slung across dozens
of lines whose ends almost never coincide with a full stop.

## Meters That Count Something Else

Accentual-syllabic verse is only one option, and historically it is a latecomer.
Old English poetry counted stresses and ignored syllable totals: four strong
beats to the line, two on either side of a firm medial break, with alliteration
binding the halves together. A line of *Beowulf* can run long or short in
syllables and still be perfectly regular, because syllables were never what was
being counted. That tradition survived in the alliterative revival of the
fourteenth century, in *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight* and *Piers Plowman*,
before giving way to the syllable-counting, rhyming line that came in through
French and Italian models.

Purely accentual meter never disappeared. Nursery rhymes are accentual — the
number of unstressed syllables between beats varies wildly and nobody notices,
because the beats arrive on time. Hopkins built a whole theory on this,
sprung rhythm, in which each foot begins with a stress and may be followed by
any number of slack syllables; he argued, with some justice, that he was
returning English verse to its native rhythm rather than inventing something
new. Syllabic verse takes the opposite approach, counting syllables and
disregarding stress entirely. Marianne Moore wrote elaborate syllabic stanzas
whose shape is visible on the page and nearly inaudible to the ear, which is a
strange and deliberate effect: a form the reader can see but not hear.

## What Scansion Is Good For

Marking a poem with breves and ictuses is not an end in itself, and treating it
as one has soured generations of students on the whole subject. Scansion is a
diagnostic. You do it when something in a line is working and you want to know
why, or when a line you have written yourself is limping and you cannot hear the
cause.

The procedure is simple enough. Read the passage aloud several times, naturally,
and mark the stresses you actually produce. Count syllables. Look for the
pattern that most of the lines conform to most of the time — that is the
template. Then go back and find the places where the poem departs from it, and
ask what is happening in the sense at exactly those places. The departures
cluster, and they cluster meaningfully: at moments of grief, violence, surprise,
or emphasis, and very often at the turn of an argument. In a sonnet the rhythm
frequently roughens right where the thought changes direction.

What emerges from doing this a few dozen times is not a set of rules but an ear.
You start to hear meter the way a musician hears a bar line — not as a
constraint imposed on the sentence from outside, but as a second pattern running
underneath the sentence, sometimes in step with it and sometimes deliberately
out. The pleasure of metrical verse is mostly the pleasure of that
counterpoint, the two patterns converging and diverging across a poem. Strip
away one of them and you lose the interference effects, which is why a
metrically perfect line and a metrically shapeless one can fail in the same way
and for opposite reasons.
