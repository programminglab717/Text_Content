# Epic Conventions: A Field Guide

Three poems, composed centuries and continents apart. One begins by shouting a
word that means something like *listen*. One announces that it sings of arms and
a man. One asks a goddess to sing the anger of a particular warrior and the
suffering it caused. A reader who knows what to look for can tell within two
lines that all three intend to be epics, because all three are performing the
same opening move — declaring a subject and then requesting help with it. The
conventions of epic are unusually easy to identify, and the reason is that most
of them were not decorative choices. They were solutions to a technical problem,
and they survived long after the problem did.

## The Opening Sequence

Nearly every epic in the European line opens with three components in quick
succession. The *proposition* states the subject in a compressed phrase: arms and
a man, the wrath of Achilles, man's first disobedience. The *invocation* asks a
muse or some other external power for assistance, which transfers responsibility
for the poem away from the poet and toward a source that cannot be interrogated.
Then comes the *epic question*, a request that the muse explain the cause — why
this happened, which god was offended, where the trouble started. Virgil asks it
explicitly a few lines in, and the answer sets the machinery of the poem running.

The fourth component is structural rather than verbal: the narrative begins *in
medias res*, in the middle of things. The Odyssey opens with its hero already
stranded most of the way through his ordeal, and the story of the years before is
told later, by Odysseus himself, at a banquet. The Aeneid opens in a storm. This
is not merely a device for grabbing attention. It lets the poet start at a point
of maximum tension, and it makes the backstory available as a set of inset
narratives that can be assigned to characters, which means the audience hears
about the fall of Troy from a man who was there rather than from a narrator.

## Why the Epithets Are There

For a long time readers treated Homer's stock phrases as charming redundancy.
Dawn is rosy-fingered every time she appears. Achilles is swift-footed even when
sitting still and sulking. The sea is wine-dark. Odysseus is much-enduring,
resourceful, godlike, depending on where in the line his name falls.

In the early twentieth century Milman Parry showed that these phrases form a
system, and that the system is metrical. A Greek hexameter line is a fixed
pattern of long and short syllables, and a name plus epithet is a prefabricated
block of the right shape to fill a particular slot in it. For each major
character, and for each of the common metrical positions, the tradition had
generally developed one and only one suitable phrase — extensive coverage with
almost no duplication. That combination, breadth without waste, is not what an
individual writer with a pen produces. It is what a tradition of oral performers
produces over generations, because a singer composing at speed in front of an
audience cannot pause to search for a word.

Parry and his student Albert Lord then went to the Balkans and recorded singers
who were still doing it, performing long heroic poems to the accompaniment of a
one-stringed fiddle, composing rather than reciting, producing a different text
every time. The singers had absorbed not memorized poems but a stock of phrases,
lines, and scene-templates that could be assembled in performance. That
discovery reframed the whole inventory of epic conventions as working equipment.

The templates operate at a larger scale too. Arming scenes follow a fixed order —
greaves, corselet, sword, shield, helmet, spear — so a singer needs to remember
only whose armor it is and what is unusual about it. Feasts, sacrifices,
arrivals, assemblies, and single combats all have standard sequences. Whole lines
recur verbatim as connective tissue: the formula for a speech beginning, for
nightfall, for a spear finding its mark. Repetition in this idiom is not a
stylistic weakness. It is the load-bearing structure, and audiences appear to
have enjoyed hearing the familiar sequence arrive on schedule.

## Standard Equipment

Beyond the formula, a set of recurring large-scale features turns up so
consistently across traditions that spotting them is a reliable way of placing a
poem in the genre.

The **epic simile** interrupts the action with an extended comparison, often ten
lines or more, that develops a scene of its own before returning to the point of
contact — a warrior's advance compared to a forest fire, a crowd compared to
disturbed bees, a wounded man compared to a poppy heavy with rain. Because the
vehicles are usually drawn from farming, weather, and domestic life, these
passages import an entire peacetime world into a poem otherwise full of killing.

The **catalogue** lists forces, ships, ancestors, or peoples at length. The
catalogue of ships in the Iliad names contingents and commanders for hundreds of
lines, and it is often skipped by modern readers, who are not its audience. For
listeners whose communities were on the list, it was the part that made the poem
theirs.

**Ekphrasis** describes a made object in such detail that the description becomes
a narrative of its own. Achilles' shield holds two cities, a wedding, a lawsuit, a
siege, ploughing, harvest, a vineyard, and dancing — the entire world that the war
is consuming, engraved on the equipment being used to consume it. Virgil answers
with a shield showing future Roman history, which lets a poem set in the distant
past display events that had not yet happened when its story takes place.

The **descent to the underworld** sends the hero among the dead for information
he cannot get from the living. Odysseus summons shades at the edge of the world
and learns the terms of his return; Aeneas goes down bodily and is shown the
souls of Romans not yet born. The convention is durable enough that Dante built a
hundred-canto poem out of its first stage, with Virgil himself as the guide.

**Funeral games**, **genealogies**, **divine councils**, the **aristeia** in which
one warrior has his moment of unstoppable dominance, and the **flyting** or
formal exchange of boasts and insults before combat all recur in the same way.
When the young Beowulf is challenged by Unferth at the Danish court, and answers
by narrating his own past exploits at length, an audience familiar with the form
knew exactly what was happening and how it would end.

## The Line Underneath

The shape of the verse determines the shape of the formulas, which is why epic
meters are so specific to their traditions. Greek and Latin epic ran on dactylic
hexameter, a long line of six feet with a predictable caesura. Old English and
Old Norse used an alliterative long line split by a pause, with the alliterating
stresses binding the two halves — a system that rewards a stock of synonyms
beginning with different sounds, which is exactly what the kenning supplied.
Sanskrit epic used the *shloka*, a compact thirty-two-syllable unit that lets the
Mahabharata run to a length far exceeding the Iliad and the Odyssey combined.
Finnish oral poetry, gathered in the nineteenth century into the Kalevala, used a
short trochaic line with heavy parallelism, each statement restated in slightly
different terms. Italian Renaissance epic settled on ottava rima, six alternating
rhymes and a closing couplet, which Camões carried into Portuguese for a poem
about the sea route to India.

Milton, writing in English in the seventeenth century, refused rhyme altogether
and used blank verse, and his opening promises things unattempted yet in prose or
rhyme and announces an intention to justify the ways of God to men. Both claims
are conventional moves — the boast of novelty and the statement of purpose — made
in an unconventional meter.

## From Engineering to Costume

The moment epics started being written rather than sung, the conventions changed
function. Virgil had no technical need for formulaic epithets; he used them
because they signaled what kind of poem the Aeneid was. The same is true of every
literary epic since. The conventions became a costume that a poem puts on to
declare its ambitions, which is a different thing from the toolkit that produced
them.

Costumes invite mockery, and the mock-epic followed promptly. Pope's *The Rape of
the Lock* applies the complete apparatus — invocation, supernatural machinery,
arming scene, single combat, epic simile — to a young woman having a lock of hair
cut off at a party. The arming is a dressing table. The combat is a card game.
The joke only works because readers knew the equipment intimately enough to feel
the mismatch, which is evidence of how thoroughly the conventions had penetrated.

They have not gone away. Derek Walcott's *Omeros* gives Caribbean fishermen the
names of Homeric heroes and runs them through wounds, sea voyages, and a descent,
in long lines arranged in threes. Oral epic itself never stopped: the story of
Sundiata, founder of the Mali empire, is still performed by West African
hereditary praise-singers who work exactly as Lord's Balkan singers did, from a
stock of formulas rather than a fixed text. The equipment is old, but it was
always meant to be used in front of a live audience, and it still is.
