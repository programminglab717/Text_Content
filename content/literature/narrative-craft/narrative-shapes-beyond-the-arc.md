# Narrative Shapes Beyond the Arc

Kurt Vonnegut liked to stand at a blackboard and draw stories as graphs. The
vertical axis ran from ill fortune at the bottom to good fortune at the top, the
horizontal from beginning to end, and then he would chalk a line. Man falls in
hole, man climbs out: a dip and a recovery. Cinderella: a low flat line, a
staircase of small gifts, a plunge at midnight, a vertical leap off the top of
the chart. He had proposed something like this as a master's thesis in
anthropology, the department had turned it down, and he mentioned the rejection
for the rest of his life with visible pleasure.

The joke in the demonstration is that the shapes are so simple, and the second
joke is that they are not universal. Vonnegut's curves assume a story is a
fortune that rises and falls. An enormous amount of narrative, including some of
the narrative most people have read, does not work that way at all, and a writer
who knows only the rising-tension arc has one tool for a job with several.

## Structure Without a Fight

The clearest alternative is kishōtenketsu, a four-part scheme inherited from
classical Chinese poetry and used across East Asian narrative ever since, from
literary fiction to the four-panel newspaper comic. The parts are introduction,
development, turn, and reconciliation.

Three of those are unremarkable. The third is the one worth studying. The turn
introduces something that appears to have nothing to do with what came before — a
new element, a different scene, an image from elsewhere — and the fourth part
establishes the relation between the two. Nothing has to be in conflict with
anything. There is no antagonist, no obstacle, no escalation.

Consider a four-panel strip: a woman waters a plant on a balcony; the plant grows
over several panels' worth of implied months; a neighbor's cat appears on the
wall; the cat is asleep in the plant's shade. That is a complete narrative in the
kishōtenketsu sense, with a genuine turn and a genuine resolution. Under the
conflict-based model it is a sequence of unrelated incidents. The scheme is not a
loophole for stories that fail to have a plot; it is a different account of what
produces the sensation of completeness, locating it in juxtaposition rather than
in causation.

Western writers meeting it for the first time often report relief, because so
much anglophone craft instruction treats antagonism as the engine of all
narrative — every scene a small battle, every chapter a setback. That is one good
engine. It is not the only one, and a great deal of quiet fiction has been
mangled in workshop by the demand that conflict be inserted into it.

## The Bag and the Arrow

Ursula Le Guin made the argument polemically in an essay from the mid-1980s. The
story of the hero, she wrote, is the story of the spear: a line, aimed, released,
striking. But the earliest and most useful tool a human group possessed was
almost certainly not a weapon. It was a container — a net, a sling, a bag — and the
work of gathering things and carrying them home makes a different kind of story,
one in which the shape is not a trajectory but an accumulation.

You can dismiss the essay's anthropology and still find the formal proposal hard
to argue with, because the books it describes plainly exist. *Mrs Dalloway* spans
a single day and its structure is a pattern of proximities: people crossing
London, a car passing, a plane writing in the sky, consciousnesses touching and
separating. Almost nothing is resolved because almost nothing was posed as a
question. The completeness is spatial rather than causal.

The picaresque works on a related principle from the other end of the social
scale. From *Lazarillo de Tormes* through *Tristram Shandy* and well past it, the
episodic novel strings incidents on a traveling character and derives its
coherence from voice and from cumulative portraiture rather than from a chain of
consequences. Aristotle disliked exactly this and said so. Several centuries of
readers have disagreed with him, and the reason they can is that a strong enough
narrating voice supplies its own continuity: the question the reader is carrying
is not *what happens next* but *what will he say about it*.

## The Shapes a Medium Imposes

It is easy to talk about structure as if it were a matter of aesthetics. Much of
the time it is a matter of commerce and physical format, and the history is
unusually legible.

Horace instructed that a play be neither shorter nor longer than five acts, and
Renaissance and neoclassical drama obeyed, which is why Shakespeare arrives in
five acts and why the analysts of the nineteenth century built five-part
diagrams. The number has no basis in the psychology of attention. It is an
inherited rule that became a convention that became a description.

The Victorian novel's three volumes came from the circulating libraries. Lending
houses charged by the volume and preferred to have one book out with three
subscribers at once, so publishers produced three-deckers at a fixed price, and
novelists wrote to that length whether or not their material justified it. The
padding in some mid-century fiction is not a failure of discipline; it is a
contractual obligation. When the largest libraries refused to keep paying the
inflated price in the mid-1890s, the three-decker died inside a couple of years,
and the single-volume novel that replaced it produced a visibly tighter kind of
book.

Serialization shaped narrative even more directly. A novel published in monthly
numbers has to deliver something satisfying every installment and leave a reason
to buy the next one, which pushes the writer toward a chain of small crests
rather than one long build — and toward large casts, since a subplot can carry a
number when the main line needs to rest. Dickens's structural habits are
inseparable from that publishing arrangement.

The pattern repeats in broadcasting. American network drama developed act breaks
positioned around commercials, and as the advertising load grew the hour drama
went from four acts to five or six, each ending on a small turn designed to hold
a viewer through the interruption. Streaming removed the breaks, and within a few
years episodes began to lose their internal act structure entirely, sprawling to
irregular lengths and ending mid-movement because nothing required them to end
anywhere. Some of that is artistic freedom and some of it is the loss of a
constraint that had been doing unacknowledged work.

## Braids, Frames, and Mosaics

Several other shapes are common enough to name, and they are not arcs.

The braid runs two or more strands in alternation, usually separated by time or
viewpoint, and generates its tension from the reader's growing suspicion about
how they connect. The strands need not converge; often the pleasure is in the
rhyme between them rather than in a meeting. A braid gives a writer a legitimate
way to cut away at the worst possible moment, which is why the form is so common
in thrillers, and a legitimate way to set two lives against each other without
comment, which is why it is equally common in literary fiction.

The frame nests one telling inside another, and each layer of nesting adds
distance and doubt. Mary Shelley puts the creature's account inside Victor's
inside a sea captain's letters home, so that the most sympathetic voice in
*Frankenstein* reaches the reader through two men with reasons to distort it.

The mosaic or story cycle assembles a whole out of parts that each stand alone.
*Dubliners* and *Winesburg, Ohio* are the usual examples, and the form has
expanded considerably since — novels built of linked stories spanning centuries,
books that take an object or a house as the through-line instead of a person.
What holds a mosaic together is a subject rather than a sequence, and the reader
assembles the shape in retrospect, which means the last piece carries enormous
weight.

## Learning a Shape

Readers are trained, and this is the part that most discussions of alternative
structure underrate. The reason the rising arc feels natural is not that it is
natural but that most people have absorbed thousands of instances of it before
they are twenty. A narrative shape is a set of expectations about what will
happen next and when the thing will be over, and a reader with no such
expectations cannot feel suspense, satisfaction, or surprise, since all three
depend on prediction.

Which means an unfamiliar shape carries a real cost. A book built on
juxtaposition rather than escalation has to teach the reader how to read it,
early and unmistakably, or it will be experienced as a conventional novel that is
going wrong. Writers who do this well usually signal it in the first few pages —
through an obvious formal pattern, a table of contents that announces the
architecture, a first chapter that cannot possibly be an ordinary first chapter.
Calvino opens *If on a winter's night a traveler* by addressing the reader
directly about the act of beginning a Calvino novel, which is an instruction
manual disguised as a joke.

The choice is not between structure and freedom. It is between shapes, and the
only real error is choosing one by default.
