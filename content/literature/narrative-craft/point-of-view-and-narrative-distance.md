# Point of View and Narrative Distance

A woman comes into her kitchen and finds a letter on the table, addressed to her
husband in handwriting she does not recognize.

That is an event. It is not yet a scene, because nobody is standing anywhere.
Before a word of it can be written, someone has to decide who is watching, how
much the watcher knows, and how close the prose is willing to sit to the inside
of that person's skull. Those three decisions are what writers mean by point of
view, and together they shape a finished page more thoroughly than plot does.
Two novels with the same outline and different narrators are not variations on a
theme. They are different books, and often they are about different things.

## The Distance Dial

The most useful way to think about point of view is not as a menu — first,
second, third — but as a dial that controls distance. John Gardner, who taught
fiction for many years and wrote about it with unusual precision, called this
psychic distance, and he demonstrated it with a run of sentences describing the
same man, each closer in than the last. The far end reads like a chronicle: the
year, the weather, a large man stepping out of a doorway. The near end is the
man's own half-formed grumbling about the cold, set down without quotation
marks, as though the narration had been contaminated by him.

Nothing in that sequence changes grammatically. Every sentence can stay in third
person and past tense. What changes is the amount of narrative apparatus sitting
between the reader and the character's consciousness. Far out, the narrator is a
historian with a wide lens and its own vocabulary. Close in, the narrator has
nearly dissolved, and the diction belongs to the character.

Most of the real work of point of view happens on this dial, and most
point-of-view trouble in draft manuscripts is distance trouble rather than
pronoun trouble. A writer settles in close, then halfway down the paragraph
pulls back to describe the character's face the way a stranger across the street
would see it. The reader is jolted without understanding why. Move the dial
deliberately and the shift reads as a change of tempo; move it by accident and it
reads as a mistake.

A reliable symptom of unwanted distance is the filter verb. *She saw the letter
on the table. She noticed the handwriting. She felt her stomach drop.* Each of
those constructions inserts a small observing frame between the reader and the
thing observed. Strip the frames and the same material comes at the reader
directly: *The letter lay on the table. The handwriting was not anyone's she
knew.* The information is identical. The experience is not. Filters are not
forbidden — they are useful precisely when you want the reader aware that
perception is happening — but used unconsciously they hold every sentence at
arm's length.

## What First Person Buys, and What It Costs

First person sounds like the easy choice because it sounds like talking. It is
not easy, and its difficulties are structural rather than stylistic.

What it buys is immediacy and voice. A first-person narrator can be funny,
evasive, self-pitying, or wrong, and the reader absorbs all of that as character
without a word of explanation. It also buys a second timeline for free: there is
the moment being narrated and the later moment of narration, and the gap between
them can carry enormous weight. A narrator looking back on a mistake knows how it
ended. A narrator recounting events as they happen does not. Deciding where the
teller is standing in time is not a minor housekeeping matter; it determines
whether the book has irony available to it.

What it costs is coverage. Everything in the novel has to pass through one
person's senses, memory, or hearsay. Anything happening in another room has to be
reported later by someone with a reason to report it. Writers solve this with
letters, eavesdropping, confessions, and coincidence, and the solutions are
visible as solutions when they are used too often. First person also costs the
reader's easy sympathy in one specific way: a narrator who describes her own
virtues is unbearable, so self-characterization has to arrive obliquely, through
what she notices and what she declines to mention.

Second person is first person's rarely used cousin. It can work — Jay
McInerney's *Bright Lights, Big City* runs on it, and Italo Calvino built *If on
a Winter's Night a Traveller* around the reader as a character — but it draws
attention to itself on every page. Over a long book that attention has to be
earning something, usually dissociation, instruction, or a game about the act of
reading itself.

## Third Person, and the Question of Whose Head

Third person is not one thing. The distinction that matters is between omniscient
narration and limited narration, and beneath that, the distinction between who
speaks and who sees. The narratologist Gérard Genette separated these two
questions cleanly — voice and focalization — and the separation resolves a great
deal of confusion. A narrator can speak in a register no character possesses
while restricting what is perceived to a single character's field of view.

True omniscience is a nineteenth-century instrument, and the great practitioners
used it for something specific. George Eliot in *Middlemarch* moves between
Dorothea's self-deception and Casaubon's dry misery and then steps back to
comment on both, and the stepping back is the point: the narrator has a moral
intelligence larger than any character's. Tolstoy does something similar across
a much wider social field. Omniscience gives you scale and judgment. It costs
intimacy, and it has fallen out of fashion partly because contemporary readers
distrust a voice that claims to know everything.

Limited third has become the workhorse of modern fiction because it combines the
intimacy of first person with the grammatical freedom of third. You stay inside
one character per scene, but you can adjust distance at will, and you can hand
the next chapter to someone else. The discipline it requires is simple to state
and hard to maintain: within a scene, do not report anything the viewpoint
character cannot know. Head-hopping — sliding into a second character's thoughts
for one convenient sentence — is the commonest failure, and it is a failure
because it silently tells the reader that nobody's interiority is private, which
dissolves the tension that limited narration exists to create.

At the far end of the dial sits objective or camera-eye narration, which reports
only what could be recorded: speech, gesture, surfaces. Hemingway's "Hills Like
White Elephants" is the standard example. Two people sit at a station bar,
discuss something they never name, and the story withholds every thought. The
effect is not coldness but pressure. The reader is forced to do the interpreting,
and interpretation performed under uncertainty feels more like life than being
told would.

## Free Indirect Style

Between direct thought and plain narration lies the technique that does more work
in the modern novel than any other. Jane Austen did not invent free indirect
style, but she made it a precision instrument, and Flaubert turned it into a
method.

The mechanics are unremarkable: keep the third person and the past tense, but let
the character's idiom, judgments, and blind spots leak into the narrator's
sentences. Instead of *She thought the evening had been a disaster,* you write
*The evening had been a disaster.* The reader knows whose verdict that is. No
attribution is required, and none is given.

What this buys is a double exposure. The sentence carries the character's
conviction and the narrator's silent assessment of it at the same time. When
Emma Woodhouse is certain she has arranged someone else's happiness, the prose
gives you her certainty undiluted, while the shape of the book quietly disagrees.
Irony produced this way is far more durable than irony produced by a narrator
telling you a character is mistaken, because the reader arrives at the judgment
independently and therefore believes it.

The technique has a cost too: ambiguity. When every sentence might belong to the
character or the narrator, a reader can lose track of who is asserting what. Most
writers handle this by anchoring the paragraph — one clearly attributed thought
near the start — and then letting the rest run free.

## Unreliability, and What Makes It Work

Every first-person narrator is unreliable in the trivial sense that people are
limited. The interesting cases are the ones where the gap between what the
narrator says and what the reader can see becomes the subject of the book.
Unreliability only functions if the reader has some independent access to the
facts. That access has to be smuggled in through details the narrator reports
without understanding: a servant's expression, a date that does not fit, a
kindness offered and refused.

Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* is built almost entirely out of this
mechanism. The butler narrating it is scrupulously honest about events and
systematically wrong about their meaning, and everything the reader learns about
his wasted life arrives through his own careful, oblivious reporting. Nothing is
revealed by a second voice. The novel simply trusts the reader to notice.

The failure mode is a narrator who lies for no reason except to enable a late
twist. Withholding is not the same as unreliability. A narrator who conceals a
central fact she thinks about constantly, purely so the author can spring it, has
broken the compact that made her worth listening to.

## Holding the Line

Once the dial is set, the rest is consistency, and consistency is mostly a matter
of noticing. Whose vocabulary is this sentence using? Could this character
actually perceive this? Is the narration describing a gesture, or interpreting
it, and is the interpretation one this person would reach?

Rules get broken productively all the time. Dickens moves between an omniscient
present-tense voice and a first-person retrospective one in *Bleak House*, and
the alternation is the structure of the book. Faulkner hands *The Sound and the
Fury* to four different consciousnesses, one of whom cannot order time at all.
But those are decisions made early and sustained, not drift. The reader will
follow a narrator almost anywhere as long as the ground underfoot stays the same
ground from one paragraph to the next.
