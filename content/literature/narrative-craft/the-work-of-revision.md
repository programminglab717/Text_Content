# The Work of Revision

The surviving drafts of *The Waste Land* are covered in another man's
handwriting. Ezra Pound went through Eliot's typescript with a pencil, striking
out whole passages, querying lines in the margin, reducing the poem by something
close to half. What came out included a long narrative opening set in Boston and
a stretch of rhymed couplets in imitation of Pope. What remained was the poem
people actually read. Eliot dedicated it to him as the better craftsman, which is
a gracious way of saying that the poem was made by deletion.

Most writing is made that way, and the fact remains oddly difficult to believe
while you are doing it. Drafting feels like the work. Revision feels like
cleanup, a chore standing between a finished thing and its readers. The order of
importance is closer to the reverse.

## Re-Vision Means Seeing It Again

The word is worth taking literally. Revision is not correction. Correcting is
what you do to a page that is right in conception and wrong in detail — the
misspelled name, the comma splice, the repeated word. That work is necessary and
takes an afternoon. Revision is the harder business of looking at what you have
built and asking whether it is the thing you meant to build, which frequently
means discovering that it is not.

The confusion between the two is the single greatest waste of effort in writing.
A great many manuscripts have been sentence-polished to a high gloss and then
dismantled, because the writer spent three months perfecting the prose of scenes
that did not survive the structural pass. Beautiful sentences are not free to
produce, and every hour spent on a paragraph makes it harder to cut, which means
premature line-editing does double damage: it consumes time and then it
manufactures attachment.

So the passes go in a fixed order, from largest to smallest, and the discipline
is in refusing to drop down a level early.

## The Structural Pass

The first question is what the book is about and whether it is about that on
every page. This sounds vague and becomes very concrete the moment you make a
reverse outline.

A reverse outline is built from the draft rather than before it. Go through the
manuscript scene by scene and write one line for each: what happens, who changes,
what the reader learns, how much time passes. Do not describe what the scene is
trying to do. Describe what it does. The gap between those two things is where
most of the diagnosis lives.

Laid out on a page or a wall of index cards, the failures become visible in a way
they never are inside the prose. You find three consecutive scenes in which a
character resists the same pressure without the pressure increasing. You find
that your subplot disappears for a hundred and forty pages and returns expecting
to be remembered. You find that a piece of information the climax depends on was
delivered once, in passing, in chapter two, when the reader had no reason to
retain it. You find the sag — almost every draft has one, usually somewhere past
the midpoint, where the initial situation has been exhausted and the endgame has
not started.

A useful standard at this stage is that every scene should be doing at least two
jobs. A scene that only conveys information is an errand; a scene that only
develops character is a portrait. The scenes that survive revision tend to be the
ones advancing the plot while altering a relationship, or delivering necessary
exposition inside an argument that the characters care about for other reasons.
When you find a single-purpose scene, the fix is usually not to cut it but to
merge it with the one next to it.

Once the shape holds, a middle pass goes through the surviving scenes one at a
time, and its questions are different. Does the scene turn? Something should be
different at the end of it than at the beginning — a balance of power, a piece of
knowledge, an intention — and scenes that end where they began are the reason a
structurally sound book can still feel static. Where does it start, and could it
start later? Whose scene is it, and is the viewpoint holding steady or drifting
into another head for the convenience of a paragraph? Is the dialogue carrying
subtext or are the characters saying what they mean, which people do far less
often than draft dialogue suggests? Is the description positioned where a person
would plausibly notice it, or delivered in a block on arrival like a real estate
listing?

These are local questions, but they are not line editing, and answering them
frequently changes what a scene is. The middle pass is where most of the
rewriting-as-opposed-to-editing happens, and where a draft stops being an account
of the story and becomes the story.

## Becoming a Stranger to Your Own Sentences

The obstacle throughout is that you cannot read your own draft. You know what you
meant, and your eye supplies it. A sentence that conveys nothing to an outside
reader will read perfectly to the person who wrote it, because that person is not
reading the sentence at all — they are consulting the memory of the intention
behind it.

Every reliable revision technique is a trick for defeating this.

Time is the most effective and the least popular. Six weeks in a drawer will do
more than any method, because memory of the intention fades faster than memory of
the words, and when you return you read what is there. Reading aloud is the next
best thing, and it catches an oddly specific class of problem: clumsy rhythm,
unintended repetition, dialogue no human would say, sentences that run out of
breath. The mouth is a slower reader than the eye and refuses to skip.

The rest are variations on making the page look unfamiliar. Print it. Change the
typeface. Read it on a phone, where the line breaks fall differently and a
paragraph you have seen a hundred times arrives in unfamiliar pieces. Read the
chapters out of order. Read the last paragraph of each scene in sequence, with
nothing between them, which exposes whether your endings all have the same
cadence — and they usually do.

One more, less obvious: hand the draft to someone and ask them not for an opinion
but for a report. Where did you get bored, where did you get lost, where did you
put it down, who did you think was speaking. Readers are unreliable about
diagnosis and almost never wrong about symptoms. If three people stall in the
same chapter, something is wrong in that chapter, whatever they say the reason
is.

## What Murdering Your Darlings Actually Meant

The phrase comes from Arthur Quiller-Couch, in a lecture on style published in
1916, and it is quoted constantly by people who have inverted its meaning. It is
routinely reassigned to Faulkner, to Wilde, to Stephen King, and taken to mean
*cut the parts you love most*, which as general advice is close to insane. You
love some passages because they are good.

Quiller-Couch was narrower and more useful. His target was the impulse toward
exceptionally fine writing — the moment when a writer feels the arrival of a
splendid image or a beautifully turned cadence and reaches for it. Obey the
impulse, he said, write the thing, and then strike it out. What he was describing
is the passage that is performing rather than working: prose in which the reader
becomes aware of the writer's hand at exactly the moment the writer wanted to be
admired.

That is a test you can actually apply. Reread the sentences you are proudest of
and ask whether they are doing anything besides being impressive. If the metaphor
illuminates, keep it. If it exists so that the metaphor may exist, it goes. The
same test catches most over-researched passages — the two paragraphs on
eighteenth-century rope-making that cost you a week in the library and serve the
scene not at all.

Cutting at the line level has a compounding effect that surprises people the
first time they measure it. Removing a modifier here and a clause there across a
chapter tightens it by a tenth without any scene being lost, and a tenth is the
difference between prose that moves and prose that has to be pushed. Writers
often discover that the best version of a paragraph is the one made of its own
second half.

## Knowing When It Is Done

There is no clean answer, and the honest version of the advice is that you stop
when the changes stop being improvements and start being alternatives.

The signal is recognizable. Early in revision every pass makes the thing better
and you can say why. Later, you replace a word and then replace it back the
following week. You are no longer fixing anything; you are stirring. Whitman
revised *Leaves of Grass* across a lifetime and some of the later versions are
diminished, the strange energy of the first edition sanded off by a more
respectable older man. Auden went through his collected poems late in life
altering and suppressing, including work other people regarded as among his best.
Revision has a direction, and past a certain point it runs backward.

The editorial relationship carries the same risk from outside. Gordon Lish's
cuts to Raymond Carver's stories were drastic, and when the uncut versions were
finally published readers could see two different writers in the same material —
one spare and withholding, one warmer and more explicit. Which is better is
genuinely arguable, which is the point: at that level of the work there is no
correct answer, only a choice about what kind of thing it will be. That choice
belongs to whoever is going to sign it.
