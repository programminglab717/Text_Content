# What a Place Does to a Story

*Bleak House* opens in fog. Not a sentence of fog — a page of it. Fog up the
river and down the river, fog on the marshes, fog in the rigging of ships, fog in
the eyes and throats of old pensioners wheezing by their firesides. Dickens
repeats the word until it stops functioning as weather, and then, having soaked
the reader in it, he arrives at the Court of Chancery, where a lawsuit has been
grinding through its own murk for so long that nobody alive remembers what it was
about.

The fog is not scenery. It is the argument of the novel delivered in advance, in
a form the reader absorbs before they know they are being told anything. That is
the case for taking setting seriously: a place, properly handled, does work that
no amount of explanation can do, because the reader experiences it rather than
receiving it.

## Weather Is Never Only Weather

The objection to this is old and has a name. John Ruskin coined *pathetic fallacy*
for the habit of granting human feeling to inanimate things — the cruel sea, the
sullen sky — and he meant it as a criticism of sloppiness, of writers who
sentimentalized nature rather than seeing it. The criticism holds against the lazy
version. Rain that falls because a character is sad is bad writing, and readers
who have been trained by a hundred films to expect a thunderstorm at the moment of
crisis will groan at it.

But weather in fiction is rarely a symbol pointing at an emotion. More often it is
a constraint. Cold forces people indoors together. Heat makes them irritable and
slow and unwilling to leave a shaded room, which is why so many novels of
simmering family conflict are set in August. Rain changes what can be carried,
how far anyone will walk, whether a conversation happens on the step or in the
kitchen. Handled this way, weather generates plot rather than commenting on it,
and it never feels like decoration because it is doing something physical.

The distinction is worth applying as a test. Take out the weather in a scene and
see what breaks. If nothing breaks, it was mood-setting and can probably be cut to
a clause. If the scene collapses — because the characters no longer have a reason
to be in the same room, or because the thing that had to be hidden is now visible
in good light — the weather was structural.

## Interiors, and What They Record

Balzac begins *Le Père Goriot* with a boarding house, and he takes his time. The
street, the exterior, the garden, and then the sitting room, which has a smell he
insists on and for which he says there is no name — the smell of a place where
people who have run out of money eat cheap food in a room nobody has aired. He
describes chipped carafes, mended linen, furniture that has been demoted from
better houses. Then he introduces the people.

Modern taste finds that opening slow, and the technique has been thinned out
considerably since. But the underlying idea is not dated at all. An interior is a
ledger. Everything in a room arrived because someone chose it, inherited it,
could not afford better, or could not bring themselves to throw it out, and the
accumulation records a history of money and taste and failure that would take
pages to narrate directly.

What separates a living interior from a furniture catalogue is selection. A room
described completely is dead on the page; a reader's mind cannot hold twenty
objects and will retain none of them. A room described through four things — the
right four — becomes vivid, because the reader completes it. And the right four
are the ones that carry information. Not the sofa, but the plastic still on the
sofa's arms. Not the bookshelf, but the gap in it.

## Knowing What a Local Knows

Place fails most often through generality. A town described as a small town with
a main street and a church is not a place; it is a category, and it belongs to
nobody. What produces conviction is the kind of knowledge that only comes from
having been somewhere — which road floods first, what the water tastes like, which
of the two petrol stations the farmers use and which the tourists do, the fact
that the good bakery closes on Mondays for reasons everyone knows and nobody can
quite explain.

Details of this class do something specific to a reader's trust. They are too
arbitrary to have been invented for effect, and their arbitrariness is precisely
what makes them credible. Once a writer has spent three of them, the reader will
accept a great deal on faith, because the writer has demonstrated they were
actually there.

This is the practical reason to write about places you know well, and the reason
research for unfamiliar places should aim at the useless rather than the
essential. Anyone can find out what a nineteenth-century cotton mill produced.
What makes the mill real on the page is the lint in the air that settled on
everything, the fact that workers learned to lip-read over the noise of the
looms, the specific heat of a room kept humid on purpose so the threads would not
snap. Those are the details that make a reader feel the author knows more than
they are saying — which is the sensation, more than any other, that produces
surrender.

## Place as a Force

Sometimes a landscape rises past setting and starts behaving like a character.
Hardy gives Egdon Heath an entire opening chapter with no people in it at all,
establishing the heath as something older and more patient than anyone who will
walk across it, and thereafter every human plan in the novel is measured against
its indifference. Melville turns the Pequod into a closed society with its own
laws, hierarchy, and weather. Mervyn Peake builds a castle so large and so
ritual-bound that its inhabitants are effectively its organs.

The mechanism in each case is the same: the place has agency over what can
happen. It permits certain actions and forbids others. Characters adapt to it,
resent it, try to leave, and are shaped by the attempt. An island novel and a
city novel are structurally different not because of what they describe but
because of what each place makes possible — how easily people can avoid each
other, how far help is, what it costs to be seen.

Mikhail Bakhtin argued that particular combinations of time and space recur in
literature as organizing units, each with its own logic: the road, where strangers
meet by chance; the provincial town, where time is cyclical and nothing changes;
the threshold, where crisis occurs. It is a useful way of looking at a manuscript
that is not working. Often the problem is that the story needs a road and has been
set in a parlour.

## Invented Places, and the Cost of Consistency

Fantasy and science fiction have the additional job of building the place from
nothing, and the discipline is harder rather than easier, because an invented
world has no reality to check itself against.

The rule most successful invented settings obey is that the world must be
consistent before it is interesting. Le Guin's Gethen works because the planet's
permanent cold and its inhabitants' biology are followed through into economics,
architecture, politics, patience, and the vocabulary of insult. Tolkien's
Middle-earth persuades partly because its languages and place-names have
internally coherent histories, so that the map reads like sedimentation rather
than invention.

The failure mode is the world assembled from features. A desert here because
deserts are evocative, a floating city there because it looks good, and no
explanation of what anybody eats. Readers do not consciously audit these things,
but they register incoherence as thinness. The single most clarifying question for
an invented setting is unglamorous: where does the food come from, who grows it,
and who takes a cut. Answer that honestly and most of the politics, geography, and
conflict of the place assemble themselves.

## Against Description

All of which can tip too far. The nineteenth-century novel could stop for three
pages of landscape because its readers had no photographs, no film, and limited
opportunity to travel, and description was genuinely supplying something
unavailable elsewhere. That is no longer the situation, and a modern reader who
hits a long static passage will skim it, because nothing is happening.

The workable approach is to deliver place in motion. A character crossing a room
notices what she notices because of what she wants; a man arriving in a town at
night sees only what the headlights pick out. Perception is selective and
purposeful, and place filtered through a purpose never reads as a pause. It also
solves the problem of how much to describe, because the character's attention does
the editing.

The fog in *Bleak House* survives this objection, incidentally, not because
Dickens was permitted more description than a modern writer but because the fog is
already doing three jobs at once — establishing London, establishing the season,
and making an argument about the law. Description earns its length when it is
never only description.
