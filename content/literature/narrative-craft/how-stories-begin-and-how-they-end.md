# How Stories Begin and How They End

"Marley was dead: to begin with." Dickens spends five words killing a man the
reader has never met, and the phrase "to begin with" does three jobs at once — it
means *first of all*, it means *at the outset of this story*, and it warns that
the condition may not hold. Before a single scene has occurred the reader knows
the register (wry, spoken aloud, slightly theatrical), the relationship (a teller
addressing a listener directly), and the fact that a ghost is coming. That is
what a first page is: not a hook, exactly, but a contract.

## What the First Page Promises

Readers make a set of decisions in the first few paragraphs, most of them
unconsciously, and almost none of them about plot.

They decide how close they are going to be standing. A narration that opens with
a character's thoughts establishes that interiority is available; one that opens
with a long shot of a landscape establishes that it is not, or not yet, and a
reader who has settled into the second mode will feel a jolt if you supply the
first in chapter four. They decide what the prose will ask of them — whether
sentences will be long, whether they will have to hold ambiguity, whether jokes
are permitted. They decide, roughly, what kind of book this is, and they will
keep believing it for a surprisingly long time.

This is why openings that misrepresent the book are more damaging than openings
that are merely slow. A literary novel that starts with a corpse to borrow some
urgency has taught the reader to expect an investigation, and the reader will
spend forty pages waiting for one instead of attending to what is actually there.
A comic novel that opens in a solemn register loses its laughs for a chapter
while the reader recalibrates. Consistency of promise matters more than
excitement.

The practical test is not *is this gripping* but *is this the same book*. Read
your first two pages and your two hundredth, and ask whether the same person
wrote them with the same intentions.

The famous openings mostly work this way rather than by grabbing. Orwell begins
*Nineteen Eighty-Four* with a bright cold April day and clocks striking thirteen,
and the single anomalous number does more world-building than a page of
exposition could, because it tells the reader that the ordinary has been altered
at the level of infrastructure. García Márquez opens *One Hundred Years of
Solitude* with a man before a firing squad remembering the afternoon his father
took him to see ice, and in one sentence installs the time-scheme the whole novel
will use: a narrator who moves freely between a future already fixed and a past
still full of wonder. Austen's first line about a single man in possession of a
good fortune announces irony as the book's governing method, so that when the
narrator later reports a character's opinion the reader already knows to check
it. None of these lines is a hook. Each one is an instruction manual.

## The False Start

Nearly every first draft begins in the wrong place, and it begins in the wrong
place for a structural reason rather than a failure of skill: the writer needed
those pages and the reader does not.

A novelist opening a new manuscript is finding the voice, working out how the
character talks, establishing for herself where everyone lives. That material
gets written down because it has to be thought, and it accumulates as background,
weather, a character waking up and moving through a morning, a page or two of
history. It is often decently written. It is also almost always the scaffolding,
and scaffolding comes down when the building can stand.

The revision move is blunt and reliable. Find the first moment in the manuscript
where something genuinely changes — a decision, an arrival, a refusal, a piece of
news — and try starting there. Delete everything before it, and then go through
what remains looking for the handful of facts from the deleted pages that the
reader actually requires. There are usually fewer than you expect, and they can
be reinserted a clause at a time once the story is already moving. A reader will
carry a great deal of unexplained information forward if something is happening.
A reader will carry almost none of it if nothing is.

The same instinct applies at a smaller scale to every chapter, which is why the
advice to enter late is one of the few craft rules that survives contact with
almost any style.

## Starting in the Middle, and Why It Often Fails

Horace praised Homer for plunging into the middle of things, and *in medias res*
has been standard advice ever since. It is good advice that is routinely misread
as *start with an explosion*.

The trouble with opening on maximum action is that tension is not a property of
events. It is a property of caring about the people the events are happening to.
A car chase involving two strangers is information, not suspense; the reader
watches it the way one watches a fire drill. Openings of this kind often produce
the opposite of their intended effect, because the writer has spent the loudest
material available at the moment when the reader is least equipped to feel it,
and everything afterward is quieter.

What Homer actually does is begin at a point of maximum consequence rather than
maximum noise — a quarrel, a refusal, a man sitting on a beach weeping — and let the
history arrive later. The distinction worth holding onto is between starting in
the middle of the *story* and starting in the middle of a *scene*. The second is
a stylistic choice. The first is structural, and it means the situation is
already loaded when the reader walks in: relationships already strained, a debt
already owed, a decision already half made.

## Endings: Closure Is Not Resolution

Endings fail more often than beginnings, and they fail less visibly, because a
reader who has come four hundred pages will usually finish regardless and simply
report afterward that the book "didn't quite land."

The most useful distinction here is between resolution and closure. Resolution
means the plot questions are answered: we learn who did it, the marriage happens
or does not, the ship reaches port. Closure is the feeling of completeness, and
it is produced by something else — by the book's thematic question receiving an
answer, or by a formal rhyme with the opening, or by a change in the narrating
voice that signals the telling has done what it set out to do.

Books can have one without the other. A mystery can resolve every thread and
still feel like a ledger being closed. *Middlemarch* resolves relatively little
in the melodramatic sense and yet arrives at one of the most complete endings in
English, because Eliot shifts register in the final pages and takes the long
view, placing her characters in a scale of time that the reader has not been
offered before. The sensation of an ending, there, comes from a change of
altitude rather than from plot.

The workshop formula descended from Aristotle — that an ending should feel
surprising and inevitable at once — is more precise than it sounds. Aristotle's
point was that the strongest reversals occur contrary to expectation but *because
of* what came before, not in spite of it. Inevitability is manufactured in the
middle of the book, not at the end. If an ending feels arbitrary, the fault is
almost never in the last chapter; it is in the twenty chapters that failed to
plant what the last chapter needed. The principle usually cited as Chekhov's gun
is the same idea running the other direction: anything loaded early must fire,
and anything that fires must have been loaded.

## The Last Page

A few practical observations about final pages, which are their own small genre.

Most drafts end one scene too late. Writers who have found the true ending keep
going for another few pages out of a sense of duty — tidying up secondary
characters, explaining what everyone did afterward, delivering an interpretation
of the events the reader has just experienced. The interpretation is the worst
offender. If the book has done its work, saying what it means diminishes it; if
it has not, saying so will not help.

Others end a scene too early, stopping at the climax itself. A climax is a
discharge of energy, and readers need a beat afterward to feel the change: a
short final scene, quiet, in which someone does something ordinary and the
reader sees that it is not ordinary any more. Call it the exhalation. It is
frequently the most memorable passage in the book precisely because nothing
happens in it.

And the last sentence carries disproportionate weight, which is why so many
great ones are rhythmic rather than informational. Fitzgerald finishes *Gatsby*
with a cadence — boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past —
that says nothing about events at all. Joyce ends *Ulysses* on a word of
affirmation repeated until it becomes music. These lines are doing to the ear
what the ending is doing to the story: producing a sense of completion by falling
into a shape that cannot continue.

Not every book should close, of course. Fowles ended *The French Lieutenant's
Woman* twice, offering incompatible outcomes and declining to choose, and the
refusal is the point. But an ambiguous ending is only earned when the ambiguity
is the subject. An ambiguous ending arrived at because the writer could not
decide reads exactly like what it is, and readers can tell the difference
immediately, even if they cannot say how.
