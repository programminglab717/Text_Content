# Writing Dialogue on the Page

Transcribe two minutes of an ordinary conversation and read it back. It is
almost unusable. People begin sentences they abandon halfway, repeat each other's
last three words, answer questions that were not asked, trail off, say *sort of*
and *you know* at intervals of a few seconds, and spend a remarkable proportion of
the exchange confirming that the other person is still listening. Meaning gets
across anyway, carried by tone, timing, face, and shared history — none of which
survives the transcription.

Written dialogue is therefore not a recording. It is a set of marks designed to
produce, in a silent reader, the impression of overheard speech. The techniques
that create that impression are frequently the opposite of what real speech does,
and learning them is mostly a matter of learning what to leave out.

## Compression Without Tidiness

The first move is to cut the connective tissue. Greetings, arrivals, the business
of sitting down, the confirmation that both parties have understood the topic —
all of it goes, and the scene starts as late as it can. If two people are meeting
to argue about money, begin somewhere in the middle of the argument and let the
reader assemble the preamble. Readers are fast. They will accept being dropped
into an exchange already underway far more readily than they will sit through its
throat-clearing.

But compression is not the same as making speech tidy. The commonest failure in
amateur dialogue is characters who speak in complete, grammatical, well-organized
sentences that answer each other directly. It reads as competent and sounds like
nobody. The trick is to keep a controlled amount of real speech's disorder —
interruption, a question ignored, a sentence that runs out of steam — while
cutting everything that carries no information.

A related discipline concerns dialect. Rendering an accent phonetically is
tempting and almost always a mistake: it slows reading to a crawl, it flattens a
person into a set of misspellings, and it implicitly positions one way of
speaking as standard and the other as deviation. Accent lives in syntax and
vocabulary rather than spelling. Word order, idiom, the rhythm of a clause, which
things a person names directly and which they talk around — these will do the
work without a single dropped *g*.

## Nobody Answers the Question

Watch what happens when dialogue is direct:

> "Are you going to leave?"
> "Yes. I've been unhappy for two years."

Nothing happens. Both lines are complete, the information transfers cleanly, and
the scene is over. Now:

> "Are you going to leave?"
> "There's a train at six."

The second version carries the same answer and does not state it. The reader has
to close the gap, and closing the gap is what makes them lean in. Obliqueness also
characterizes: a person who answers that way is someone who cannot say the plain
thing, and now we know something about them that no description would have
delivered.

Most good dialogue runs on a simple principle — the characters are conducting two
conversations at once. There is the surface conversation, about trains or dinner
or the state of the garden, and underneath it the actual negotiation, which is
about power, or forgiveness, or whether the marriage survives. Only the surface
appears on the page. The reader tracks the other one because they are alert to
exactly this in life.

Hemingway described his method as leaving out the parts he knew, on the theory
that the omission would strengthen what remained as long as the writer genuinely
knew what was missing. That last condition is the load-bearing one. Subtext
produced by a writer who has not decided what the scene is really about is not
subtext. It is vagueness, and readers can tell the difference within a page.

## Attribution: The Case for "Said"

Beginning writers replace *said* with a thesaurus — characters expostulate,
opine, interject, riposte. Elmore Leonard put the objection bluntly in his
much-quoted rules of writing: use *said*, and avoid the adverb that props it up.

The reason is functional rather than aesthetic. *Said* has become almost
invisible on the page; a reader's eye takes it as a punctuation mark indicating
who is speaking and moves on. *Expostulated* stops the eye. Worse, a colorful
attribution usually signals that the line itself is not doing its job. If a
character has to *snarl* a line for the reader to know it is hostile, the line is
not hostile enough.

The same applies to adverbs. "I don't mind," she said bitterly, is an admission
that "I don't mind" does not read as bitter. Rewrite the line so the bitterness
is in the words, and the adverb becomes unnecessary — which it always was.

Better than *said*, much of the time, is a physical beat. Instead of tagging a
line, put an action in its own sentence next to it:

> "I don't mind." She put the plate down harder than she needed to.

The beat identifies the speaker, supplies the tone, and advances the scene
physically, all in one stroke. Used constantly it becomes a tic — every line
accompanied by a shrug or a sip of coffee — so it wants rationing. But in a long
two-person exchange, beats are what keep the reader from losing track of who is
talking without a *said* on every line.

Attribution can often be dropped entirely. Once two voices are distinct enough, a
page of unattributed alternation reads perfectly clearly and moves at speed. This
only works if the voices really are distinct, which brings us to the harder part.

## Making Two People Sound Different

Cover the attributions on a page of your dialogue and see whether you can still
tell who is speaking. Most drafts fail this test, because all the characters are
speaking the writer's idiolect with minor adjustments.

Voice differences that actually register on the page are mostly structural:

- Sentence length and its variation. One character speaks in long subordinated
  sentences; another never exceeds eight words.
- Whether a person answers the question asked, answers a different one, or asks
  another in return.
- Register — whether someone says *deceased*, *dead*, or *gone*, and whether they
  reach for abstraction or for the concrete when under stress.
- What they will not say. Silence around a topic is as identifying as a
  catchphrase and considerably less crude.

Profession and background supply diction, but the deeper differences come from
what the character is trying to do in the conversation. A person who wants
approval, a person who wants to end the exchange, and a person who wants to
establish that they are the most reasonable one present will produce three
completely different rhythms from the same material.

## The Shape It Makes on the Page

Dialogue is also a visual element. A page of short exchanges has white space
running down it, and white space reads as speed; a reader's sense that a scene is
accelerating is partly a response to line length. A page of dense paragraph with
one quoted sentence embedded reads slowly. Writers use this deliberately —
tightening the lines as an argument escalates, letting a paragraph of narration
sit between two lines of dialogue to create a pause the reader actually feels as
a pause.

Which is why the timing of a response can be conveyed by placement alone. If a
character is asked a difficult question and the next line of dialogue comes
immediately, the answer was quick. If two sentences of description about the
weather intervene, the answer was slow, and the slowness means something. No
adverb required.

## Speeches, and When to Allow One

There is a point at which all of this advice about obliqueness and compression has
to be suspended. Sometimes a character needs to talk at length — a confession, an
accusation, a teacher explaining something they care about too much.

Long speeches fail when they exist to transfer information the reader needs and
the writer could not place elsewhere. They succeed when the act of speaking at
length is itself the event: when a person who has not said the thing for twenty
years finally says it, and the length is the pressure behind it releasing. The
test is whether the speech costs the speaker anything. If it does, a reader will
follow it for pages. If it does not, they will start skimming after four lines,
and no amount of craft in the sentences will bring them back.
