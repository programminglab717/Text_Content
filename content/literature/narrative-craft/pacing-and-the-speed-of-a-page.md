# Pacing and the Speed of a Page

Two chapters can run to the same length — four thousand words each, say — and
one will take a reader twenty minutes while the other takes an hour. Readers
call that difference pacing and leave it there, because pacing is a word that
names an effect without identifying a cause. The causes, though, are mechanical,
and nearly all of them are sitting in plain sight on the page.

## Two Different Speeds

The first thing worth separating is narrative velocity from reading velocity.
They are not the same thing, and confusing them produces manuscripts that feel
simultaneously rushed and sluggish.

Narrative velocity is how much story-time passes per page. A single sentence can
cross a decade. Reading velocity is how fast the eye and the mind actually move
through the text. A page of clipped dialogue covering ninety seconds of a
kitchen argument goes down in one swallow; a paragraph summarizing a character's
entire twenties may need rereading, because summary is dense, has no moment-to-
moment shape, and offers nothing to picture except abstractions.

The two speeds frequently run in opposite directions. The fastest-reading prose
in a novel is often the prose in which almost nothing happens: two people
talking, a man walking down a corridor, a hand pausing on a doorknob. The
slowest-reading passages are regularly the ones covering the most ground. This
is why "cut to the chase" is bad advice if taken literally. A chase rendered as
summary — *he pursued her six blocks and lost her near the river* — is slower on
the page than an unhurried conversation written out in full.

Once you notice this, a lot of received wisdom reorganizes itself. Writers are
told to compress the boring parts, and they should, but compression is not the
same as acceleration. Compressing a scene into summary makes the book shorter.
It does not necessarily make it feel faster, and if you compress the wrong
things you get a book that covers a great deal and reads like a ledger.

## Scene, Summary, and the Joint Between Them

Almost all narrative alternates between dramatized scene and compressed summary,
and the craft lies less in either mode than in the joints. A writer with good
control can move from years to seconds inside a single paragraph and the reader
never registers the gear change.

The classic transition runs from wide to narrow: a sentence or two establishing
that something went on for a while, then a hard cut to one particular instance
of it. *For three months they ate dinner in near silence. Then, on a Thursday in
March, she put down her fork and asked him where the money had gone.* The
summary earns the scene. The scene redeems the summary. A novel made entirely of
scenes exhausts a reader by refusing to say which moments matter; a novel made
entirely of summary never lets the reader be present for anything.

Where this goes wrong most often is in the dramatizing of the unremarkable.
Beginning manuscripts tend to render in full detail things that ought to take
four words — the drive to the meeting, the making of coffee, the arrival of a
character who has to cross a lobby, wait for an elevator, and greet a
receptionist before the actual scene can begin. These stretches are not badly
written. They are correctly written and wrongly chosen, and they are the single
most common reason a competent draft drags.

The cure is not to speed the prose up. It is to enter later and leave earlier.
Most scenes can begin a page after where they were first written and end a
paragraph before, and the reader will supply the missing approach and departure
without noticing that anything was removed.

## The Machinery in the Sentences

Below the level of scene structure, pace is controlled by syntax, and the
controls are surprisingly blunt.

Short sentences read fast because each full stop is a small completion, and
completions are satisfying. Long sentences with multiple subordinate clauses
slow the eye because the mind has to hold the grammatical shape open until it
resolves. A run of short declaratives produces urgency; a run of long ones
produces contemplation, or tedium, depending on whether the reader wants to be
contemplating. The effect is amplified enormously by variation. Ten short
sentences in a row stop being fast and start being a drumbeat. One short
sentence after four long ones lands like a slap.

Paragraph length does similar work at a larger scale, partly for a reason that
has nothing to do with language: white space signals ease. A page broken into
six paragraphs looks easier than a page of unbroken text, and readers behave
accordingly before they have read a word of it. Dialogue is the extreme case,
since each speaker gets a fresh line, and a page of exchanged dialogue may be a
third white. That visual lightness is a real part of why dialogue-heavy chapters
feel quick.

Concrete nouns and active verbs move faster than abstraction, not because of
some rule about vigor but because they require less translation. *The dog was
struck by the car* asks the reader to do a small amount of work that *the car hit
the dog* does not. Multiply that small tax across ten thousand sentences and you
have a book that feels heavy without any individual sentence being at fault.

Filtering words do the same damage. *She saw the light go out in the upstairs
window* puts a narrator between the reader and the window. *The light went out in
the upstairs window* does not. Removing the filter is not always right — sometimes
you want the reader aware of the act of perceiving — but a draft full of *she
noticed*, *he felt*, *she realized* is a draft holding its own material at arm's
length, and it reads slowly for that reason.

## Questions and the Rate at Which They Are Answered

None of the above explains why a technically graceful book can still be
unreadable. The deeper engine of pace is not sentence rhythm at all. It is the
management of unanswered questions.

A reader continues because something has been raised and not yet settled. The
questions can be enormous (who killed him, will they survive the winter) or
tiny, and the tiny ones do more work than writers usually credit. Why is she
lying about where she was? What is in the envelope? Why did he flinch at the
name? A page holds attention when at least one such question is live, and it
loses attention the moment all of them are closed.

Good pacing is therefore a matter of overlap. New questions open before old ones
close, so that there is never a flat stretch where everything the reader wanted
to know is known and nothing new has been raised. Serial novelists learned this
mechanically — Dickens had to leave a reason to buy next month's number — but the
principle scales down to the paragraph. The flat spots in a draft are almost
always places where a question was answered too early, or answered and not
replaced.

The opposite failure is worth naming too, because it is common in thrillers and
in literary fiction that has absorbed thriller habits. Withholding is not the
same as generating interest. A narrator who coyly refuses to say what she is
thinking, or a chapter that ends mid-sentence for no structural reason, produces
irritation rather than momentum. Curiosity is created by information, not by its
absence: the reader has to be given enough to form a question worth carrying.

## Breaks, and Where to Put Them

The final lever is architectural. Chapter and section breaks are the punctuation
of a book, and they do two contradictory things at once.

A break is a rest. It tells the reader this is a safe place to stop, which is why
short chapters make long books feel manageable and why a four-hundred-page novel
in eight chapters intimidates in a way the same novel in forty does not. But a
break is also an accelerator, because it permits an unmarked jump — of hours, of
viewpoint, of place — without a sentence of transition. Every break you insert
lets you cut something out.

Where you place the break determines which effect dominates. Ending a chapter at
the natural resting point, after the tension has discharged, gives the reader
permission to put the book down. Ending it a beat earlier, on the question
rather than the answer, makes stopping uncomfortable. Neither is correct. A
novel in which every chapter ends on a cliff becomes numbing, and one that
always lets the air out becomes inert. What matters is that the choice is a
choice, made scene by scene, rather than an accident of where the material
happened to run out.

And there is a case for slowness that no discussion of pace should skip. Speed
is a means, not a virtue. The passages readers remember from the books that
matter to them are frequently the slow ones — a long description of light on
water, an argument that goes nowhere, a funeral. Those passages work because the
book earned the reader's patience elsewhere and then spent it deliberately. A
novel that never slows down has nothing to slow down for. The skill is not in
making prose fast. It is in knowing, at any given paragraph, exactly how fast
you want it to be, and being able to make it that.
