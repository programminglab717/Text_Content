# A Field Guide to Characterization

You can usually identify someone you know from a hundred metres away, in poor
light, by the way they walk. You cannot describe that walk. Ask anyone to
characterize a close friend and you will get adjectives — generous, anxious,
funny — that would fit several thousand other people equally well. The
information we actually use to recognize people is not stored as adjectives, and
this is the whole problem of writing characters: the qualities we can name are
the ones that convey least.

What follows is a guide to the techniques that do the work instead. None of them
is a rule. They are specimens, collected from books that got it right, and the
useful thing about specimens is that you can hold them up against a draft and see
whether what you have is the real animal or a taxidermied version.

## Wanting Something, and Being Stopped

A character who wants nothing cannot be written about for long. This is less a
moral claim than a mechanical one: desire is what turns a person into a source of
action, and action is what a reader can observe.

The want does not have to be large. It has to be specific and it has to be
obstructed. A man who wants to be happy is inert. A man who wants his brother to
admit, out loud, at their mother's funeral, that he was the one who took the
money, is already in motion. Note that the specific version carries the general
one inside it. Concrete wants imply abstract ones; abstract wants imply nothing.

There is a second want underneath the first, and the gap between them is where
most of the interesting writing happens. The stated want is what the character
would say if asked. The buried want is what the behavior reveals. Sometimes these
align. When they do not, the character acquires depth without a word of
explanation, because readers are extremely good at spotting the discrepancy
between what someone claims to be after and what they keep doing.

## The Telling Particular

The single most reliable technique is the small, physical, slightly odd detail
that implies a history.

A man who irons his shoelaces. A woman who keeps every receipt in a shoebox
organized by month but has not filed a tax return in six years. A boy who, asked
a question, always looks at the person's mouth rather than their eyes. None of
these is a summary of character. Each one is evidence, and evidence invites the
reader to do the inferring, which is why it sticks.

Three properties separate a telling particular from decoration:

- It implies something the text does not say. Ironed shoelaces suggest a
  relationship to control that no sentence about perfectionism could establish
  as economically.
- It is specific to this person, not to a category. "She wore a cardigan" places
  someone in a demographic. "She wore her dead sister's cardigan, which was a
  size too small" places someone in a life.
- It recurs, or it changes. A detail introduced once is a flourish. A detail
  that appears three times and is absent the fourth time is a plot point.

Objects work particularly well because possession is a record of choices. What a
person has kept, thrown away, borrowed and not returned, repaired rather than
replaced — a room's contents will characterize its occupant faster than any
amount of interior monologue, and with less risk of the writer's hand showing.

## Flat and Round, and the Uses of Both

E. M. Forster drew a distinction between flat characters, who can be summarized
in a sentence and who behave consistently every time they appear, and round ones,
who are capable of surprising the reader convincingly. The distinction has been
misread for a century as a ranking. It is not one.

Flat characters are a technology for managing a reader's attention. A novel with
forty rounded characters would be unreadable, because the reader would not know
where to concentrate. Dickens built a career on flat characters rendered with such
specificity — a catchphrase, a gesture, a smell — that they are more memorable
than most rounded ones. The flatness is what makes them instantly recognizable
each time they return, which is exactly what a crowded book requires.

The test is not whether a character is round but whether their degree of
roundness matches their function. A landlady who appears twice needs a single
vivid trait. A protagonist who appears throughout needs contradiction. Trouble
comes from mismatch in either direction: a minor character given a full interior
life pulls focus, and a central character with one trait becomes a device.

Convincing surprise, which is Forster's criterion for roundness, has a
precondition. The surprise must be retroactively inevitable. When a character
does something unexpected, the reader should feel a small readjustment — *of
course* — rather than an objection. That feeling comes from material planted
earlier and not explained at the time. Round characters are built backward.

## Contradiction

Real people hold incompatible things without noticing. The devoted father who is
casually cruel to waiters. The pacifist with a violent temper in traffic. The
generous host who cannot bring herself to lend a book.

Writers are often warned against inconsistency, and the warning is wrong as
stated. What readers reject is not contradiction but unmotivated variation — a
character who is timid in one scene and bold in the next because each scene
needed a different response. Genuine contradiction is stable. It shows up the
same way every time, in the same sort of circumstance, and the reader gradually
understands the rule governing it.

The most productive contradictions sit close to the character's strength. The
surgeon's steadiness, which saves lives on the table, is the same steadiness that
makes him unbearable at home when his wife needs him to be upset about something.
Characterization of this kind generates plot for free, because the quality that
solves one problem creates another.

## The Gap Between Saying and Doing

Readers weight evidence in a fixed order, and it is worth knowing what that order
is. Action outranks speech. Speech about others outranks speech about oneself.
What a character notices outranks what they announce. And anything the character
does under pressure, when there is no time to compose a version of themselves,
outranks all of it.

This ordering explains why direct assertion fails so reliably. A narrator who
says a character is brave has spent a sentence and gained nothing. A character
who volunteers for something unpleasant, unprompted, while visibly frightened,
has been characterized permanently.

It also explains why perception is such an efficient tool. Two people walk into
the same hospital waiting room. One registers the cost of the chairs. One
registers which of the other people there are alone. Neither has said anything,
and both have been substantially described. Filtering a scene through what a
character selects out of the available detail characterizes continuously, in the
background, without stopping the story — which is the closest thing to a free
lunch that fiction offers.

## Backstory, Sparingly

The received advice is that a writer should know everything about a character:
childhood, schooling, first heartbreak, what they eat for breakfast. This is
sound as preparation and disastrous as practice, because a writer who knows all
of it tends to put a good deal of it on the page.

Backstory is load-bearing only when it explains a behavior the reader has already
watched and found puzzling. Delivered before the behavior, it is a briefing;
delivered after, it is a revelation. The sequence matters more than the content.
Most first drafts front-load — three paragraphs of history before the character
has done anything worth explaining — and most revisions consist of cutting that
material and redistributing the surviving ten percent into the places where the
reader has begun asking questions.

The same economy applies to physical description. A reader does not need a
character's full inventory of height, hair, eye color, and build. One or two
features, chosen for what they imply rather than for completeness, and the reader
builds the rest — usually more vividly than the writer could have specified it,
and with more investment, because they made it themselves.

## Change, and Its Alternatives

Characters are supposed to change, according to nearly every manual, and often
they do. But the more precise version is that a character must be *tested* —
placed in a situation where the strategy that has worked their whole life stops
working — and change is only one of the available outcomes.

A character can refuse to change and be destroyed by the refusal. A character can
change in a way that costs them more than it gains. A character can change in one
domain while remaining exactly as they were in another, which is the commonest
outcome in life and the rarest in fiction. What is not available is the character
who passes through the test unchanged and unmarked, because that tells the reader
the test was never real, and once a reader suspects the pressure is fake, nothing
the character does afterward carries any weight at all.
