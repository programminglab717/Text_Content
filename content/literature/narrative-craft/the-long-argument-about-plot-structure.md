# The Long Argument About Plot Structure

The king died and then the queen died. E. M. Forster, lecturing at Cambridge in
the 1920s, offered that as an example of a story — one thing, then another
thing. Then he changed a single phrase. The king died, and then the queen died
of grief. That, he said, is a plot.

The difference is causation, and everything written about structure in the
twenty-five centuries before and the century since has been an attempt to work
out what follows from it. If events in a narrative are connected by *because*
rather than *and then*, a reader stops merely receiving information and starts
predicting. Prediction creates suspense, suspense creates attention, and
attention is the only currency a storyteller has. Plot is the machinery that
converts one into the other.

## Aristotle's Shape

The oldest surviving analysis of that machinery comes from a set of lecture notes
on Greek tragedy. Aristotle was not prescribing; he was describing what the plays
that worked had in common, and he noticed that the good ones were built rather
than accumulated.

His central claim is that a plot must have a beginning, a middle, and an end, and
he meant something more demanding than the obvious. A beginning is an event that
does not require a prior event to make sense of it. An end is one that requires
nothing after it. A middle follows from what came before and produces what comes
after. By that standard a great many narratives have no beginning at all: they
start in the middle of an ongoing situation and simply stop when the author runs
out of material.

He also identified the two turns that give a tragedy its force. *Peripeteia* is
reversal — the moment when an action produces the opposite of its intended
effect. *Anagnorisis* is recognition — the moment when a character learns
something that reorganizes everything already shown. Oedipus interrogating
witnesses in order to find the man responsible for the plague, and discovering
that he is that man, achieves both in the same stroke, which is why Aristotle kept
returning to that play. The lesson embedded in the example is that the strongest
reversals come from the protagonist's own efforts. A disaster that arrives from
outside is misfortune. A disaster a character builds for himself, competently, out
of his best qualities, is tragedy.

Aristotle's other demand is unity of action, and it is the one most often ignored.
A plot is not unified because it happens to one person. It is unified because
removing any part of it would break the rest. That test is unforgiving and
extremely useful. Applied to a stalled draft it tends to identify, within an
hour, exactly which subplot is dead weight.

## Freytag's Pyramid and What It Left Out

The diagram most people carry around in their heads comes from a German
playwright and critic named Gustav Freytag, who published a study of dramatic
technique in the 1860s. Freytag proposed that a five-act play rises through
complication to a climax at the center and then falls, through the consequences
of that climax, to a catastrophe. Drawn on a blackboard it makes a triangle.

Two things about the pyramid are worth knowing. The first is that Freytag was
analyzing a specific tradition — classical and Shakespearean five-act drama — and
the symmetry he found was partly a property of that form rather than of narrative
in general. The second is that his climax sits in the middle. The long descent
afterward, in which the consequences work themselves out, is structurally as
important as the ascent. Modern popular versions of the pyramid have shoved the
climax to roughly ninety percent of the way through and shrunk the falling action
to a page or two, which is a real change in the shape of stories and not a
neutral one. It makes for propulsive endings and thin ones. The aftermath is
where a reader finds out what the events meant.

## The Structuralists Go Looking for a Skeleton

A different line of inquiry began with folklore. Vladimir Propp examined a large
body of Russian fairy tales in the 1920s and argued that beneath their varying
surfaces lay a fixed sequence of functions — an interdiction is issued, the
interdiction is violated, a villain causes harm, a hero departs, a donor tests
him, a magical agent is acquired, and so on. Characters were interchangeable.
The functions were not, and they always occurred in the same order, though any
individual tale might skip some.

Joseph Campbell, working from comparative mythology rather than a single national
corpus, arrived at a broader and looser claim: that hero myths across cultures
trace a recurring arc of departure, ordeal, and return. Campbell's pattern has
been enormously influential, particularly on screenwriters, and it has also been
criticized for the obvious reason that a template flexible enough to fit
everything explains nothing. The criticism is fair as scholarship and beside the
point as craft. Nobody writing a novel needs the monomyth to be universally true.
They need to know that a protagonist who leaves a known world, is changed by an
ordeal, and returns unable to fit the place she left is a shape audiences
recognize in their bones.

## The Templates Get Commercial

By the late twentieth century, structural advice had migrated from criticism to
the screenwriting manual, and the manuals were specific in a way Aristotle never
was. Syd Field's three-act model divided a feature screenplay into a setup, a
long confrontation, and a resolution, with plot points at roughly the
quarter-marks turning the action in a new direction. Later systems added more
furniture: a midpoint that raises the stakes or flips the protagonist from
reacting to acting, a low moment near the end of the second act, a break into the
third.

These templates work, in the narrow sense that films built on them tend to hold
an audience for two hours. They work because they encode some real observations
about attention. A reader or viewer needs the central question posed early enough
to start predicting. The middle, which is the part most likely to sag, needs its
own escalations rather than a single continuous rise. Something near the midpoint
has to prevent the story from becoming a repeating pattern of attempt and
setback.

They also produce a distinctive kind of damage when followed literally. Beats
placed by page count rather than by causation feel arbitrary, and readers register
the arbitrariness even when they cannot name it. A dark night of the soul that
arrives because the outline says it should, rather than because the character's
strategy has genuinely failed, reads as weather rather than consequence. The
template describes the shape a well-built plot tends to take. It cannot supply
the causation that produced the shape, and causation is the only part that is
actually load-bearing.

## Structure as a Consequence, Not a Container

The more durable way to think about all of this is to stop treating structure as
a mold to pour a story into.

A plot, built from the inside, is the record of a character wanting something
badly enough to act, acting, and being answered by the world. The answer has to
be specific, and it has to change the situation, and it has to make the next
action different from the one before. Do that honestly for a few cycles and a
recognizable shape appears without being imposed. The stakes rise because failed
attempts eliminate the easy options. A midpoint reversal happens because somewhere
in the middle the character learns something that makes her old strategy
untenable. The climax lands because it is the last remaining move.

Two practical tests come out of this. The first is the *therefore* test, which
several writers of episodic television have articulated in one form or another:
walk through the beats of your story and see whether they are connected by
*therefore* and *but*, or by *and then*. A sequence that only supports *and then*
is a chronicle, not a plot.

The second is subtraction. Take any scene and imagine it gone. If the rest of the
story still works, the scene is decoration, however good it is. This is
Aristotle's unity test in working clothes, and it is brutal on the passages
writers most enjoy having written — the elegant digression, the vivid minor
character, the setpiece invented before the story knew what it needed.

What no template can decide is where to begin and where to stop, and those two
choices carry as much meaning as anything between them. Beginning a marriage plot
at the wedding rather than the courtship makes an entirely different book from
the same material. Ending a war novel at the armistice rather than four years
later makes a different argument about what war is. Aristotle's insistence that
an ending require nothing after it was never a claim about tidiness. It was a
claim that the writer has to know what the story is about, because the story
stops when that subject is exhausted — and not, as he might have said had he
lived to see the third-act template, when the page count runs out.
