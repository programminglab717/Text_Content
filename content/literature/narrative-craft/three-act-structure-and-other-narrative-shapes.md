# Three-Act Structure and Other Narrative Shapes

Kurt Vonnegut liked to stand at a blackboard and draw stories as graphs. The
vertical axis ran from ill fortune at the bottom to good fortune at the top, the
horizontal from beginning to end, and then he would chalk a line. Man falls in
hole, man climbs out of hole: a dip and a recovery. Cinderella: a low flat line,
a staircase of small gifts, a plunge at midnight, a vertical leap off the top of
the chart. He had submitted something like this as a master's thesis in
anthropology and the department had turned it down, a rejection he mentioned for
the rest of his life with visible pleasure.

The graphs are funny and they are also a fair description of what every structural
model in circulation actually is: a line drawn afterward, over stories that were
already there. Whether such a line is worth anything depends entirely on what you
try to do with it.

## What Aristotle Actually Said

The *Poetics* is the ancestor of all of this, and it is much less prescriptive
than its reputation. Aristotle observed that a tragedy represents an action that
is whole and complete, and that a whole has a beginning, a middle, and an end —
which sounds like a truism until you read his definitions. A beginning is
something that does not necessarily follow from anything else but from which
other things follow. An end is what follows necessarily from what came before and
leads to nothing further. A middle follows one thing and is followed by another.

The definition is about causal chains, not about proportions or page counts. What
Aristotle is insisting on is that the parts of a plot connect by necessity or
probability rather than by sequence — that events happen *because of* one another,
not merely after. He was contemptuous of episodic plots for exactly this reason.
He also named the two devices he thought produced the strongest effect: the
reversal, in which a course of action turns to its opposite, and the recognition,
in which ignorance turns to knowledge. When the two coincide, as they do when
Oedipus finds out what he has done, you get the strongest form.

E. M. Forster made the same distinction in 1927 with an example everybody
remembers: the king died and then the queen died is a story; the king died and
then the queen died of grief is a plot. Two words, and the sequence becomes a
structure.

## Freytag's Pyramid Belongs to Somebody Else's Theater

The triangle taught in schools comes from Gustav Freytag, whose *Technik des
Dramas* appeared in 1863. It has five parts, not three: exposition, rising
action, climax, falling action, and catastrophe.

Two things about it are almost always lost in transmission. The first is that
Freytag was analyzing a specific body of work — Greek tragedy and Shakespeare —
within a five-act convention that Horace had prescribed and the Renaissance had
inherited. He was describing the machine he had in front of him. The second and
more consequential point is that his climax sits in the *middle*. The pyramid is
symmetrical. Half the play comes after the peak, and that descending half is
where tragedy does its characteristic work: the protagonist's fortunes turn at
the center and then the consequences grind forward to the catastrophe.

Applied to a modern novel or film, where the climax typically arrives with
ninety percent of the running time gone and the falling action lasts about four
minutes, the pyramid simply does not fit. It is drawn on the board anyway,
usually skewed to put the peak near the right-hand edge, at which point it has
stopped being Freytag's idea and become a generic bump.

## How Three Acts Became a Rule

The three-act model in its current form is recent and specifically
cinematic. Syd Field's *Screenplay*, published in 1979, laid out a paradigm:
setup, confrontation, resolution, with the second act roughly twice the length of
either of the others, and with turning points — he called them plot points — at the
ends of the first and second acts, landing near particular page numbers in a
standard-length script.

Field was doing what Freytag did, generalizing from the films he had read and
watched. The difference is what happened next. *Screenplay* and its many
successors were read not only by writers but by the people who commission and
buy scripts, and a description that development executives use to evaluate
submissions stops being a description. Within a decade the paradigm had become a
requirement, then a vocabulary, then a set of expectations in the audience
itself. Viewers who have never heard the term can feel when a film's second act
is running long. The map redrew the territory.

Whether that is a loss is arguable. The three-act shape is genuinely robust for
a two-hour narrative watched in one sitting by a large audience, which is a real
engineering problem and not a trivial one. The loss is in the transfer of the
model to forms it was never derived from — to novels, which are read in
interrupted fragments over days; to short stories, which often work by
withholding the third act entirely; to television, whose act structure was for
decades dictated by the placement of commercial breaks rather than by anything
in the story.

## Other Shapes

Nothing about narrative requires a pyramid. Several traditions have arrived at
quite different diagrams.

Kishōtenketsu, the four-part scheme inherited from classical Chinese poetry and
widely used across East Asian narrative, runs introduction, development, twist,
and reconciliation. The distinctive move is the third part: something is
introduced that seems unrelated to what came before, and the fourth part
establishes the relation between them. No conflict is required at any stage. A
four-panel comic in which a grandmother waters a plant, the plant grows, a
neighbor's cat appears on a wall, and the last panel shows the cat asleep in the
shade of the plant is a complete narrative in this scheme and a pile of
unmotivated incident in Aristotle's. Western writers encountering it for the
first time often find it a relief, because it detaches the idea of structure from
the idea of antagonism.

The monomyth is the other model with a serious institutional presence. Joseph
Campbell's *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* proposed in 1949 that myths worldwide
follow a single deep pattern of departure, initiation, and return, subdivided
into stages with names like the call to adventure and the crossing of the
threshold. Christopher Vogler compressed it into a practical memo for film
executives, and it has been the backbone of studio development ever since.
Comparative mythologists have been hard on Campbell, largely on the grounds that
the pattern fits everything because it is specified loosely enough to fit
anything, and that assembling it required ignoring what makes individual
traditions distinct. Both criticisms are fair and neither has made it less
popular, because as a generative prompt it works: it gives a stuck writer a list
of things that could happen next.

Then there are the shapes that are not arcs at all. The picaresque strings
episodes on a traveling character and takes its coherence from voice rather than
causation. Braided novels run parallel strands that never quite meet until the
last movement. Frame narratives nest. And Ursula Le Guin, in an essay from the
mid-1980s, argued that the whole apparatus of rising tension and climax was a
particular cultural preference dressed up as a law — that the earliest tool was
probably a container rather than a weapon, and that a novel might just as
properly be a bag holding things in relation as an arrow aimed at a target. The
essay is polemical and its central point is difficult to refute: a very large
number of the books people love have weak plots and are not damaged by it.

## What the Diagrams Are Good For

The useful position is that these models are diagnostic rather than generative.
They are poor at telling you what to write and quite good at telling you what has
gone wrong.

A writer who plots by the paradigm — inserting a turning point at the
twenty-five-percent mark because the chart says so — produces the recognizable
hollow object that hits every beat and means nothing, because the beats were the
goal instead of the byproduct. But a writer three hundred pages into a draft that
has gone slack can lay any of these templates over it and learn something real.
Freytag will ask what turned at the midpoint. Field will ask what is driving the
long middle. Campbell will ask what the protagonist refused and what it cost.
Kishōtenketsu will ask whether anything unexpected has entered.

None of these questions requires believing that stories have a true shape. They
are ways of interrogating a draft from outside, which is difficult to do
otherwise, and the answer is frequently that the structure is fine and the
problem is somewhere the diagram cannot see.
