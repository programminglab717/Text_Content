# Unreliable Narration and the Reader as Detective

In the most famous scene of Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day*, the
butler Stevens is serving at an important conference while his father lies dying
in an upstairs room. He does not go up. He keeps the wine moving, manages a
guest's blistered feet, and gets through the evening without a lapse. Looking
back, he tells us he remembers that night with something close to triumph.

Nothing in the sentence is false. Stevens really did feel triumph, and he really
did perform flawlessly. The reader, meanwhile, is reading a different scene
altogether — one about a man who has traded a life for a standard of service, and
who has arranged his memory so that the trade looks like a victory. Two accounts
occupy the same paragraph. That doubling is what unreliable narration is, and
almost everything interesting about the technique follows from working out how
the second account gets onto the page when the narrator is the only one holding
the pen.

## Booth's Word, and What It Was For

The term arrived with Wayne Booth's *The Rhetoric of Fiction* in 1961. Booth was
trying to describe the relationship between a narrator and what he called the
implied author — the sensibility a reader reconstructs from the whole book, as
distinct from both the flesh-and-blood writer and the voice doing the telling. A
narrator is unreliable, in Booth's formulation, when he speaks or acts at odds
with that implied sensibility.

That definition is more useful than the casual one, which treats unreliability as
simply lying. Lying is a small subcategory. What Booth is pointing at is a
structural gap: the book knows more, or differently, than its narrator does, and
the reader is invited into the space between them.

It also rules out a great deal that gets loosely called unreliable. Every
first-person narrator is limited. Every one of them is partial, forgetful, and
working from inside a single skull. That is not unreliability; that is narration.
Unreliability requires that the text actively signal a divergence — that it
supply the reader with grounds to doubt. Without those grounds you have a
narrator who is merely human, which is fine but not a technique.

## The Second Channel

Here is the mechanical problem. If the narrator controls every word, how does
contradicting evidence reach the reader?

It leaks. The commonest leak is dialogue, because other characters say things the
narrator can report but not control. When a secondary character responds to our
narrator with alarm, or pity, or a change of subject, the reader registers a
reaction the narrator has not accounted for. The narrator can dismiss it; the
reader files it. Ishiguro uses this constantly. Miss Kenton keeps saying things
to Stevens that he transcribes with perfect fidelity and complete
incomprehension, and the novel's whole emotional argument lives in that gap.

The second leak is behavioral. A narrator can misdescribe his motives but not his
actions without the book collapsing, so actions become the fixed points a reader
navigates by. He says he is untroubled; he has driven four hundred miles to see
her. The third leak is stylistic: the prose itself gives the game away, through
over-insistence, through evasive abstraction at precisely the wrong moments,
through a rhetorical polish so excessive it becomes suspect. Humbert Humbert's
gorgeous sentences in *Lolita* are not decoration. They are the defense counsel's
performance, and Nabokov keeps letting small unornamented facts through the
seams — the sound of a girl crying at night in a motel room — that the style cannot
metabolize.

The fourth leak is arithmetic. Dates that do not line up, an account given twice
with different details, a document included in the text that says something other
than what the narrator claims it says. Nabokov built an entire novel out of this
in *Pale Fire*, where a lunatic commentator annotates another man's poem and the
poem sits there on the page, visibly not being about what he says it is about.

## Four Kinds of Untrustworthiness

The taxonomies proliferate, but four categories cover most of the territory, and
they differ less in technique than in what the reader is being asked to do.

There is the deceiver, who knows the truth and withholds or falsifies it. Agatha
Christie's Dr. Sheppard in *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* is the pure case, and
the diarists of modern domestic thrillers are his descendants. Then there is the
deluded narrator, who is not lying because he believes himself — Stevens, or the
governess in *The Turn of the Screw*, whose account is constructed so carefully
that after more than a century readers still cannot agree whether there are
ghosts in the house or only in her.

The third is the naive narrator, who reports accurately and interprets wrongly,
usually because he is a child or an outsider. Huck Finn tells us he has decided to
go to hell rather than turn Jim in, and he means it as a confession of
wickedness; the reader understands it as the moral summit of the book. That is
the naive narrator's peculiar power. The reader is not detecting a deception but
completing a judgment the narrator is unable to make, and the gap generates not
suspicion but tenderness.

The fourth is the constrained narrator, who is honest and simply cannot know.
John Dowell in Ford Madox Ford's *The Good Soldier* says repeatedly that he does
not understand the events he is relating, and he is telling the truth. The
novel's unreliability is not moral. It is epistemological, a demonstration that a
man can live beside a catastrophe for nine years and see none of it.

Frame narration produces a fifth case that sits slightly outside the scheme. When
a story is relayed at second or third hand — Lockwood in *Wuthering Heights*
setting down what Nelly Dean tells him, over weeks, about events she witnessed as
a servant with strong opinions about everyone involved — the distortion is
cumulative and no single teller has to be dishonest. Nelly is partisan. Lockwood
is obtuse. Neither is lying, and the reader receives the Earnshaws and Lintons
through two successive filters, each of which subtracts something different. The
technique is older than the terminology and much of the nineteenth century runs
on it; what it buys is deniability, a way to present extreme material while
keeping the question of its accuracy permanently open.

## The Fair-Play Argument

When *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* appeared in 1926 a good many readers felt
cheated, and the complaint has a certain logic: the detective story is a contract
in which the reader is promised access to the same evidence as the detective, and
a murdering narrator seems to void it.

The standard defense is that Sheppard never states a falsehood. He omits. He
records that he did what needed doing in the study and moves briskly on, and the
sentence is literally accurate — the reader supplied the innocent reading. That
defense is more than a technicality, because it identifies the real rule. An
unreliable narrator has to be playable. Everything necessary to reach the true
account must be present in the text, arranged so the reader will probably miss
it, but findable on a second pass. A twist that rests on information withheld
entirely is not unreliable narration; it is an author changing the facts.

This is the standard most failures miss. A narrator revealed on the last page to
have been lying about everything, with no accumulated strangeness pointing that
way, produces annoyance rather than vertigo — the pleasure of rereading is exactly
the pleasure of finding that the evidence was there. It also requires that the
book maintain a stable reality for the narrator to be unreliable *about*. If
nothing in the fiction can be established independently of the telling, doubt has
nothing to attach to, and the effect dissolves into a general haze in which every
statement is equally provisional. A reader can only feel misled if there is
somewhere to be misled from. The opposite failure is
oversignaling: a narrator so flagrantly mad from page one that the reader never
extends trust, and so is never required to give any up. Unreliability without a
prior investment of belief costs the reader nothing, and a technique that costs
nothing returns nothing.

## When the Text Is a Pile of Documents

Unreliability changes character entirely when a novel is assembled rather than
narrated — when it presents itself as a collection of letters, journals,
transcripts, depositions and clippings. Then the doubt is a property of the
archive instead of a person, and it comes from the joins.

Wilkie Collins built *The Woman in White* on an explicit legal conceit: the
story is offered as testimony, each witness setting down only what they can
personally swear to, handing off to the next when their knowledge runs out. The
form promises rigor and delivers something stranger, because testimony given in
good faith by people with incompatible interests does not add up to a clean
account. Bram Stoker's *Dracula* goes further. It is a dossier of journals,
letters, telegrams, newspaper cuttings and a diary kept on phonograph
cylinders, compiled after the fact by the survivors — and Stoker has one of them
observe at the close that the collection contains scarcely an authentic
original, being mostly typescript, and could not prove anything to anyone.

What this buys a writer is a set of leaks the single narrator does not have. A
letter is written *to* somebody, for a purpose, by a person who expects it to be
read, which means every document in the pile is already a performance. A diary
entry can be composed for a reader the diarist denies imagining. Two accounts of
the same afternoon can be placed a hundred pages apart and left to disagree,
with no narrator anywhere in the book to adjudicate. Gillian Flynn's *Gone Girl*
is a modern working of exactly this device: the diary is the most persuasive
thing in the novel and also the most thoroughly manufactured, and it persuades
because readers have a deep prior conviction that a private journal is where
someone tells the truth.

The same doubling is available in third person, incidentally, through free
indirect style — a narration that takes on a character's vocabulary and
judgments without announcing the loan. But the assembled-document novel makes
the epistemology visible on the surface. The reader can see the seams, and is
being asked, quite openly, to do the work of a historian with a bad archive.

What all of this is finally for is worth stating plainly, because unreliable
narration is often discussed as though it were a magic trick with a reveal. The
trick is the least of it. A narrator who cannot be trusted forces the reader into
an active relation with the text — weighing testimony, holding two versions at
once, deciding what to believe. That is not a decorative effect. It reproduces,
inside the artificial safety of a book, the ordinary condition of knowing other
people: the fact that everyone's account of themselves is an argument, that
self-knowledge is partial, and that the story someone tells about their own life
is the last place to look for what happened in it. Stevens is not a puzzle to be
solved. He is a man whose explanation of himself is the injury.
