# African Novelists and the Oral Tradition

Early in *Things Fall Apart*, Chinua Achebe pauses the narrative to explain
something about how the people of Umuofia speak. Among the Igbo, he writes, the
art of conversation is regarded very highly, and proverbs are the palm-oil with
which words are eaten. It is a small aside, easy to read past. It is also a
statement of method. The novel that follows is built out of proverbs, songs,
folktales told to children at night, and the formal rhetoric of men addressing a
gathering — a set of verbal forms that existed for centuries before anyone
thought to write them down, dropped into a European prose genre barely two
hundred years old and made to carry its weight.

That collision is the central fact of the modern African novel, and it has been
handled in radically different ways by writers who agree on almost nothing else.

## The Office of the Speaker

It helps to be precise about what "oral tradition" means, because the phrase
flattens an enormous range of specialized professions. In the Mande-speaking
regions of West Africa, the jeli — often called a griot in French sources —
belongs to an inherited caste with defined social obligations: keeper of
genealogies, praise-singer, negotiator, the person who knows which families are
owed what. The epic of Sunjata, which narrates the founding of the Mali empire,
exists in dozens of performed versions, each shaped by the particular jeli, the
particular patron, and the length of the occasion. When the Guinean writer
Djibril Tamsir Niane published a prose version in the middle of the twentieth
century, drawn from the recitation of a single jeli, he produced something
useful and something misleading at once: a fixed text of a thing whose nature is
to be unfixed.

Elsewhere the specialization takes other shapes. Zulu izibongo are dense praise
poems built out of compressed epithets, performed at speed and at volume, in
which a king's reign is compacted into a series of names for him. Yoruba ijala
belongs to hunters and has its own tonal and rhythmic rules. Somali poetry
sustained an oral culture of extraordinary formal difficulty — alliterative,
memorized verbatim, circulated across hundreds of miles by people who could
repeat a composition exactly after hearing it once, and argued over the way
literate cultures argue over manuscripts. The Ugandan linguist Pio Zirimu coined
the word *orature* to describe all this, precisely to stop people from calling it
"oral literature," a phrase that quietly makes writing the standard and speech
the deviation.

What these forms share is not looseness but constraint. A performed text has to
be held in memory, delivered in real time, and adjusted to an audience that is
present and reacting. That produces formulaic phrasing, heavy patterning,
repetition with variation, digression as a structural principle rather than a
fault, and a narrator who is visibly a person in a room rather than a
disembodied intelligence. Every one of those features is a problem for the
conventional novel, which assumes a silent reader, a fixed text, and a narrator
who can vanish.

## Achebe's Repair Work

Achebe's solution was to bend English rather than abandon it. He argued in an
essay on the African writer and the English language that the language would
have to be altered to carry an experience it had not been shaped for — a new
English, he said, still in communion with its ancestral home but adjusted to
different surroundings. In practice this meant importing the rhythms and
indirections of Igbo speech into English syntax: the ceremonial circling before
a point is made, the argument conducted entirely through competing proverbs, the
refusal to say a hard thing plainly. When Okonkwo's friend Obierika reflects on
the arrival of the missionaries, he does not editorialize; he tells a story about
a man who brought home ant-infested faggots and summoned the lizards. The
judgment is in the image.

Crucially, Achebe kept the free indirect narration of the European novel while
letting the community's collective voice bleed into it. The result is a book in
which it is often unclear whether a statement belongs to the narrator or to
Umuofia's general opinion — which is the point, since the tragedy is precisely
about a man measuring himself against collective opinion.

## Tutuola and the Refusal to Translate

Four years before *Things Fall Apart*, Amos Tutuola published *The Palm-Wine
Drinkard*, and the difference between the two books marks out the far end of the
spectrum. Tutuola wrote in an English that was not standard and made no apology
for it, transcribing the structure of Yoruba folktale directly: a quest that
proceeds by episodic encounter, a hero who solves problems by transformation,
monsters catalogued with the flat literalism of someone describing a neighbour.
British reviewers were enthusiastic in a way that embarrassed many educated West
Africans, who suspected the book was being praised as a curiosity — proof of a
primitive genius rather than a writer.

The suspicion was understandable and mostly wrong. Tutuola was working in a line
that already existed in print: D. O. Fagunwa had been writing book-length prose
narratives in Yoruba, drawing on the same folkloric material, since the
nineteen-thirties. What Tutuola did was carry the form across into English
without smoothing it, and the awkwardness readers noticed is partly the friction
of a genuine transfer. Wole Soyinka, who later translated Fagunwa, understood
the lineage well enough to defend it.

## Ngũgĩ's Break

Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o took the argument to its logical end and decided that bending
English was not enough. In *Decolonising the Mind* he announced that he would
write his fiction and drama in Gikuyu, on the grounds that language carries the
culture that made it and that an African literature composed in European
languages is, at best, an Afro-European one.

The decision was not abstract. He had worked on a community theatre project at
Kamĩrĩĩthũ, where villagers who had never acted developed and performed a play
in their own language about land and labour; the Kenyan authorities closed it
down and later demolished the open-air theatre. Detained without trial, Ngũgĩ
wrote a novel in Gikuyu on prison-issue toilet paper. *Caitaani Mũtharaba-inĩ* —
*Devil on the Cross* — is narrated by a figure modelled on the gicaandi player, a
traditional performer, and it opens by addressing the reader as a listener who
has asked to hear the story. Its satire works by exaggeration, catalogue and
direct address, devices that belong to performance rather than to the
psychological novel. Ngũgĩ later described reading early drafts aloud to workers
and farmers and revising according to what held their attention, which is a
composition method the oral poets would have recognized.

## Beyond the Ancestral Village

The danger of all this is that orality becomes decorative — a proverb sprinkled
in, a folktale inset in italics, local colour for an audience abroad. The
strongest recent work avoids it by making the oral form structural rather than
ornamental.

Ben Okri's *The Famished Road* takes the Yoruba conception of the abiku, the
spirit-child who is repeatedly born and repeatedly dies, and uses it not as a
symbol but as the narrator's actual ontology. The novel's refusal to distinguish
between the road, the bar, the forest and the spirit world is not magical realism
imported from Latin America; it follows from a cosmology in which those places
are continuous. Ousmane Sembène, having concluded that the novel reached too few
people in a society where most could not read French, moved to film and described
cinema as the evening school of his people — a reversion, in effect, to an
audience gathered in one place at one time.

Ngũgĩ's later *Wizard of the Crow*, written in Gikuyu and enormous, is
constructed as a sequence of tales told by characters to other characters, each
narrator interrupting and correcting the last. Nuruddin Farah built his Somali
novels around a culture where poetry is a political instrument and an insult in
verse can travel faster than a messenger.

The point these writers keep arriving at, by different routes, is that the
opposition between oral and written is less stable than it looks. A jeli
composing before a patron and a novelist revising a manuscript are both working
under formal constraint toward an audience they have to hold. What changed was
the distribution of the text and the disappearance of the performer's body. The
best African novelists have spent seventy years trying to put a speaker back in
the room.
