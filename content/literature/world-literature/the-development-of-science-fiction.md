# The Development of Science Fiction

The summer of 1816 was cold and wet across Europe. A volcano in the Indonesian
archipelago had thrown enough material into the stratosphere the previous year to
dim the sun and wreck harvests from New England to Switzerland, and a small
English party renting a villa on Lake Geneva found themselves kept indoors for
days on end. They read German ghost stories aloud and Byron proposed that each of
them write one. Most of the group lost interest. Mary Shelley, then eighteen,
produced a story about a student of natural philosophy who assembles a living man
from dead tissue and then cannot bear to look at what he has made.

*Frankenstein* is conventionally nominated as the first science fiction novel,
an argument made forcefully by Brian Aldiss and disputed ever since. The case
rests on a single structural feature. Victor Frankenstein does not invoke a
demon, recite a formula, or strike a bargain. He studies, experiments, and
succeeds by means the novel presents as continuous with the chemistry and anatomy
being taught at real universities. The horror that follows is a consequence of
knowledge applied without judgment, which is a recognizably modern anxiety and
not a supernatural one.

## Ancestors Without Descendants

There were earlier voyages to impossible places. Lucian of Samosata, writing in
Greek in the second century, sent a ship to the moon and staged a war between the
lunar and solar empires, announcing in his preface that everything following was
a lie — a satire on travellers' tales rather than a speculation. Johannes Kepler
wrote a dream-narrative in which a traveller observes the heavens from the lunar
surface, using the fiction to work out what celestial mechanics would look like
from a different vantage point; it was published after his death and is genuinely
astronomical in intent. Cyrano de Bergerac imagined a series of increasingly
ingenious methods of reaching the moon, at least one of them by accident.

These are ancestors in the sense that they share a family resemblance. They are
not a tradition, because none of them produced a line of successors working on
the same problem. A literature needs a readership and a delivery mechanism before
it can become a genre, and those arrived in the nineteenth century with cheap
printing, mass literacy and a public that had watched railways, telegraphy and
anaesthesia arrive inside a single lifetime.

## Verne and Wells: Two Different Machines

Jules Verne wrote for a publisher, Pierre-Jules Hetzel, who ran an improving
family magazine and wanted adventure stories that taught geography and physics.
The arrangement shaped the work. Verne's novels are built on extrapolation from
real engineering — the submarine, the rocket-launching cannon, the balloon — and
they linger over specifications, provisions, latitudes and the practical problems
of getting there. When Verne submitted a bleak novel about a future Paris of
finance and machinery in which an artist starves, Hetzel rejected it as too
gloomy and commercially hopeless, and it sat unpublished for well over a century.

H. G. Wells worked the other way around. He had studied biology under Thomas
Henry Huxley, and his scientific romances are thought experiments rather than
engineering projects. The time machine is described in a paragraph and then
dropped, because its mechanism is irrelevant; what matters is that it delivers a
Victorian gentleman into a future where the leisured and the labouring classes
have speciated into two kinds of creature, one of which eats the other. *The War
of the Worlds* applies the logic of colonial conquest to England and lets the
invaders be destroyed by bacteria, which is both a plot resolution and a point
about evolution. Wells's imagination was fundamentally pessimistic in a way
Verne's was not, and nearly everything the genre later did with dystopia,
apocalypse and deep time descends from him.

## The Pulps Give It a Name

The genre acquired its identity and its walls at once, in 1926, when Hugo
Gernsback launched *Amazing Stories* as the first magazine devoted entirely to
the form. He initially called it "scientifiction," which nobody could say, and
settled on science fiction. He filled early issues with reprinted Verne, Wells
and Poe, then began buying new work from readers who had written in.

That last detail mattered more than the magazine's editorial quality, which was
poor. Gernsback printed correspondents' full addresses in the letter column.
Readers began writing to each other, then forming local clubs, then producing
their own mimeographed magazines, then holding conventions. Science fiction
became the rare literature with an organized, self-aware, intensely argumentative
audience that partly overlapped with its writers, and that social structure has
shaped it ever since — including the awards, one of which is named after
Gernsback.

The pulps paid by the word and paid badly, which encouraged speed, plot and
clarity, and discouraged almost everything else. The decisive editorial
intervention came when John W. Campbell took over *Astounding* at the end of the
nineteen-thirties and began demanding that stories treat their premises with
consistency: work out the consequences, respect the physics you have chosen, and
write characters who behave like competent professionals rather than pulp heroes.
He bullied, hectored and fed plot ideas to a group of very young writers who
included Isaac Asimov, Robert Heinlein and Theodore Sturgeon, and the resulting
decade is still called the Golden Age by people who were teenagers during it.

Two techniques from this period became permanent equipment. One is the
rule-bound premise, exemplified by Asimov's laws of robotics, which turned the
robot from a menace into a logic puzzle and generated stories by finding cases
where the rules conflict. The other is indirect exposition: the habit of dropping
a strange detail into an ordinary sentence and letting the reader infer the world
behind it. Heinlein's sentence in which a door dilates is the standard example —
no explanation, no pause, and an entire architecture implied by one verb.

## Growing Up, Twice

Golden Age science fiction was competent and narrow. It expanded twice, in
different directions.

The first expansion came with the magazines founded around 1950. *Galaxy* and
*The Magazine of Fantasy and Science Fiction* had no interest in engineering
puzzles and a great deal of interest in social satire, and the decade produced
work aimed squarely at advertising, consumerism, conformity and suburban dread.
Frederik Pohl and Cyril Kornbluth imagined a world run outright by advertising
agencies. Alfred Bester wrote two novels of enormous stylistic aggression, one
about murder in a society of telepaths and one about a brutalized man who
acquires the ability to teleport and uses it for revenge. Theodore Sturgeon,
defending the field against critics who pointed at the worst of it, observed that
ninety percent of everything is rubbish — a remark that has outlived most of the
fiction it was defending.

The second expansion was noisier. When Michael Moorcock took over the British
magazine *New Worlds* in the mid-sixties, he turned it toward literary
experiment, typographic games and subject matter the American magazines would not
print. J. G. Ballard had already argued that the real unexplored territory was
not outer space but inner space — the psychology of a landscape of motorways,
drained swimming pools and abandoned launch complexes. Harlan Ellison assembled
an anthology explicitly advertised as containing stories no editor would buy.
Samuel Delany brought a prose density and a sexual frankness the field had not
seen. The New Wave produced a lot of unreadable work and permanently raised the
ceiling.

Running alongside it, and outlasting it, was the arrival of writers who used the
genre's freedom to rebuild social arrangements from the ground up. Ursula K. Le
Guin populated a planet with people who have no fixed sex, then sent a male envoy
among them and let his assumptions do the damage; she later wrote an ambiguous
anarchist utopia in which the good society is poor, arid and quarrelsome. Joanna
Russ wrote a furious, formally fractured novel with four versions of the same
woman. Alice Sheldon published for years as James Tiptree Jr., accumulating
critical praise that included confident assessments of the masculinity of the
prose, and was eventually identified. Octavia Butler wrote about power,
dependency and survival with a directness the field had mostly avoided, sending a
modern Black woman back into antebellum Maryland and refusing to make the trip
redemptive.

## Consoles, and the Rest of the World

Cyberpunk arrived in the mid-eighties as a self-conscious movement with a
manifesto-writing publicist in Bruce Sterling and an unexpected masterpiece in
William Gibson's *Neuromancer*. Its contribution was to stop imagining the future
as a clean municipal project and start imagining it as something corroded,
corporate and leaky — computation as a place you could be, cities as
infrastructure with people living in its gaps, the street finding its own uses
for things. Within a decade its vocabulary had been absorbed by journalism,
advertising and the technology industry, which is the fate of successful science
fiction and possibly its most reliable measure.

The Anglo-American account is only part of it. Stanisław Lem wrote fiction about
the limits of understanding — an ocean that may be a mind and cannot be
interpreted by any of the disciplines brought to bear on it — and held American
science fiction in low enough regard to get his honorary membership in its
professional association withdrawn. Arkady and Boris Strugatsky worked under
Soviet censorship and developed a mode in which the strangeness is never
explained, most famously in a novel about scavengers looting the debris of an
alien visit that never acknowledged humanity at all. Chinese science fiction,
long marginal, reached a global readership when Liu Cixin's trilogy appeared in
English and its first volume won the field's main novel award — the first work in
translation to do so.

What all of this shares is a device the critic Darko Suvin named the novum: one
concrete change from the world as it is, introduced rigorously enough that the
consequences can be reasoned about. Everything else in the genre — the rockets,
the robots, the drowned cities, the telepaths — is an instance of that single
move.
