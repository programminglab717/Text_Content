# The Boom and What Came Before It

In 1881 a civil servant in Rio de Janeiro published a novel narrated by a man who
is already dead, opens by dedicating the book to the worm that got to his corpse
first, and spends the next two hundred pages reviewing a life of wasted
opportunity with the amused detachment of someone who no longer has anything at
stake. Joaquim Maria Machado de Assis was the grandson of freed slaves, epileptic,
self-taught, and by then the most respected man of letters in Brazil. His
Posthumous Memoirs of Brás Cubas has short chapters, direct addresses to the
reader, a chapter consisting largely of punctuation, and a moral intelligence
that never announces itself. It also went almost entirely unread outside Brazil
for the better part of a century.

That delay is the recurring fact of Latin American writing. The work was being
done. The channels through which literature acquires an international reputation
ran the other way — toward Paris, toward Madrid, toward London — and for four
centuries almost everything produced between the Rio Grande and Tierra del Fuego
reached the rest of the world late, in fragments, or not at all. When that
finally reversed, in the 1960s, it reversed so abruptly that it got a name.

## Writing Under the Colony

The Spanish Crown formally prohibited shipping works of fiction to its American
territories, on the theory that invented stories would confuse recent converts.
The rule was evaded constantly — copies of Don Quixote reached Lima within
months of publication — but it indicates the atmosphere. Printing presses
operated in Mexico City and Lima within a few decades of the conquest, and what
they produced was catechisms, grammars of indigenous languages, legal documents,
and devotional verse.

The genuinely remarkable colonial writing came from people caught between the two
worlds. The Popol Vuh, the K'iche' Maya account of creation and of the hero twins
who defeat the lords of the underworld, survives because someone transcribed it
in alphabetic script during the colonial period and a Dominican friar later
copied and translated it. Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, an Andean nobleman, spent
years composing an immense illustrated letter to the king of Spain cataloguing
colonial abuses; it never reached him, and the manuscript sat unread in a
Copenhagen library until the twentieth century. The Inca Garcilaso de la Vega,
son of a Spanish captain and an Inca noblewoman, wrote a history of his mother's
people from Spain, in Spanish, for readers who had never seen the Andes.

And in a Mexico City convent, Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz assembled a library,
wrote love poetry, devotional drama, and a long philosophical poem about the mind
attempting and failing to comprehend the universe in a single night. When
ecclesiastical authorities pressed her, she produced a defense of a woman's right
to study that reads as fresh now as any document of its century. She was
eventually made to give up her books.

## Independence, and the Problem of What to Write About

Political independence in the early nineteenth century created a set of new
republics and an immediate literary difficulty: what is a national literature of
a country three years old? The answers tended toward landscape and social type.
Argentine writers produced the gaucho — first as a figure of barbarism, in
Sarmiento's part-biography, part-polemic about a provincial warlord, and then as
a figure of tragic dignity in José Hernández's long poem about a conscripted
horseman who deserts to live among the Indians. Cuba produced José Martí, who
wrote essays, verse, and journalism while organizing an independence war he died
in.

Then came modernismo, the first movement to travel outward rather than inward.
Rubén Darío, a Nicaraguan who worked as a diplomat and journalist across two
continents, brought swans, French symbolist music, and an enormously expanded
metrical range into Spanish verse, and the effect on the language was permanent —
including in Spain, where younger poets read him as a liberation. It was the
first time the traffic ran from America to Europe rather than the reverse.

## The Land, Then the Vanguard

The 1920s produced a run of novels in which the real antagonist is geography. A
Colombian novel sent its narrator into the rubber camps of the Amazon and let the
forest swallow him. A Venezuelan novel set a landowning woman against a
reforming man on the llanos, and its author later served briefly as president of
his country. An Argentine novel turned a ranch hand into an initiation into
national character. These books were widely read and are now often treated as a
dead end, but they established something necessary: that the material of serious
fiction could be local, specific, and unglamorous.

Simultaneously, and in near-total opposition, came the avant-garde. In Chile,
Vicente Huidobro declared that a poet should not describe a tree but make one. In
Peru, César Vallejo published a book of poems that broke Spanish syntax and
orthography apart in ways that still look violent, and then spent his short
remaining life in European exile and poverty. In Brazil, a modernist week in São
Paulo in 1922 detonated a movement whose central manifesto proposed cannibalism
as a cultural method — swallow the European influence, digest it, turn it into
something Brazilian. That idea has proved more durable than almost any other
formulation of the continent's relationship to its sources.

Borges belongs here too, though he outlasted the vanguard by half a century. His
short fictions of the 1940s — libraries containing every possible book, a map the
size of its territory, a man who writes Don Quixote word for word by becoming
someone for whom it would be original — treated erudition as raw material and
gave later writers permission to be playful about enormous questions. He wrote no
novels, worked for years in a municipal library, and went blind around the time
he was appointed director of the national one.

It is worth saying plainly that poetry, not fiction, was the continent's
strongest suit for most of this stretch. Gabriela Mistral, a Chilean rural
schoolteacher who wrote about children, grief, and the Andes, became the first
Latin American to win the Nobel Prize in Literature, two decades before any
novelist from the region was seriously considered. Pablo Neruda moved from
adolescent love poems that sold in millions of copies through a dense, decaying,
surrealist middle period to a vast attempt at writing the whole hemisphere's
history in verse, with a long consular and Communist political career running
alongside. Octavio Paz wrote a book-length essay on Mexican character and
solitude that Mexicans have been arguing with ever since, and a body of poetry
shaped by his years in India. Readers who know the region only through its
novelists have the proportions backward.

## The Boom

By the early 1960s several things converged. The Cuban Revolution made Latin
America an object of intense international interest and gave writers across the
continent a shared political reference point. A generation of novelists who had
read Faulkner, Joyce, and Woolf in translation began publishing at roughly the
same moment. Spanish publishers, particularly in Barcelona, went looking for
them, and a formidable literary agent named Carmen Balcells built careers,
negotiated foreign rights, and in some cases paid her authors salaries so they
could write.

The books came fast: an Argentine novel with chapters meant to be read in two
different orders, a Peruvian novel about a military academy that the academy
burned copies of, a Mexican novel narrated from a dying man's bed across shifting
persons and tenses, and in 1967 a Colombian novel tracing a family and a town
through a hundred years of civil wars, banana companies, insomnia plagues, and
ascensions. One Hundred Years of Solitude sold in quantities nobody in Spanish
publishing had seen, and it fixed an image of Latin American writing in the world
that its author never entirely wanted.

The label that stuck was magical realism, and it had older roots — a Cuban
novelist had argued two decades earlier that the marvelous was not a technique in
the Americas but a description of ordinary conditions, and a Mexican writer had
already produced a short, perfect novel in which a man goes looking for his
father in a village where everyone turns out to be dead. But the label flattened
a very diverse group. The Boom included realists, formal experimenters,
political novelists, and at least one writer whose principal subject was
totalitarian bureaucracy. It was also, conspicuously, a group of men; the women
writing at the same time were not marketed as part of it, and Clarice Lispector,
who was doing something at least as radical in Brazilian Portuguese, was read
seriously abroad only much later.

Translation deserves more credit than it usually receives in this story. The
English-language Boom was substantially the work of a handful of translators —
Gregory Rabassa, who brought over Cortazar and Garcia Marquez, and later Edith
Grossman, among others — working at a moment when American and British
publishers were suddenly willing to take Spanish-language fiction seriously.
Books that had circulated for years in Spanish arrived in English within months
of each other, which is one reason the phenomenon looked, from outside, like a
sudden eruption rather than the culmination of forty years of work.

The political solidarity did not survive the decade. When a Cuban poet was
arrested and made to deliver a public self-denunciation, the writers split over
whether to protest, and friendships ended permanently. Several of the group moved
steadily rightward afterward; others did not.

## After

The generation that followed spent a good deal of energy escaping the
inheritance. In the 1990s a group of younger writers published an anthology whose
title punned on Macondo and McDonald's, arguing that their actual continent was
urban, mediated, full of imported television and shopping malls, and that flying
grandmothers were a tourist product. Roberto Bolaño, a Chilean who spent most of
his life in Mexico and Spain, wrote sprawling novels about poets who never
publish and murders that never get solved, and became after his early death the
most imitated Latin American writer since García Márquez.

Elsewhere the energy went into testimony — first-person accounts of massacre,
disappearance, and dictatorship, recorded and edited by writers who insisted the
form mattered less than the record. And more recently the continent's most
visible fiction in translation has been written by women: novels about femicide
in Veracruz, about environmental poisoning in rural Argentina, about ghosts that
are indistinguishable from the political history that produced them. The
international attention is no longer an event that needs a name. It is just the
normal condition, which is what the writers of Machado de Assis's generation
never got.
