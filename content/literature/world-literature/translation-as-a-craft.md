# Translation as a Craft

The *Odyssey* introduces its hero with an adjective: *polytropos*. Literally it
means many-turned, or many-turning, and it can point in several directions at
once — a man who has travelled widely, a man who has been knocked about by
events, a man who is devious. Greek allows all three simultaneously. English
does not. Richmond Lattimore gave "the man of many ways." Robert Fagles gave "the
man of twists and turns." Emily Wilson opened her version with "Tell me about a
complicated man," and took some criticism for the plainness of it, which rather
missed that "complicated" is one of the few English words that can carry both the
admiring and the suspicious sense at once.

Nobody was wrong. There is no correct answer, and the belief that one exists
somewhere, waiting to be found by a sufficiently careful person, is the main
thing that prevents people from translating well.

## The Unit Is Not the Word

Beginners translate words, then discover that the sentence they have assembled
is not English. The first real skill is learning what size of thing you are
actually moving across.

Sometimes it is the clause. German holds its verb until the end of a subordinate
clause, so a long German sentence keeps the reader in suspense in a way that
English syntax cannot replicate without sounding strained; you either accept the
loss or restructure the paragraph so the suspense lands somewhere else. Sometimes
the unit is the idiom, and the only honest move is to find an unrelated English
idiom doing comparable work. Sometimes it is the whole paragraph — a joke set up
in one language may need its setup and punchline swapped to survive.

Register is the thing amateurs miss most often. Every language slices the
formal-to-casual continuum differently, and a word that sits comfortably in the
middle of a French sentence may have no English equivalent that is not either too
literary or too slangy. Japanese encodes the relationship between speaker and
listener in the verb itself. Arabic and Greek carry a classical register that has
no modern English counterpart at all, because English never had a prestige
written variety that diverged that far from speech.

Dryden, writing a preface to some Ovid translations in the seventeenth century,
sorted the options into three: metaphrase, word for word; paraphrase, sense for
sense with latitude; and imitation, in which the translator takes the original as
a starting point and departs freely. He thought the middle course correct, and
most working translators have agreed with him ever since, while disagreeing
violently about where its boundaries lie.

## Toward the Reader or Toward the Writer

Schleiermacher framed the same problem more usefully in a lecture given in
Berlin early in the nineteenth century. There are only two methods, he argued:
either the translator leaves the author alone and moves the reader toward him, or
leaves the reader alone and moves the author toward the reader. Mixing them
produces incoherence.

In modern criticism these are called foreignizing and domesticating. A
domesticating translation converts currency, finds a local equivalent for the
dish, drops the honorific, smooths the syntax until the book reads as though it
had been written in English. A foreignizing one keeps the unfamiliar word,
retains a sentence rhythm that feels imported, and lets the reader do some work.
Neither is a virtue by itself. Domestication can be an act of hospitality or an
act of erasure. Foreignization can be scrupulous or can be a way of making a
perfectly ordinary novel look exotic to readers who cannot check.

The practical form of the decision is usually small and repeated. Does the
Russian patronymic stay? Do you keep *sensei* or write "teacher"? If a character
eats something with no English name, do you name it and let context carry it, or
substitute, or add a clause of explanation the original did not have? Any single
choice is trivial. Five hundred of them make the book.

## The Container Problem

Verse is where the difficulty becomes acute, because form in poetry is not
decoration.

Pushkin's *Eugene Onegin* is written in a stanza he invented: fourteen lines of
iambic tetrameter locked into a fixed rhyme scheme that alternates between
feminine and masculine endings in a set pattern. Russian, with its inflected
endings, produces rhymes easily. English does not, and English tetrameter tends
toward the jingle. Vladimir Nabokov concluded that any attempt to reproduce the
stanza in English would necessarily falsify the sense, and produced instead a
deliberately unlovely literal version in four volumes, most of the bulk being
commentary. Edmund Wilson reviewed it harshly, Nabokov replied, and the two
former friends conducted a public quarrel in print that ended the friendship.
Both were defending something real: Wilson that a poem rendered as prose cribs is
no longer a poem, Nabokov that a poem rendered with invented rhymes is no longer
Pushkin.

The working compromise is compensation. You lose an effect at one point and
manufacture an equivalent effect somewhere nearby — a pun that will not survive
in line three is replaced by a different pun in line five, so that the texture of
wordplay is preserved even though no individual joke is. This requires
bookkeeping and nerve, and it is the reason good verse translators are usually
poets.

## How the Work Actually Goes

Read the whole thing first, in the original, before typing a word. This sounds
obvious and is frequently skipped, and the cost is that decisions made on page
twenty turn out to be wrong by page three hundred.

Then decide a governing principle and write it down. Contemporary or period
diction? Contractions in dialogue or not? What happens to names, titles, songs,
the untranslatable pun in chapter four? A translation is a long accumulation of
rulings, and consistency is what makes the result feel like a book rather than a
committee.

Draft fast and revise slowly. Most translators produce a rough version that
stays close to the original and then work away from it, because it is far easier
to loosen a stiff sentence than to check a fluent one back against a text you
have stopped consulting. Read the revision aloud. Translationese — that flat,
faintly foreign, characterless English that afflicts so much translated fiction —
is usually inaudible on the page and obvious in the mouth. It comes from
preserving the original's sentence boundaries and connective habits out of a
misplaced sense of fidelity.

Watch the false friends, which multiply in related languages. And watch the
opposite trap: over-domesticating a word because the literal version looks
strange, when the strangeness was in the original and was doing something.

Collaboration is one answer to the problem that few people have equal command of
both languages. Richard Pevear and Larissa Volokhonsky divide the Russian
novelists between them — she produces a close draft with notes on what the
Russian is doing, he works it into English, and they argue over the result. The
method has been criticized for producing prose that reads oddly in English, which
is roughly the criticism its defenders would call the point. It stands in
deliberate opposition to Constance Garnett, who translated most of the Russian
canon at enormous speed a century earlier and made Tolstoy, Dostoevsky and
Chekhov all sound like refined Edwardian novelists — and who, whatever her
limitations, is the reason English readers knew those books at all.

## Whose Name Is on the Cover

Compare three English *Tale of Genji*. Arthur Waley's, from the nineteen-twenties,
is expansive, ornamented, and freely cut; it reads like a late-Victorian novel and
was the version that made Murasaki Shikibu famous in English. Edward
Seidensticker's is lean and closer, trimming Waley's embroidery. Royall Tyler's
goes further in the other direction, preserving the Heian court's habit of
referring to people by office rather than name, so that the reader must track
characters through shifting titles exactly as the original audience did. Three
translators, one text, three different novels — and the differences between them
are larger than the differences between many pairs of unrelated books.

That is the case for printing the translator's name on the cover, and for the
fact that translations date while originals do not. A novel written in 1870 stays
in 1870. A translation made in 1910 is a document of 1910's English, and after
sixty or eighty years it starts to sound like a period costume that the original
never wore. Retranslation is not a judgment on the earlier translator; it is
maintenance.

Gregory Rabassa waited to start *One Hundred Years of Solitude* until he had
finished another book, and García Márquez waited three years for him rather than
take someone else. Readers who know both languages have often said the English is
the equal of the Spanish. Whether or not that is literally assessable, the remark
identifies the ambition correctly: not to produce a serviceable report on a
foreign book, but to write, in English, something worth reading for its own sake
that happens to be the same work.
