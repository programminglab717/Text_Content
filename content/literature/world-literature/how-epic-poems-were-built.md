# How Epic Poems Were Built

In the early 1930s a young American classicist named Milman Parry hauled
recording equipment into the mountains of what was then Yugoslavia and set it up
in coffeehouses where men still sang long narrative poems to the scrape of a
one-stringed fiddle. He had not come for those songs. He had come for Homer.
Parry had already argued, from internal evidence alone, that the Iliad and the
Odyssey carried the fingerprints of poems composed without writing, and he wanted
to watch living singers do what he believed Homer had done. He died in an
accident before the work was finished. His student Albert Lord completed it and
published the conclusions a generation later, and between them they permanently
altered what a classicist could believe about the oldest long poems in the
European tradition.

The singers, the guslari, were not reciting memorized text. Ask one to repeat a
song he had performed the day before and he would give you a poem that was
recognizably the same story at a noticeably different length, with lines that had
not been there before and lines that had quietly gone missing. Ask him whether he
had sung it word for word as he learned it, and he would say yes, and mean it,
because in his usage word for word meant faithful to the story. He was composing
as he sang, at something close to the speed of ordinary speech, holding a
narrative of thousands of lines in shape while generating each individual line
fresh. That is a real skill. It took years to acquire and it had very little to
do with memory as a schoolchild would understand the word.

## The Formula and the Line

The trick rests on a stock of ready-made phrases sized to fit the metrical slots
of the verse. Greek epic is composed in dactylic hexameter, a demanding line
with a fixed shape, and a poet improvising in it needs phrases that will drop
into particular positions without breaking the rhythm. So Achilles is
swift-footed, the dawn is rosy-fingered, the sea is wine-dark, and Odysseus is
resourceful — not because the poet has run out of adjectives but because each of
those name-and-epithet combinations occupies a precise metrical shape and can be
deployed the instant that shape is needed.

Parry's sharpest observation was about thrift. A traditional system does not
accumulate redundant options. For a given character in a given case, at a given
position in the line, there is usually exactly one epithet available, and the
alternatives that would do the same metrical job simply do not exist. That is
the signature of a working tool rather than a stylistic flourish. Ornament
proliferates; equipment does not.

The one device that seems to resist the formulaic account is the extended simile,
and it is worth noticing why. When the Iliad pauses to compare a charging army to
a forest fire on a mountain ridge, or a wounded man's collapse to a poppy bending
under rain, the comparison runs on for lines and reaches entirely outside the
war — into farming, weather, herding, the work of women at a loom. These passages
are not interchangeable units. They function as relief valves, admitting the
ordinary peacetime world into a poem otherwise confined to a battlefield, and
they are one reason the Iliad feels larger than its subject. A tradition supplies
the toolkit; it does not supply that.

Above the level of the phrase sits the theme, or type-scene: a recurring block of
narrative action with a conventional sequence. The arming of the warrior. The
arrival of a guest and the hospitality that must follow. The assembly where
someone rises to speak. The single combat with its boasts, its spear cast, its
stripping of armor. A singer who knows a hundred type-scenes can assemble a
stretch of story without deciding anything at the level of the individual word.
And above that sits the story-pattern itself — the return of the long-absent
husband, the wrath and reconciliation, the journey to the land of the dead.

None of this makes the poems mechanical, which is the objection the theory
usually meets first. A fixed keyboard is not a limit on what a pianist can play.
The formulaic system is the instrument, and the difference between a village
singer with a few hundred lines and a master with a repertoire of tens of
thousands is audible to any audience that has heard both. What the theory
dissolves is not artistry but a particular romantic picture of a solitary genius
at a writing desk. Whatever Homer was, he worked inside a tradition that had been
refining its own tools for centuries before he used them.

## The Same Machinery, Everywhere

Once you know what to look for, versions of this craft turn up across the world,
each shaped by the language it grew in. Sanskrit epic runs in the sloka, a
flexible couplet that the Mahabharata and the Ramayana use at enormous length —
the Mahabharata being less a single poem than an accreted library of story,
genealogy, law, and philosophy wrapped around a dynastic war. Old English works
by alliteration rather than quantity, binding two half-lines together by repeated
initial sounds, and Beowulf's compressed compound phrasings are its equivalent of
the epithet. The Old French chanson de geste organizes itself in laisses, runs of
lines bound by a single assonance, which is why the Song of Roland can circle the
same moment from slightly different angles without embarrassment.

In Mali and the surrounding Mande-speaking region, the epic of Sunjata is the
property of hereditary professionals, the jeliw, who hold genealogies as well as
narrative and who perform with instrumental accompaniment for patrons whose
ancestry they are qualified to recite. In Kyrgyzstan, the Manas is still sung,
and the great performers are known by name and reputation the way concert
soloists are elsewhere. In Finland and Karelia, Elias Lönnrot spent the 1830s and
1840s collecting sung material in a distinctive trochaic meter and stitched it
into the Kalevala, an edited construction that nevertheless rests on genuinely
oral sources — and that promptly became the founding literary document of a
national movement.

The Epic of Gilgamesh shows the other end of the process. It survives because it
was written, in cuneiform on clay, and the version most readers meet is a
standard edition associated with a scribe named Sîn-lēqi-unninni and recovered
largely from the library at Nineveh. Behind that polished text lie older Sumerian
poems about the same king, circulating separately, in different arrangements.
Writing did not create Gilgamesh. Writing caught it, at one particular moment,
and preserved that moment for thousands of years after the tradition that fed it
had gone silent.

## What the Poems Were For

Epics do work that has nothing to do with entertainment, which is why they are so
often willing to stop dead and list things. The catalogue of ships in the Iliad
is a roll of communities and their contingents, a map of who counted and how much
— exactly the kind of information a society without archives needs to keep
somewhere. Genealogies serve the same function: they establish claims. Long
digressions on how a particular weapon was made, or which god quarreled with
which ancestor, encode technical and religious knowledge in a form that travels.

An epic also fixes a group's account of itself. The Shahnameh gave Persian
speakers a continuous narrative of kingship running from the first mythical
rulers to the Arab conquest, composed by Ferdowsi in tens of thousands of
couplets at a moment when the language and its past were under pressure. The
Aeneid handed Rome an origin that connected it to Troy and, not incidentally, to
the family of the emperor who was Virgil's patron. These poems are arguments
about legitimacy as much as they are stories, and audiences understood them that
way.

The repetition that modern readers find slow is part of the same design. In
performance, nobody can turn back a page. Epithets remind you who is speaking.
Repeated formulas let attention lapse and recover without loss. Type-scenes give
the audience a template so that departures from it register as meaningful — when
the expected hospitality is refused, or the arming scene is interrupted,
everybody present feels the deviation immediately because everybody knows the
pattern.

Length is the other thing worth keeping in mind, because it governs everything
else. A full performance of a major epic is not an evening's diversion. Lord's
singers could extend or compress almost at will, and the great Balkan songs ran
for hours; the Manas in Kyrgyz tradition is vastly longer than the Iliad and the
Odyssey combined, and no performance covers it. Audiences did not consume these
poems whole. They heard episodes, on occasions that mattered — a festival, a
wedding, a patron's hall in winter, a long night in a coffeehouse during
Ramadan — and they already knew the outcome. Suspense in the ordinary sense was
never the point. The pleasure lay in how a particular singer handled a scene
everyone had heard before, which is closer to how an audience listens to a
familiar aria than to how a reader consumes a thriller.

## After the Singing

Literary epic begins when a poet who can write decides to do deliberately what
the tradition had done by necessity. Virgil is the pivotal case: he adopts
Homeric machinery, epithets and similes and divine interventions and all, while
composing slowly, revising, and building a structure whose internal
cross-references only reward a reader who can flip backward. Milton did the same
in English, blind and dictating, folding classical epic into Christian
cosmology. Camões wrote Portuguese maritime expansion into the same frame.
Centuries later, Derek Walcott used Homeric names and a loosened hexameter to
write about fishermen in St. Lucia, and the tension between the borrowed frame
and the local subject is a large part of what the poem is about.

Meanwhile the older kind did not simply stop. It contracted, migrated into
ballads and romances and eventually into prose, and in some places it went on
unbroken. Parry and Lord were able to do their fieldwork because a living oral
epic tradition still existed in the 1930s within a few days' travel of major
European universities. Recordings of it survive in a university archive in
Massachusetts — hours of singing, in a language most of the people who study
Homer cannot read, collected to settle a question about a poet who may or may
not have existed three thousand years ago.
