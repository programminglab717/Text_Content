# Where the Novel Came From

Samuel Richardson was a successful London printer in his fifties, with no
literary ambitions anyone had noticed, when two booksellers asked him to compile
a volume of model letters — the sort of thing a person of limited education could
consult when obliged to write to a landlord, or decline an invitation, or
condole with a widow. He took the commission. Somewhere in the middle of it he
found himself writing letters from a young servant to her parents about the
attentions of her employer, and instead of stopping at a specimen he kept going.
The result, published in 1740 as Pamela, was read aloud in households across
England, argued about from pulpits, pirated, sequelled, and parodied within
months. It also became the standard exhibit in a story that literary history has
been telling itself ever since: that the novel was invented in
eighteenth-century England, by and for a rising commercial middle class, and that
it arrived with a new technique for making fiction feel like the record of an
actual life.

That story has a canonical version, set out by the critic Ian Watt in the 1950s,
and it is not wrong so much as local. Watt argued that what distinguishes the new
fiction is formal realism: particular people with ordinary names, in named
places, at specified times, moving through a world governed by cause and effect
rather than by romance convention. Defoe gives his castaway an inventory and a
calendar. Richardson gives his heroine an hour-by-hour account of her own
anxiety. Fielding, who despised Pamela enough to write a short obscene parody of
it before producing two great novels of his own, gives you a plot engineered like
a clock. Underneath all of it, Watt saw the same forces: Protestant habits of
self-examination, an economy that made individual striving legible, and a print
market able to reward a writer directly for volume.

## What the Standard Story Leaves Out

The trouble is that long prose fiction about invented people was not new. Greek
and Roman readers had it: Heliodorus and Longus wrote romances with separated
lovers, shipwrecks, and recognitions; Petronius wrote a sprawling satiric
narrative full of freedmen talking shop at dinner; Apuleius wrote a novel about a
man turned into a donkey that contains as much ordinary Mediterranean life as any
picaresque. In Japan, Murasaki Shikibu had produced a work of several hundred
thousand words in the early eleventh century, tracking a court society across
generations with a psychological attention nobody in Europe would match for
seven hundred years. In China, a tradition of vernacular fiction developed out of
storytelling and reached, by the eighteenth century, a novel of family decline
and adolescent interiority — Cao Xueqin's Dream of the Red Chamber — that answers
almost every specification Watt laid down.

Closer to home, the pieces were assembled well before 1740. The anonymous
Lazarillo de Tormes, published in the mid-sixteenth century, invented the
low-born first-person narrator who survives by his wits and reports on his
betters. Cervantes put a reader of romances into a landscape of inns and pigs and
laundry and produced, in two parts a decade apart, a book so self-aware that its
second half is partly about the reception of its first. Madame de Lafayette wrote
a short, cold, exact novel of court intrigue in the 1670s whose subject is a
woman's private deliberation. Aphra Behn wrote fiction for money in Restoration
London and framed it as eyewitness testimony.

The vocabulary itself records the confusion. For a long time English had two
words and no settled boundary between them. A romance dealt in the marvelous,
the remote, the aristocratic — knights, enchantments, improbable coincidence. A
novel, borrowed from the Italian novella, implied something new and short and
plausible, a piece of news about people who might exist. Writers exploited the
distinction when it suited them and ignored it when it did not; Fielding called
his books comic epic poems in prose partly to escape the association with cheap
romance, and Walter Scott would later revive the older word deliberately.
Critics who argue about when the novel began are frequently arguing about which
of those two words they are willing to let govern the question.

So the honest formulation is not that the novel was invented in Britain but that
something changed in the eighteenth century in the status and quantity of prose
fiction — and that the change was as much commercial as formal. Novels became a
reliable product. Writers could live off them. Readers began to expect a steady
supply.

## The Machinery Underneath

Print was the precondition, but print had existed for two and a half centuries.
What was new was an industry organized around continuous publication for a
general audience. The English copyright statute of 1710 gave authors and
booksellers a legal interest they could sell and defend, which made it worth
speculating on new writing rather than reprinting safe classics. Paper remained
expensive, so books were expensive, and the answer was the circulating library:
subscribe for a few shillings a quarter and borrow as much as you could read.
Libraries, not individual buyers, became the real customers, which is why so many
novels were issued in multiple volumes — a three-volume work could be lent to
three subscribers at once.

The economics shaped the writing. Serial publication in monthly parts, which
Dickens made standard in the 1830s after the first numbers of the Pickwick
papers caught on, rewarded a chapter that ended in a position of suspense and
punished a story that resolved too early. Writers learned to build in recurring
characters that readers would pay to meet again. The nineteenth-century three-
decker, propped up by subscription libraries that would buy in bulk, encouraged
length that was sometimes structural and sometimes merely contractual. When those
libraries lost their grip late in the century, the three-volume novel collapsed
within a few years and the single-volume book we now take for granted replaced
it. The shape of fiction followed the shape of its distribution with very little
delay.

How many people actually read these books is harder to establish than how many
copies were printed, and the gap runs in one direction only. A novel in a
household was read aloud in the evening to everyone present, including servants
and children and the illiterate. A library copy passed through a dozen hands
before it fell apart. Serial parts were shared, resold, and read in coffeehouses
and barbershops by people who never bought one. Whatever the sales figures were,
the effective audience was substantially larger, and it included a great many
readers whom publishers were not thinking about and moralists very much were.

## Learning to Get Inside a Head

The formal achievement that most justifies treating the novel as a distinct
invention took about a century to mature, and it is narrower than realism. It is
the technique of rendering a character's thought in the narrator's voice — what
grammarians call free indirect discourse, where third-person past-tense narration
takes on the vocabulary, the mistakes, and the self-deception of the person being
described. Jane Austen uses it so pervasively that readers often miss it: a
sentence stating that a match was a very good one may be the narrator's judgment,
or the heroine's, or a piece of received opinion the novel is quietly mocking,
and the ambiguity is the point. Flaubert industrialized it. George Eliot used it
to move between many consciousnesses in a single provincial town without ever
fully endorsing any of them.

Epistolary novels had reached toward the same effect by brute force, giving you
letters written under pressure, sometimes hours after an event the writer has not
yet understood. Richardson's Clarissa runs to a length few modern readers attempt
precisely because it insists on staying inside the hour. Rousseau, writing a
generation later, found that the form could produce a kind of emotional
contagion; his novel of thwarted love sold spectacularly and was read as a moral
document. Goethe's young Werther set off a fashion in waistcoats and an outbreak
of imitators across Europe. Laclos turned the same machinery inside out, using
letters not to expose sincerity but to expose calculation, with two
correspondents coolly drafting the seduction of a third.

## The Long Suspicion

Almost from the start, novel reading was treated as a vice. The complaints were
consistent: it wasted time, it inflamed the imagination, it fed young women
expectations no ordinary life could satisfy, and it substituted invented people
for real duties. Cervantes had already built a comic machine out of that anxiety,
and the joke proved durable — Charlotte Lennox wrote a mid-century novel about a
young woman who reads French romances and conducts herself accordingly, and Jane
Austen wrote her own version, published only after her death, in which a girl
raised on Gothic fiction misreads an ordinary household as a scene of atrocity.
The defenses tended to be defensive. Fielding insisted on moral design.
Richardson insisted his books were cautionary.

What is striking, looking back, is how completely the suspicion transferred
rather than dissolved. The arguments made against novels in the 1750s were
recycled almost word for word against sensation fiction in the 1860s, against
cinema, against comics, against television, against video games. The form that
was once the corrupter of youth became, within a century and a half, the thing
that schools assigned to protect youth from the newer corrupters. Unauthorized
continuations of Pamela appeared within months, and scenes from it were printed
on fans and sold as engravings, which is roughly what a franchise looked like
before the word existed. Two hundred years later the same book was being taught
as a document of national literary origin.

None of which settles the question of where the novel came from. It probably came
from several places at once, and the tidy account that puts its birth in a London
printing house in 1740 survives mainly because that is where the record is
thickest and the language is English.
