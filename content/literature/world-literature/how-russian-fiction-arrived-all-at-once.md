# How Russian Fiction Arrived All at Once

Imagine a subscriber in a provincial Russian town in the first months of 1866,
cutting open the new issue of The Russian Messenger. Inside, among the political
commentary and the reviews, is the opening installment of a story about an
impoverished former student in Petersburg who has been thinking about killing an
old woman who lends money against pledges. The subscriber will wait a month for
the next part, and another month after that, and the novel will not finish until
the end of the year. This was the ordinary way Russians encountered the books we
now shelve as masterpieces: in pieces, interleaved with essays on the peasant
question, inside a periodical that was simultaneously a magazine, a political
platform, and a substitute for the public sphere the state would not permit.

What makes the Russian nineteenth century strange is how compressed it is. Take
a language with no substantial prose fiction of its own in 1820, put it under an
autocracy with preventive censorship, and within roughly two generations it
produces a body of novels that much of the world still treats as the high-water
mark of the form. There is no gradual accumulation of minor practitioners, no
long apprenticeship of second-rate books teaching the culture how to read.
Something like the whole thing arrives at once.

## Starting Without a Language

Part of the reason is that the raw material had to be manufactured first.
Educated Russians of the early nineteenth century conducted their emotional and
intellectual lives substantially in French; the written Russian available to them
was split between a heavy Church Slavonic register suited to sermons and a
colloquial speech nobody thought fit for serious writing. War and Peace opens
with a drawing room conversation in French, and Tolstoy is not being decorative —
he is recording how that class actually talked.

Pushkin is the hinge. He wrote a language that could handle a folktale, a love
letter, a political epigram, and a dry ironic aside without changing gears, and
he did it so completely that Russians afterward measured prose against him
whether or not they liked him. Eugene Onegin, a novel in verse built from
fourteen-line stanzas of remarkable metrical tightness, contains almost all the
later material in embryo: the superfluous man with too much education and no
occupation, the provincial girl whose seriousness exceeds his, the duel, the
missed chance, the ironic narrator who keeps interrupting. Then Gogol gave the
tradition its other half — grotesque, nervous, unreliable, deeply funny, and
concerned with clerks and swindlers rather than officers and heiresses. A famous
remark about the whole school emerging from beneath Gogol's overcoat has been
attributed to Dostoevsky for a century, probably wrongly, but it survives because
the claim underneath it is defensible.

## The Thick Journals

Nothing in Russian literature makes sense without the tolstye zhurnaly, the thick
journals. Each was several hundred pages a month, each had a political
orientation that readers understood immediately, and each ran fiction alongside
criticism, translated science, and as much commentary on social questions as the
censors would tolerate. Because open political organization was impossible, the
journals became the place where positions were staked out, and a novel published
in one of them was read as an intervention.

This had direct consequences for the books. Serialization stretched composition
over years, and writers revised in public, responding to what readers and critics
had said about earlier installments. Editors exercised real power. Mikhail
Katkov, who ran The Russian Messenger, published a startling proportion of the
canon and also refused the final part of Anna Karenina because he disagreed with
Levin's skepticism about Russian volunteers fighting in the Balkans; Tolstoy
issued it separately rather than yield. Censorship worked less by outright
suppression than by making certain subjects require indirection, which is one
reason Russian fiction became so skilled at putting arguments into the mouths of
characters and letting the reader assemble the conclusion.

The critics mattered as much as the editors. Vissarion Belinsky effectively
invented the Russian habit of reading novels as social evidence, and his heirs on
the radical left judged fiction almost entirely by its usefulness. Dostoevsky was
arrested as a young man partly for reading aloud, in a private circle, Belinsky's
furious open letter to Gogol; he was marched out for execution and reprieved on
the scaffold before being sent to Siberia. Literature in this environment was not
a leisure pursuit that occasionally brushed against politics. It was the
politics.

## The Accursed Questions

Russian novelists inherited a set of problems their contemporaries elsewhere
could treat as optional: whether God exists, what justifies a society built on
other people's forced labor, whether Russia belongs to Europe, and what a decent
person is obliged to do about any of it. The serfs were emancipated in 1861,
which settled nothing and opened everything. Turgenev's hunting sketches, printed
a decade before the reform, had already shown educated readers peasants who
thought and grieved and made jokes, and the book's effect on opinion was
substantial enough that it is regularly cited in political histories rather than
just literary ones.

The debates then ran straight through the fiction. Turgenev's Fathers and Sons
gave the culture the word nihilist and a young doctor who dissects frogs and
refuses to admire sunsets, and both the radicals and the conservatives concluded
the portrait was an attack on them. Chernyshevsky answered from prison with a
didactic novel about rational egoism and cooperative workshops that hardly anyone
now reads for pleasure and that shaped a generation of revolutionaries.
Dostoevsky answered Chernyshevsky with a short, poisonous monologue by a man in a
basement who insists that human beings will choose their own harm simply to prove
they are not piano keys. That exchange — a novel, a rebuttal novel, a rebuttal to
the rebuttal — is the ordinary texture of the period.

## Two Ways to Build a Novel

Tolstoy and Dostoevsky are usually paired, and the pairing is useful mainly for
how completely they differ as engineers. Tolstoy's method is accumulation of
physical and psychological detail until a scene becomes undeniable, combined
with a habit of describing familiar things as if encountering them for the first
time — an opera as meaningless noise and painted cardboard, a battle as confusion
in which nobody can see anything. The formalist critic Viktor Shklovsky later
built a whole theory of literary technique out of that one Tolstoyan move. The
narrator stands above the characters, knows more than they do, and is not shy
about saying so.

Dostoevsky does the opposite. His novels are built out of voices that refuse to
be subordinated, characters arguing at the top of their intelligence with
positions the author finds monstrous, and scandalous scenes in which a dozen
people converge in one room and everything is said. Mikhail Bakhtin named this
polyphony, and the point of the term is that the author declines the privilege of
settling the dispute. A Dostoevsky novel is closer to a stenographic record of an
unresolvable argument than to a report on a world.

Neither was writing tidy books, and their contemporaries abroad noticed. Henry
James's complaint about large loose baggy monsters was aimed partly at War and
Peace, and he was not wrong about the shape — only about whether the shape was a
defect. The Russian novel of this period carries essays, historical polemic,
theology, and agricultural theory inside it without apology, because the
periodical it grew in did the same.

## The Exit

The tradition ends not with a bigger novel but with a smaller form. Chekhov, a
doctor from a family recently descended from serfs, wrote hundreds of stories,
declined to supply conclusions, and made a method out of leaving the decisive
thing unstated. His characters talk past each other and fail to change their
lives, and the stories stop rather than resolve. He also traveled across Siberia
in his thirties to survey the penal colony on Sakhalin and came back with a book
of statistics and interviews that reads nothing like his fiction — a last version
of the old assumption that a Russian writer has obligations outside literature.

The rest of the world met all of this late and in bulk, mostly in English through
Constance Garnett's enormous run of translations around the turn of the century.
Readers encountering Tolstoy, Dostoevsky, Turgenev, and Chekhov within a few
years of each other, in a uniform style, could be forgiven for concluding that
Russian writers all sounded alike. They do not. But the accident of translation
helped fix the idea of a single Russian nineteenth century, arriving all at once,
which is at least half true.
