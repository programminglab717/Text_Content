# The Japanese Tradition, Form by Form

A man who spent the night with a woman at the Heian court was expected to send
her a poem the next morning, ideally before the dew had dried. The poem was
short — thirty-one syllables, in a fixed pattern — and almost everything about
its delivery carried meaning. The paper's color and weight. The handwriting,
which was read as an index of character. The flowering branch it was tied to,
which had to suit both the season and the tone. A clumsy morning poem could end
an attachment. This was not literature as a leisure activity; it was a social
instrument, and competence in it determined how one was regarded.

That is a useful place to start, because Japanese literature is best approached
through its forms rather than its periods. The tradition is unusually continuous
— texts from a thousand years ago are still read, still alluded to, and in some
cases still imitated — and the through-line is a set of formal containers that
each generation inherited, stretched, and passed on.

## Waka, and the Weight of Thirty-One Syllables

The earliest surviving anthology, compiled in the eighth century, gathers several
thousand poems and is remarkable for how wide a social range it admits: emperors,
court officials, and also frontier guards posted far from home and anonymous
voices from the provinces. It was written down using Chinese characters for
their sound values, a laborious system devised because Japanese had no script of
its own and Chinese writing fit the language badly.

Two centuries later the first imperially commissioned anthology set the terms
that would govern court poetry for the next eight hundred years. Its preface,
written in the newly developed phonetic script, made an argument that Japanese
readers have quoted ever since: that poetry grows from the human heart as
naturally as a plant from a seed, and that everything alive produces song. The
poems themselves narrowed the range dramatically. Seasons, longing, separation,
the passage of time. Within that narrow field, technique became extraordinarily
refined — fixed epithets inherited from older poetry, words chosen to carry two
meanings at once, and allusive variation, in which a poet deliberately embedded a
phrase from a famous older poem so that the earlier work would sound faintly
under the new one.

Court poetry was competitive and public. Poetry contests paired compositions
against each other and had judges deliver verdicts. Selection into an imperial
anthology was a form of immortality that families pursued across generations.

## Prose in a Woman's Hand

The phonetic script was initially considered the women's hand; serious men wrote
Chinese. That snobbery produced one of the luckiest accidents in literary
history, because it left Japanese prose to women who were not writing for
official purposes at all.

Around the turn of the eleventh century, a woman serving at court whom we know by
the nickname Murasaki Shikibu produced an enormous narrative following a
prince of great beauty and questionable conduct through decades of love affairs,
political maneuvering, and eventual decline — and then continued past his death
into a bleaker, stranger final movement centered on his nominal descendants. The
Tale of Genji is psychologically exacting, structurally ambitious, and saturated
with poems that the characters exchange at moments when prose would be crude. A
contemporary, Sei Shōnagon, wrote something entirely different: a notebook of
observations, opinions, and lists — things that should be small, things that make
the heart beat faster, people who look pleased with themselves — with a
conversational malice that reads as astonishingly modern.

No manuscript in Murasaki's hand survives, and the text as we have it was
stabilized centuries later by scholar-poets who collated competing copies. That
editorial work was worth doing because Genji had become professional equipment:
poets were expected to know its scenes and its hundreds of embedded verses well
enough to allude to them, and commentaries, digests, and character indexes were
produced for people who needed the reference without the reading time. A work of
court fiction by a woman ended up functioning as a canonical text in a poetic
system run almost entirely by men.

Alongside these ran the diary tradition, also largely female, recording court
life, travel, grief, and religious feeling in a mix of prose and verse. One of
the founding examples was written by a man pretending to be a woman so that he
could use the phonetic script without loss of dignity.

## The Chanted Tale and the Ghost Play

Power shifted from the court to provincial warrior houses, and the literature
shifted with it. The great chronicle of the war between the Taira and Minamoto
clans was not primarily a book. It was a performance repertoire, chanted by
blind itinerant monks accompanying themselves on a four-stringed lute, and it
existed in many versions before it was ever fixed on paper. It opens by sounding
a temple bell and announcing that nothing prospers for long, and then spends
hundreds of pages proving it — a warrior aristocracy destroying itself in a few
years, with individual deaths rendered in close, unsparing detail.

Out of this material, and out of temple ritual and village dance, came Nō. By the
late fourteenth century a performer named Zeami, working under shogunal
patronage, had shaped it into something with no European parallel: a slow,
masked, chanted drama in which a traveling priest typically encounters a stranger
who turns out to be the ghost of someone who died on that spot, and who must
recount and re-enact the attachment binding them to the world. Zeami also wrote
treatises on training and performance, circulated in secret within his family,
that remain among the most searching things anyone has written about acting.

## Linked Verse, and How the Haiku Broke Off

Poets had long played a game of joining verses: one person supplies a
three-line unit, the next attaches a two-line unit that reinterprets it, the next
attaches another three-line unit that reinterprets that, and so on. By the
medieval period this had hardened into renga, a highly regulated sequence, often
a hundred links long, governed by rules about how often the moon could appear,
where blossoms were permitted, and how far one had to travel before returning to
a subject. The essential pleasure is that each link changes the meaning of the
one before it; no stable narrative is allowed to form.

A more popular, comic version flourished in the seventeenth century, and it was
inside that tradition that Matsuo Bashō worked. He kept the humor but restored
the seriousness, and he developed the opening verse — the hokku, which had to
establish season and place — into something that could stand alone. He also
wrote travel accounts interleaving prose and verse, the most famous following a
long walk into the northern provinces, visiting sites made sacred by earlier
poems. Two later masters extended the form in different directions: one a painter
whose verses are intensely visual, the other a poet of poverty and small animals
whose work is often barely holding off grief. Only in the Meiji era did a critic
detach the form from linked verse entirely, rename it haiku, and argue for
sketching from life rather than piling up allusion.

## The Merchant City

Edo-period Japan built a commercial publishing industry on woodblock printing,
with illustrated books, lending shops that carried them to customers, and a
readership of townspeople rather than aristocrats. Ihara Saikaku wrote fast,
funny prose about merchants calculating their way through love and money. The
theaters supplied the other half of the popular imagination: kabuki, and the
puppet stage, for which Chikamatsu Monzaemon wrote domestic tragedies — often
about a shop clerk and a courtesan whose situation admits no solution except a
double suicide, staged as a final journey with the chanter narrating every step.
Several of these were based on recent local scandals and reached the stage within
weeks.

The book trade that supported all this was substantial and varied. Cheap
illustrated fiction circulated alongside guidebooks to the pleasure districts,
parodies, ghost-story collections, and enormous serialized adventure novels that
took their authors decades to finish and their readers nearly as long to follow.
Illustration was not decoration but a structural part of the page, and many of
the artists whose prints now hang in museums earned their living doing book work.
Periodic government crackdowns on frivolous or morally suspect publishing pushed
writers toward historical settings and allegory, which is a familiar pattern
wherever censors and popular fiction share a city.

## Making a Modern Language

When Japan reopened in the nineteenth century, writers confronted a problem of
plumbing rather than inspiration: the written language was nothing like the
spoken one, and neither was suited to the interior realism arriving in
translation from Europe. The movement to unify speech and writing occupied
decades, and the first novels attempted in the new manner were written by people
who were also translating Russian fiction and inventing vocabulary as they went.

Natsume Sōseki, sent to London on a government scholarship and thoroughly
miserable there, came back and wrote the books that made modern Japanese prose
feel inevitable — including a late novel, narrated by a student, about an older
man carrying a betrayal he can only confess in a long letter. A distinctively
Japanese genre emerged alongside it: the I-novel, a confessional first-person
form whose value was judged largely by its apparent sincerity. Tanizaki went the
other way, into obsession, fetish, and a deep attachment to a pre-modern
aesthetic; he also translated Genji into modern Japanese more than once.

Japanese literature reached English readers through a small number of dedicated
translators — Arthur Waley, whose free and beautiful Genji of the 1920s
enchanted a generation of British readers, and later Edward Seidensticker and
Donald Keene, who translated and taught and largely built the field abroad. Their
choices shaped the foreign picture considerably. For decades the writers exported
were the ones who could be read as characteristically Japanese in a way Western
readers already expected: restrained, elegiac, preoccupied with seasons and
loss.

The postwar decades produced Kawabata's austere novels of aging and loss, Abe
Kōbō's claustrophobic parables, Mishima's violent aestheticism and violent death,
and Ōe's fiction of disability, Hiroshima, and political conscience. More
recently the visible energy has come substantially from women — novels of
constrained labor, bodies, and refusal that are being translated faster than any
Japanese fiction has been before.

And the oldest form has not gone anywhere. Every new year the imperial household
holds a poetry gathering on an announced theme, and ordinary people send in
thirty-one-syllable poems by the tens of thousands, a handful of which are chanted
aloud at the palace. The paper is no longer tied to a flowering branch.
