# The Arabic Literary Tradition

The convention was that a poem began at a ruin. The speaker, travelling with
companions, comes upon the traces of an abandoned campsite — a fire pit, tent
pegs, the dark stains where a household stood before the tribe moved on — and
halts to weep over it. Imru' al-Qays opens his great ode this way, and so do
dozens of poets after him, for centuries, long after anyone in the audience had
personally struck camp in the desert. The ruined campsite, the *atlal*, became
one of the most durable opening gestures in world poetry, and its persistence
tells you something essential about how Arabic literature works: the tradition
is intensely formal, intensely self-aware, and utterly comfortable with the idea
that originality consists of doing an old thing exceptionally well.

## The Poem Before the Book

Pre-Islamic poetry was oral, but oral in a demanding technical sense. The
*qasida*, the long ode, runs to dozens or hundreds of lines, every one of them
ending in the same rhyme consonant and every one of them following a fixed
quantitative pattern of long and short syllables. Holding that in the head
requires professional memory. Poets had *ruwat*, transmitters, who memorized
their compositions and carried them; a transmitter was often an apprentice poet
himself, and the relationship formed a chain of custody for the corpus.

The classic ode has a tripartite shape. It begins with the *nasib*, the erotic
and elegiac movement at the campsite. It moves to the *rahil*, the journey — a
long stretch given over to the she-camel, the terrain, the animals encountered
along the way, described with a precision that can feel almost zoological. It
ends in boast or praise, the poet asserting the standing of his tribe or the
generosity of his patron. Seven or so of these odes were later singled out as
the *mu'allaqat*, the "suspended" poems, with a persistent legend that they were
hung in gold letters at the Kaaba. The story is probably later invention; the
poems' status is not.

Formal analysis arrived early. In the eighth century al-Khalil ibn Ahmad set out
a system of metres — sixteen of them in the standard reckoning — derived by
cataloguing the patterns actually used, and the scheme has governed the scansion
of Arabic verse ever since. It was an extraordinary piece of descriptive
linguistics, and it belongs to the same period in which Arabic grammar,
lexicography and Qur'anic scholarship were all being systematized at once, in
Basra and Kufa, by scholars who often regarded desert Bedouin speech as the
authoritative standard and travelled out to record it.

## Prose Grows a Spine

The Qur'an complicated everything. Its language is neither verse nor ordinary
prose; its own claim to inimitability became a theological doctrine and a
literary problem, and generations of critics wrote about what exactly made a
text inimitable. The effect on prose was enormous. Rhymed and rhythmically
balanced prose — *saj'* — became a high style, and the Qur'an's authority as a
stylistic reference point meant that Arabic prose developed under the constant
pressure of a text nobody was supposed to be able to match.

Secular prose flourished anyway. Al-Jahiz, writing in ninth-century Basra and
Baghdad, produced an enormous, digressive body of work — a book on animals that
is equally about theology, poetry, anecdote and the habits of dogs; a book on
misers that is one of the funniest things in the language — and effectively
invented a register of learned, ironic, conversational essay-writing. This was
*adab*: at once the body of knowledge a cultivated person should possess and the
manner in which it should be displayed.

The most purely literary prose form was the *maqama*, devised by Badi' al-Zaman
al-Hamadhani and brought to its baroque peak by al-Hariri. A maqama is a short
episode in rhymed prose: a narrator encounters, in some city, a silver-tongued
vagabond who talks a crowd out of its money by means of a virtuoso display of
eloquence, is unmasked, and disappears. The plots are almost beside the point.
The form exists to show off — puns, palindromes, letters that read the same
undotted, entire passages that can be scanned two ways — and it was imitated for
the better part of a millennium, in Hebrew and Persian as well as Arabic.

## Andalus and the Strophic Turn

In Muslim Spain the monorhymed ode acquired a rival. The *muwashshah* is a
strophic poem with a changing rhyme scheme, and it closes with a *kharja*, a
final snatch of speech often placed in a woman's mouth and often written not in
classical Arabic but in colloquial Arabic or in the Romance vernacular of the
peninsula. When scholars began reading these Romance kharjas in the twentieth
century, they turned out to be among the earliest recorded lyric fragments in
any Romance language, which set off a long and unresolved argument about how
much medieval European love poetry owes to Andalusi models.

Andalus produced prose of the first rank as well. Ibn Hazm's treatise on love,
*The Ring of the Dove*, mixes theory with remembered anecdote and has the odd
intimacy of a man describing his own neighbourhood. Ibn Tufayl's *Hayy ibn
Yaqzan* imagines a child raised alone on an island who reasons his way from
first observations to metaphysics — a philosophical novel three centuries before
anyone used that phrase, and a direct ancestor of a strand of European fiction
that includes Defoe.

## The Nights, and What Europe Did With Them

*The Thousand and One Nights* is the most famous Arabic book in the world and
for a long time was among the least respected within Arabic letters. Its core
descends from a Persian collection, itself drawing on Indian story material, and
it accreted layers over centuries — Baghdadi tales, Egyptian tales, material
added by copyists and, eventually, by translators. It is written largely in
middle registers rather than the high classical language, and traditional
critics regarded it as entertainment for the marketplace rather than literature.

The frame story is its genius. A king who kills each new wife at dawn is held
off by a storyteller who leaves every night's tale unfinished, so that
narration itself becomes the thing postponing death. Tales nest inside tales
several layers deep. When Antoine Galland translated it into French in the early
eighteenth century, adding stories he had heard from a Syrian informant — the
Aladdin and Ali Baba material has no known Arabic manuscript older than his
version — he created a European bestseller and, in the process, a durable
Western fantasy of the East. Nineteenth-century English translations layered on
more. Arabic writers only began reclaiming the Nights as serious literary
inheritance in the twentieth century, once European enthusiasm had made the case
for them.

## The Nahda and What Came After

The *nahda* — the awakening, running through the nineteenth century and into the
twentieth — was a period of translation, journalism, printing and argument,
centred on Cairo and Beirut. Printing presses made periodicals possible;
periodicals made serialized fiction possible; and the novel, which had no
classical Arabic precedent, arrived as a borrowed form that had to be naturalized
fast. Jurji Zaydan wrote historical novels that taught Arab history to a mass
readership. Muhammad Husayn Haykal's *Zaynab*, a rural love story published
early in the twentieth century, is conventionally named the first Arabic novel,
though the claim depends on how you define the term. Taha Husayn, blind from
childhood and educated at al-Azhar before Paris, wrote an autobiography of his
early years that remains one of the most widely read prose works in modern
Arabic — and separately caused an enormous scandal by applying European textual
criticism to pre-Islamic poetry and suggesting much of it was later forgery.

Naguib Mahfouz spent decades writing Cairo: first historical romances, then the
dense realist sequence following one family through the first half of the
century, then allegorical and experimental work that got him denounced from
pulpits. He received the Nobel Prize in 1988, the first Arabic-language writer to
do so, and was stabbed in the neck by an assailant years later over a novel he
had published decades before.

Poetry broke its own frame around the same time. In Baghdad in the late
nineteen-forties, Nazik al-Mala'ika and Badr Shakir al-Sayyab began writing
*al-shi'r al-hurr* — free verse, which in Arabic meant not the abandonment of
metre but the abandonment of the fixed-length line, letting the poet vary how
many metrical feet each line carried. It was a modest-sounding technical change
that broke a fifteen-hundred-year-old convention and opened the way for
everything after: Adonis's mythological density, Mahmoud Darwish turning the
Palestinian predicament into a lyric idiom capable of enormous range, the
colloquial poets who abandoned the classical register altogether.

That register question has never been settled. Written Arabic remains
substantially different from any of the spoken varieties, and every novelist
faces a decision on the first page of dialogue: whether a Cairo taxi driver
speaks classical Arabic that nobody speaks, colloquial Egyptian that a Moroccan
reader will struggle with, or some negotiated middle. Tayeb Salih's *Season of
Migration to the North*, the Sudanese novel that answers *Heart of Darkness* by
sending its protagonist the other way, handles it by keeping a narrator whose
formal Arabic is itself a symptom — the voice of a man educated into a language
that has put distance between him and his village.
