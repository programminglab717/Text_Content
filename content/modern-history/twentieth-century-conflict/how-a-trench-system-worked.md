# How a Trench System Worked

A trench was never a straight line. Seen from the air it looked like the teeth of a saw: a short length of fire trench, then a thick block of earth jutting back into the parapet, then another length, and so on for miles. The blocks were traverses, and their purpose was arithmetic. A shell landing in a straight ditch sends fragments down its whole length; a shell landing in a fire bay eight or ten yards long kills the men in that bay and no others. Enfilade fire from an enemy who breaks into the line runs into the same obstruction. Almost every feature of a developed trench system was of this kind — an unglamorous, specific answer to a way of dying that somebody had already observed.

The systems that grew across France and Belgium from the autumn of 1914 were not planned. They accreted. A scrape dug by tired men under fire became a connected ditch, then a revetted one, then a line with a second line behind it and a third behind that, wire belts, tramways, buried telephone cable and a mapped network of names — troop trench, communication trench, dressing station — that sometimes covered ground the original diggers had chosen purely because it was where they stopped.

## Why the Line Formed at All

The deadlock came from a temporary imbalance between firepower and movement. By 1914 a European infantryman had a magazine rifle accurate well beyond the distance at which he could see a target; a battery of quick-firing field guns could put shrapnel over a wide area every few seconds; a water-cooled machine gun manned by a trained crew could sweep a fixed lane indefinitely. Against these things a man walking upright had no answer, and neither did a horse. Barbed wire, cheap and easy to lay, held attackers stationary exactly where the guns were registered.

Defence had thus become far cheaper than attack — but only tactically. Operationally, nobody could exploit a success, because everything that moved faster than a walking man was tied to roads and rails behind the line, and the shelling destroyed roads. When the western armies failed to outflank each other in the race north to the Channel in late 1914, there were no flanks left to turn. From Switzerland to the sea the front was continuous, and it stayed within a few dozen kilometres of that position for over three years.

## The Anatomy of the Line

A front-line trench was dug deep enough that a man of average height could walk along it without showing his head, then made deeper in effect by heaping the spoil in front as a parapet and behind as a parados — the rear bank mattering because a man silhouetted against the sky by a flare or a shellburst is a target from both directions. The floor was floored with duckboards over a drainage sump, because water is the trench's permanent enemy; in Flanders the water table sat close enough to the surface that whole sectors were built up in sandbagged breastworks above ground rather than dug down.

Along the forward face ran a firestep, a ledge cut a couple of feet up so that men could stand and shoot over the parapet. Sides were revetted with brushwood hurdles, corrugated iron, expanded metal or sandbags, because unsupported earth collapses when it gets wet and when it is shaken by shellfire, which it was constantly.

Behind the fire trench lay support and reserve lines, linked to it and to the rear by communication trenches, themselves often a mile or more long and frequently the most exhausting part of a soldier's existence, since everything — rations, ammunition, wounded, wiring stakes, hot food in insulated containers that arrived cold — travelled along them at night, in single file, in mud.

Dugouts differed sharply by army and by geology. The British Army's official view for much of the war was that deep, comfortable shelters made troops reluctant to man the parapet, so British dugouts were often shallow scrapes roofed with corrugated iron. German doctrine was the reverse: on chalk downland in Picardy the Germans sank shafts ten metres and more, with timbered stairways, bunks and electric light, from which garrisons emerged after a bombardment had passed to find attackers still crossing no man's land. That difference cost the British dearly on the Somme.

Later in the war both sides abandoned the idea of a single strongly held front line. German defence in depth spread the garrison across several kilometres of outposts, machine-gun nests in concrete pillboxes, and counter-attack divisions held far enough back to be out of range, so that an attacker who took the first trench found himself in a zone rather than through a wall.

## The Routine

Units rotated. A battalion might spend a week in the front line, a week in support, a week in reserve and a week out of the line resting, refitting and providing working parties; the proportions varied enormously by army, by sector and by the tempo of operations. Very few men spent months on end in a fire trench, though the distinction meant less than it sounds, since reserve positions were shelled too.

The day began and ended with stand-to: every man on the firestep, weapons manned, at dawn and dusk, the two periods when an attack was most likely because the light was uncertain. In between, daylight belonged to snipers and observers, and most work was done in darkness. Wiring parties crawled out to repair the belts. Patrols went into no man's land — a gap that could be several hundred metres or, in places, close enough that grenades were thrown by hand and both sides heard each other's conversations. Listening posts sat in shell holes forward of the line. Carrying parties moved up the communication trenches. Trench mortars fired, and the men in the sector being mortared cursed the mortar crews, who dropped their tubes and walked away before the retaliation arrived.

The rest was maintenance: pumping, bailing, rebuilding parapets, revetting, and the endless business of keeping a body functional in a ditch. Lice were universal and impossible to eradicate; rats fed on rations and on the dead; latrines were pits in short saps off the main trench. Prolonged immersion in cold water produced trench foot, which could progress to gangrene, and armies attacked it with the tools of discipline — compulsory foot inspections, dry socks, whale oil — with reasonable success.

## What Actually Killed People

Artillery did, for the most part. Estimates vary by army and battle, but shell and mortar fire accounted for the majority of wounds on the Western Front, a reversal of every earlier war, in which small arms and disease had dominated. Shell fragments produce filthy wounds, and the soil of northern France, heavily manured for centuries, was rich in the bacteria that cause gas gangrene and tetanus; surgical practice was forced to develop rapid debridement and antiseptic irrigation in response.

Gas arrived in its first large use at Ypres in the spring of 1915, chlorine rolling forward on the wind, and its later forms — phosgene, which killed hours after exposure, and mustard, which blistered skin and eyes and contaminated ground for days — were more effective. Gas killed a far smaller proportion of casualties than the fear it generated suggests, because respirators improved quickly, but it degraded everything: men fought, dug and carried in masks, and a gassed sector was slow and frightening ground.

Beyond any battle there was wastage — the daily, unremarkable toll of snipers, random shells, accidents and sickness in a quiet sector, which in a large army ran to a constant stream of casualties even when nothing was happening. And there was the psychological cost, which the armies initially misunderstood as cowardice or as a physical concussion of the brain and eventually came to treat, imperfectly, as what it was.

The set-piece battles then sat on top of that baseline. The first day of the Somme, in July 1916, cost the British Army close to sixty thousand casualties, around twenty thousand of them killed, in a single morning on a front of some miles. Verdun ground on for most of a year and consumed a comparable share of the French and German armies.

## Getting Out of the Ditch

The deadlock broke because the attackers solved a set of technical problems, not because anyone found a way to make men braver.

Artillery became a science. Survey and mapping let guns be laid accurately without ranging shots, so surprise returned; sound ranging and flash spotting located enemy batteries by microphone arrays and cross-bearings; meteorological data and individual gun-wear corrections allowed predicted fire. Barrages shifted from flattening everything — which merely warned the defender and churned the ground into an obstacle — to a creeping curtain moving a hundred yards at a time just ahead of the infantry, and to counter-battery programmes aimed at silencing the enemy's guns rather than his trenches.

The infantry reorganised around small teams carrying light machine guns, rifle grenades and mortars, trained to bypass strongpoints and push through gaps instead of advancing in a line. German assault detachments used these methods with startling effect in the spring offensives of 1918; the British, French, Australian and Canadian formations that broke the German line that summer combined them with tanks, ground-attack aircraft and radio-linked artillery observation into something recognisable as modern combined-arms warfare.

By then the trenches themselves were being left behind. The last hundred days of the war on the Western Front were fought in the open, across country, at a pace nobody in 1915 would have believed possible — and they were still enormously costly, which is the part of the story that the interest in tactics tends to bury.
