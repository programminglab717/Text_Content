# Why Europe Went to War in 1914

The car took a wrong turn. After a bomb thrown that morning had already failed, Franz Ferdinand's driver misunderstood a change of route, and reversed slowly on a Sarajevo corner where a nineteen-year-old Bosnian Serb named Gavrilo Princip happened to be standing. He fired twice. The heir to the Habsburg throne and his wife Sophie were dead within minutes. Five weeks later the armies of six great powers were in motion, and a continent that had not fought a general war in ninety-nine years was fighting one.

The disproportion between the trigger and the explosion is the entire problem. Political assassination was not rare in that Europe — an Austrian empress, a French president, an Italian king, an American president and a Russian tsar had all been killed within living memory, and none of those killings had produced anything remotely like a general war. Something about the structure of Europe in 1914 converted a Balkan murder into a catastrophe. Explaining that structure has occupied historians for over a century, and the arguments have never fully settled, partly because the evidence is enormous and partly because the question is not really "what happened" but "who is to blame", which is a different kind of question.

## Two Armed Camps

Otto von Bismarck built the German Empire through three short, decisive wars and then spent nearly twenty years making sure nobody could do the same to him. His system was a web of overlapping, sometimes contradictory agreements designed to keep France isolated and to keep Austria-Hungary and Russia from fighting each other over the Balkans. It required constant maintenance and a diplomat willing to lie to three partners at once, and when Wilhelm II dismissed Bismarck in 1890 and allowed the Reinsurance Treaty with Russia to lapse, the thing that had been prevented duly happened. France, which had money, and Russia, which needed it, signed a military alliance in the early 1890s. Germany now had a potential two-front war written into its geography.

Britain came in later and more ambiguously. The Entente Cordiale with France settled colonial quarrels rather than promising military support; the convention with Russia did much the same in Persia and Central Asia. But British opinion had been pushed towards France by a self-inflicted German wound: the decision, pressed by Admiral Tirpitz, to build a battle fleet capable of threatening the Royal Navy in the North Sea. Naval building was expensive, visible and easily understood by newspaper readers, and it converted a vague continental rivalry into a public contest measured in dreadnoughts. Germany could not win that race and did not abandon it, which was the worst of both outcomes: it alienated Britain without achieving naval parity.

By 1914, then, the continent had sorted itself into two groupings, one of them — the Triple Alliance — considerably less solid than it looked, since Italy's commitments were conditional and its ambitions pointed largely at Austrian territory. Alliances by themselves do not cause wars. What they do is convert local disputes into general ones, by ensuring that a quarrel between two states drags in four more.

## Timetables

The second structural problem was that the general staffs had built war plans which, once begun, were difficult to stop and dangerous to delay.

Mobilisation in 1914 meant moving millions of reservists to depots, issuing equipment, and then feeding trains onto a fixed network in a sequence worked out years in advance and rehearsed on paper. Everything depended on schedule. A Russian army that mobilised slowly across an enormous and thinly-tracked country was not necessarily a lesser threat than a German one that mobilised fast, but it meant the two sides experienced time differently. German planners were convinced that their only chance against a war on two fronts lay in beating France quickly before Russia's mass arrived, and their plan — descended from Schlieffen's, modified by the younger Moltke — required a wide sweep through neutral Belgium to get around the French fortress line. That single technical choice made British intervention far more likely, since Belgian neutrality was a treaty obligation and, more practically, a strategic interest of the first order for a naval power facing the Channel ports.

The consequence was that mobilisation became almost indistinguishable from war. When Russia ordered general mobilisation at the end of July, German planners regarded it not as a bargaining move but as the start of a clock they could not afford to let run. The soldiers had, in effect, taken a decision out of the politicians' hands years earlier by designing plans with no intermediate settings. There was no German option for mobilising against Russia alone; the machine went west first, whatever the diplomatic situation.

## The Balkans and the Dying Empires

The Balkans supplied the quarrel because the Ottoman Empire was retreating from Europe and leaving behind new states that wanted more territory than there was to distribute. Serbia had doubled in size in the Balkan Wars of 1912 and 1913 and was full of confidence and of officers who belonged to a conspiratorial network with links inside the Serbian state, including the one that armed Princip and his companions.

Austria-Hungary's fear was existential rather than territorial. A multinational empire of perhaps eleven recognised nationalities could not tolerate a successful, expanding South Slav state on its border acting as a magnet for its own Slav subjects — or so its leadership believed, having already been humiliated in the annexation crisis of 1908 and having declined to fight on several previous occasions. Vienna's chief of staff had urged a preventive war against Serbia repeatedly for years. After Sarajevo, the argument that the monarchy must act or be seen to be finished carried the day.

Russia's stake was partly Slav solidarity, mostly prestige, and specifically the Straits, through which most of its grain exports passed. Having backed down in 1908 and again during the Balkan Wars, Russian ministers believed a third retreat would end their standing as a great power. That belief — that this time we cannot be seen to give way — was held simultaneously in Vienna, St Petersburg and Berlin, and it is as close to a single cause as the crisis has.

## Thirty Days

The July crisis is best understood as a sequence of choices, each defensible in isolation and disastrous in combination. Vienna decided on war with Serbia within a week, then spent three more weeks preparing an ultimatum, which gave the impression to the rest of Europe that the crisis was fading. Berlin, asked whether it would stand behind its ally, gave the so-called blank cheque — an unconditional assurance offered in the expectation that a fast Austrian strike would localise the affair and split the entente. The ultimatum, when it arrived on 23 July, was written to be rejected; Serbia accepted almost all of it anyway, which made no difference. Austria-Hungary declared war on 28 July and shelled Belgrade.

Then the timetables took over. Russia moved first to partial, then to general mobilisation, the tsar reversing himself under pressure from ministers who told him a partial measure would wreck the plan for the general one. Germany issued ultimatums to Russia and France, declared war on Russia on 1 August and on France on 3 August, and crossed into Belgium. Britain, whose cabinet had been genuinely divided until the last days, declared war on 4 August. The last exchanges between the crowned cousins in Berlin, St Petersburg and London read like men waving at each other from separate trains already moving.

What is striking in the documents is how little any of it was chosen by publics. Crowds cheered in capitals — though the enthusiasm was more urban, more middle-class and more short-lived than the photographs suggest — but the decisions were taken by a few dozen men in a handful of rooms, several of them working from assumptions about their enemies' intentions that were simply wrong.

## Assigning Blame

Article 231 of the Versailles treaty assigned responsibility to Germany and its allies, a clause drafted mainly to establish legal liability for reparations and read in Germany as a moral verdict. The interwar decades produced a reaction: a diplomatic history of shared blame, of an accident, of a Europe that had slid into catastrophe.

In the 1960s the German historian Fritz Fischer went back to the German archives and argued that Berlin had deliberately courted a general war in pursuit of continental dominance, citing among other things the sweeping war aims drafted within weeks of the fighting starting. The resulting controversy was ferocious, and its residue is still visible: few historians now accept Germany's sole guilt in Fischer's strong form, but almost none return to the comfortable notion of a blameless slide. More recent accounts have emphasised how each government, acting on fears it considered reasonable, made choices that raised the risk for everyone, and have restored Vienna, St Petersburg and Belgrade to the centre of the story.

Both emphases can be true. A system in which the plans were rigid, the alliances automatic, the military advice unchecked and the assumption universal that a war would be short was a system that could be tipped over by a comparatively small push. That does not make the men who pushed innocent. Close to ten million soldiers died in the four years that followed, along with millions of civilians, and several of the political questions that the war was supposedly settling were still open when it ended.
