# How Europe's Democracies Fell Between the Wars

Mussolini came to power by sleeping car. While his blackshirt columns straggled towards the capital in the rain in October 1922, poorly armed and easily stopped by the regular army had anyone ordered it, he waited in Milan near the Swiss border and took the overnight train down only after the king had declined to sign a decree of martial law and had invited him to form a government. He arrived in Rome in the morning, in a bowler hat, as constitutionally appointed prime minister of a parliamentary monarchy. The March on Rome was staged afterwards.

The detail matters because it sets the pattern for most of what followed in Europe over the next fifteen years. Very few interwar dictatorships were installed by a frontal assault on the state. They were handed power, or half of it, by frightened conservatives who believed they could use the new men and dispose of them later. The violence came before, to make the country ungovernable, and after, to make the seizure permanent. The moment of transfer itself usually looked legal.

## The Men Who Came Back

The raw material was the war. Millions of men returned from four years of industrial killing into societies that had lost the habit of paying for them, and a minority of them — the minority that mattered — had found in the trenches a comradeship and a clarity they could not reproduce in civilian life. Paramilitary formations grew out of that: the Freikorps in Germany, hired by a Social Democratic government to crush the revolutionary left and then unwilling to disband; the squadristi in the Italian countryside, financed by landowners to break agricultural unions, arriving by lorry to burn a labour exchange and beat its officials; the Heimwehr in Austria; a dozen leagues and militias elsewhere.

Behind them stood the shadow of 1917. The Bolshevik revolution, the brief Soviet republics in Hungary and Bavaria, the factory occupations in northern Italy and the land seizures across central Europe convinced propertied Europeans that a communist takeover was a live and imminent prospect. It was mostly not, after about 1921. But the fear was real, and it made men who thought of themselves as respectable willing to tolerate street violence as long as it was directed leftwards, and willing later to accept a dictatorship on the understanding that the alternative was worse.

To that add the grievances of the peace settlement — Italy's "mutilated victory", Hungary's amputation, Germany's war guilt clause and lost territory — and, from 1930, mass unemployment of a kind no existing welfare arrangement could absorb.

## Italy: The Template

Fascism as a movement was assembled out of scraps: ex-socialists, futurists, war veterans, syndicalists, nationalists. Its programme mattered far less than its method, which was the organised squad, and its promise, which was national renewal through discipline and force. What Mussolini added after 1922 was patience. He governed for two years in coalition, with a parliament and a press, and it was only after his followers murdered the socialist deputy Giacomo Matteotti in 1924 — Matteotti having just catalogued fascist electoral violence from the floor of the chamber — that the regime faced a real crisis. The opposition withdrew from parliament in protest; Mussolini waited; and in January 1925 he stood up and claimed political responsibility for everything that had happened, daring anyone to act on it. Nobody did.

The dictatorship proper was built in the following two years by statute: opposition parties dissolved, a free press ended, prefects empowered, a special tribunal for political offences established, local elected mayors replaced by appointees. Mussolini's Italy was less murderous than what came later in Germany or Russia — its domestic repression ran to confinement in remote villages and island camps more often than to execution — and considerably less efficient than its own propaganda claimed. Its corporate state, presented as a third way between capitalism and socialism, was largely a facade over ordinary business interests. But it was the working demonstration that a modern European constitutional state could be converted into a single-party dictatorship from within, and it was watched closely.

## Germany: Legality as Method

The Weimar Republic was a democracy with structural weaknesses — proportional representation that fractured the chamber, a directly elected president with emergency powers under Article 48, a judiciary and officer corps that had never accepted it — and with an unusually large number of citizens who wanted it gone. Even so, it stabilised after 1924, and Hitler's party polled in low single figures in the 1928 elections, a fringe of a fringe.

What changed was the slump and, just as importantly, the collapse of parliamentary government before the Nazis were anywhere near power. From 1930 the chancellor governed by presidential decree because no majority coalition could be assembled, and each election called to resolve the deadlock made the chamber less workable. The Nazi vote climbed to nearly a fifth in 1930 and to just over a third in the summer of 1932, then fell back in November. The party was past its peak and running out of money when a circle of conservatives around the aged President Hindenburg — convinced they could box Hitler in with a cabinet of their own men — persuaded him to appoint Hitler chancellor on 30 January 1933.

The consolidation that followed took less than eighteen months and made the Italian version look leisurely. The Reichstag fire a month later produced an emergency decree suspending civil liberties, which was never rescinded; thousands of communists and socialists were arrested in the following weeks. In March a Reichstag with its communist deputies excluded or in custody, and the building surrounded by SA men, passed an Enabling Act transferring legislative power to the cabinet, with the Catholic Centre Party voting in favour and only the Social Democrats against. Trade unions were absorbed in May, all other parties banned by July, the state governments brought under central control, the civil service purged. In June 1934 Hitler had the leadership of his own paramilitary wing shot, along with assorted old enemies, which reassured the army; when Hindenburg died weeks later the offices of president and chancellor were merged and every soldier swore a personal oath of loyalty to Hitler by name.

## The Soviet Variant

Stalin's state belongs in the same decade but not the same lineage. It grew out of a revolution rather than against one, and its violence was directed first at categories rather than at opponents. Collectivisation from 1929 forced peasants into state and collective farms, deported those labelled kulaks by the hundreds of thousands, and extracted grain from the countryside at levels that produced catastrophic famine in 1932 and 1933, worst in Ukraine, Kazakhstan and the north Caucasus, killing millions. The Five-Year Plans built an industrial base at appalling human cost, much of it in camps.

Then the apparatus turned inward. The show trials of the mid-1930s destroyed the surviving Bolshevik leadership; the terror of 1937 and 1938 executed hundreds of thousands under quotas issued from the centre and sent millions more to the Gulag, and stripped the Red Army of most of its senior officers on the eve of a war. Where fascist regimes co-opted existing elites — army, church, industry, monarchy — the Soviet system destroyed and replaced its own.

## The Quieter Dictatorships

The famous regimes distract from how ordinary the collapse of parliamentary government became. Poland was taken over by Piłsudski in a military coup in 1926 and run afterwards by his colleagues. Hungary had an authoritarian regency with a parliament of restricted franchise. Portugal acquired an economics professor, Salazar, who built a clerical-corporatist state that outlasted every other regime of the era. Yugoslavia's king suspended the constitution and ruled directly; Romania's did the same later. Lithuania, Estonia and Latvia all went the same way. Austria's chancellor Dollfuss dispensed with parliament in 1933, shelled the socialist housing blocks of Vienna in a short civil war in 1934, and was murdered months later by Austrian Nazis. Greece got a dictatorship in 1936. Spain's republic, born in 1931, was attacked by a military rising in 1936 that turned into a three-year civil war, fought with German and Italian aircraft on one side and Soviet equipment and international volunteers on the other, and ended in Franco's victory.

Most of these were not fascist regimes, and the distinction is worth keeping. A conservative or military dictatorship of the Iberian, Balkan or Baltic type wanted the population quiet: politics suppressed, strikes broken, the church and the army restored to influence, the old social order frozen in place. It banned parties, censored newspapers and jailed opponents, and then largely left people alone provided they stayed out of public life. Fascism wanted the opposite — permanent mobilisation, mass rallies, youth organisations, labour fronts, a claim on private conviction as well as public behaviour. The Italian and German regimes built machinery for that claim: a controlled press and radio, uniformed youth movements that competed with families for children's time, block wardens, and a political police that depended heavily on denunciations from ordinary neighbours. Terror was part of it, but so was genuine consent, bought with employment, national revival and, in Germany, the removal of groups most people had been taught to regard as outsiders.

Japan's path had no European equivalent and is often mislabelled. There was no mass party, no charismatic dictator and no seizure of power, but a steady collapse of civilian authority as army officers acted independently overseas and assassinated politicians at home — a prime minister shot in his residence in 1932, an attempted coup in Tokyo in 1936 — until the cabinet could no longer restrain the military.

By the end of the 1930s, functioning parliamentary democracy in Europe survived in Britain and Ireland, France, the Low Countries, Switzerland, Scandinavia and Finland, and Czechoslovakia until it was dismembered. What those survivors mostly had in common was not immunity from the Depression but older parties with deep roots, an absence of serious territorial grievance, and governing elites who, when they had to choose between constitutional rule and a strongman promising order, chose differently than the men in Rome and Berlin had.
