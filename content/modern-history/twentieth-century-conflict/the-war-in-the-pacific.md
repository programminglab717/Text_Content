# The War in the Pacific

The six carriers that struck Pearl Harbor took the northern route because nobody sailed there in winter. For eleven days the force ran east under radio silence through heavy seas, refuelling from tankers in swells that swept men off the decks, and on the morning of 7 December 1941 it reached a launch point a couple of hundred miles north of Oahu without being seen. Everything about that approach — the range, the silence, the weather risk — followed from the condition that would shape the entire war beginning that morning. It was fought across distances no previous conflict had needed to reckon with, and the side that solved the problem of moving fuel, aircraft, food and ammunition across an ocean was going to win it.

## Why Japan Went South

Japan had been fighting in China since 1937, and had been detaching pieces of it since the seizure of Manchuria in 1931. The China war swallowed divisions and equipment without producing a decision, and it made Japan dependent on imports — oil, scrap steel, machine tools — most of which came from the United States. When Japanese troops moved into southern Indochina in the summer of 1941, Washington froze Japanese assets and the oil stopped.

For a navy holding perhaps two years of reserves and sitting on no domestic oilfields, the embargo turned strategy into arithmetic. Either Japan withdrew from China, which the army would not contemplate, or it took the oil of the Dutch East Indies, the rubber and tin of Malaya and the rice of Burma and Indochina. Taking them meant war with Britain and the Netherlands, and almost certainly with the United States, whose Philippine bases sat directly across the sea lanes those resources would have to travel.

What resulted was less a strategy than a wager. Cripple the American battle fleet, seize the southern resource area inside six months, fortify a defensive perimeter across the western Pacific, and hope the Americans decided that retaking it cost more than it was worth. The plan had no answer to the question of what a fully mobilised American economy could build, because there was no answer that favoured Japan.

The raid itself was a tactical success that solved nothing. Battleships were sunk or wrecked in the shallow mud of the harbour, but the American carriers were at sea, and the attackers never touched the fuel farms or the repair yards — the two things on Oahu that could not have been replaced quickly. Within two years most of the sunken battleships were back in service, refloated and modernised, while the harbour that would have taken years to rebuild went on working through the entire war.

## A Hundred Days

The offensive that followed remains one of the fastest conquests in modern history. Within hours of Pearl Harbor, Japanese forces were landing in Malaya and bombing Hong Kong, Guam, Wake and the Philippines. Two days later, British aircraft flying from Indochina sank the battleship *Prince of Wales* and the battlecruiser *Repulse* at sea and under way — the plainest demonstration yet that a capital ship without air cover was simply a large target.

Down the Malayan peninsula, Japanese infantry with bicycles and light tanks repeatedly outflanked defenders who expected an attack along the roads, and Singapore surrendered on 15 February 1942 in the largest capitulation in British military history. The Dutch East Indies fell by March. In the Philippines, American and Filipino forces held the Bataan peninsula until April and the fortress island of Corregidor until May; the prisoners taken on Bataan were marched north in heat without water or medical care, and thousands died on the road. British forces retreated out of Burma into India in a monsoon, and the Japanese perimeter came to run from the Aleutians through the Marianas and the Solomons to the frontier of Assam.

For the peoples inside that perimeter, the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere meant requisition of rice, conscription of labour, and the collapse of the shipping that had fed rice-importing regions. Northern Vietnam suffered a catastrophic famine in the winter of 1944–45 in which something close to a million people died, a disaster produced by a combination of Japanese and French requisitioning, wartime transport failure and bad harvests.

## Carriers, and the Battles Nobody Could See

In May 1942, in the Coral Sea, two fleets fought for two days without ever coming within sight of one another. It was the first naval battle in history decided entirely by aircraft launched beyond the horizon, and it cost the Japanese a fleet carrier damaged badly enough to miss what came next.

What came next was Midway, in June, where American codebreakers had reconstructed enough of the Japanese operational plan to let three carriers wait in ambush. Four Japanese fleet carriers were sunk in a single day. The loss of the ships mattered; the loss of the aircrew and, more slowly, of the maintenance crews and the training pipeline that had produced them mattered more.

Midway is often described as the turning point, which flatters a single day at the expense of a longer and uglier campaign. The fighting that actually broke Japanese offensive capacity was Guadalcanal, six months of attrition on an island neither side had planned to contest, where the Marines held an airfield, the two navies fought a series of savage night actions in the strait the sailors named Ironbottom Sound, and both sides fed aircraft and pilots into a grinder. The Americans could replace what they lost. The Japanese could not, and after February 1943 they never again mounted a sustained offensive.

## Two Axes and a Submarine Campaign

The Allied counteroffensive advanced along two lines that were never fully reconciled: MacArthur's forces working up the north coast of New Guinea toward the Philippines, and Nimitz's fleet driving west across the central Pacific through the Gilberts, Marshalls and Marianas. The governing idea was to take only what was needed. Fortified bases like Rabaul and Truk were bombed, blockaded and bypassed, their garrisons left to farm gardens and starve until the surrender.

Where landings were made, they were appalling. At Tarawa in November 1943 the reef stopped the landing craft short and Marines waded several hundred yards under fire; roughly a thousand of them died taking an atoll in three days. The Marianas, seized the following summer, gave the Americans airfields from which B-29s could reach the Japanese home islands.

None of this would have been possible without an unglamorous innovation: the fleet train. Oilers, ammunition ships, repair vessels, floating drydocks and refrigerated stores let an American fleet remain on station for months at a time, refuelling and rearming at sea, hundreds of miles from any base. It was arguably the single greatest Allied technical achievement of the Pacific war.

Meanwhile American submarines were quietly deciding the outcome. For roughly the first eighteen months they were crippled by the Mark 14 torpedo, which ran too deep and whose contact exploder frequently failed, a defect the ordnance bureau denied for an embarrassingly long time. Once the torpedoes worked, the boats destroyed the Japanese merchant marine. Japan had entered the war with too few escorts, no convoy doctrine worth the name and no capacity to replace lost hulls. By 1945 oil imports had effectively ceased, pilots were being sent into combat with a fraction of the flying hours their predecessors had, and major warships sat in harbour for lack of fuel. It was also, in its legal logic, unrestricted warfare against merchant shipping — the offence for which German submarine commanders were arraigned at Nuremberg.

## The Last Year

At Leyte Gulf in October 1944, in the largest naval engagement ever fought, the remains of the Japanese fleet were spent in a complicated plan to disrupt the landings. It was there that organised kamikaze attacks began: aircraft flown deliberately into ships by pilots with minimal training, a tactic that reflected the exhaustion of everything else.

Then Iwo Jima, and then Okinawa, eighty-odd days of fighting on an island with a substantial civilian population. Something like a quarter of Okinawa's people died, killed by bombardment, caught between the armies, or pressed into mass suicide. Planners in Washington read the casualty figures as a preview of what invading the home islands would cost.

In the same months the air campaign changed character. Stripped of guns and flying low at night with incendiaries, B-29s burned Tokyo on 9–10 March 1945 in the deadliest air raid ever conducted; something on the order of a hundred thousand people died in a few hours in a city of wood and paper, and dozens of other cities followed through the spring and summer. Hiroshima was destroyed on 6 August and Nagasaki on 9 August. Between them, on 8 August, the Soviet Union declared war and swept into Manchuria, destroying the Kwantung Army in a matter of days and removing the last Japanese hope of a mediated peace. The surrender was announced on 15 August. Historians still argue about the relative weight of the bombs, the Soviet entry and the blockade in producing it, and the argument is not frivolous on either side.

## The Accounting

The great majority of the dead were Asian civilians, and most of them were Chinese: estimates for China alone generally run between fifteen and twenty million. Japan lost some three million people, most of them in the last year. Allied prisoners of the Japanese died at roughly a quarter — a rate many times that of prisoners held in Germany — and the forced labourers moved around the empire, including the women coerced into military brothels and the subjects of the biological warfare programme in Manchuria, were never counted at all.

And the settlements the war produced set up the next three decades of fighting. Korea was divided along a line drawn in a night by two American officers with a map. The Chinese civil war resumed almost immediately. The French came back to Indochina and the Dutch to Indonesia, into countries that had watched European rule collapse in a matter of weeks in 1942 and had no intention of accepting it again.
