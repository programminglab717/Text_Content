# How the Interwar Economy Came Apart

In May 1931 the Creditanstalt, the largest bank in Austria and an institution old enough and grand enough that a Rothschild sat at the head of it, admitted that it had lost most of its capital. Vienna was a provincial capital by then, the financial centre of an empire that no longer existed, and a reasonable observer might have expected the failure to stay local. It did not. Depositors pulled money out of Austria, then out of Hungary, then out of Germany, where a large Berlin bank collapsed in July and the government closed the banks and froze foreign balances. Sterling came under pressure next, and in September Britain — which had spent six years and a great deal of domestic misery getting back onto the gold standard — went off it.

That chain is the interwar economy in miniature: a shock in one country propagating through a set of institutions built to hold currencies fixed, and doing far more damage in transit than at the source. The Depression was not a single event in New York in October 1929. It was a decade-long failure to rebuild an international monetary order after a war that had broken the old one.

## The Debts the War Left

Governments had financed the First World War the way governments always finance wars: by borrowing at home, by printing, and by borrowing abroad. Nobody paid for it out of taxation, and the price levels of every combatant rose sharply. Britain and France borrowed from the United States on a scale that converted America, in four years, from a net debtor into the world's great creditor. The gold that had circulated through the pre-war system flowed heavily towards the American Treasury and stayed there.

Onto that structure the peace settlement laid reparations. The Versailles treaty required Germany to pay without initially naming a sum; a commission fixed it in 1921 at a figure that Germans universally regarded as impossible and that outside economists, John Maynard Keynes most famously, argued was both unpayable and self-defeating. The narrower technical objection has held up well. A country can only pay a foreign debt by running an export surplus, and the same victorious powers demanding payment were raising tariffs against German goods. Meanwhile France and Britain owed the Americans, and made it plain that their willingness to be generous about German reparations depended on American willingness to be generous about inter-Allied debts, which was nil. Washington's position, stated more bluntly by Calvin Coolidge than by anyone else, was that the money had been borrowed and should be repaid.

## The German Inflation and Its Residue

German inflation began during the war and accelerated afterwards, and in 1923 it became something with almost no precedent. French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr in January of that year to extract payment in kind after a default on coal and timber deliveries; the German government financed a campaign of passive resistance by the Ruhr workforce with the printing press, and the mark disintegrated. Prices doubled in days, then in hours. Wages were paid twice daily and spent immediately. The currency finally stabilised in November with the introduction of a new mark, nominally backed by a mortgage on German land, and a reformed Reichsbank that refused to discount government paper.

The social residue mattered more than the monetary one. Anyone holding cash, war bonds, savings accounts, insurance policies or fixed rents was wiped out — which meant, in practice, the urban professional and rentier classes, exactly the people whose loyalty a parliamentary republic most needed. Debtors, farmers with mortgages and industrialists who had borrowed to buy real assets did well. The lesson widely drawn in Germany was that inflation was the supreme economic evil, a conviction that would make the deflationary policy of the early 1930s politically easier to defend than it deserved to be.

The stabilisation was followed by five reasonably prosperous years, financed to a remarkable degree by American money. Under the Dawes arrangement of 1924 the reparations schedule was eased and a foreign loan floated; thereafter American investors bought German municipal and corporate bonds enthusiastically. The circuit ran from New York to Germany, from Germany to Britain and France as reparations, and from them back to the United States as debt service. It worked as long as the first leg kept flowing. It was short-term lending against long-term obligations, and it could be reversed at speed.

## Gold, Restored and Overvalued

The consensus of the 1920s held that the route back to normality lay through the pre-war gold standard, and country after country returned to gold between 1925 and 1928. Britain went back at the pre-war parity, a decision taken by Churchill as Chancellor on the advice of the Treasury and the Bank of England and against Keynes's loud objection. The old parity overvalued sterling against the dollar by roughly ten per cent, which is a lot when your exports are coal, cotton textiles, ships and steel and your competitors are cheaper. Restoring competitiveness then required cutting costs — meaning wages — which produced a bitter coal dispute and the General Strike of 1926, and left British unemployment stubbornly high through a decade that was booming elsewhere.

The restored system had a deeper flaw. It looked like the pre-war gold standard but lacked its machinery. Gold was concentrated in the United States and France rather than distributed; the Bank of England no longer had the reserves to act as the system's manager; and countries that accumulated gold sterilised the inflows rather than letting prices rise, so all the adjustment burden fell on the deficit countries, in the form of deflation. Under a fixed exchange rate, a country losing gold must raise interest rates and squeeze domestic demand whatever else is happening to its economy. That constraint is the single most important fact about the years after 1929.

## The Break

Agricultural prices had been sagging through the late 1920s as wartime expansion in the Americas and Australia met recovering European harvests; farmers in Kansas and Saskatchewan and the Argentine pampas were already in difficulty while stock prices climbed. American lending to Germany dried up in 1928, partly drawn home by the boom on Wall Street, which meant the whole circuit was already under strain when the New York market broke in October 1929.

The crash destroyed wealth and confidence, but it was what followed that turned a recession into the Depression. American banks failed in waves through 1930 and 1931, each wave destroying deposits and contracting the money supply, and the Federal Reserve — divided, wedded to a doctrine that credit should only be supplied against real commercial bills, and worried about the gold standard — let it happen. The Smoot-Hawley tariff of 1930 raised American duties sharply and invited retaliation; world trade shrank by roughly two thirds in value in three years, a collapse that had no precedent and that pushed every economy inward. Then came the European banking crisis of 1931, and the end of sterling's convertibility, and the fragmentation of the world into a sterling bloc, a dollar bloc, a French-led gold bloc and a Central European zone of exchange controls and barter deals.

The human arithmetic was brutal. Roughly a quarter of the American workforce was out of work at the trough; German unemployment reached about six million in the winter of 1932-33, in an economy where insurance benefits ran out long before the jobs came back. Deflation made debts heavier in real terms even as incomes fell, so farmers lost land they could have kept at stable prices and firms that were solvent on paper could not service loans. In the American plains, drought and the exhaustion of soil that had been ploughed too hard during the wartime boom added dust storms to the price collapse and drove tenant families west.

## Who Got Out First

The clearest pattern in the recovery is that countries which abandoned gold early recovered early. Britain and the sterling area left in 1931, cut interest rates, and saw output turn up from 1932, led by housebuilding. The United States devalued the dollar in 1933 and, with banking reform and deposit insurance, stopped the bank runs; recovery was rapid until a premature tightening produced a sharp relapse in 1937. The gold bloc countries — France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Poland — held on, kept deflating, and had the worst of the 1930s, with France still below its 1929 output level as the decade ended.

Germany's route was different again. It kept the nominal gold parity of the mark but made it meaningless with exchange controls, then reflated through public works, road building and, increasingly and then overwhelmingly, rearmament, financed by concealed deficits and by a rigged clearing system that forced trading partners in southeastern Europe to take German manufactures in exchange for their grain and oil. It produced full employment, a severe shortage of foreign exchange, and an economy structurally oriented towards war.

What the whole decade demonstrated, in a way that reshaped economic policy for a generation, was that a fixed exchange-rate system with no lender of last resort and no mechanism to force surplus countries to adjust will transmit a shock everywhere and let it run. The men who met at Bretton Woods in 1944 had all watched it happen, and most of the architecture they built was aimed at that memory.
