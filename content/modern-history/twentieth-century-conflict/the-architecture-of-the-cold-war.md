# The Architecture of the Cold War

The line that divided Europe for more than forty years was not designed. It was drawn by where the armies happened to stop in the spring of 1945, ratified afterwards by conferences that could not have moved it much in any case, and then hardened into fences, watchtowers and minefields. In central Germany, soldiers of the two most powerful military alliances in history stood a few hundred metres apart for two generations, photographed each other, listened to each other's radio traffic, and never fought.

That is the strange fact the whole period turns on. Two states with the means to destroy each other several times over spent four decades in confrontation without a direct war. Understanding how that happened means looking at the machinery — the alliances, the doctrines, the weapons and the places where the violence went instead.

## Where the Line Came From

The wartime alliance was a marriage of convenience and everybody knew it. Stalin's demand was for a belt of friendly states between Germany and the Soviet border, and the meaning of friendly hardened steadily: coalition governments, then coalition governments with communist interior ministries, then single-party states. Between 1945 and 1948 the transformation was completed across eastern Europe, ending with a coup in Czechoslovakia, the one country in the region with a genuine democratic tradition and a genuine popular communist vote.

The American response was assembled in the same period and with similar improvisation. Aid to Greece and Turkey in 1947 came wrapped in a declaration that the United States would support free peoples resisting subjugation, which committed it to a great deal more than Greece. The Marshall Plan offered reconstruction money to all of Europe, including the Soviet sphere, on terms — economic openness, shared accounting — that Moscow was never going to accept, and its refusal on behalf of its clients confirmed the division.

Berlin made the confrontation physical. The city sat deep inside the Soviet zone and was itself divided into sectors, an arrangement that only worked while the occupiers cooperated. When the Western powers moved toward a separate West German currency in 1948, Soviet forces closed the land routes, and the Western allies supplied two million people by air for nearly a year rather than either abandon the city or force the roads. The blockade failed, and it produced NATO, two German states, and a habit of treating Berlin as the place where the line would be tested.

Then, in quick succession, the Soviet Union tested an atomic bomb, the Chinese Communists won their civil war, and North Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel. Korea converted a European political struggle into a global military one. American defence spending multiplied, NATO acquired an integrated command and permanent forces, West Germany was rearmed, and the Soviet bloc answered with the Warsaw Pact.

## Deterrence as a Machine

Nuclear strategy in the 1950s rested on the promise of massive retaliation: any serious Soviet aggression would be answered with strategic nuclear attack. The doctrine was cheap, which was its attraction, and it became less credible every year as Soviet delivery capability grew. A threat to commit suicide over a border incident in a country nobody could find on a map does not deter much.

What replaced it was flexible response — a ladder of options from conventional defence up through battlefield nuclear weapons to strategic exchange — and, underneath the doctrine, a technical condition that mattered more than any of it. Each side worked to guarantee that enough of its arsenal would survive a first strike to devastate the attacker in reply. Missiles went into hardened silos. Bombers stood on alert. Above all, submarines carrying ballistic missiles went to sea, where they could not be found and therefore could not be destroyed pre-emptively.

Assured second-strike capability is what made the standoff stable, and it produced a genuinely counterintuitive conclusion that both sides eventually accepted in treaty form: defending your population against missiles is destabilising, because a side that believes it can survive a retaliation might be tempted to go first. The anti-ballistic missile treaty of 1972 was an agreement to remain mutually vulnerable on purpose.

Stability of that kind is not the same as safety, and the record of near-misses is long. In October 1962, with American ships dropping practice depth charges on a Soviet submarine near the Cuban quarantine line, the crew — out of radio contact, and carrying a nuclear-armed torpedo — debated using it, and one officer's refusal to consent was what prevented it. The Americans did not know that Soviet forces on Cuba already had tactical nuclear weapons. Two decades later, a Soviet duty officer at an early-warning centre decided that a satellite report of incoming American missiles was a malfunction, and reported it as such. It was sunlight on high cloud. In the same period, a NATO command exercise realistic enough to alarm Soviet intelligence coincided with a stretch of unusually bad relations and was read in Moscow, briefly, as possible preparation for a real attack.

## Two Blocs, Policed Differently

Both alliances disciplined their members, and the asymmetry in how is one of the clearest structural facts of the period.

The Soviet bloc was policed internally, by force, on the territory of its own allies. Strikes in East Germany in 1953 were put down by Soviet tanks. The Hungarian revolution of 1956 was crushed by a full-scale invasion that killed thousands and drove hundreds of thousands into exile. When the Czechoslovak leadership attempted a reform programme in 1968, Warsaw Pact troops occupied the country, and the justification was elevated into doctrine: socialist states had a limited sovereignty, and the bloc would intervene where socialism was threatened. When an independent trade union movement gathered ten million members in Poland in 1980, martial law imposed by Poland's own government pre-empted the same intervention.

The Western alliance had exits, and members used them. France pulled out of NATO's integrated command and stayed in the alliance; the United States had to argue its allies into policies rather than order them. But the coercion did not disappear, it relocated. It operated in countries outside the alliance and particularly in the Western hemisphere and the Middle East, through covert action against governments judged unreliable: the overthrow of an Iranian prime minister who had nationalised the oil industry, of a Guatemalan president who had expropriated plantation land, of a Chilean government elected on a socialist programme. NATO membership was also compatible, for years at a time, with dictatorship in Portugal, Greece and Turkey.

## Where It Was Actually Fought

Calling the period cold describes Europe and nowhere else. The superpowers avoided fighting each other and fought everywhere else through clients, and the dead are counted in the millions.

Korea killed on the order of two to three million people, most of them Korean civilians, and left the peninsula divided on roughly the line it started from. The wars in Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia killed several million more and ended with a regional catastrophe in Cambodia that was itself a product of the wider war. In Angola, independence in 1975 turned immediately into a civil war in which Cuban troops fought South African ones and both superpowers armed their clients for fifteen years. The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979 produced a decade of war that killed perhaps a million Afghans, displaced millions more and armed a generation of fighters whose careers did not end when the Soviets left. In Central America, insurgencies and the counterinsurgencies mounted against them destroyed towns and killed civilians on a scale later documented by truth commissions.

The pattern is consistent enough to be worth stating plainly: the nuclear balance that kept Europe quiet displaced conflict onto countries that had not chosen the quarrel, where proxy war was cheap for the sponsors and ruinous for everyone else.

## The Contest of Economies

The confrontation was also a spending competition, and it deformed both economies, unequally.

In the United States the permanent arms industry that emerged was substantial enough for an outgoing president who had commanded the Normandy invasion to warn the country about its political influence. It also funded a great deal of science: the space programme, the early internet, semiconductors, materials research, university expansion, all pulled forward by the argument that the Soviets might get there first.

The Soviet Union carried a far heavier burden on a far smaller base, plausibly three or four times the American share of national output, and did so with an economy structurally unable to convert military excellence into civilian prosperity. Soviet industry was good at producing more of an established thing and bad at producing a new thing, a weakness that mattered less in tanks than in computing. Agriculture never recovered from collectivisation, and by the 1970s a superpower was buying grain from its adversary. High oil prices after 1973 concealed the stagnation for a decade; when prices fell in the mid-1980s, the concealment went with them.

## How It Stopped

What happened at the end was not a collapse under military pressure but a decision. Gorbachev came to power in 1985 convinced that the system needed reform and that the arms race and the Afghan war were unaffordable. He withdrew from Afghanistan, signed a treaty eliminating an entire class of missiles, argued at a summit in Iceland about abolishing nuclear weapons altogether, and told the eastern European leaderships that Soviet troops would not be coming to save them.

That last message is the one that matters. The revolutions of 1989 succeeded because the guarantee of intervention had been withdrawn, and in almost every case they were extraordinarily peaceful — round tables, mass demonstrations, a border guard at a Berlin crossing deciding on his own initiative to open the gate. Romania was the bloody exception. Within two years Germany was reunified and the Soviet Union had dissolved into fifteen states, following an attempted coup by men trying to preserve it.

The structure went away. The weapons it was built around did not; they were counted, divided among successor states, partially withdrawn and partially retained, and they are still there. Measured against its purpose, the whole apparatus of deterrence succeeded, and the success is visible only as an absence — a war that did not happen. It is worth holding both halves of that thought at once, because the record shows how narrowly it held, and how much of the holding came down to individual people deciding, under pressure and without instructions, not to.
