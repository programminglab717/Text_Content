# Life on the Home Front

The first thing the war did to British civilians was paint their kerbstones white. Headlights were masked down to a slit, street lamps went out, shop windows were shuttered at dusk, and torches had to be pointed at the pavement through a layer of tissue paper. In the first winter of the war, before a single bomb fell on a British city, more people were being killed on the roads in the dark than by any enemy action. That is the shape of the home front in miniature: not a grand sacrifice but a thousand small alterations to ordinary life, most of them administrative, some of them lethal, all of them arriving without much warning and staying for years.

## The Arithmetic of the Ration Book

Rationing is usually remembered as scarcity, but its purpose was distribution. Wartime Britain imported a large share of its food across an ocean patrolled by submarines, and the state's problem was not merely that there was less but that a market left alone would allocate what remained by price. So the government issued ration books and set entitlements: bacon, butter and sugar first, then meat by price rather than weight, then cheese, tea, jam, eggs and fats. Tinned and packaged goods went onto a points system that let households choose, which mattered more psychologically than it sounds. Clothing was rationed by coupon, and a standardised Utility range set the amount of cloth and the number of buttons a garment could use. Bread, oddly, was never rationed during the war itself — that came afterwards, in 1946, when the situation in liberated Europe was worse than anything Britain had faced.

The result was strange. Overall consumption fell and the food was monotonous, but the diet of the poorest households improved. Nutritionists had spent the 1930s documenting deficiency diseases in industrial cities; wartime allocation, subsidised prices, milk and cod liver oil for children and expectant mothers, and communal feeding in schools and factory canteens narrowed the gap between the best-fed and the worst-fed in Britain more sharply than any peacetime policy had. People planted potatoes and cabbages on tennis courts, railway embankments and bomb sites, kept hens in back gardens, and joined pig clubs. Every scrap of this was propagandised, but the vegetables were real.

Elsewhere the arithmetic was the opposite. Germany kept its own civilian rations tolerable deep into the war by taking food out of occupied Europe, and did so according to an explicit racial hierarchy of entitlement. The result was famine imposed on others: in Greece in the winter of 1941–42, in besieged Leningrad, where the siege lasted nearly nine hundred days and something close to a million civilians died, and in the western Netherlands in the last winter of the war, where transport was cut and tens of thousands starved in a country with a modern food system. Soviet prisoners of war died in German captivity in millions, largely by deliberate starvation.

In Bengal in 1943, several million people died in a famine that owed nothing to bombing and everything to war administration: rice imports from Japanese-occupied Burma cut off, shipping diverted to military use, provinces blocking grain exports to one another, a policy of removing boats and stocks from coastal districts to deny them to an invader, and a government that would not use the word famine or admit the scale in time.

## Who Made the Things

The characteristic image of the home front is a woman at a lathe, and it is accurate as far as it goes. Britain went furthest, becoming in late 1941 the first country to conscript women, initially single women in their twenties, directing them into the auxiliary services, the Land Army, transport, and above all the filling factories and machine shops. American women moved into shipyards and aircraft plants in enormous numbers and were told, with equal enthusiasm, to leave them again in 1945. In the Soviet Union women became the majority of the industrial workforce and served in combat as well: as snipers, as anti-aircraft gunners, and in aviation regiments flying obsolete biplanes on night harassment raids.

Germany is the instructive exception. Nazi ideology had spent a decade insisting that a woman's place was domestic, and the regime remained reluctant to conscript German women into factories even as its manpower situation deteriorated. It filled the gap instead with foreign labour: millions of workers deported from occupied Europe, prisoners of war put to work in violation of every convention, and eventually concentration camp inmates worked to death in underground assembly plants. A German factory in 1944 was often a place where a shrinking core of German workers supervised a much larger body of people who had been brought there by force.

The industrial story of the war also includes one feat that deserves to be better known. When German armies overran the western Soviet Union in 1941, Soviet authorities dismantled well over a thousand major factories, loaded them onto trains with their workers, and rebuilt them beyond the Volga and the Urals, in some cases producing again within months under canvas and in the open air in winter. Simultaneously the Americans were learning to build merchant ships by welding prefabricated sections together, cutting construction times from many months to a few weeks, and turning out hulls faster than submarines could sink them.

## Under the Bombs

Air raid precautions had been planned for years on the assumption that bombing would produce mass panic and psychiatric collapse. It did not, though the expectation shaped a great deal of policy. What it produced instead was exhaustion and a grinding, improvised domesticity: sleeping in a corrugated steel Anderson shelter half-buried in the garden, or under a steel table in the front room, or on the platform of a Tube station the authorities had initially tried to keep people out of and then equipped with bunks and canteens. Deaths came from the raids and also from the crush and the chaos around them; one of the worst single incidents in London was a stairway crush at a shelter entrance in which nearly two hundred people died and no bomb was involved.

At the start of the war around a million and a half British children, mothers with infants and teachers were evacuated from cities in a few days, shipped to rural billets chosen by little more than a show of hands in a village hall. Many drifted home during the quiet months and were evacuated again later. The programme exposed urban poverty to rural households in a way that embarrassed the country, and the resulting arguments fed directly into postwar welfare policy.

German and Japanese civilians experienced the same war without the consolation of winning it. Hamburg burned in a firestorm in 1943 that killed around forty thousand people; Dresden, whose death toll was inflated for decades by propaganda on several sides, is now put by careful reconstruction at something like twenty-five thousand. Japanese cities, built largely of wood, were evacuated of schoolchildren to the countryside in 1944, and organised into neighbourhood associations responsible for rationing, fire watching and mutual surveillance.

## The Powers the State Took

Total war is an administrative condition as much as a military one. Governments acquired, generally by emergency legislation passed quickly and renewed indefinitely, the power to direct labour, requisition property, fix prices, censor mail, detain without trial and tell people where to live. Britain sent conscripts down coal mines by ballot. Censors opened letters and reported systematically on what the population was saying. Mass Observation, a private social research project, recorded the same thing from the inside, and its files remain one of the best records of ordinary opinion in any period.

Some of those powers were used against minorities in ways that later required apology. The United States removed well over a hundred thousand people of Japanese ancestry, most of them American citizens, from the West Coast and held them in camps for the duration on no individual evidence of disloyalty. Britain interned tens of thousands of enemy aliens in 1940, including large numbers of Jewish refugees who had fled the very regime they were now suspected of serving, and shipped some overseas; one such ship was torpedoed with heavy loss of life.

Everywhere, controls created their own shadow economy. In Britain the black market was a nuisance and a joke, embodied in the figure of the spiv with a suitcase. In occupied Europe it was survival, and the distinction between illegal trading and resistance was often invisible.

## What It Left

The machinery built to run a war turned out to be a template. A state that could ration food, direct labour, evacuate children and run mass medical inspections was visibly capable of doing other things, and populations that had been asked for so much began to expect something in return. When a dry civil servant's report on social insurance was published in Britain at the end of 1942, it sold in numbers usually reserved for popular fiction, because it promised that the postwar country would not simply revert. The postwar settlement in Britain — a national health service, family allowances, secondary education for all — was drafted, argued over and largely legislated by people who had spent the war administering scarcity. In the United States, the veterans' legislation of 1944 sent a generation to college and underwrote a housing boom, and the movement of Black workers into northern war industries, along with the promise of fair employment extracted from the federal government under threat of a protest march, laid groundwork that would be built on for the next twenty years.

The kerbstones were repainted and the blackout curtains came down. The ration books stayed, in Britain, until the middle of the 1950s.
