# How the European Empires Came Apart

The ceremonies were almost always at midnight. A flag comes down a pole, another goes up, a band plays a new anthem, a member of a royal family or a departing governor reads out a message of goodwill, and a territory that had been administered from Europe becomes a state. Photographs of those nights exist for dozens of countries, and they all look much the same: floodlights, a parade ground, a crowd, rain in some of them.

The tidiness is misleading. Behind the ceremonies lie a set of processes that were frequently violent, almost always rushed, and rarely under the control of the people signing the documents. Between the middle of the 1940s and the middle of the 1970s, European rule over most of Asia and Africa ended. In some places it ended in negotiation. In others it took a war of a decade or more, and in a few the war outlasted the empire that started it.

## The Spell That Broke in 1942

European authority in Asia rested partly on force and considerably on prestige — the assumption, carefully maintained, that these people could not be beaten. In the first months of 1942 the Japanese army beat them comprehensively, in public, in front of the populations they governed. Hong Kong, Malaya, Singapore, the Dutch East Indies, Burma and the Philippines fell in weeks. Whatever the occupation that followed turned out to be like, and it was frequently worse than what it replaced, the demonstration could not be withdrawn.

The war also put enormous numbers of colonial subjects into uniform. India raised the largest volunteer army in history, some two and a half million men, and fought with it in North Africa, Italy and Burma. West and East African divisions campaigned in the Burmese jungle. French colonial regiments from West and North Africa did much of the hard fighting for the Free French, and were then quietly withdrawn and replaced by white troops before the liberation of Paris, for reasons of political appearance. Men who had been told they were fighting for freedom against racial tyranny came home to administrations that had no intention of extending the argument to them. In Senegal, returning prisoners of war protesting over pay were fired on by French troops. In eastern Algeria, demonstrations on the day Germany surrendered were met with a repression that killed thousands and is still argued over.

The Atlantic Charter had promised, in 1941, to respect the right of all peoples to choose their own government. Churchill stated flatly that this did not apply to the British Empire. Nationalist leaders from Accra to Hanoi had read the document and drawn the opposite conclusion, and they quoted it back for the next twenty years.

## India, and What Independence Cost

India was the hinge. The Congress movement, having been offered postwar dominion status and found it inadequate, launched the Quit India campaign in 1942; the entire leadership spent the rest of the war in prison. The famine in Bengal the following year destroyed whatever remained of the claim that British administration was at least competent. When the war ended, the new Labour government in London faced an India it could no longer afford to garrison, an Indian Army whose loyalty it had stopped taking for granted after mutinies in the navy and unrest over the trials of soldiers who had defected to a Japanese-sponsored Indian force, and a Treasury in debt to India rather than the other way round.

The withdrawal was therefore both a decision and a retreat, and it was conducted at speed. The date was brought forward by nearly a year. A British lawyer who had never set foot in India was given about five weeks to draw the boundary between India and Pakistan through Punjab and Bengal, and the lines were not published until after the handover. What followed was one of the largest and fastest movements of people in history — ten to fifteen million crossing in both directions — accompanied by massacres, abductions and the destruction of communities that had been mixed for centuries. The dead are usually estimated in the hundreds of thousands and may have approached a million.

The first great act of decolonization was also its first great catastrophe, and the lesson drawn in London was ambiguous: independence was inevitable, but haste was expensive. The lesson was not consistently applied.

## Where the Settlers Were

A pattern is visible across the whole period, and it is not subtle. Where Europeans governed but did not settle in large numbers, power was usually transferred after negotiation, strikes, riots and imprisonments, but without prolonged war. Where a substantial settler population had made its home and owned the best land, decolonization meant a long and vicious conflict, because the metropolitan government was not merely giving up an administration but expropriating its own citizens.

The Dutch tried to return to Indonesia in 1945 and found a republic already declared. Two large military campaigns, euphemistically called police actions, achieved control of the cities and none of the countryside, and the Netherlands gave way at the end of 1949 under heavy American pressure. France tried the same in Indochina, where a declaration of independence in 1945 had quoted the American Declaration almost verbatim; the war that began in 1946 ended in 1954 with a French garrison surrounded in a valley at Dien Bien Phu by an army that had carried its artillery up the surrounding hills by hand.

Algeria was the extreme case, because France had declared it not a colony but part of France, and roughly a million people of European origin lived there. The insurrection that began in 1954 produced eight years of war in which the French army made systematic use of torture, cleared populations into camps, and won militarily in Algiers while losing politically everywhere else. It brought down the Fourth Republic, brought de Gaulle back to power, and ended in a settler exodus and the abandonment of the Algerian auxiliaries who had fought for France, many of whom were killed after the French left. The total dead have never been agreed; the range of serious estimates is wide and the low end is still enormous.

In Kenya, where white farmers held the highlands, the response to the Mau Mau revolt in the 1950s involved detention without trial on a mass scale, forced resettlement of most of the Kikuyu population into guarded villages, and systematic brutality in the camps that the British government eventually acknowledged decades later in an out-of-court settlement with elderly survivors. Portugal, under a dictatorship that refused to concede the principle at all, fought three colonial wars simultaneously in Angola, Guinea-Bissau and Mozambique from the early 1960s until its own army overthrew the regime in 1974 — the only case in which the colonies effectively brought down the metropolis. White Rhodesia declared its own independence rather than accept majority rule and fought until 1979.

## Suez, Bandung, and the Rush

Two events in the middle of the 1950s changed the tempo. At Bandung in 1955, delegations from twenty-nine Asian and African states met without a European power in the room, and the idea of a bloc that belonged to neither Washington nor Moscow acquired an address. The following year, Britain and France, in secret coordination with Israel, invaded Egypt after Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal, and were stopped not by Egyptian arms but by a run on sterling and an American refusal to help. The demonstration that the old imperial powers could no longer act independently of the United States was absorbed quickly in London and slowly in Paris.

After that the pace became extraordinary. Ghana became independent in 1957, the first sub-Saharan African colony to do so; in 1960 alone seventeen African countries followed, and a British prime minister told the South African parliament that a wind of change was blowing through the continent whether anyone liked it or not. In much of French and British Africa the transfers were negotiated in a few years by officials who a decade earlier had been planning for generations of tutelage.

Independence also arrived inside a Cold War that supplied both patrons and traps. Movements that could not obtain weapons from the West obtained them from the Soviet Union or China, and were then classified in Washington as communist, which occasionally made it true. Where a nationalist government nationalised foreign assets, it risked covert action. The Congo became independent in 1960 and within months had lost its richest province to a secession backed by Belgian mining interests, seen its prime minister deposed and murdered with foreign complicity, and begun a slide into three decades of dictatorship.

## The Inheritance

What the new states inherited was specific and constraining. The borders were the administrative lines of the empires, drawn for European convenience and frequently cutting through peoples; the Organisation of African Unity chose early to freeze them anyway, on the reasonable ground that reopening them would mean endless war. The economies had been built to export one or two commodities to the metropolis, with railways running from mine to port and almost never from one colony to another. The senior civil service and the officer corps had been staffed by Europeans nearly to the end, so that some countries began with a handful of university graduates. The instruments of government that had been most fully developed were the ones designed for control: emergency powers, detention laws, paramilitary police.

It is too easy to run the argument in either direction. Colonial rule did not determine every subsequent failure, and treating post-independence leaders as objects rather than agents does them no honour. But the starting conditions were not chosen by the people who had to govern under them, and a state that begins with borders it did not draw, an economy pointed at somebody else's market, and a police force built to suppress its own population is starting a long way back.
