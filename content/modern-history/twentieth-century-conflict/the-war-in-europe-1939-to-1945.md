# The War in Europe, 1939 to 1945

The first shots were fired by a museum piece. The Schleswig-Holstein was an obsolete pre-dreadnought battleship used for training cadets, and she had come to Danzig on a courtesy visit, moored within sight of the small Polish military depot on the Westerplatte peninsula. At dawn on 1 September 1939 she opened fire on it at point-blank range. The garrison of fewer than two hundred men held out for a week against naval guns, dive bombers and infantry assault, which was a week longer than anyone expected and made no difference to anything.

Within a month Poland had ceased to exist as a state. The German invasion came from three directions, supported by an air force that attacked railways, roads and columns of refugees; the Soviet Union, acting on the secret protocol of the pact signed in Moscow a week before the war began, invaded from the east on 17 September and took the agreed share. Britain and France had declared war on 3 September and did essentially nothing. The Polish government went into exile, its army partly escaping to fight for six years in other people's campaigns, and the country was divided between two occupiers who both began immediately to destroy its educated class.

## The Fall of France

The winter that followed was so quiet in the west that people called it the phoney war. It ended in April 1940 with the German seizure of Denmark and Norway, and on 10 May with an offensive against the Low Countries and France that produced the most shocking military result of the century.

The French army was not small, badly equipped or short of tanks; in raw numbers it matched or exceeded the Germans in most categories. What it lacked was a doctrine for operating at speed and a communications system that could keep up with events. The German plan sent its armoured weight through the Ardennes, hilly wooded country the French high command considered unsuitable for large formations, crossed the Meuse at Sedan on the fifth day, and then drove west to the Channel rather than south towards Paris, cutting the best Allied armies off in Belgium. Once the front was torn, French headquarters spent days reacting to a situation that had already changed. Over a third of a million British and French troops were lifted off the beaches at Dunkirk, without their equipment. Italy joined in on the winning side on 10 June, Paris fell four days later, and an armistice was signed on 22 June in the same railway carriage used for the German surrender of 1918, which Hitler had brought out of a museum for the purpose.

Britain then fought alone in Europe for a year. The German attempt to win air superiority over the Channel in the summer and autumn of 1940 failed against a defence organised around radar stations, filter rooms and telephone lines — the first integrated air defence system in history — and the invasion was postponed indefinitely. The night bombing of British cities that followed killed some forty thousand civilians without breaking anything that mattered, a lesson about the limits of strategic bombing that both sides would spend the rest of the war ignoring.

## The War in the East

On 22 June 1941 Germany invaded the Soviet Union with over three million men, the largest invasion ever mounted. It was conceived from the start as a different kind of war. Orders issued before it began exempted German soldiers from prosecution for crimes against Soviet civilians and required the execution of captured political officers; planners in Berlin calculated, on paper and in advance, that diverting Ukrainian grain to Germany would starve tens of millions of Soviet city dwellers, and treated that as an acceptable consequence.

Militarily the first months were a disaster for the Red Army, which lost entire fronts in encirclements at Minsk, Smolensk and Kiev. But the Germans had planned for a campaign of weeks and had no logistical capacity for more; the drive on Moscow stalled in December in front of fresh Siberian divisions and a counterattack nobody in Berlin had thought possible. Leningrad was encircled instead of taken and besieged for close to nine hundred days, during which roughly a million of its inhabitants died, mostly of starvation in the first winter.

The scale of what followed is hard to hold in the mind. The Eastern Front consumed the great majority of German ground strength for the rest of the war. Stalingrad, fought street by street through the autumn of 1942, ended with a Soviet encirclement and the surrender of an entire German army in February 1943; of the prisoners taken, only a small fraction ever came home. At Kursk that summer the Germans attacked a prepared defensive system and lost the initiative for good. In the summer of 1944, while western attention was on Normandy, the Soviet offensive in Belorussia destroyed Army Group Centre outright — a defeat larger than Stalingrad, and far less remembered in the west. More than three million Soviet prisoners of war died in German captivity, most of them in the first year, shot or worked or simply starved to death in open enclosures.

## Murder as Policy

Alongside the fighting, and increasingly independent of it, the German state carried out the systematic murder of European Jews. It began with mass shootings behind the advancing front in 1941, carried out by police battalions, SS task forces and local auxiliaries, at ravines and pits outside hundreds of towns — thirty-three thousand people killed at Babi Yar outside Kiev over two days being only the largest single instance. Shooting proved slow and, by the perpetrators' own accounts, hard on the shooters. From late 1941 the killing was industrialised at dedicated extermination camps in occupied Poland, where Jews deported from ghettos and from across the continent were gassed on arrival and their bodies burned.

About six million Jews were murdered, roughly two thirds of the Jewish population of Europe, and whole communities that had existed for centuries were erased in months. The same apparatus killed hundreds of thousands of Roma, the disabled patients of German asylums under a programme that had begun before the war and gave the killers their technique, Polish priests and teachers and officers, Soviet commissars, homosexuals, and anyone caught sheltering the hunted. None of this was a by-product of combat. It required trains, budgets, timetables, contractors and clerks, and it continued when the trains were needed at the front.

## The Atlantic and the Air

Britain's survival and the buildup of American force in Europe depended on the convoy routes, and the U-boat campaign against them came closer to succeeding than anything else Germany attempted after 1940. The counter was a combination of things rather than a single invention: escort groups trained to work together, long-range aircraft closing the mid-ocean air gap, centimetric radar that could find a surfaced boat at night, high-frequency direction finding, and the reading of German naval signal traffic. In the spring of 1943 the exchange rate turned decisively, and the U-boat fleet was withdrawn from the North Atlantic having lost most of its crews.

The bomber offensive over Germany was the western Allies' main way of striking the continent for three years, and it remains the most argued-over part of their war. Precision bombing by day proved impossible for the RAF and murderously costly for the Americans until long-range fighter escorts arrived; area bombing by night against cities was accurate enough to be done and killed several hundred thousand German civilians, in firestorms at Hamburg and Dresden and in hundreds of smaller raids. It did not break civilian morale. It did force Germany to divert enormous resources into air defence, wreck the synthetic fuel plants on which the Luftwaffe and the panzer divisions ran, and destroy the transport network in the last winter. Bomber Command lost some fifty-five thousand aircrew, a casualty rate closer to that of First World War infantry subalterns than to anything else in the Second.

## Closing the Ring

The western Allies fought their way back onto the continent from the south first: victory in North Africa by mid-1943, then Sicily, then a slow and bloody campaign up the Italian peninsula that pinned German divisions but never threatened Germany itself. Mussolini was deposed by his own Fascist Grand Council and his king in July 1943; the Germans occupied Italy, installed him as a puppet in the north, and turned a co-belligerent into an occupied country with a partisan war inside it.

The Normandy landings on 6 June 1944 put over a hundred thousand men ashore in a day and, after seven weeks of attritional fighting in the hedgerows, broke out and cleared France in a matter of weeks. A last German counteroffensive in the Ardennes that December bought a few weeks at the cost of the reserves needed to defend the Rhine. In the east the Red Army halted on the Vistula while the Warsaw uprising was crushed, then resumed in January and reached the Oder. Germany was invaded from both sides, fought to the end at the insistence of a leadership that regarded the destruction of its own country as deserved, and surrendered in the first week of May 1945 after Hitler shot himself in a bunker under a burning Berlin.

What was left is the part that gets summarised too quickly. The Soviet Union lost something in the order of twenty-seven million people, most of them civilians. Poland lost roughly a fifth of its prewar population. Germany's cities were rubble, its eastern provinces gone, and some twelve million ethnic Germans were expelled westward in conditions that killed many of them. Soviet troops entering Germany committed mass rape on a scale that official histories on both sides avoided for decades. Tens of millions of people were somewhere other than home — freed prisoners, survivors with no community to return to, forced labourers, refugees moving in every direction across a continent with no functioning railways, currency or government.
