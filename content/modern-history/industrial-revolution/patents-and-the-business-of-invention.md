# Patents and the Business of Invention

Bolted to the frame of a Boulton and Watt engine was a small sealed counter,
driven off the beam, recording every stroke the machine made. It was not there
for the engineer. It was there because the firm did not sell engines outright;
it sold the right to use Watt's separate condenser, and it charged for that
right as a share of the coal the customer saved compared with the old
atmospheric engine he would otherwise have run. The counter measured the
royalty. A Cornish mine adventurer paying that royalty every quarter, on an
engine standing on his own land, built partly by his own smiths, had a vivid
and unsympathetic understanding of what a patent was: a legal claim on the
productivity of his mine, held by two men in Birmingham, and good until 1800
because Parliament had passed a special act extending it.

That extension is the hinge of a long argument. Watt's condenser was a genuine
and important invention, and the twenty-five extra years let Boulton finance
the difficult work of turning it into a manufacturing business. The same
twenty-five years, on the other view, let the partners spend the 1790s suing
rivals rather than developing high-pressure steam, which they disliked and
which had to wait for Trevithick and the expiry of the patent before it went
anywhere. Both claims are plausible, neither can be tested, and the whole
history of industrial patenting has the same shape: an arrangement everyone
agreed was necessary, that nobody could show was working.

## A Bargain With the Crown

English patents did not begin as property. They began as royal favours — open
letters, letters patent, granting a named person an exclusive privilege — and
by the early seventeenth century they had become a notorious instrument of
fiscal abuse, sold to courtiers as monopolies over soap, playing cards, and
other things nobody had invented. The Statute of Monopolies of 1624 abolished
the practice and carved out a single exception: a grant of up to fourteen years
to the true and first inventor of a new manufacture. Fourteen was two terms of
apprenticeship, the idea being that an inventor should have long enough to
train up a generation of workmen in his art.

The disclosure requirement came later, through the courts rather than by
statute. By the later eighteenth century it was settled doctrine that a patent
was void unless the inventor enrolled a written specification describing the
invention fully enough for a skilled practitioner to reproduce it. That turned
the patent into an exchange: the public grants a temporary monopoly, and in
return the knowledge enters the record instead of dying with its holder.
Whether the exchange was honoured in practice is another matter. Specifications
were routinely drafted to satisfy the letter of the requirement while obscuring
what actually mattered — the tempering, the proportions, the trick of the
thing — and litigation frequently turned on whether a specification taught
anything at all. Arkwright's great carding patent was thrown out in the 1780s
on grounds of that kind, to the delight of every spinner in Lancashire.

## The Obstacle Course

Getting a patent in England before the middle of the nineteenth century was an
administrative ordeal designed by nobody. The petition had to travel through a
sequence of offices, each surviving from some older stage of Crown procedure,
each taking a fee and a signature: the Home Secretary, the law officers, the
Signet Office, the Privy Seal, and finally the Great Seal. Dickens wrote a
short piece in which a working blacksmith with a good idea counts up the
offices he has been sent to, and arrives at a number past thirty. Worse, a
patent for England did not cover Scotland or Ireland, which required separate
applications and separate fees. The whole business ran to something on the
order of a hundred pounds for England alone and perhaps three times that for
all three kingdoms — several years of a skilled workman's wages, payable before
anyone knew whether the invention was worth anything.

The 1852 reform swept most of that away. One application now produced one
patent valid across the United Kingdom, granted by a new Patent Office, with a
small fee at the outset and much larger payments due at renewal, so that a
patentee had to decide after a few years whether his monopoly was earning
enough to be worth keeping. The reform also, almost as an afterthought, did
something historically invaluable: it set about printing, indexing and selling
the entire backlog of English specifications to 1617. The man in charge of
that, Bennet Woodcroft, was an inventor himself and an obsessive; he also
began collecting the physical machines, and the engines he rescued formed the
nucleus of what became the Science Museum's collection.

## The American Contrast

The United States built a different system almost from the start, and the
difference shows in who used it. Fees were set low, deliberately, at a small
fraction of the British level. After 1836 the Patent Office employed examiners
who checked applications against prior art before granting, which reduced the
number of worthless patents in circulation and made a granted patent something
a buyer could take seriously. Patents were freely assignable, and a market grew
in them, with agents and brokers and trade journals advertising rights for sale
by state and by county.

The effect was that American patenting reached much further down the social
scale than British. A farmer with an improved plough or a mechanic with a
better churn could realistically file, and enormous numbers did. Abraham
Lincoln, who patented a scheme for lifting riverboats over shoals and remains
the only American president to have held a patent, said in a lecture that the
patent system added the fuel of interest to the fire of genius. It is a
politician's line about a policy he had used, but it captures what the low-fee,
examined, assignable American patent was actually for: not protecting great
inventors, but persuading ordinary competent people that tinkering might pay.

## Making Money From a Monopoly

A patent is not income. Converting one into money required a business model,
and the period produced most of the models still in use. The simplest was to
work the invention yourself and let the patent keep competitors out, which is
what Bessemer eventually did with his converter after his licensees found that
British ores ruined the steel and demanded their money back. The second was to
license, as Boulton and Watt did, which requires a way of measuring use —
hence the stroke counter — and a willingness to litigate. The third was to sell
outright, which is what most individual inventors without capital ended up
doing, usually too early and too cheaply.

The fourth model emerged from deadlock. By the 1850s the American sewing
machine industry had reached a state in which every serious manufacturer
infringed somebody else's patent: Elias Howe held the lockstitch claim, Singer
held others essential to a usable machine, and the litigation was consuming the
profits of the whole trade. The resolution was a combination in which the
principal holders pooled their patents, licensed the pool to manufacturers at a
fixed royalty per machine, and divided the proceeds among themselves, with a
cut reserved for Howe. It was the first patent pool, and it established the
pattern by which a thicket of overlapping claims gets converted into a cartel
with a legal foundation.

Litigation itself became a business. Some of the most profitable patents of the
century — Neilson's hot blast for ironmaking is the standard example — earned
more in damages and settlements than in ordinary royalties, and were defended
with an aggression that shaped whole industries. Meanwhile the drafting of
specifications became a trade of its own, and by the second half of the century
patent agents were a recognizable profession with their own institute, their
own periodicals, and a working knowledge of how to write a claim broad enough
to catch a competitor without being broad enough to be void.

The logical end of all this was to treat invention itself as a product. Edison's
laboratory was organized to generate patentable results on a schedule, staffed
with machinists and chemists, funded by the sale and licensing of what it
produced. The individual inventor in a workshop did not disappear, but he was
joined and eventually overshadowed by a corporate research operation whose
output was measured in filings.

## The Case Against, and What People Did Instead

For a couple of decades in the middle of the century, serious people thought
patents should be abolished. The free-trade argument was straightforward: a
patent is a monopoly, monopolies raise prices and misallocate effort, and the
case for this particular monopoly rested on an unproved assertion that
invention would otherwise stop. Prominent economic journalism took that line,
a royal commission examined the system without much enthusiasm for it, and the
movement had real legislative consequences abroad. The Netherlands repealed its
patent law in 1869 and did without one for over forty years. Switzerland went
even longer before adopting any patent protection, and its chemical and
textile-machinery industries did conspicuously well in the meantime.

The abolitionists had a point that the evidence still supports: a great deal of
invention happened outside the patent system entirely. Some industries relied
on secrecy, which works well when a process is hard to reverse-engineer from
the product. Some relied on lead time, since being first to market with a
cheaper cloth mattered more than being able to sue the second. Governments and
societies offered prizes, and occasionally got what they paid for; the French
prize for a method of preserving food produced the canning industry. And in
some trades the participants simply published. Cornish mine engineers, freed
from Watt's royalties after 1800, began circulating a monthly report comparing
the fuel duty of every major pumping engine in the district by name, which
turned engine design into an open competition and drove efficiency upward at a
rate the patent era had not managed. Ironmasters in the north-east did
something similar with blast furnace dimensions decades later, sharing designs
and performance figures because each furnace built taught everyone something
the next one could use.

None of that proves patents were unnecessary, any more than Watt's counter
proves they were essential. What it suggests is that the patent was one
instrument among several for making knowledge pay, that its usefulness varied
enormously by industry, and that the nineteenth century settled on it less
because the case was won than because the system already existed and the
alternatives were harder to administer.
