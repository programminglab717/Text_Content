# The Electric Telegraph and the Speed of News

On the first day of January 1845, a man boarded a train at Slough for
Paddington believing, reasonably enough, that he had left his pursuers behind.
He had poisoned a woman in a cottage near the station and been seen leaving.
He was wrong about the pursuers, because the Great Western Railway had strung
a set of wires alongside its track, and a description of him — a man dressed
in the garb of a Quaker, though the alphabet on the instrument had no letter Q,
so the clerk spelled it "kwaker" and trusted the man at the other end to work
it out — reached London before the train did. He was followed from the
platform and arrested. The trial made the telegraph famous in a way that no
demonstration before a learned society had managed. A newspaper remarked that
the wires had hanged him.

What the wires actually did was much stranger than catching a murderer. For
the whole of human history, information had travelled at the speed of a horse,
a ship or a semaphore arm on a clear day. Within about thirty years of that
arrest, a price quoted in Chicago could be known in Liverpool while the
Liverpool market was still open. Almost every other Victorian technology made
something existing faster or cheaper. The telegraph made a category of
experience — knowing what was happening somewhere else, now — that had not
previously existed.

## What Actually Travelled Down the Wire

The underlying physics was simple and had been available, in principle, since
Volta's pile and Oersted's discovery that a current deflects a compass needle.
Close a circuit at one end of a long conductor and something detectable happens
at the other end almost instantly. The whole art of telegraphy lay in making
that detection reliable, cheap, and translatable into language.

Two answers emerged more or less simultaneously, and they reflected their
countries. Cooke and Wheatstone in England built needle telegraphs: current
through coils swung magnetized needles left or right on a dial, and the
combination of deflections pointed at a letter on a diamond-shaped grid. The
first working version used five needles and six wires, and it had the
considerable virtue that a railway clerk could be taught to use it in an
afternoon, because the machine spelled out the letters itself. It had the
corresponding vice that six wires cost six times as much as one, and the
alphabet had to be trimmed to twenty letters to fit the grid. Later versions
came down to two needles and then one, at the cost of requiring operators who
had memorized a code of left-and-right flicks.

Samuel Morse, working with Alfred Vail and drawing on Joseph Henry's work on
electromagnets, went the other way. One wire, one key, one electromagnet at the
far end, and all the complexity pushed into the code. The first instruments
embossed marks on a moving paper tape; operators quickly discovered they could
read the clicks of the armature faster by ear than they could read the tape,
and the sounder replaced the register. The code itself is a small masterpiece
of engineering economy. Vail is usually credited with assigning the shortest
symbols to the commonest letters, and with working out those frequencies by
counting the type in a printer's case rather than by any statistical study —
a single dot for E, a dot and a dash for A, four dashes for nothing anyone
needed often. Skilled operators reached speeds of thirty or forty words a
minute and developed a distinctive rhythm, a "fist", that colleagues recognized
down the line the way you recognize a voice.

## The Line Itself

The wire turned out to be the hard part. Cooke and Wheatstone's first
installations buried the conductors in iron tubes beside the track, where
moisture and mechanical damage destroyed the insulation within months.
Telegraphy moved overhead onto poles, with the conductor supported on glass or
glazed earthenware insulators that kept the current from leaking into the wet
timber. It was also discovered early that the return path did not need a
second wire: the earth itself would serve, if both ends were connected to
buried plates. That halved the copper, or rather the iron, since most land
lines used galvanized iron wire, which is cheaper and stronger and considerably
worse at conducting than copper.

Distance was the next problem. A long line has resistance, and resistance eats
the current until the electromagnet at the far end no longer has the force to
move its armature. The solution was the relay, an instrument sensitive enough
to respond to a feeble incoming signal and use it to close a second, local
circuit with a fresh battery behind it. That is a repeater, and it means a
message can be regenerated indefinitely without degrading, which is why
telegraph signalling is fundamentally different from shouting down a pipe. It
is also, incidentally, the first widely deployed device in which one electric
circuit controls another — the ancestor of every switching system that
followed.

Relays, sounders, keys and batteries were cheap. What was expensive was the
line, and the industry spent the rest of the century squeezing more messages
onto the wire it already had. Duplex working let two messages pass in opposite
directions at once by arranging bridge circuits so each operator's instrument
ignored its own transmission. Edison's quadruplex doubled that again by adding
a second variable — reversing polarity as well as varying current — so that
four messages shared one conductor. Wheatstone's automatic system took the
human hand out of it entirely, punching a paper tape in advance and running it
through a transmitter far faster than any wrist could work a key, with a
receiving tape printed for clerks to transcribe at leisure.

## Under the Sea

Overhead lines stop at the coast. Submarine telegraphy waited for gutta-percha,
a latex from Malayan trees that is rigid at air temperature, softens when
heated, and — crucially — does not degrade in cold seawater. A cross-Channel
cable laid in 1850 worked for a matter of hours before it was destroyed,
reputedly by a fisherman who hauled up a piece of it. The following year an
armoured version succeeded, and London and Paris were connected.

The Atlantic was a different order of problem, and it produced the first great
public failure of Victorian engineering. An attempt in 1857 parted the cable in
deep water. A second in 1858 worked, and Queen Victoria and President Buchanan
exchanged congratulations, but the messages took hours each to send and the
cable died within weeks — partly because the company's electrician, convinced
the fault was weak signal, had been driving it with induction coils at
punishing voltages and had cooked the insulation. Not until 1866, with the
Great Eastern as the only ship large enough to carry the whole cable, was a
permanent connection made; the same voyage grappled up the broken cable from
the previous year and completed it too, so the Atlantic gained two lines at
once.

The intellectual residue of those failures mattered more than the cables. A
long submarine cable is a capacitor: a conductor separated by a thin dielectric
from a vast conducting sheet of seawater. Send a sharp pulse into one end and a
smeared, sluggish hump emerges from the other, the smearing getting worse with
the square of the length. Faraday had demonstrated the effect, and William
Thomson — later Lord Kelvin — turned it into design rules, arguing for large
conductors, thick insulation, and above all for detecting instruments so
sensitive that no brute force was needed at the sending end. His mirror
galvanometer threw a spot of light across a scale from a tiny mirror on a
suspended magnet; his later siphon recorder wrote the signal in ink on a moving
strip. Cable engineering became a quantitative discipline, with its own units,
its own testing rooms, and its own habit of measuring everything before
committing it to the sea.

## The Consequences Nobody Ordered

Railways adopted the telegraph first because they needed it: a single track
with trains running both ways is safe only if someone at one end knows what is
at the other, and block signalling made timetables enforceable rather than
aspirational. News agencies came next. Paul Reuter, who had bridged a gap in
the German network with carrier pigeons, built a business on the fact that
being first with a price or a proclamation was worth money to someone.

That is the deepest change. Before the wire, distant markets were separate
markets, and the merchant's profession consisted largely of knowing things his
counterpart did not yet know. Afterward, prices in connected markets converged
within the cost of shipping, arbitrage became a matter of minutes rather than
voyages, and commodity trading reorganized itself around futures — contracts on
things not yet grown, which only make sense when everyone can see the same
information at the same time. Time itself was standardized by wire: railway
companies distributed a single time signal along their lines and insisted that
station clocks obey it, killing off local solar time in one country after
another.

Empires ran on it too, and knew they did. A telegram from London reached a
governor in India in hours, which changed the meaning of a colonial
appointment: a man who had once acted on his own judgment because instructions
took five months now had to answer for departures from orders that arrived the
same week. Governments noticed the leverage. Britain took its inland telegraphs
into the Post Office in 1870; in the United States the business consolidated
instead into a single dominant private company. Either way, the wire that had
begun as a railway signalling convenience had become infrastructure that no
state was willing to leave entirely alone.
