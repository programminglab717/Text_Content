# Agricultural Improvement and the Feeding of Industry

For most of European history, a substantial share of the best arable land grew
nothing at all in any given year. Under the old three-field system a parish
sowed winter grain on one field, spring grain on another, and left the third
bare: ploughed several times to kill weeds, grazed lightly, and otherwise idle
while it recovered whatever it was that made grain grow. Nobody knew what that
something was. They knew only that continuous cropping exhausted the ground,
that dung helped, and that dung was limited by the number of animals a farm
could keep through the winter — which was limited in turn by the amount of hay
and straw it could grow, which was limited by the land not under fallow. The
system closed on itself. Breaking that circle, more than any single machine,
is what allowed a country to stop feeding itself with most of its population.

## The Fallow Problem

The escape was a rotation in which every field carried a crop every year and
the soil still improved. The version associated with Norfolk ran to four
courses: wheat, then turnips, then barley, then clover or a clover-and-ryegrass
ley, and back to wheat. Each element did work. Turnips, sown in rows and hoed
between, let a farmer clean a field of weeds while producing a bulky crop; more
importantly they could be stored or left in the ground and eaten by sheep
folded on the field in winter, so that the flock fertilized the land exactly
where the manure was wanted. Clover, cut for hay or grazed, produced heavy
fodder and left the ground demonstrably richer for the following wheat.

Why clover enriched the soil was not understood for well over a century after
farmers began relying on it. The bacteria in its root nodules that fix
atmospheric nitrogen were not identified until late in the nineteenth century.
What the improvers had was an empirical regularity, worked out through trial,
correspondence, and the observation of neighbours, and codified into a sequence
that could be taught. That is characteristic of the whole movement: the
practice ran decades ahead of the explanation, and the people who got the
practice right were often wrong about why it worked. Jethro Tull's seed drill
and horse hoe were genuine advances — sowing in straight rows at a controlled
depth wastes less seed and permits mechanical weeding — but Tull's own theory,
that finely pulverized soil fed plants directly and made manure unnecessary,
was simply mistaken.

## Livestock as Machinery

The same period saw animals treated, for the first time, as things to be
designed. Robert Bakewell of Dishley in Leicestershire is the figure usually
credited, and his methods were startling to contemporaries: he bred close
relatives to each other to fix desirable traits, kept detailed records, and
hired out his rams by the season rather than selling them, which let him retain
control of his bloodlines and compare the offspring of a given sire across many
different flocks. That last practice amounts to progeny testing, and it turned
breeding from a matter of a good eye into something closer to an experiment.
His New Leicester sheep put on fat quickly on modest keep, which was what a
market of growing towns wanted.

Others followed with cattle, and within a few decades the leading breeders were
publishing herd books that recorded pedigrees and made a documented ancestry
into a saleable asset. Prize bullocks were painted, exhibited and toured. Some
of the results were grotesque by any modern standard of animal welfare or of
meat quality, and the fashion for sheer fat eventually reversed. But the
underlying change held: livestock became improvable capital rather than fixed
inheritance, and breeds became defined populations with owners, standards and
markets.

## Enclosure, and the Argument About It

None of this fitted comfortably into open-field farming, where a holding
consisted of scattered strips in communal fields, cropped in step with
everyone else and thrown open to common grazing after harvest. A man who wanted
to try a four-course rotation, or to keep a flock of a particular breed away
from the parish ram, needed his land in one ring-fenced block under his own
control. Enclosure supplied that, and from the middle of the eighteenth century
into the 1820s it was carried out mainly by private acts of Parliament, village
by village, with appointed commissioners redistributing the land and
extinguishing common rights.

The consequences are still argued over, and the argument is not really about
the facts. Enclosure did consolidate holdings, did permit rotations and
drainage schemes that open fields obstructed, and did raise output per acre on
much of the land it touched. It also extinguished the use rights — grazing a
cow on the waste, gathering fuel, gleaning after harvest — on which cottagers
without land depended for the margin between poverty and destitution. The legal
costs of enclosure were charged against the new allotments, which pressed many
small proprietors into selling. Whether the productivity gains required
enclosure is the sharper question, and historians have shown that a good deal
of improvement happened on land already held in severalty, or on open-field
parishes where the cultivators simply agreed among themselves to change the
rotation. The strongest version of the case for enclosure is not that it
invented improvement but that it removed a veto.

## Bones, Guano and the Chemistry of Yield

By the 1830s the limiting factor had shifted again. Rotations and folded sheep
supplied as much fertility as a closed farm could generate; further gains meant
importing it. Farmers first bought bones, crushed for their phosphate, in such
quantity that continental critics accused England of stripping the
battlefields of Europe to feed its wheat. Then came Peruvian guano, an
accumulation of seabird droppings mined from rainless islands and carried
around Cape Horn, so rich and so immediately effective that its arrival
reorganized the fertilizer trade and, later, the politics of the Pacific coast
of South America. Chilean nitrate followed.

Chemistry arrived in the middle of this. Liebig's argument that plants take
specific mineral elements from the soil, and that yields can be restored by
replacing exactly those elements, reframed manuring as accounting. He was wrong
about nitrogen, believing plants drew enough from the air and rain, and the
error mattered, because nitrogen turned out to be the binding constraint on
cereal yields. Settling that question took sustained field experiment rather
than laboratory argument. John Bennet Lawes, who had patented a process for
treating phosphates with sulphuric acid to make superphosphate and built a
business on it, set up long-running trials on his family estate at Rothamsted
with the chemist Joseph Henry Gilbert, sowing the same plots with the same
treatments year after year. Some of those plots have now been cropped
continuously for well over a century and a half, and they remain the reason we
can say anything quantitative about what happens to soil under sustained
management.

Underdrainage belongs in the same story. Heavy clay land in a wet country is
cold, late and unworkable, and laying lines of tile a few feet down transforms
it. Early drains were made of stones, brushwood or hand-shaped horseshoe tiles
and were expensive enough to be a gentleman's project. Machines that extruded
cylindrical clay pipes cut the cost sharply in the 1840s, and public loan
schemes let landowners borrow against the improved value. Millions of acres of
English clay were drained in a generation, most of it invisible, much of it
still functioning.

## Iron in the Field

Mechanization came late to farming and unevenly. Threshing, the great
winter task of flailing grain from straw by hand, was the first to go: Andrew
Meikle's threshing machine and its many imitators spread through the arable
districts from the late eighteenth century. It was also the most bitterly
resented, because flailing was precisely the work that kept labourers employed
between harvests. In 1830 farm workers across the southern counties burned
ricks and broke threshing machines in the riots that went under the name of
Captain Swing, and the machines retreated from some districts for years
afterward.

Harvesting resisted longer, because cutting and gathering standing grain
without shattering the ears is a harder mechanical problem than beating a sheaf.
Reaping machines from American builders were tried in English wheat at the
Great Exhibition, and over the following decades the reaper and then the
self-binder spread — fastest where labour was scarce and fields were large,
which meant the American Midwest far more than the English shires. Steam went
into the fields too, though rarely under its own wheels: the practical
arrangement was a pair of engines at opposite headlands hauling a
balance plough back and forth on a cable, which was economic only on large,
heavy, regular fields and was usually owned by a contractor rather than a
farmer. The portable engine driving a travelling threshing set from farm to
farm was far more common, and the image of a smoking engine parked in a
stackyard belongs to the second half of the century as firmly as the mill
chimney belongs to the town.

## The Reckoning

The mid-Victorian decades of high farming — heavy inputs, drained land, bought
fertilizer, confident tenants on long leases — ended in the late 1870s, and not
because the farming was wrong. A run of appalling wet seasons coincided with
the arrival of prairie wheat, moved to the coast on new American railways and
across the Atlantic in steel steamships at freight rates that kept falling.
Grain prices collapsed and stayed down. The Corn Laws had already been repealed
decades earlier, so there was no protection to remove and none was restored.
Arable districts in the east of England went out of wheat, land went down to
grass, rents fell and some of the improved acreage reverted. Farmers near
cities turned to milk, which could not be imported, and to market gardening;
then refrigerated ships began landing mutton from New Zealand and beef from
Argentina, and even that shelter narrowed.

What survived was the structural fact the improvers had created. A country that
had learned to grow far more food per acre and per worker could afford to stop
growing much of its own, and buy it instead with cotton cloth, rails and coal.
