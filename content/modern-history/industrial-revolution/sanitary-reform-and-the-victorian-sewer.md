# Sanitary Reform and the Victorian Sewer

In the hot summer of 1858 the Thames at Westminster became unbearable. Members
of Parliament sitting in a new building with large river-facing windows found
that the committee rooms on that side could not be used; the curtains were
soaked in chloride of lime and hung across the openings, and the House
discussed adjourning to somewhere upwind. The river had been the city's sewer
for generations, but the previous decades had multiplied the load — more
people, more water closets, more cesspools emptied into drains that emptied
into the tideway, where the ebb carried the discharge down and the flood
carried it back up again. Within a few weeks of the smell reaching the
legislature, an act was passed authorizing the Metropolitan Board of Works to
borrow three million pounds and build whatever its engineer thought necessary,
without further permission from anyone. Nothing in the long history of British
sanitary reform moved as quickly as that, and the reason it moved is not
flattering.

## Right Plumbing, Wrong Theory

The prevailing explanation of epidemic disease held that it arose from
miasma: foul air generated by decomposing organic matter. Fevers clustered
where the stink was worst, which they genuinely did, and the theory accounted
for the pattern without requiring anyone to believe in invisible agents passing
from person to person. Edwin Chadwick believed it with total conviction,
arguing in effect that intense smell simply was disease, and his belief had the
useful property of pointing at exactly the right remedy for almost entirely the
wrong reason. If putrefaction near dwellings caused fever, then the answer was
to remove every trace of refuse and sewage from inhabited places as fast as
possible — which is also the answer if the real agents are waterborne bacteria,
because both the smell and the cholera travel in the same material.

Chadwick had trained as a barrister and worked as Jeremy Bentham's secretary,
and he brought to sanitation an administrator's appetite for system. His
1842 report on the sanitary condition of the labouring population, assembled
from returns by Poor Law officers and local doctors, was a compendium of
particulars: undrained courts, shared privies serving dozens of families,
graveyards packed past capacity, cellars where water stood. It sold in numbers
no government report had reached before.

## The Argument From the Rates

What made the report politically potent was not its horror but its accounting.
Chadwick argued that disease was the principal manufacturer of pauperism. A
labourer killed by typhus in his thirties left a widow and children who fell
on the poor rate, and the cost of that relief, accumulated across a district,
exceeded what it would have cost to drain and supply the district properly.
Sanitation, in this telling, was not charity. It was a cheaper way of doing
something the ratepayers were already paying for.

He had a technical scheme to match. The existing sewers were large brick
tunnels with flat bottoms, built to carry away storm water, in which solids
settled and rotted and had to be dug out by hand. Chadwick wanted small-bore
glazed pipes, egg-shaped in section so that a small flow ran deep and fast in
the narrow invert, flushed continuously by a constant supply of piped water
rather than the intermittent supply that filled a street butt twice a week. He
described the whole thing as an arterial and venous circulation for the town,
clean water in and foul water out, and he expected the foul end to pay for
itself by selling sewage to farmers as liquid manure. That last piece never
worked anywhere at scale, and his insistence on it cost him credibility.

The Public Health Act of 1848 created a General Board of Health and allowed —
in places with high enough death rates, required — localities to set up boards
with powers over drainage, water and nuisances. It was resisted ferociously,
and much of the resistance was not stupid. Vestries and improvement
commissioners objected to being overruled from London by a man with no elected
mandate. Water companies objected to being told what to charge and where to
draw from. Property owners objected to compulsory connection charges. Chadwick
was abrasive, certain, and incapable of conciliation, and by 1854 he had been
levered out of office, cheered off by newspapers that preferred the risk of
cholera to the certainty of being ordered about.

## Broad Street

That same year cholera returned, and in Soho it killed with a speed that
outran any explanation based on general filthiness. John Snow, a physician
best known at the time for his work on anaesthesia, had published five years
earlier an argument that cholera was not airborne at all. The disease attacked
the gut rather than the lungs; it passed between people in circumstances that
suggested something swallowed; and it spread along water supplies. In the
Golden Square outbreak he mapped the deaths, found them clustering around a
public pump in Broad Street, and pressed the local board of guardians to remove
the handle.

The anomalies mattered more than the cluster. A workhouse inside the affected
area with its own well was barely touched. A brewery whose men drank beer
rather than water lost nobody. A widow who had moved away from the
neighbourhood but liked the taste of Broad Street water, and had a bottle of it
carted to her regularly, died of cholera in a district with no other cases.
Snow's clerical collaborator Henry Whitehead, who had set out to disprove him,
worked backward to an infant who had fallen ill days before the outbreak in a
house beside the well, whose soiled napkins had been rinsed into a cesspool
whose brickwork leaked into the water.

Snow's larger demonstration was more rigorous still. In south London, two
companies supplied water to the same streets, sometimes to adjacent houses,
because their mains had been laid in competition years before. One drew from
the Thames in the heart of the sewage-laden tideway; the other had moved its
intake upstream. Snow went house to house, identified the supplier of each, and
found cholera mortality among the customers of the downstream company many
times that of their neighbours. Residents, rents, trades and air were the same.
Only the water differed.

It did not settle the question. The official inquiry into the Soho outbreak
concluded that the evidence for the pump was unpersuasive and that the cause
lay in the atmosphere; the handle went back on. Snow died before the germ
theory gave his argument a mechanism, and most sanitary authorities went on
thinking in terms of foul air for another two decades. The practical
convergence of the two theories on the same remedies is the reason this delay
did less damage than it might have.

## Bazalgette's Arithmetic

The engineer the Metropolitan Board of Works had appointed was Joseph
Bazalgette, and the scheme he built after 1858 remains the largest single
public work of Victorian London. Instead of trying to keep sewage out of the
sewers, he intercepted it. A set of large brick sewers ran west to east across
the grain of the existing drainage, catching the outfalls before they reached
the river and carrying everything to outfalls far downstream, where it could be
released on the ebb tide. Where gravity ran out, pumping stations lifted the
flow from low-level to high-level lines, housed in buildings with cathedral
ironwork and polychrome brick that make an oddly grand statement about pumping.
Along the north bank the low-level sewer was buried inside a new embankment
that also reclaimed acres of foreshore and carried a railway, which is why the
Thames at Westminster today is a channel with walls rather than a tidal mud
slope.

Two decisions made it last. Bazalgette calculated the required capacity from
the population and the rainfall, and then doubled the diameter, on the
reasoning that the city would grow and the work would only be done once. And
he standardized on Portland cement, then a new and variable material, with
testing of batches strict enough to force improvements on the industry
supplying it. Hundreds of millions of bricks went into the job. The system was
extended and its outfalls eventually given treatment rather than simply
discharging, but the arteries he laid still carry London's sewage.

## Who Was Actually Saved

Urban death rates in Britain began falling in earnest from the 1870s, and
cholera never returned after the East London outbreak of 1866, which was itself
traced to a company supplying unfiltered water in breach of the law. Typhoid
declined; so did the general burden of diarrhoeal disease among adults. The
legislative machinery had by then caught up: a mandatory act in 1866 replacing
permissive powers, a consolidating act in 1875 that required every district to
appoint a medical officer of health and gave model byelaws for drainage and
housing. Liverpool had appointed the first such officer decades earlier, and
the City of London the second, and the profession those men created — men who
counted deaths by street, inspected dairies, and wrote annual reports that
named landlords — did much of the work that statutes only authorized.

How much of the mortality decline sanitation caused is a genuinely open
question. One influential argument held that better nutrition, not public
intervention, drove most of it, since some diseases fell before any effective
medicine or plumbing reached them. The counter-argument points out that
mortality in the fastest-growing towns worsened before it improved, that the
improvement tracks the arrival of municipal water and sewerage district by
district, and that the money and the compulsion arrived first in exactly the
places where the gains were largest. One fact resists both accounts: infant
mortality stayed obstinately high until around the turn of the century, long
after the sewers were in. Babies were dying of contaminated milk and summer
diarrhoea in the houses, not of anything the trunk sewer could reach, and
bringing that number down took a different campaign, conducted by health
visitors in kitchens rather than engineers in tunnels.
