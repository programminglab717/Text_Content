# The Factory Day and the Fight to Shorten It

The bell went before dawn. At many mills the gate was shut a few minutes after it, and anyone who arrived to find it closed either lost the morning's pay or was locked out for the whole day, depending on the master. Inside, the fines were listed: so much for being late, so much for opening a window, for leaving a lamp burning, for whistling, for washing a face during working hours. Workers complained constantly that the mill clock ran fast in the morning and slow in the evening, and since the master owned the clock there was no appeal. What the factory sold its owner was not principally machinery. It was the ability to buy a quantity of human time and make sure he received it.

This is the part of the factory system that is easiest to miss, because machines are conspicuous and habits are not. A handloom weaver in a cellar worked long hours, often brutal ones, but he worked in bursts — slack at the start of the week, frantic at the end, with Saint Monday taken off if the previous week had gone well. Agricultural labour was governed by weather and daylight and the demands of animals. Task and season, not the clock. A steam-driven mill inverts that completely. The engine runs, the shafting turns, and every machine in the building either produces or wastes the power being supplied to it. Idleness by one person is loss to everyone, so attendance must be simultaneous, punctual and continuous. The historian E. P. Thompson built an influential argument around exactly this shift, and the evidence for it is everywhere in the period: in the fines books, in the sermons against idleness, in the spread of cheap watches among working men, and in how bitterly the first generation of factory hands resented a discipline their parents had never known.

## The Children

Water-powered mills stood in river valleys where there were few people, so the early spinners went to the parish authorities of distant towns and took pauper children as apprentices, bound until adulthood, housed in an apprentice block beside the works. The arrangement suited everyone with a vote in it. The parish was relieved of the cost of keeping the child; the master got labour that could not leave.

When steam moved the mills into towns, the apprentice system faded and the children came instead from local families, sent by parents who needed the wage. In spinning they worked as piecers, walking behind the mule carriage to catch and twist together broken ends, covering many miles a day in a room kept hot and humid so the yarn would not break, breathing air thick with cotton fly. Smaller children were scavengers, crawling beneath the machinery to sweep up loose cotton while it ran. Line shafting overhead was unguarded, and so were the gears and belts driven from it; hair, sleeves and fingers went into them with regularity. Doctors who examined mill children testified to curved spines and bowed legs from standing twelve and thirteen hours a day on growing bones, and to a deep, chronic exhaustion that made them stupid in the evening schools reformers kept trying to establish.

Parliament noticed early and achieved almost nothing for thirty years. An act of 1802 limited apprentices in cotton and woollen mills to twelve hours and required some instruction; it applied only to apprentices, and by then most mill children were not apprentices. An act of 1819 barred children under nine from cotton mills and capped the under-sixteens at twelve hours. Both laws were to be enforced by local magistrates, in districts where the magistrates were often mill owners or their relations, on the evidence of workers who would be dismissed for giving it. Nobody was watching, so nothing changed.

## Oastler, Sadler and the Short-Time Committees

The agitation that finally moved things came out of the West Riding rather than Lancashire. In 1830 Richard Oastler, a Tory land steward and an opponent of colonial slavery, published a letter in the Leeds press under the heading of Yorkshire slavery, arguing that men who denounced the plantations while working children thirteen hours a day in worsted mills a mile from their own houses were hypocrites. The comparison was inflammatory and it was meant to be. It also landed at exactly the moment when the campaign against West Indian slavery was reaching its climax, and it gave the factory question a vocabulary and an audience.

Short-time committees formed in the textile towns — working men, mostly spinners and weavers, organized to press for a ten-hour day and to gather evidence. They found parliamentary champions in Michael Sadler and, after Sadler lost his seat, in Lord Ashley, later Earl of Shaftesbury. Sadler's select committee took testimony from mill workers in 1832 and published it, and the effect of that document on educated opinion was considerable, because it consisted of ordinary people describing in their own words what a thirteen-hour day had done to their children. Mill owners protested, with some justice, that the committee had heard only one side. A royal commission was appointed to hear the other, went into the mills, and returned with recommendations that conceded the central point: the state would have to regulate the hours of children, and it would have to do so itself.

## Four Inspectors

The Factory Act of 1833 is the hinge. Its provisions were modest enough — no children under nine in textile mills, an eight- or nine-hour limit for those between nine and thirteen with two hours of schooling required, twelve hours for young persons under eighteen, no night work for either group. What made it different from every previous attempt was a short clause creating a salaried inspectorate. Four inspectors, with subordinate superintendents, were given the right to enter any mill at any hour without notice, to question workers, to make regulations and to prosecute in their own name. Four men for the entire kingdom is an absurd provision on its face. It was also the beginning of the modern regulatory state, and the inspectors used their position far more aggressively than the Act's drafters can have expected.

They immediately ran into the two problems that defeat every labour law. The first was proof of age. A child's age was whatever the parents said it was, and parents needed the wage; inspectors fell back on surgeons certifying whether a child looked thirteen, which was guesswork. Civil registration of births from 1837 slowly supplied the missing instrument, and Leonard Horner, the inspector for the northern district and the most formidable of the four, pushed hard to make certificates the basis of enforcement.

The second problem was relays. If children could work only part of the day, a mill could employ them in shifts, sending a child out for three hours and back in again, so that the adults' machines never stopped and no inspector arriving at any given moment could reconstruct who had worked when. Horner spent years arguing that the working day itself had to be fixed by law, not merely each individual's hours, and the argument was eventually won in 1850 with a statute that set the limits of the day and required meal times to be taken simultaneously by everybody in the building.

In between, the law tightened twice. An act of 1844 halved young children's hours and required them to attend school for the other half of the day, limited women to twelve hours, obliged owners to fence horizontal shafting, and required serious accidents to be reported. An act of 1847 gave women and young persons the ten hours the short-time committees had demanded for seventeen years. And here the interdependence of factory work did the reformers' work for them: a mule cannot be spun without piecers, a room cannot run without the women tending most of the machines, so a limit on the protected categories became in practice a limit on the whole mill. Reformers had understood this from the start, which is why they pressed the case through children rather than through adult men, whose hours Parliament in that period regarded as a matter of free contract.

## What Else Was Moving

None of this happened in a quiet room. Handloom weavers and framework knitters had already broken machinery across the Midlands and the north in 1811 and 1812, an offence Parliament made capital, over the objection of a young Lord Byron in his maiden speech. The Combination Acts outlawed trade unions outright until the 1820s, and after their repeal organized labour remained legally precarious: six Dorset farm labourers were transported to Australia in 1834 for administering an oath in the course of forming a union. Chartism, with its demand for the vote, ran through the same towns and the same people as the ten-hours campaign, and in the strike wave of 1842 the two were entirely mixed together, with crowds drawing the plugs from mill boilers to stop production.

Other industries were investigated as the factory question opened the door. The same royal commission machinery went down the coal pits and produced the Mines Act of 1842. Elsewhere there was no protection at all for decades: dressmakers, potters, brickyard children, chimney sweeps' boys, all documented and all largely untouched until much later. A worker injured by unfenced machinery had almost no legal remedy, since the courts held that he had accepted the risk and that his employer was not liable for a fellow workman's negligence, a doctrine not seriously dented until the 1880s.

Whether the working population as a whole gained or lost across these decades has been argued about ever since, and honestly so, because the evidence points both ways: wages in money terms rose slowly, the things wages bought became cheaper, and at the same time town mortality was appalling, the recorded heights of recruits fell, and the people living through it were in no doubt that something had gone badly wrong. What is not in doubt is that the legal working day arrived through pressure from people who could not vote, was resisted by employers who predicted ruin and were wrong, and remained radically incomplete for a long time afterwards. The half-time system, under which a child spent part of the day at a machine and part at a desk, carried on in the English textile districts into the twentieth century, and was not finally abolished until the end of the First World War.
