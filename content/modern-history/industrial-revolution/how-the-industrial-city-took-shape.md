# How the Industrial City Took Shape

At half past five on a winter morning in a Lancashire mill town, the loudest
sound in the street was clogs. Hundreds of pairs of them on stone setts,
converging out of the courts and terraces toward a single gate, with the
knocker-up going ahead down the row tapping at bedroom windows with a long
pole. The engine whistle fixed the hour, and the hour was the same for
everybody. That convergence — thousands of people walking to one building at
one time, six days a week — did more to determine the shape of the industrial
city than any architect or any plan.

English towns had grown before, but they had grown slowly and for other
reasons: a cathedral, a market charter, a harbour, an assize court. Manchester
in the middle of the eighteenth century was a sizable market town with a
finishing trade in linens and fustians and no borough corporation worth the
name. Within a century it was among the largest cities in Europe, and the
places around it — Oldham, Bolton, Blackburn, Rochdale — had gone from villages
to towns of their own. The same thing happened in the West Riding around wool,
in the Black Country around iron and nails, on Clydeside around ships, and in
Merthyr Tydfil, which grew from a hamlet in a Welsh valley into the largest
town in Wales because there happened to be ironstone, coal and limestone within
a few miles of each other.

## Why the Cities Landed Where They Did

The first generation of large textile mills were not urban at all. Water frames
needed falling water, and falling water is found in narrow upland valleys, so
Arkwright built at Cromford and Greg at Styal and dozens of others along the
Derwent, the Goyt and the tributaries of the Irwell. These were company
villages in the countryside, and their owners had to import a workforce,
sometimes by taking pauper apprentices in batches from distant parishes and
housing them in dormitories. The arrangement was awkward, expensive and
inflexible.

Steam dissolved the constraint. A rotative engine could be put wherever coal
could be delivered cheaply, which meant on or beside a coalfield, preferably
with canal or river access. Manufacturers stopped chasing water and started
chasing labour, capital, machine shops, cotton brokers, insurance and the
second-hand market in machinery — all of which were most abundant where other
manufacturers already were. Once a district had a few hundred spinners it
acquired the ancillary trades that made it cheaper to be the next spinner
there than anywhere else: the roller-makers, the belt and strap suppliers, the
men who could re-babbitt a bearing overnight. Growth fed on itself. The
industrial city was, among other things, a very efficient machine for reducing
the cost of solving industrial problems.

## The Walking City

Nobody could commute. Horse omnibuses were slow and priced for clerks rather
than piecers, and the working day left no margin for a long journey at either
end. A mill hand had to live within perhaps twenty minutes' walk of the mill
gate, which meant that as the mill workforce grew the same fixed ring of land
around it had to hold more and more people. Density was the only variable
available, and speculative builders pushed it as far as the ground allowed.

The result was a set of housing types now largely erased. The back-to-back
terrace, built in blocks four dwellings deep with party walls on three sides,
gave each family one room per floor, no through ventilation and no rear yard.
The court — a narrow entry from the street opening into a paved well surrounded
by dwellings — packed houses onto the interiors of blocks that had once been
gardens. In Liverpool and Manchester the cellars beneath such houses were let
separately as dwellings, below street level and below the water table, reached
down a few steps that also carried surface drainage. Much of this was built
quickly and badly by small speculators on short leases, with thin walls,
unseasoned timber, and party walls that stopped short of the roof. There were
no building standards to speak of before local improvement acts began imposing
them, and no general national code until the byelaw legislation of the
mid-1870s produced the wider, straighter, through-ventilated terraces with rear
alleys that survive in their millions.

What the courts and cellars lacked was not only air but water and drainage, and
that shortfall had consequences fierce enough to demand their own account. Here
the point is narrower: the physical congestion was not an accident of greed
alone. It was the geometric consequence of a labour market that required tens
of thousands of people to stand at machines at the same moment within walking
distance of a chimney.

## Who Came

Industrial cities did not, for most of the period, reproduce themselves. Death
rates in the worst districts were high enough that burials frequently exceeded
births, and the towns grew anyway because migrants kept arriving faster than
residents died. They came from the agricultural counties of the south and east,
where winter work was scarce and enclosure had thinned out the smallholding
class; from the Highlands after the clearances; from rural Wales; and above all
from Ireland, in a steady stream before the Famine and a flood after it.
Migration was usually short-distance and stepwise — a village to a market town,
a market town to the city — and it ran along chains of kin, so that particular
streets filled up with people from particular parishes and kept their accents
and their saints' days for a generation.

The newcomers found a labour market unlike anything in the countryside. It paid
in cash, weekly, and it paid women and children at rates that made a family
wage possible only by sending several members out to work. It was also brutally
cyclical. A downturn in the American cotton trade or a panic in the London
money market could put a whole town on short time within weeks, and there was no
garden, no gleaning and no common to fall back on. The friendly society, the
burial club, the pawnshop and the co-operative store were responses to that
exposure, and they became as characteristic of the industrial town as the mill
itself. So were the chapel and the public house, which between them supplied
most of the available sociability, and the mechanics' institute, the brass band
and the football club, which came later and were often founded by employers who
had views about how their hands should spend Saturday.

## Government Invented on the Run

The institutional problem was that these places had no government adequate to
them. Manchester, Birmingham and Leeds were governed into the nineteenth
century by manorial officers and parish vestries designed for a rural
population of a few thousand. What filled the gap first was the improvement
commission: a body created by a private act of Parliament for a specific town
and a specific list of powers — paving, lighting, watching, cleansing — funded
by a local rate and staffed by whichever ratepayers cared to serve. It was
piecemeal and often ineffective, but it established the principle that a town
could tax itself to buy collective goods.

Municipal incorporation followed, and with it a slow, contested expansion of
what a corporation might legitimately own and do. By the 1870s the more
ambitious councils had bought out the private gas and water companies, cleared
slums, built markets, libraries, baths and art galleries, and discovered that
running utilities at a modest profit was a way of holding down the rates.
Birmingham under Joseph Chamberlain became the showpiece of the approach, and
its critics called it gas-and-water socialism, which its practitioners were
happy enough to accept. The great town halls of the period — Leeds, Manchester,
Bradford — were arguments in stone about the dignity of municipal power, built
by men who had grown up in places that had none.

## Decompression

The walking city lasted only as long as walking was the only option. Horse
trams from the 1870s, electric trams from the 1890s, and suburban railways
running cheap early-morning fares under statutory obligation gradually let
people live further from work than they could afford to walk. The effect was to
sort the city by income along the tram routes: clerks and foremen moved out to
the new bylaw terraces and semi-detached villas on the edge, while the oldest
and densest districts at the centre were left to the poorest, to recent
migrants, and eventually to warehouses and offices that could pay more for the
land than any tenant could.

A handful of employers tried to solve the problem by building the city over
again from scratch. Titus Salt moved his whole mill and workforce out of
Bradford to Saltaire on the Aire; the Cadburys built Bournville, the Levers
Port Sunlight, each with its allotments, its institute, its careful absence of
a public house. They were genuinely better places to live and they were also
demonstrations, meant to be visited and argued from, and the arguments ran
straight into the garden city movement at the end of the century. But they
housed a rounding error of the industrial population. The vast majority of
people who made cotton, iron, wool, coal and ships lived in streets thrown up
in a hurry by builders whose names nobody recorded, on land whose value had
been created by the very crowding that made living on it hard.
