# From Newcomen to High-Pressure Steam

A Cornish engine house is built like a small fort: granite walls a yard thick at the base, a tall arched window at the front, and one wall heavier than all the others because the great cast-iron beam pivots on it. The outer end of that beam projects into the open air above the shaft and nods up and down, slowly, like the head of a drinking animal. Everything else is hidden inside. What the building is actually doing is holding a lever steady while the lever tries to tear itself loose, a dozen or so times a minute, for decades on end.

Those engines existed for one reason. Mines flood. Tin and copper workings in Cornwall, coal pits in Northumberland and Durham, lead mines in the Pennines — as the shallow ore was worked out, every one of them went deeper and every one of them ran into groundwater. An adit driven in from a hillside carried water off by gravity, but only down to the valley floor. Below that it had to be lifted, and lifting it with horses walking in circles cost more per gallon the deeper the shaft went. By the end of the seventeenth century there were rich seams that everybody knew about and nobody could work.

## Buying Vacuum with Coal

Thomas Savery patented a fire-driven pump in 1698 and sold it to mine owners as The Miner's Friend. It had no piston at all. Steam filled a closed vessel, cold water poured over the outside condensed it, and the vacuum drew water up a pipe; boiler steam then pushed that water on and out. But a vacuum will raise water only about thirty feet, and going deeper meant pressures the soldered joints of the period could not survive.

The machine that worked came from Thomas Newcomen, an ironmonger of Dartmouth who supplied tools to the Cornish mines and therefore understood precisely what the problem was worth. By 1712 his engines were at work. Newcomen put a piston in an open-topped cylinder and hung it from one end of a pivoted wooden beam, with the mine's heavy pump rods hanging from the other. The weight of the rods pulled their end of the beam down, which raised the piston and drew in steam at barely above atmospheric pressure. A jet of cold water was then squirted into the cylinder. The steam collapsed into a spoonful of water, the pressure beneath the piston fell almost to nothing, and the weight of the atmosphere — fifteen pounds on every square inch of a piston that might be several feet across — drove it down and hauled the pump rods up. Nothing in the cycle ran at any real pressure, which is why it could be built at all with the metalworking of the day.

The waste was enormous and perfectly obvious. Each stroke chilled the cylinder with the condensing spray, and each stroke then had to reheat that mass of metal with fresh steam before the next charge would fill it properly. Most of the fuel went into warming iron back up. At a colliery this hardly signified, since small coal was nearly worthless at the pithead. In Cornwall, where every ton of coal arrived by sea, it signified a great deal, and the mine adventurers there spent the eighteenth century complaining about their fuel bills.

## The Separate Condenser

In the winter of 1763 a model Newcomen engine belonging to the natural philosophy class at Glasgow University was sent to the college's instrument maker for repair. James Watt got it working and found that it would run only a few strokes before exhausting its little boiler. He measured what it consumed and found that the steam swallowed by each stroke was many times the volume of the cylinder. Thinking it through with the help of Joseph Black's idea of latent heat — that turning water into steam absorbs a great deal of heat without raising its temperature at all — he saw that the fault was structural. The cylinder had to be hot to accept steam and cold to condense it, and no amount of care would let a single piece of metal be both at once.

The remedy came to him, by his own later account, during a Sunday walk on Glasgow Green in 1765: condense the steam somewhere else. Connect the cylinder through a valve to a second vessel kept permanently cold and emptied by its own pump, open the valve, and the steam will rush out of the hot cylinder into the cold condenser of its own accord and collapse there. The working cylinder then never has to cool down. Watt went further and kept it deliberately hot, wrapping it in a steam jacket and closing the top so that steam, rather than cold outside air, pressed on the upper face of the piston. An engine built this way used roughly a third of the coal of a Newcomen engine doing the same work.

Turning the idea into a business took another ten years. Watt was careful, anxious and without capital; his first backer, John Roebuck of the Carron ironworks, went bankrupt; and the cylinders available to him were so far from round that much of the advantage leaked past the piston. Two things rescued the scheme. One was Matthew Boulton, the Birmingham manufacturer who took over Roebuck's share in 1775 and supplied both money and the Soho works. The other was John Wilkinson's boring mill, devised for cannon, which could machine a large cylinder straight enough for a packed piston to seal against it. Watt's engine was not buildable until somebody else could make an accurate hole.

Boulton and Watt then did something nearly as original as the condenser: they sold savings instead of machines. The customer built his own engine house, bought much of the ironwork locally, and paid the partnership an annual premium fixed at a share — generally a third — of the coal saved against a Newcomen engine of equal duty. Calculating that saving required measurement, which is part of why the firm became so preoccupied with instruments. Parliament extended Watt's 1769 patent to the end of the century, so the arrangement held for twenty-five years and made both men rich.

## Making It Go Round

Pumping is reciprocating work, and for pumping the nodding beam is a natural fit. Mills, forges and breweries wanted a turning shaft instead. A crank makes that conversion, as every treadle lathe proves, but the application of the crank to a steam engine had been patented by somebody else. Watt's shop went around the obstruction with the sun-and-planet gear: a pinion fixed rigidly to the connecting rod walks around a wheel on the output shaft, turning it twice for every stroke of the piston, with the incidental benefit of gearing up the speed.

A rotative engine raised a second difficulty. A pump does not care if one stroke comes slowly; a mill full of spindles cares very much. Watt fitted a centrifugal governor borrowed from millwrights' practice — two weighted arms on a spinning spindle that fly outward as speed rises and, through a linkage, close down the throttle valve. It is a feedback loop, built and sold a century before anyone had the mathematics to describe its behaviour, and it held mill shafting steady enough to spin fine yarn.

Other refinements followed. Double action admitted steam alternately to both faces of the piston, so the engine pushed on the return stroke as well. The parallel motion, a linkage of three bars that Watt regarded as his most ingenious piece of work, let a rigid piston rod connect to the swinging arc of a beam without bending anything. And the indicator, developed in the works by Watt's assistant John Southern, traced a closed loop of steam pressure against piston position on a card. The area inside that loop is the work done in the stroke. The firm kept the device to itself for years, because it let them diagnose a sick engine and prove their fuel savings while everyone else was guessing. Output also needed a unit a customer could picture, and the one that stuck was Watt's own horsepower, reckoned from what a strong mill horse could do hauling on a rope. It was a generous horse, and it has outlived the engines it was invented to sell.

## Strong Steam

Everything Watt built ran at barely more than atmospheric pressure, because he thought high pressure reckless, and given the boilermaking of his day he was largely right. The breadth of his patent gave that opinion the force of law until 1800. Within a few years of its expiry Richard Trevithick, a Cornishman with none of Watt's caution, was building engines that dispensed with the condenser entirely and simply exhausted to the air. That throws away the vacuum, but it also throws away a large, heavy, water-hungry piece of apparatus, and at several atmospheres a small cylinder can do the work of a very large one.

This is a change in kind rather than degree. A Watt engine's power was a function of its size; a high-pressure engine's power came out of the steam, so the machine itself could shrink. Trevithick put one on wheels on a Camborne road in 1801 and on rails at Penydarren in 1804, where it hauled iron and passengers and cracked the tramway's cast-iron plates under its own weight. The same logic produced Cornwall's giant pumping engines, which used strong steam expansively — admit a short charge, shut the valve, and let the trapped steam push the piston down the rest of the stroke as its own pressure falls away. Less work per stroke, far more work per pound of coal. Cornish mine agents took to publishing the duty of their engines, the weight of water raised one foot for a bushel of coal burned, and engineers competed in public to raise it.

Expansion led naturally to compounding: take steam that has finished its work in a small high-pressure cylinder and let it expand again in a larger one, so the total temperature drop is divided and neither cylinder is chilled across the whole range. Jonathan Hornblower built such an engine in the 1780s and was throttled by Watt's patent; Arthur Woolf later made the principle pay in Cornwall. By the end of the century triple-expansion engines were driving ships across oceans.

The bill for strong steam was paid in boiler explosions. A riveted shell holding water at fifty or a hundred pounds to the square inch stores a great deal of energy, and when a seam lets go the superheated water flashes to steam at once and the boiler leaves the building. Safety valves were fitted, and were sometimes screwed down by men paid by output. Associations of mill owners eventually clubbed together to employ inspectors of their own, mostly because a neighbour's explosion was bad for the whole trade.

It is worth being clear about the order of events. Engines had been draining mines and driving mills for well over a century before anyone had a correct account of what heat is. Sadi Carnot, a young French officer trying to understand why British engines were so much better than French ones, published a short book in 1824 arguing that the work obtainable from heat depends on the temperature difference through which it falls, and not at all on the substance carrying it. He still imagined heat as a fluid and was nearly ignored for twenty years, but the argument was right, and it fixed a ceiling on efficiency no valve gear could exceed. Practical men had been running a heat engine profitably for generations without knowing, in any deep sense, what they were doing. The theory, when it arrived, did not tell them to build something else. It told them how much of what they were already chasing was actually there to be had.
