# Canals, Navvies and the Coming of the Railway

In 1761 people travelled some distance to stand beside the River Irwell at Barton and watch boats sail over their heads. The Duke of Bridgewater's new canal crossed the river on a stone aqueduct nearly forty feet up, and the sight of a loaded barge moving calmly through the air above another waterway struck contemporaries as faintly indecent, a trick that ought not to work. It worked because a canal is not a river. It is a long, level, artificial pond with a waterproof floor, and once you accept that, there is no particular reason it cannot be carried over a valley, through a hill, or up a staircase of locks.

The reason anyone went to that trouble was the price of moving heavy things. A pack horse carries something like an eighth of a ton on its back. Harnessed to a cart on a good turnpike it might pull a couple of tons, on a bad road in winter rather less, and in many districts the roads were genuinely impassable to wheels for months of the year. Put the same horse on a towpath with a rope over its shoulder and it will move thirty or fifty tons of coal at a walking pace all day. That ratio is the whole economic argument for canals, and it applies with particular force to goods that are cheap, heavy and useless unless delivered in bulk. Coal, limestone, iron, grain, pottery clay, manure.

## The Duke's Ditch

Francis Egerton, third Duke of Bridgewater, owned coal at Worsley that he could not sell in Manchester at a competitive price because the last few miles of cartage ate the margin. His solution, executed by the millwright James Brindley and the Duke's agent John Gilbert, was to cut a canal from the mine to the town — and not merely to the mine, but into it, so that boats were loaded underground at the coal face along miles of navigable levels driven into the hillside. When the canal opened, the price of coal in Manchester is said to have fallen by roughly half, and every landowner and merchant in England drew the obvious conclusion.

Brindley became the presiding engineer of the first canal generation, and his instincts shaped British waterways for a century. He distrusted locks, since every lock costs water and time, so he took his canals around the contours of hills in long meandering levels that look absurd on a map and were cheap to build. He lined the channel with puddled clay, worked with water and trodden by men and cattle until it was plastic and impermeable, a technique so effective that the same material still holds most of the system. And he built narrow: locks about seven feet wide, taking a boat seventy feet long and six feet ten inches in the beam, which set the size of the English narrowboat permanently and, less happily, limited what the network could ever carry.

The Trent and Mersey was his great scheme, cutting across the country from one tidal river to the other and tunnelling through Harecastle hill. Josiah Wedgwood cut the first sod, for reasons that had nothing to do with civic ceremony: he made fragile goods, packhorse carriage broke them in quantity, and a canal running past his new works at Etruria meant flint and clay in and finished ware out, intact. Others followed in numbers through the 1780s and 1790s, until the promotion of canal companies became a speculative frenzy in which schemes were subscribed by people who could not have found the route on a map. Some of the resulting canals never paid a dividend. The engineering, meanwhile, grew more confident: Thomas Telford, working with William Jessop, carried the Ellesmere Canal across the Dee valley at Pontcysyllte in a cast-iron trough on stone piers, a structure that still looks like an act of nerve.

## Where the Water Ran Out

Canals had hard limits, and they were reached quickly. The most awkward was water itself. A canal loses a lockful every time a boat goes through, and the summit level, being the highest point, has no natural supply; companies built reservoirs, cut feeders from distant streams, and pumped water back uphill with steam engines to keep trading. A dry summer stopped traffic. So did a hard frost, sometimes for weeks, and ice-breaking boats rocked by gangs of men were a regular winter expense.

Then there was speed. A horse walks. A journey from the Midlands to London took days, which is tolerable for coal and intolerable for anything perishable or urgent. Lock flights created queues. Neighbouring companies had built to incompatible dimensions, so cargo bound across the country might need transshipment, and each company charged its own tolls on its own stretch. A canal monopoly, once established, behaved exactly as monopolies do. The Liverpool merchants who later financed a railway to Manchester were not animated by a love of locomotives. They were annoyed about carriage rates on the Bridgewater.

## From Waggonway to Locomotive

The other half of the answer had been sitting in the coalfields for two centuries. Tyneside collieries laid wooden rails from pit to riverside staith, so that a horse could draw a loaded chaldron wagon down a graded route and bring the empty back up, and by the early eighteenth century these waggonways included embankments and masonry bridges built purely for freight. Wooden rails wore out and were plated with iron, then replaced by cast iron, then by rolled wrought iron, which did not snap under a heavy wheel. Two rival traditions grew up: plateways with an L-shaped rail guiding plain wheels, and edge rails with the flange on the wheel. The second won, and every railway wheel since has carried its guidance with it.

What the waggonways lacked was a prime mover better than a horse. Richard Trevithick supplied one at Penydarren in South Wales in 1804, settling a wager between ironmasters by running a high-pressure locomotive along a tramroad with ten tons of iron and a crowd of passengers behind it. It worked, and it broke the cast-iron plates, which is the recurring problem of early locomotives: the engine is heavier than the track was designed for. For twenty years locomotives remained a colliery speciality, useful where coal was free and the track could be rebuilt.

The Stockton and Darlington, opened in 1825 to bring south Durham coal to the Tees, was conceived as an iron turnpike. Anyone could pay a toll and run their own wagons on it, hauled by their own horses; the company also used stationary engines on the inclines and a few locomotives built by George Stephenson, who had come up through the Killingworth pits as an engineman. The mixture was chaotic and instructive. Running a public railway turned out to require controlling who moved on it and when — a discovery that made railways into operating companies rather than landlords.

The Liverpool and Manchester, opened in 1830, settled the rest. It was double track, locomotive-worked throughout, carried passengers and goods between two large cities, and it had to cross Chat Moss, a peat bog that swallowed the first attempts at embankment until the line was floated across on a raft of brushwood and heather. To choose a form of power the directors held a competition at Rainhill in 1829, where Robert Stephenson's Rocket ran away with the prize by combining a boiler full of small tubes with an exhaust blast up the chimney, so that working the engine hard made its own fire burn harder. The opening ceremony killed William Huskisson, a former cabinet minister, who stepped down onto the opposite track in front of an approaching engine — the first casualty of a kind of accident that nobody had yet learned to fear. The line's other surprise was commercial. Built for cotton, it made a great deal of its money carrying people.

## Navvies, Mania and the Reordering of the Map

The lines were dug by men who had learned the trade on the canals, where they were called navigators and then, inevitably, navvies. They worked with pick, shovel, barrow and black powder, living in camps near the works, moving on when the contract ended. On deep cuttings they ran loaded barrows up steep planks counterbalanced by a horse at the top, a practice that killed and maimed men routinely. Kilsby tunnel on the London and Birmingham hit quicksand that had to be pumped continuously for months. The Woodhead tunnel through the Pennines became notorious for its casualties. Britain's railway earthworks were built almost entirely by muscle, and the profession that built them was rough, well paid by the standards of farm labour, and widely feared by the villages it passed through.

Capital arrived in waves. The mania of the mid-1840s saw Parliament pass hundreds of railway acts in a few sessions, share prices climb on schemes with no prospect of traffic, and George Hudson, the York draper who had assembled a sprawling empire of lines, presented as a financial genius until his accounting was examined. The bubble burst and ruined a great many investors. It also left behind, at their expense, most of a national network.

One argument had to be settled before that network could function. George Stephenson had adopted a gauge of four feet eight and a half inches from waggonway practice; Isambard Kingdom Brunel built the Great Western to seven feet, correctly arguing that it rode better and could carry more. Where the two systems met, at Gloucester, every passenger and every parcel had to change trains, and the resulting spectacle helped persuade a royal commission and then Parliament to standardize on the narrower gauge in 1846. The Great Western went on running broad-gauge trains for another forty-six years before converting the last of its main line over a single weekend.

What followed was less a transport improvement than a rearrangement of ordinary life. Fresh fish reached inland towns; milk came in from country stations instead of from cows kept in cellars; newspapers printed in London were read the same day in Newcastle. The 1844 Railway Regulation Act obliged companies to run at least one cheap train a day over every line, in covered carriages, at a penny a mile, which put long journeys within reach of people who had never made one. Excursion traffic invented the day trip. And because a timetable cannot work when every town keeps its own solar noon, the companies adopted London time and had it distributed by telegraph, so that the clock on the station wall gradually became the clock everyone else set their watch by.
