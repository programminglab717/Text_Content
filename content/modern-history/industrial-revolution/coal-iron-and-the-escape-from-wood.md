# Coal, Iron and the Escape from Wood

Charcoal is made by stacking cordwood into a dome, covering it with turf and earth, setting it alight at the centre and then starving it of air for several days while a collier sits up at night watching for breaches in the crust. What comes out is almost pure carbon, light, clean-burning, hot. What goes in is a great deal of timber. A single charcoal blast furnace, running a campaign of a few months a year, consumed the sustainable annual growth of several thousand acres of coppice woodland, and it had to be woodland close by, because charcoal is too fragile to cart far without crumbling to dust.

That is the constraint worth holding in mind before anything else about the industrial revolution makes sense. Every economy before the eighteenth century ran on the products of photosynthesis in the current year or the last few decades: food for people, fodder for draught animals, timber and charcoal for heat, wind and falling water for the little mechanical work that was not done by muscle. All of it was bounded by land. You could make more iron only by planting more trees or by taking land away from grain, and you could not do either quickly. The economic historian E. A. Wrigley has made this the centre of his account of British industrialization, and the framing is hard to improve on: the question is not why output grew, but how a society got out from under an annual energy budget fixed by acreage.

Britain got out from under it by digging.

## The Advantages of a Wet Island with Coal in It

British coal was unusually accessible. Seams outcropped at the surface in the North East, in South Wales, in Lanarkshire, in Yorkshire and the Midlands, and in Staffordshire where the Ten Yard Seam was thick enough to be quarried almost like stone. Better still, much of it lay near navigable water. Coal from Tyne and Wear had been shipped down the east coast to London since the Middle Ages, in such quantity that Londoners called it sea coal and complained bitterly about the smoke; John Evelyn wrote a pamphlet in the 1660s proposing to move the offending trades out of the city altogether. That coastal trade built a fleet, trained seamen, and taught a whole industry how to handle bulk cargo long before anything recognizable as a factory existed.

Coal first replaced wood in the crude business of making heat: evaporating brine for salt, burning limestone, firing bricks, melting glass, drying malt, and warming houses. Several of those trades had to be reinvented in the process, because coal smoke spoils what it touches. Glassmakers put their batch in covered pots. Maltsters and brewers learned to use coke rather than raw coal so the smoke would not taint the beer, which incidentally explains why pale ales became possible. Each of these adaptations was a small technical education in how to burn the mineral fuel without ruining the product, and the accumulated lessons were waiting when somebody tried the same thing with iron.

## Deeper, Wetter, More Dangerous

Shallow coal runs out. Bell pits — a shaft down to the seam, the coal worked outwards until the roof threatened, then abandoned and another sunk beside it — gave way to proper shafts with pillars of coal left standing to hold up the roof, and eventually to longwall working, where the whole face advances and the roof behind is allowed to settle onto packed waste. Depth brought three enemies: water, bad air and the sheer difficulty of getting men and coal up and down.

Water was answered by the steam engine, which is the tightest feedback loop in the whole period. Newcomen engines burned coal to drain coal mines, at a site where the cost of fuel was near zero, and every engine sold made deeper pits possible, which produced more coal, which fed more engines. Winding followed: horse gins gave way to steam winding engines, hemp rope to wire, corves to iron tubs on rails.

Bad air was harder. Coal seams hold methane, and a mine must be ventilated by forcing a current of air down one shaft, around every working place, and up another, usually by keeping a furnace burning at the bottom of the upcast shaft to draw the column. The air was steered through the workings by wooden doors, and the doors were opened and shut by small children who sat alone in the dark for the length of a shift. If the current failed or a fall of roof released gas suddenly, firedamp gathered at the roof and waited for a naked flame. After an explosion killed most of the men and boys at Felling colliery in 1812, a society of Durham gentlemen asked Humphry Davy to find a remedy, and he produced the safety lamp — a flame enclosed in fine wire gauze, which conducts heat away fast enough that the flame cannot pass through to ignite gas outside. George Stephenson, a colliery engineman, arrived at a similar lamp at almost the same time, and the two sides argued about priority for years.

The lamp is a genuinely elegant piece of physics, and its consequences were not what its inventors intended. A lamp that could be carried safely into gassy ground was immediately used to work gassy ground that had previously been left alone. Deaths did not fall. The chief effect of the safety lamp was to extend the reach of mining, which is a pattern worth recognizing: a safety device that lowers the cost of danger tends to be spent on more danger.

What eventually restrained the industry was not technology but evidence. A royal commission on children's employment sent investigators underground in the early 1840s and published their findings with illustrations — women harnessed to loaded tubs crawling along low roadways, boys of six or seven working as trappers — and the reaction in Parliament produced the Mines Act of 1842, which barred women and girls and boys under ten from working below ground. It was an incomplete law, weakly inspected at first. It was also the first time anyone had told a British industry that a category of person could not be employed at all.

## Coke, and the Iron It Made Possible

Iron smelting was the last major use of charcoal to give way, because iron is the fussiest customer. Raw coal cannot be charged into a blast furnace: it softens and crushes under the weight above it, choking the passage of gas, and its sulphur ruins the metal. Coke — coal baked in heaps or ovens to drive off the volatile matter — is hard, porous and strong enough to carry a tall burden without collapsing, which is exactly the property charcoal lacks. Abraham Darby achieved coke smelting at Coalbrookdale in 1709 and used the resulting iron for thin-walled cast pots, a market he understood because he had been in the brass trade.

The technique spread slowly for half a century, partly because coke iron was ill-suited to the forge, and partly because charcoal iron remained available. When it did spread, the effect was compounding: coke furnaces could be built larger than charcoal ones, and larger furnaces needed stronger blast, which meant steam-driven blowing cylinders rather than water-driven bellows, which meant furnaces could be sited on coalfields rather than on streams.

Turning brittle pig iron into tough, forgeable wrought iron was the remaining bottleneck, and it was still being done with charcoal in small hearths. Henry Cort's puddling process of the 1780s put the pig iron in a reverberatory furnace, where the flame plays over the metal from a separate grate and the coal never touches it. A puddler stirred the melt with a long bar while carbon burned out of it and the iron gradually stiffened into pasty lumps that he gathered and dragged out to be hammered and rolled. It was appalling work, done in front of an open furnace mouth, and it wore men out young. It also multiplied output enormously and made possible the rails, boiler plate, bridges and ship frames of the following century. Cort himself lost his patents and died in poverty after his partner was found to have embezzled the money that backed them.

One more change belongs here. James Beaumont Neilson, a Glasgow gas engineer, found in 1828 that heating the blast before blowing it into the furnace cut fuel consumption sharply rather than, as intuition suggested, wasting heat. Hot blast turned Scotland's abundant but stubborn blackband ironstone into a cheap raw material and made the Clyde an iron district within a decade.

## What the Coalfields Looked Like Afterwards

The coal measures are a layered sequence, and coal is not the only useful thing in them. Ironstone occurs in the same strata, and so does fireclay for furnace linings, with limestone for flux often outcropping nearby. That is why the industrial map of Britain looks the way it does: the Black Country, Merthyr and the Welsh valleys, Coatbridge and Airdrie, the West Riding, each a place where fuel, ore and flux could be raised within a few miles of one another and moved by canal, tramway and then railway to the coast.

Living there was another matter. Pit villages went up without drains. Spoil heaps rose and sometimes slid. Subsidence cracked the houses that mining had paid for. Winding accidents, roof falls, inrushes and explosions killed steadily, and the dust filled miners' lungs with a slow disease that was treated for most of the century as an unremarkable feature of the trade. The escape from the land constraint was real — Britain in the nineteenth century commanded heat and mechanical work on a scale no organic economy could have approached — but it was an escape financed by drawing down a stock rather than living off a flow, and by concentrating the cost of doing so in a small number of districts and a particular set of lungs.
