# How Cotton Spinning Was Mechanized

A woman at a spinning wheel makes one thread. She draws a few fibres out of a mass of carded cotton with her fingers, lets the twist run up into them, and winds the result onto a spindle, and then she does it again, and the rhythm of that is roughly the rhythm of a heartbeat. She cannot make two threads at once, because she has only one pair of hands and the drawing-out must be felt rather than watched. Everything that happened to Lancashire in the space of two generations comes out of attempts to get round that single sentence.

Before the machines, cloth was made under what is usually called the putting-out system. A merchant bought raw fibre, distributed it to households across a district, collected the yarn, put it out again to weavers, and collected cloth. Nobody owned a workplace; the equipment sat in cottages, and the family decided when to work. The arrangement had one structural weakness, and it was arithmetic. Spinning is slower than weaving. It took the output of several spinners to keep one weaver at his loom, which meant that a weaving household depended on women and children within a wide radius, and that any increase in weaving speed immediately starved the loom.

## The Yarn Famine

That increase arrived in 1733, when John Kay patented the flying shuttle. Instead of passing the weft from hand to hand through the warp, the weaver jerked a cord and a hammer knocked the shuttle along a grooved race, out and back. One man could now weave cloth wider than his own arm span, and could weave it considerably faster. Adoption was slow and Kay himself did badly out of it, but by the middle of the century weavers across the north were complaining that they could not get yarn. Spinners raised their prices. Merchants offered premiums for machines that would spin more.

The prizes attracted a great deal of failure first. Lewis Paul and John Wyatt tried roller spinning in the 1730s, drawing a rope of cotton through successive pairs of rollers, the second pair turning faster than the first so that the fibres slid past each other and the rope thinned. The principle was exactly right and remains the basis of spinning today. The execution defeated them: the spacing of the rollers has to be matched to the length of the fibre, the pressure on them has to be nicely judged, and cotton that is not properly prepared simply snaps or clogs. Their mills failed.

## Jenny, Frame and Mule

James Hargreaves, a weaver and carpenter in Lancashire, solved a different piece of the problem in the 1760s. His spinning jenny mounted a row of spindles in a frame and clamped the rovings in a sliding bar. Draw the bar away from the spindles while turning them and every roving is drawn out and twisted at once; push the bar back with a wire guiding the threads down and every spindle winds on. It duplicated the hand spinner's motion rather than replacing it, which is why one person could work eight spindles at first and dozens later. Crucially, the jenny was small, cheap, hand-cranked and could sit in a cottage, so it spread through the domestic system rather than displacing it. Its yarn was soft and loosely twisted, fine for weft but too weak for warp.

Richard Arkwright, a Preston barber and wig-maker with an instinct for other people's ideas and for capital, went back to rollers. His water frame, patented in 1769, drew the roving through weighted roller pairs and then twisted and wound it in one continuous operation with a flyer running around a bobbin, the arrangement already familiar from the Saxony wheel. The yarn it produced was hard, strong and even — warp yarn. Before the water frame, British cotton goods mostly had linen warps with cotton weft, because no home-spun cotton thread could stand the tension of the loom. After it, cloth could be cotton all through.

A water frame was too heavy to crank by hand, and that fact reorganized an industry. Arkwright, with the hosiers Jedediah Strutt and Samuel Need behind him, built a mill at Cromford in the Derwent valley in 1771 and took his power from water — partly from a mine drainage level whose flow was warm enough not to freeze in winter, which tells you how carefully these sites were chosen. The building was several storeys of identical rooms, lit by rows of small windows, with a bell and a gate and two shifts working around the clock. Most of the hands were children, because the work was light and repetitive and children were cheap and could be indentured as parish apprentices. Arkwright built houses, a chapel and an inn, held a village fair, and ran a workforce to a timetable that nothing in rural England had prepared anyone for.

Samuel Crompton, working quietly at Hall i' th' Wood near Bolton, put the two lines together around 1779. His mule — the name acknowledges the hybrid — passed the roving through drafting rollers and then handed it to spindles mounted on a carriage that ran out on rails away from the rollers. The carriage drew the thread further as it went and twisted it as it drew, so the fibre was stretched gently and progressively rather than yanked; then the carriage returned and the thread wound on. The mule made yarn that was both fine and strong, in counts nobody in Europe had spun before, and it let Lancashire compete with the muslins of Bengal. Crompton had no money to patent it, showed it to his neighbours, and watched it spread. A parliamentary grant many years later was thin compensation.

## The Chain Around the Spindle

A spinning machine is only ever one link. Feed it badly prepared cotton and it stops every few seconds, so mechanization ran backwards down the chain into carding, where wire-covered cylinders comb the fibres into a continuous web, and into roving frames that give that web just enough twist to survive handling. It ran forwards too. The strong warp had to be sized with paste to stand up to abrasion in the loom; the finished cloth had to be bleached, and bleaching had traditionally meant months spread out on grass crofts, soaked in sour milk and lye, occupying land near every textile town. Chlorine chemistry ended that. Bleaching powder, made commercially in Glasgow around the end of the century, reduced months to days and released the fields.

The raw material came from somewhere too, and this is the part of the story that a machine diagram conceals. Britain grew no cotton. Early supplies came from the West Indies and Brazil; the cotton that fed the mills through the great expansion came overwhelmingly from the American South, where Eli Whitney's gin of 1793 made short-staple upland cotton worth growing, and where growing it meant slavery on an expanding frontier. The mechanization of spinning in Lancashire and the spread of the plantation system across Alabama and Mississippi were not two histories that happened to coincide. They were one commercial system with an ocean in the middle of it.

## Why Lancashire

Geography did not have to produce a single cotton district, but it did. Lancashire had damp air off the Irish Sea, which matters more than it sounds: cotton fibre is stronger and less brittle at high humidity, and a mill in a dry climate breaks more threads. It had soft water running off gritstone hills, useful for bleaching and dyeing. It had Liverpool, the port that handled the raw cotton and the export cloth. It had coal directly beneath it. And it had an existing textile trade in fustians, with merchants who already understood how to organize dispersed production.

Water power came first, which put the early mills in valleys — the Derwent, the streams of the Pennine edge — where the fall was reliable. Steam untied that knot. Once a rotative engine could turn line shafting steadily, a mill could be built where the coal, the labour and the warehouses were, and Manchester, Oldham, Bolton and Stockport filled with tall brick blocks. Fire was the standing terror of a building full of oil, cotton dust and candles, so builders learned to use iron columns and brick arches instead of timber floors, an approach worked out in a flax mill at Shrewsbury in the 1790s and copied for decades afterwards.

## The Loom Catches Up, Slowly

Spinning ran ahead of weaving for half a century. Edmund Cartwright, a clergyman with no practical knowledge of the trade, patented a power loom in 1785 and it was close to useless; the difficulty was never the basic motion but everything around it. The warp had to be dressed and sized before it could be woven mechanically. The machine had to stop instantly when a thread broke, or it would weave a fault into yards of cloth. Getting reliable stopping motions and a loom that could be tended by one person watching several machines took the work of Horrocks, Roberts and others into the 1820s.

Until then, cheap machine yarn made handloom weaving extremely attractive. Weavers earned well in the 1790s, took apprentices, and were joined by tens of thousands of newcomers with nothing but a loom in a cellar. Then power looms arrived in numbers and the trade collapsed in slow motion across the 1820s, 1830s and 1840s, with wages sinking year by year while men kept weaving because there was nothing else they knew and the loom was already paid for. The handloom weaver became the standing image of what mechanization did to people who had backed the wrong process.

Spinning changed differently. When Richard Roberts produced a self-acting mule in the 1820s, the machine took over the skilled judgement of the carriage's return, but the mule that resulted was enormous and needed a man to mind it and children called piecers to run along the carriage joining broken ends. The trade stayed male, stayed relatively well paid, and defended itself through organization for the rest of the century. Two machines descended from the same idea, and they produced two entirely different kinds of working life.
