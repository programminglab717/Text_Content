# Negotiating the End of Apartheid

The hall where South Africa was rebuilt sat next to the runways at Johannesburg's
airport. Delegations from more than twenty parties — a governing National Party
that had run the country for four decades, a liberation movement legalized only
three years earlier, Zulu traditionalists, white separatists, homeland
administrations, communists, and several organizations with almost no following at
all — worked through constitutional drafts in a conference centre at Kempton Park
while jets climbed overhead. Outside, in the townships of the Vaal and the hostels
of the Reef, people were being killed at a rate that made the negotiations look
like a fiction the participants had agreed to maintain.

What happened there is genuinely unusual. A government with an intact army, a
capable intelligence service, nuclear weapons it had already decided to dismantle,
and no prospect of battlefield defeat negotiated itself out of power and handed the
state to the movement it had spent thirty years imprisoning. Understanding why
requires taking seriously both what apartheid actually was and what had stopped
working about it.

## The Machinery

Segregation in South Africa long predated 1948. What the National Party built after
its election victory that year was something more systematic: an administrative
apparatus that assigned every person a racial classification by law, and then made
almost every other fact about their life follow from it. The Population Registration
Act created the classification. The Group Areas Act determined where members of each
group could live and trade, and was used to clear established communities — District
Six in Cape Town, Sophiatown in Johannesburg — and dump their residents on the urban
periphery. Separate and deliberately inferior schooling was legislated for African
children, its architect arguing openly in parliament that there was no point
educating them for positions they would never be allowed to hold.

Underneath all of it lay labour control. The pass laws required African men, and
later women, to carry documents authorizing their presence in white urban areas, and
hundreds of thousands were arrested every year for failing to produce them. The
homelands policy extended the logic to its conclusion: ten rural territories, poor
and fragmented, were designated as the national homes of African ethnic groups, and
Africans were progressively stripped of South African citizenship and made nationals
of places most of them had never lived. Four homelands were declared independent
during the 1970s and 1980s. No other country recognized any of them.

Resistance ran throughout, and so did repression. Police fired on a crowd
protesting the pass laws at Sharpeville in 1960 and killed sixty-nine people, many
shot in the back. The African National Congress and the Pan Africanist Congress were
banned, turned to sabotage, and had their leadership captured; Nelson Mandela and
his co-accused received life sentences at the Rivonia trial and went to Robben
Island. Schoolchildren in Soweto marched against compulsory instruction in Afrikaans
in 1976 and were shot at, which set off a national revolt and drove a generation
into exile and into the underground.

## Why the Government Moved

By the late 1980s several pressures had converged. The township uprising that began
in 1984 against a constitutional reform giving parliamentary representation to
coloured and Indian South Africans while excluding Africans entirely had made local
government unworkable across much of the country. Successive states of emergency,
mass detentions, and troops in the townships contained the revolt without ending it.

Money mattered more than is usually credited. In August 1985 P.W. Botha gave a
speech in Durban that had been trailed as the announcement of fundamental reform and
turned out to be a defiant refusal to be pushed. Foreign banks, already nervous,
declined to roll over South Africa's short-term debt, and the government was forced
into a standstill on repayments and exchange controls. That was the sanction that
bit hardest, and it was imposed by lenders rather than legislatures. Formal measures
followed, including an American sanctions act passed over a presidential veto, along
with a steady withdrawal of foreign firms under pressure from shareholders and
campus campaigns.

The strategic arguments were dissolving too. An expensive war in Angola and the
occupation of Namibia were settled at the end of the decade, and Namibia became
independent. The Soviet Union was retreating from foreign commitments and would soon
cease to exist, which removed both the ANC's principal external backer and the
government's most useful justification for calling every opponent a communist proxy.
Business leaders were increasingly saying in public that an economy needing skilled
labour could not keep the majority of its population badly educated and legally
immobile. Then Botha suffered a stroke in 1989 and was manoeuvred out by his own
cabinet, and F.W. de Klerk, a party loyalist with no reformist reputation, took over.

## Talks Before the Talks

Contact had been going on quietly for years. Mandela, moved off Robben Island and
eventually into a warder's cottage at Victor Verster prison, had opened discussions
with the justice minister and the head of the intelligence service, keeping his
colleagues informed but negotiating essentially alone and at some risk to his
standing. He met Botha for tea shortly before the stroke. Separately, groups of
Afrikaner academics, journalists, and businessmen had been meeting exiled ANC
officials in Senegal and in England, discovering that the people they had been told
were terrorists were lawyers and intellectuals who talked about constitutions.

On the second of February 1990, de Klerk opened parliament by announcing the
unbanning of the ANC, the PAC, and the Communist Party, the release of political
prisoners, and a moratorium on executions. Mandela walked out of Victor Verster nine
days later, after twenty-seven years, and gave a speech that reassured almost nobody
who had hoped he had mellowed.

## Four Years on a Knife Edge

The negotiations that followed were repeatedly wrecked and repeatedly restarted.
Early agreements covered the return of exiles and the suspension of the armed
struggle. A formal convention convened at the end of 1991 and deadlocked the
following May over the majorities required to adopt a constitution — the government
wanted thresholds high enough to give minority parties an effective veto.

Then came the killings. A massacre at Boipatong in June 1992 led the ANC to walk out
of talks and launch a campaign of strikes and marches; in September, Ciskei homeland
soldiers fired on one of those marches at Bisho and killed around thirty people.
Violence between ANC supporters and the Inkatha Freedom Party in Natal and on the
Reef killed thousands over these years, and evidence accumulated that elements of
the security forces were arming and training one side — a shadowy capacity the press
called the third force. Whether de Klerk controlled it, tolerated it, or merely
failed to stop it remains the most contested question of the period.

Two things kept the process alive. One was a referendum de Klerk called among white
voters in March 1992, asking whether he should continue negotiating; roughly two
thirds said yes, which settled the question of whether his own community would
follow him. The other was a proposal from Joe Slovo, the Communist Party leader, for
what he called sunset clauses: a government of national unity sharing power for a
fixed transitional period, guarantees of employment and pensions for existing civil
servants and soldiers, and a mechanism for amnesty. Slovo's point was blunt. The ANC
had not won a war, the other side still controlled the state's coercive apparatus,
and a settlement that left every general and tax official facing unemployment and
prosecution would not be signed.

The closest call came in April 1993, when Chris Hani — the most popular figure in
the movement after Mandela, and the one with the most credibility among angry young
militants — was shot dead outside his house by a Polish immigrant acting with a
white conservative parliamentarian. The assassins were caught because an Afrikaans
neighbour had written down the car's registration and called the police. Mandela
went on state television, a leader with no office speaking as though he already held
one, and told the country that a white woman's action had made the arrest possible
and that the response must be discipline rather than revenge. Within days the
parties had set an election date.

An interim constitution was agreed in November 1993, binding the future elected
assembly to a list of principles it could not abandon. Right-wing resistance
collapsed in an ugly farce in March 1994, when armed white paramilitaries drove into
Bophuthatswana to prop up its ruler and were shot dead beside their cars by the
homeland's own police in front of television crews. Inkatha, which had boycotted
until the final week, was added to the ballots with adhesive stickers.

## What the Bargain Bought, and What It Left

Voting began on the twenty-seventh of April 1994, and the images are the ones that
survived: queues kilometres long winding across open ground, people standing for
hours in the sun, many of them elderly and voting for the first time. The ANC took
just under two thirds of the vote, short of the share that would have let it write
the constitution alone. Mandela was inaugurated in May, with de Klerk as one of his
deputies.

The amnesty problem was handed to a commission chaired by Desmond Tutu, which offered
individual amnesty in exchange for full public disclosure of politically motivated
crimes — neither trials nor forgetting, but testimony traded for immunity, delivered
in hearings broadcast to the country. Its findings implicated the state far more
heavily than the liberation movements, and its reparations recommendations were
largely ignored.

What the negotiations did not touch was ownership. Property rights were written into
the constitution, the central bank kept its independence, and land redistribution was
left to a willing-buyer process that has moved very little land. South Africa
acquired one of the most admired constitutions in the world and kept one of the most
unequal economies, and the distance between those two facts has defined its politics
ever since.
