# What It Takes to Eradicate a Disease

In October 1977 a hospital cook in Merca, Somalia, named Ali Maow Maalin came
down with a rash. He had helped guide a vehicle carrying two children with
smallpox to an isolation ward, without being vaccinated himself, and he
caught it. He recovered. He was the last person on earth to contract smallpox
naturally, and the teams that traced and vaccinated everyone he had been near
spent the following weeks confirming that the chain stopped with him. The
World Health Organization certified the world free of the disease in 1980,
which made smallpox the only human infection ever driven out of existence.

It is worth being clear about how unusual that is. Eradication has been
attempted for several diseases and achieved for one. The reasons have less to
do with heroism than with biology, logistics and politics in roughly equal
measure, and a campaign that misjudges any of the three fails no matter how
much money it has.

## Why Smallpox Could Be Beaten

A disease is a plausible candidate for eradication only if several conditions
happen to hold at once, and smallpox held nearly all of them.

It had no animal reservoir. The virus lived in humans and nowhere else, so
interrupting human-to-human transmission everywhere ended it permanently.
Yellow fever, by contrast, cycles endlessly in forest monkeys and can never
be eradicated, only controlled. It produced an unmistakable rash — dense,
centrifugal, appearing on palms and soles — which meant a case could be
recognized by a village health worker with a laminated photograph rather than
a laboratory. It had no chronic carriers: people either died or recovered
with lasting immunity, and nobody shed the virus quietly for years. It
transmitted relatively slowly, usually requiring prolonged face-to-face
contact, which gave trackers time to get ahead of it. And the vaccine was
remarkably good — one dose gave long protection, it tolerated heat better
than most, and it left a visible scar, so a health worker could verify
coverage in a village by looking at arms.

The delivery technology helped too. The bifurcated needle, a cheap forked
piece of wire that holds one dose in the gap between its prongs, made the
procedure easy to teach in an afternoon and wasted a fraction of the vaccine
that older methods did. Small engineering decisions of this kind decide
campaigns more often than anyone outside them expects.

## Surveillance and Containment

The original plan, when the intensified programme began in 1967, was mass
vaccination: reach eighty per cent of the population of every endemic country
and transmission should collapse. In practice this ran into the arithmetic of
very large, poorly mapped populations, and in some places coverage climbed
while cases stubbornly did not fall.

The change that worked came out of an improvisation in eastern Nigeria, where
a team facing a vaccine shortage decided to concentrate what they had around
known cases instead of spreading it evenly. They stopped the outbreak with a
small fraction of the doses the textbook called for. Generalized, the method
became surveillance and containment, or ring vaccination: find every case
fast, isolate the patient, vaccinate the household, the neighbours and the
contacts of contacts, and then keep watching that ring for the next three
weeks, which is a little longer than the incubation period. The virus is not
chased across a country; it is fenced in wherever it appears.

This shifts the hard work from vaccination to detection. Campaigns began
paying rewards for reported cases, distributing recognition cards in markets,
and checking schools, prisons and refugee camps. They searched house to house
in the last endemic districts. The last chains of transmission were in the
Horn of Africa in places with active conflict, nomadic populations and almost
no roads, and finishing there took years of work far out of proportion to the
number of cases remaining. That is the general pattern: the last one per cent
of an eradication campaign costs more than the first ninety.

## The Cold Chain and the Last Mile

A vaccine that has been too warm for too long is a syringe full of nothing,
and the person who receives it is worse off than before because everyone now
believes they are protected. Keeping biological material cold from a factory
to a village at the end of a footpath is the unglamorous core of immunization
work.

The chain runs from refrigerated national stores down through regional depots
to district fridges, and then out on the last leg in insulated carriers with
ice packs that a worker carries on a motorcycle or a donkey or on foot. Every
link can fail, and failures are silent. The most elegant fix is the vaccine
vial monitor: a small heat-sensitive square printed on the label that darkens
irreversibly as the vial accumulates heat exposure. When the square is as
dark as the reference ring around it, the vial is discarded. It costs almost
nothing, requires no training beyond matching two shades, and it let
campaigns push vaccine further beyond the fridge than had previously been
considered safe. Solar-powered refrigerators that need no fuel deliveries did
something similar for fixed clinics.

The last mile is also a political problem. Polio's remaining strongholds have
been places with weak states, active insurgency, or both, and vaccinators
have been shot in several of them. Trust is the scarce resource. A 2003
boycott in northern Nigeria, driven by a rumour that the vaccine was a
sterilization plot, allowed the virus to reseed a dozen countries that had
already stopped transmission. Years later, an intelligence operation that
used a fake hepatitis vaccination campaign as cover in Pakistan confirmed for
a great many people the exact suspicion that public health workers had spent
decades trying to dispel. Campaigns can survive logistics failures. They
struggle to survive being believed to be something else.

## When There Is No Vaccine

Two of the most successful global campaigns have had no vaccine at all.

Guinea worm disease comes from drinking water containing copepods infected
with larvae. The worm matures inside the body for about a year and then
emerges, slowly and agonizingly, through a blister in the leg, usually while
the sufferer stands in water to ease the burning, at which point it releases
the next generation of larvae. There is no drug and no immunity. The
campaign, run for decades by the Carter Center alongside the health
ministries of the endemic countries, attacked the cycle at every point with
almost embarrassingly simple tools: cloth filters and drinking-straw filters
for individuals, a larvicide for ponds, new boreholes where possible, and
above all case containment — finding each person with an emerging worm and
keeping them out of the water source until it is fully extracted. From an
estimated three and a half million cases a year in the mid-1980s, the annual
human count has fallen into the low double digits. The final complication is
a reservoir nobody anticipated: infections in dogs, which do not respond to
health education.

River blindness took a different route. Ivermectin, a drug developed for
veterinary use, turned out to clear the microfilariae that cause the disease
with a single annual dose. In 1987 its manufacturer committed to donating it
for as long as it was needed, which removed price from the equation and left
only distribution. The answer there was community-directed treatment:
villages select their own distributors, who collect the tablets, dose their
neighbours by height using a simple measuring stick, and keep the register.
It is cheap because it is not really a medical system at all.

Malaria shows the other side of the ledger. A global eradication programme in
the 1950s and 1960s, built largely on DDT spraying, achieved a great deal in
some regions, ran into mosquito resistance, parasite resistance to
chloroquine, funding fatigue and administrative exhaustion in others, and was
abandoned in the late 1960s. Much of the gain reversed. The current effort is
deliberately more modest in its vocabulary, built on insecticide-treated
nets, rapid diagnostic tests, artemisinin combination therapy and, recently,
vaccines of partial efficacy, and it speaks of elimination country by country
rather than eradication.

## Campaigns and the Systems Underneath

The recurring criticism of the great campaigns is that they are vertical. A
single disease gets its own funding stream, its own vehicles, its own data
system and often the best staff in the district, pulled away from a clinic
that is short of everything. When the campaign ends, the vehicles leave.
Measles, which is roughly the most contagious infection known and requires
very high coverage to stop, cannot be beaten this way at all; it needs a
routine immunization system that reaches nearly every child every year, which
is to say it needs functioning primary care.

The honest answer is that the argument is not as clean as either side likes.
Polio infrastructure in several countries has been the backbone of outbreak
response for entirely different diseases, and the surveillance networks built
to find paralysis cases have found cholera, measles and Ebola. Campaigns can
leave systems behind if they are designed to. But the funding logic that
makes an eradication effort possible — a clear enemy, a finish line, a
photograph of the last case — is precisely the logic that makes patient,
unglamorous system-building hard to pay for. The last case is the story. The
clinic that keeps the next one from happening is not.
