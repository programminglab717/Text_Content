# The Self-Built City

Look at the rooftops in Cairo, Lima, Lagos, Istanbul or Mexico City and you
will see the same thing repeated across whole districts: short lengths of
reinforcing bar standing up out of the concrete columns, rusting, waiting.
They are not unfinished in the sense of abandoned. They are a plan. The
family below will add a floor when there is money for a floor, in five years
or fifteen, probably for a son who is not yet married. The house is a savings
account that you can live inside while you fill it.

Most of the urban fabric built in the world over the past sixty years was
produced this way, by its occupants, incrementally, outside any formal
building process. Something like a billion people live in settlements
officially classed as informal. They are not a temporary aberration awaiting
proper development. They are the ordinary method.

## The Sequence Runs Backwards

The planned model of city growth, the one taught in schools of planning and
written into most national building codes, has a fixed order of operations.
Land is surveyed and subdivided. Roads, water mains, sewers and power are
laid in. Plots are sold with clear title. Houses are built to code,
inspected, and occupied.

Where cities are growing fastest, that sequence runs in reverse, and every
step is provisional. Someone occupies land — through an organized overnight
invasion of a hillside, or by buying a plot from a customary chief or a
speculator who subdivided farmland without permission, which is neither theft
nor a legal purchase but something in between. A shelter goes up in a
weekend: timber, corrugated sheet, whatever is at hand, because the first
requirement is to be visibly present. Then, over years, it consolidates. The
sheet walls are replaced with block. A concrete slab goes on. A tap appears
in the yard, then a meter, then eventually a paved street, usually after a
long campaign by a residents' association that has learned exactly which
councillor to lobby in which election year. Title, if it arrives at all,
arrives last, decades after the fact.

Calling these places lawless misreads them badly. They are densely governed,
just not by the state. Claims are recorded by community associations,
witnessed by neighbours, evidenced by receipts and utility bills and the
simple fact of long occupation. People buy and sell these claims at real
prices and lend against them informally. What they lack is not order but
enforceability against the outside — which is exactly what makes a
bulldozer arriving with a court order such an existential event, and why the
fear of eviction, more than the absence of a deed, is what suppresses
investment in a house.

There was a period when the policy conclusion drawn from this was simple:
hand out titles and the rest will follow, because a titled house can be
mortgaged and a mortgage is capital. The record of large titling programmes
has been mixed. Formal title tends to improve tenure security and sometimes
raises property values; it rather less often produces the wave of bank
lending that was promised, because banks have their own reasons for not
lending against a small house in a neighbourhood they consider risky, and
because many owners have no wish to stake their home on a loan.

## Growth Without the Factories

The European and North American cities of the nineteenth century grew because
factories were hiring. Urbanization and industrialization moved together,
which is why the two words are still used almost interchangeably in ordinary
conversation. Much of the growth in African and South Asian cities has
followed a different logic. People have moved to cities that are not
manufacturing centres, where the largest employers are the informal trades:
retail at every scale from a shop to a tray carried on the head, transport,
construction, domestic work, repair, food. In some resource-exporting
countries the cities swelled during commodity booms that created very little
urban employment at all.

This matters for what the resulting cities look like. Industrial cities were
shaped by the journey to the factory gate and the need for workers to live
within walking distance of it. Cities whose economies are dispersed across a
million tiny enterprises have no such organizing gravity; they sprawl, and
the poor end up where land is cheapest, which is usually far out, which is
why transport costs eat such a large share of a poor household's budget.

It also matters for the standard expectation that urbanization will pull
incomes up. Density does make certain things cheaper — markets,
information, the chance of finding work — but a city where most new
arrivals go into low-productivity services does not automatically generate
the surplus that pays for sewers and schools. Natural increase now drives a
large share of urban growth anyway. The children born in these cities are not
migrants and cannot be argued about as if they were.

## Who Actually Supplies the City

Where public provision stops, private provision appears, generally at worse
terms for the poorest. This is the most reliable pattern in the whole subject.

Water is the clearest case. A household connected to a municipal main pays a
regulated tariff per litre. A household in an unserved settlement buys from a
vendor with a cart or a tanker, or from a neighbour who has a connection and
resells, and typically pays several times as much per litre for water of
uncertain quality. The same inversion applies to electricity drawn through
informal connections, to credit, to rent paid weekly in cash. Being poor is
expensive, and in cities it is expensive in a specifically infrastructural
way.

Movement is supplied the same way. The minibus networks that carry most
people in most of these cities — matatu in Nairobi, danfo in Lagos, tro-tro
in Accra, jeepney in Manila, angkot in Indonesian towns, colectivo across
Latin America — run without timetables, subsidies or, in most cases, fixed
schedules. Routes emerge from demand and are defended by associations that
function as regulators, cartels and welfare funds at once. They are cheap,
ubiquitous, dangerous, polluting and extremely responsive. Motorcycle taxis
have taken over the shortest trips almost everywhere, because they are the
only vehicles that can get through both the congestion and the unpaved lanes.

Attempts to replace these networks wholesale have mostly failed; attempts to
work alongside them have done better. Bus rapid transit, which puts
high-capacity buses in dedicated lanes with prepaid boarding, was developed
in Curitiba in the 1970s precisely because the city could not afford a metro,
and its descendants in Bogotá, Lagos, Ahmedabad and elsewhere carry
metro-scale ridership at a fraction of the construction cost. The systems
that work tend to have bought out or absorbed the operators they displaced
rather than simply outlawing them.

## Building on the Ground Nobody Wanted

Self-built settlement goes where land is available, and land is available
where nobody else wanted it: steep slopes, ravines, floodplains, tidal flats,
the margins of rubbish dumps, the strips beside railway tracks and under
high-voltage lines. Those are also the places most exposed to disaster, which
is why a heavy season of rain produces landslides in the hillside districts
of one city and waist-deep flooding in the low-lying districts of another,
year after year, in the same neighbourhoods.

The exposure compounds. Dense low-rise settlement with metal roofs and no
tree cover gets dangerously hot, and heat kills quietly. Drainage designed
for a smaller city is blocked by construction and by uncollected waste, so
ordinary rain becomes a flood. Fires spread fast through close-packed
structures that fire engines cannot reach. Building further out onto marginal
ground to escape one risk usually means accepting another.

## What Upgrading Looks Like When It Works

For decades the standard response to informal settlement was demolition, and
it never worked: the residents had nowhere else to go and the settlement
reappeared, either in the same place or on worse ground further out. The
alternative that has actually improved lives is in-situ upgrading — accept
that the neighbourhood exists, guarantee that it will not be cleared, and
then put in the things households cannot build themselves.

The specific ingredients recur across very different countries. Secure tenure
first, even in a collective form that stops short of individual freehold,
because nothing else gets invested until eviction is off the table. Then
water, drainage, sanitation, street lighting and a paved spine wide enough
for an ambulance and a fire engine. Then connection to the rest of the city:
Medellín's cable cars, which turned a forty-minute climb from the hillside
barrios into a short ride, were less a transport project than a statement
that those districts were part of the city. Thailand's community upgrading
programme went further and gave the money to community organizations
themselves, letting them design and contract the work, on the reasoning that
residents know their own lanes better than a municipal engineer does.

None of it is cheap, and it is chronically underfunded because city
governments in most of these countries collect very little tax and control
even less of it. But the arithmetic of the alternative is worse. A city that
clears a settlement pays for the clearance, pays again when it reappears, and
gets nothing in between.
