# The Slow Assembly of European Integration

On the ninth of May 1950, the French foreign minister Robert Schuman called
journalists to a room at the Quai d'Orsay and read out a statement of a few
hundred words. He proposed that France and Germany place the whole of their coal
and steel production under a common authority, open to other European countries
that wished to join. The choice of industries was not arbitrary and Schuman said
so. Coal and steel were what armies were made of. Putting them under joint
management, he argued, would make a war between France and Germany not merely
unthinkable but materially impossible — you cannot rearm in secret if your
neighbours run your foundries with you.

Five years after the end of the war this was an extraordinary thing for a French
government to say about Germany. The Ruhr was still under international control,
the Saar was still disputed, and French policy up to that point had been aimed at
keeping German industry permanently weak. The declaration reversed it. The text had
been drafted largely by Jean Monnet, a cognac merchant turned planner who had spent
the war coordinating Allied supply and had no patience for grand constitutional
schemes. His method, which shaped everything that followed, was to avoid asking
governments to surrender sovereignty in principle and instead to create a specific
joint institution with real powers over a limited domain, on the theory that
practical entanglement in one sector would generate pressure for integration in the
next.

## The Community Method

The European Coal and Steel Community came into being in 1952 with six members:
France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. It had a
High Authority that could make decisions binding on firms in the member states
without going through national governments, an assembly, a council of ministers,
and a court. That set of organs — an executive body representing the common
interest, a council representing the states, a parliament, and a court to settle
disputes — is essentially the structure the European Union still has.

The next step was more ambitious and it failed badly. With the Korean War raising
the question of German rearmament, the six negotiated a European Defence Community
with a common army. The treaty was signed, and then in 1954 the French National
Assembly refused to ratify it. Defence was too close to the core of what a state
is. The lesson the integrationists drew was to go back to economics, where progress
was less visible and less contentious. A conference at Messina in 1955 set up a
committee under the Belgian Paul-Henri Spaak, and its report became the basis for
the two treaties signed in Rome in 1957: one creating a European Economic Community
and one creating Euratom.

The EEC treaty committed the six to a customs union — internal tariffs abolished,
a common external tariff — and to the free movement of goods, services, capital,
and people. It also committed them to a common agricultural policy, which was the
price of French participation. France wanted guaranteed outlets for its farmers in
exchange for opening its industry to German competition. The result absorbed an
enormous share of the common budget for decades and produced the surpluses that
became known as butter mountains and wine lakes, but it bought the bargain that
made everything else possible.

## The Court Nobody Was Watching

Through the 1960s the political side of integration stalled. Charles de Gaulle
vetoed British membership twice, on the grounds that Britain was too Atlantic in
its orientation to be a plausible European. In 1965 he withdrew French
representatives from the Council entirely for six months, the so-called empty chair
crisis, in protest against proposals to move to majority voting and to give the
Community its own revenues. The dispute was settled by an understanding that any
member state could block a decision touching its vital interests, which effectively
restored the national veto for the next twenty years.

While ministers were deadlocked, the Court of Justice was quietly rewriting the
constitutional position. In a case brought by a Dutch transport firm over a customs
classification, the Court held in 1963 that the treaty created rights which
individuals could invoke in their own national courts. The following year, in a
dispute over an Italian electricity bill, it held that Community law took precedence
over conflicting national law, including later national law. Neither proposition is
stated in the treaties. Both were accepted, gradually and with some grumbling, by
national supreme courts. By the end of the 1970s the Court had also ruled that a
product lawfully sold in one member state should generally be sellable in the
others, a principle of mutual recognition that removed the need to harmonize every
national standard before trade could flow.

Enlargement finally came in 1973, when Britain, Ireland, and Denmark joined after
de Gaulle had left office. Greece followed at the start of the 1980s, Spain and
Portugal a few years later, all three of them newly emerged from dictatorship and
all three understanding membership partly as an anchor for democracy.

## Single Market, Single Currency

The 1970s were a bad decade for the project. The collapse of fixed exchange rates,
two oil shocks, and rising unemployment pushed governments back toward national
remedies, and commentators began writing about eurosclerosis. Recovery began in the
middle of the 1980s under a Commission led by Jacques Delors, with a white paper
listing several hundred specific barriers to trade within the Community and a
deadline for removing them. To make that programme achievable, the member states
agreed in the Single European Act to decide internal market measures by qualified
majority rather than unanimity. It was the first serious breach in the national
veto and it changed the pace of everything.

Completing a market in capital made fixed exchange rates fragile, and fragile
exchange rates threatened the single market, so the logic ran on toward monetary
union. The Maastricht Treaty, signed in 1992, created the European Union as an
umbrella over the existing communities, added citizenship and a common foreign
policy framework, and set out a timetable and convergence criteria for a single
currency. It also nearly failed: Danish voters rejected it in a referendum and only
approved it after opt-outs were negotiated, and the French approved it by the
narrowest of margins. The euro arrived as an accounting currency at the end of the
decade and as notes and coins a few years later, with a central bank in Frankfurt
mandated to target price stability.

What Maastricht did not create was a common treasury, a shared debt instrument, or
a mechanism for supervising banks across borders. Member states kept their own
fiscal policies, restrained only by deficit rules that were bent almost as soon as
large countries found them inconvenient. A currency union with a single monetary
policy, nineteen fiscal policies, and no system for handling a bank failure in one
country that threatened creditors in another was an incomplete structure, and it was
known to be incomplete at the time. The argument was that the gaps would be filled
when necessity arose.

## The Decade of Crises

Necessity arrived in 2010. Greece revealed that its deficits had been far larger
than reported, lenders re-priced the risk of every heavily indebted eurozone
government, and the absence of a lender of last resort for sovereigns turned a
solvency problem into a panic. What followed was improvised: emergency loan
facilities, programmes supervised by the Commission, the central bank, and the IMF,
austerity conditions that produced deep contractions in southern Europe, and finally
a statement by the ECB president that the bank would do whatever it took within its
mandate to preserve the euro — words that calmed markets more effectively than any
of the funds. Banking supervision was partly centralized afterward, which is roughly
how the system has always worked: crisis, improvisation, then partial ratification
of the improvisation.

The other crises were harder. A constitutional treaty was rejected by French and
Dutch voters in 2005 and its substance was repackaged into the Lisbon Treaty, which
entered into force in 2009 without referendums in most countries — a manoeuvre that
did lasting damage to the claim that the project rested on consent. The arrival of
large numbers of asylum seekers in 2015 exposed how little solidarity the borderless
travel area had generated. And in 2016 the British voted to leave, becoming the
first member state to reverse the direction the whole enterprise had assumed was
one-way.

Monnet's wager was that functional cooperation would eventually produce political
community — that people who shared a market, a currency, and a court would come to
think of themselves as sharing something larger. The market, the currency, and the
court all exist and work. The sense of common membership they were supposed to
generate has arrived unevenly and mostly among the people who benefit most
visibly. What the six founders built has no precise name: more than a treaty
organization, less than a federation, and still, seventy years on, argued about in
exactly those terms.
