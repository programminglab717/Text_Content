# What Information Technology Did to Ordinary Life

Open a desk drawer from 1985 and you find the working infrastructure of a
life. An address book with crossings-out and a few numbers pencilled in the
margin. A chequebook and its stubs. A road map folded wrong. Paper receipts
under a bulldog clip, a spiral pad of phone messages taken down for other
people, a card from the dentist reminding you of an appointment six months
out. Every object in that drawer did one specific job, and nearly all of
those jobs are now performed by a single device that also plays music.

That is the shape of the change, and it explains why it has been so difficult
to argue about clearly. Information technology did not arrive the way the
railway or the contraceptive pill arrived, as one invention with one obvious
social consequence. It arrived as a long absorption — one paper system at a
time, each individual replacement plainly convenient, the cumulative effect
something nobody sat down and chose.

## Institutions Got There First

The popular story begins in a garage. The actual sequence begins with
payroll. The first two decades of serious business computing went into the
least romantic work in any large organization: wages, invoicing, insurance
tables, census tabulation, airline seat inventory, bank cheque clearing.
These were tasks with enormous clerical volume and rules simple enough to
write down, which is precisely what early machines could handle.

The social effect was invisible to most people and substantial anyway. It
made certain kinds of administration cheap enough to be worth doing, so there
was more of it. Credit scoring spread because sorting a few million payment
histories stopped requiring a building full of clerks. Direct mail became
targeted. Customer records followed you between branches of a bank instead of
living in one filing cabinet in one town. A person's dealings with
institutions began to accumulate into something that could be retrieved in
seconds by a stranger, and this happened well before anyone outside those
institutions had a computer at all.

The first machines most ordinary people ever dealt with directly were of this
institutional kind. An airline reservation system that held every seat on
every flight in one place, updated as agents around the country sold them,
was operating by the mid-1960s, and it changed the business of flying more
than any aircraft did. Barclays put a cash machine outside a branch in
Enfield in 1967, and within twenty years the expectation that money could be
obtained at two in the morning from a hole in a wall had become so ordinary
that the older arrangement — banking hours, a teller, a passbook, a
signature someone recognized — seemed faintly absurd. Neither of these felt
like computing to the people using them. They felt like the world getting
slightly more obliging.

The personal computer's real disruption was not that it put computing in
homes — that took another decade to matter — but that it moved this power
out from under the data-processing department. The spreadsheet is the
clearest case. Before it, a financial analyst who wanted to model next year
under three different assumptions submitted a request and waited for a batch
job to run overnight. After it, she did it herself before lunch, and then did
it eleven more times because it now cost nothing to ask. Whole layers of
analysis and second-guessing became possible, which is one reason the era of
the spreadsheet is also the era of the leveraged buyout.

## The Typing Pool and What Replaced It

Offices in 1970 contained job categories that have simply ceased to exist.
Typing pools. Switchboard operators. Filing clerks whose entire employment
was knowing where things were. Compositors who set newspaper type in hot
metal, a skilled trade with long apprenticeships that vanished inside about a
decade when photocomposition and then desktop systems let journalists key
their own copy. The bitter industrial disputes of the 1980s in British and
American newspapers were fought over exactly this, and the unions lost.

What is often missed is that the work did not disappear so much as move
sideways, onto people who used to have it done for them. A manager in 1975
dictated a letter and someone else typed it. A manager in 1995 typed it
himself, formatted it himself, and filed it himself in a directory structure
of his own invention. The same happened outside the office: passengers became
their own travel agents, bank customers their own tellers, shoppers their own
checkout operators. Firms shed labour costs by handing small parcels of work
to everybody else, and because each parcel is small, almost nobody
experiences it as work.

The other effect on work is measurement. A warehouse picker's route, a
delivery driver's stops, a call-centre agent's talk time, a remote worker's
idle cursor — all of these became recordable at essentially no cost, and
what is recorded tends to be managed. Supervision used to require a
supervisor physically watching, which limited how much of it there could be.
Now the system keeps the record and the human simply reviews the outliers.
This is not a small shift in the texture of a working day, and it has fallen
hardest on jobs that were already the least autonomous.

## Plans Went Soft

Before the mobile telephone, meeting a friend in a city required a promise
made in advance and then kept: under the station clock at six. If one of you
was late, the other waited, and after half an hour had to decide alone what
that meant. The plan was the commitment, and arrangements had a formality to
them because there was no way to amend one in flight.

Afterwards, plans became provisional. "Text me when you're close." Groups
converge on a destination without ever having agreed on it in advance.
Something real was gained here — coordination among more than two people
used to be genuinely laborious, which is why so much social life was
organized around fixed recurring occasions — and something real was lost,
which is the ordinary practice of waiting, and of being briefly unreachable.

Navigation went the same way. Finding an unfamiliar address used to mean a
map, a written note of the turns, and a reasonable chance of stopping to ask
someone. London taxi drivers spent years acquiring the Knowledge, a feat of
memorized geography that a satellite receiver and a street database rendered,
if not worthless, then no longer a barrier to entry. Getting lost stopped
being a normal feature of travel and became a minor failure. It also
redistributed traffic: routing software, indifferent to whether a street is
residential, sends queues of strangers down lanes that were quiet for a
century, which is a genuinely new kind of neighbourhood grievance.

The same reachability ran straight through the wall between work and home.
Email at first was a convenience that saved paper. Then it was on a laptop
that came home in a bag. Then it was on a phone, on a bedside table, and the
question of whether one was at work became a matter of personal discipline
rather than a matter of where one's body was. No policy decision produced
this. It emerged from a sequence of individually sensible conveniences, and
the professions that resisted it hardest were the ones with strong enough
occupational norms to say no.

## The Newspaper Came Apart

For most of the twentieth century a local newspaper's classified advertising
— jobs, cars, houses, personals, lost cats — quietly paid for its city
hall reporter and sometimes for a foreign bureau. Readers did not think of
themselves as funding journalism, and advertisers did not think of themselves
as funding it either. The bundle simply held.

The internet unbundled it with remarkable speed. Job listings went to job
sites, cars to car sites, property to property portals, personals to dating
apps, each of which did that one job better than a column of six-point type
ever had. None of them inherited any obligation to cover a council meeting.
Metropolitan papers were hurt; small local papers, which had no national
audience to pivot toward, were often destroyed outright. The resulting gaps
in local coverage are among the more concrete social costs of the whole
transition, and they are largely irreversible.

Publishing itself, meanwhile, went from expensive to free. When the cost of
producing and distributing a copy fell to roughly nothing, the scarce
resource stopped being distribution and became attention, and whatever
systems allocate attention became the thing worth fighting over. Ranking and
recommendation are not neutral pipes; they select, and what they select for
is measured engagement, which is not the same as importance, accuracy, or the
public interest. Material that provokes a strong quick reaction moves further
than material that requires patience. This is not a conspiracy, only an
incentive, which makes it harder to fix.

## What Got Cheap and What Got Dear

Copying got cheap, and with it went the business model of every industry
built on controlling copies — records, films, software, books, news —
which rebuilt itself, unevenly, around access and subscription instead of
ownership. Distance got cheap; a software team spread over four continents is
now unremarkable, and so is a grandmother who sees her grandchildren daily
from two thousand miles away. Searching got cheap. Remembering got cheap
enough that most people no longer hold a single telephone number in their
head.

The things that got dear are subtler. Attention, obviously. Privacy, which
used to be protected mostly by the sheer inconvenience of assembling
scattered facts about a person and now must be protected deliberately or not
at all. Verification: when anything can be produced and circulated cheaply,
establishing that a document or an image is what it claims to be takes real
effort. And uninterrupted time, which has quietly become a luxury good,
sought out at retreats and in rules about phones at dinner tables.

Underneath all of it sits an asymmetry. Individuals became far more legible
to institutions than institutions became to individuals. A bank, an employer,
an insurer or a government can assemble a detailed account of a person in
moments; the reverse enquiry remains slow, partial and often obstructed. The
same infrastructure cuts both ways in other respects — it has let a state
monitor a dissident's contacts and let the dissident publish to the world
without permission from anyone — but the default direction of visibility is
downward, and the effort required to look back up has not fallen nearly as
fast as the effort required to look down.

A child born now will never have to memorize a phone number, and will also
never have an afternoon during which nobody can reach her. Those are not two
separate facts about her life. They are the same fact, seen from either side.
