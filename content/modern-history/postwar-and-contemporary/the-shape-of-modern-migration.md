# The Shape of Modern Migration

Drive through parts of central Kerala and you pass houses that are
conspicuously larger than the plots and the local economy would seem to
justify: two and three storeys, tiled, with gateposts and sometimes a
satellite dish, standing among older tile-roofed places of a quarter the
size. They were paid for from the Gulf. A man went to Dubai or Doha or Riyadh
on a two-year contract, and then another, and the money came home in monthly
instalments for twenty years, and the house is what it turned into. In some
villages every third family has someone working a thousand miles away, and
the local economy runs on the arrivals of that money rather than on anything
produced nearby.

That pattern — a specific place in one country tied to a specific place in
another by a channel that has been running for decades — is a far better
picture of how migration actually works than the image of an undifferentiated
flow from poor countries to rich ones. Migration is not diffusion. It runs in
corridors, along tracks laid down by earlier movers, and it is remarkably
persistent once established.

## Most Movement Never Crosses a Border

The first correction to almost any conversation on this subject is scale.
International migrants are a small fraction of humanity — somewhere in the
range of three or four per cent, a share that has been fairly stable for
decades even as the absolute number has grown with world population. Internal
migration is several times larger. China's movement of people from rural
counties to coastal cities over the past four decades, several hundred
million of them, is the largest migration in recorded history, and none of it
crossed an international boundary. India's internal movement, complicated by
language and state borders, is on a comparable scale. Nigeria, Brazil,
Indonesia and Vietnam all have internal flows that dwarf their emigration.

Where people do cross borders, most do not travel far. The typical
international migrant moves to a neighbouring or nearby country, often one
with a shared language or a colonial-era connection: Zimbabweans to South
Africa, Burkinabè to Côte d'Ivoire, Nicaraguans to Costa Rica, Nepalis to
India, Burmese to Thailand, Ukrainians to Poland. Movement between poorer
countries is roughly as large as movement from poorer to richer ones, and
receives a fraction of the attention.

## Corridors and the Logic of Networks

Why do migrants from one district end up concentrated in one foreign city,
sometimes in one industry within it? Because the first arrivals reduce the
cost of everything for those who follow. They know which employer hires,
which landlord rents without documents, how the paperwork works, where to
sleep for the first month. A migration that begins as an accident — a
wartime recruitment, a labour agreement, a single family's departure —
becomes self-sustaining once the network is thick enough, and it can continue
long after the economic conditions that started it have disappeared.

This is why corridors look so arbitrary until you know their history. Turkish
workers in Germany and Moroccans in Belgium and the Netherlands trace back to
guest-worker agreements signed in the 1960s, when Western European economies
short of labour recruited on the explicit understanding that the workers
would go home. Most of the recruitment stopped after the oil shock of 1973.
The populations did not go home; they brought their families, which is the
standard outcome when a temporary programme meets a permanent labour demand.

A related pattern explains why emigration often rises as a poor country gets
richer rather than falling. Moving internationally costs money — for
travel, documents, an agent's fee, and the months of no income at the other
end. The very poorest cannot afford it. As incomes rise, more households can,
and aspirations rise too, so emigration climbs. Only at a considerably higher
level of national income does it flatten and reverse, as it did in Italy,
South Korea, Ireland and Poland in turn. Development is not a short-term
substitute for migration. For a long stretch it is a cause of it.

## The Money That Goes Back

Remittances are the most underappreciated financial flow in the world
economy. The total sent home by migrant workers exceeds all official
development assistance by a wide margin, and in a number of countries it
amounts to a large share of national income. It behaves differently from aid
or investment, too: it arrives in small amounts directly into households, it
is spent mostly on food, school fees, medicine, housing and debt, and it
tends to rise rather than fall when the origin country suffers a disaster or
a downturn. Families send more precisely when things are worse at home.

The costs of sending have been a long-running scandal. Fees and exchange-rate
margins of several per cent on a few hundred dollars, charged to people at
the bottom of the labour market, add up to an enormous transfer out of the
pockets of the poorest, and the most expensive corridors have historically
been the poorest ones — remittances within Africa in particular. Mobile
money and digital transfer have pushed costs down substantially where
regulation allowed them to.

What the money does at the other end is argued about with some heat. It
plainly reduces poverty in receiving households and keeps children in school
longer, especially girls. It also drives up local land and construction
prices, can leave whole districts dependent on a single foreign labour
market, and buys imports rather than building local industry. And the
household that receives it has paid for it in a form that does not appear in
any account: years of a parent's absence, marriages conducted by telephone,
children raised by grandparents, the specific and widespread phenomenon of
the transnational family.

The sending countries are not all passive in this. The Philippines built an
entire state apparatus around labour export, with pre-departure training,
accredited agencies, welfare funds and diplomatic effort spent on the
treatment of its nationals abroad. Filipino seafarers crew a startling share
of the world's merchant fleet, and Filipino nurses and care workers staff
hospitals from London to Riyadh — which raises the other side of the
ledger, since a country that trains nurses for export is also a country whose
own clinics are short of nurses.

## Displacement and Who Actually Hosts It

Forced displacement has grown sharply this century, driven by Syria,
Afghanistan, South Sudan, Myanmar, Venezuela, Ukraine and Sudan among others.
The pattern of who absorbs it is consistently misunderstood in the countries
that argue about it most. The large majority of refugees are hosted by low-
and middle-income countries, usually the ones immediately next door: Turkey,
Pakistan, Iran, Uganda, Colombia, Bangladesh, Chad. The rich democracies
receive a modest proportion, and their politics are convulsed by it anyway.

The legal machinery dates from 1951 and was built for a European problem —
the displaced of the Second World War — before a 1967 protocol removed its
geographic and temporal limits. Its definition of a refugee turns on a
well-founded fear of persecution on specific grounds, which does not cleanly
cover people fleeing generalized violence, gang control, state collapse,
drought or sea-level rise. Regional instruments in Africa and Latin America
adopted broader definitions decades ago; the global one has not moved. Much
of the friction in asylum systems now comes from people whose situations are
genuinely desperate and genuinely outside the category the system was written
for.

A great deal of displacement is also internal, and internally displaced
people have weaker protection than refugees because they remain under the
authority of the state that failed them. On climate, the evidence so far
points away from the picture of vast international waves and toward
short-distance, largely internal movement, with environmental stress acting
through crops, water and prices rather than as a separate cause.

## What Borders Do to Behaviour

Enforcement changes migration less than it changes migrants, and often in the
opposite direction from the one intended. The clearest illustration is the
Mexico–United States corridor. For much of the twentieth century it was
circular: men came north for a season's work, returned, came again. Crossing
was difficult but survivable, and the expectation of being able to cross
again made going home easy. As enforcement hardened from the mid-1980s
onward, the cost and risk of each crossing rose steeply, and the rational
response for someone already inside was to stop going back. The circular flow
turned into permanent settlement, and families followed. The border did not
so much stop the movement as trap it.

The other consequence of hard borders is that they create a market. Where a
route cannot be walked, it can be bought, and the smuggling economies that
have grown around the Sahara, the Mediterranean, the Darién Gap and the Bay
of Bengal are sophisticated, adaptable and lethal. Each closed route pushes
traffic onto a more dangerous one, which raises prices and raises the death
rate.

Meanwhile the demographic pressure underneath keeps building. The rich world
is ageing, its care homes and hospitals and farms and construction sites are
short of workers, and its politics are less and less able to say so out loud.
That gap between what labour markets are asking for and what electorates will
tolerate is where most migration policy actually gets made, which is why so
much of it consists of temporary schemes, quiet tolerance and periodic
crackdowns rather than anything anyone would design on purpose.
