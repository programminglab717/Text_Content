# Bretton Woods and the Dollar Order

In July 1944 several hundred delegates from forty-four countries checked into a
resort hotel at the foot of Mount Washington in New Hampshire. The Normandy
landings were a few weeks old and the outcome of the war was no longer seriously
in doubt, but the shape of what came after it was entirely open. For three weeks
the delegations argued in committee rooms and hotel corridors about exchange
rates, gold, and who would be obliged to adjust when trade went out of balance.
The Soviet delegation attended, signed the final act, and then never ratified it.
Almost everyone else did, and the arrangement they produced governed the money of
the capitalist world for roughly a generation.

What the negotiators had in front of them was not an abstract design problem. It
was the memory of the 1930s. After the First World War the major economies had
tried to rebuild the classical gold standard, often at prewar parities that no
longer reflected anything real, and the strain had shown up as deflation,
unemployment, and bank failures. When the system broke, it broke in the worst
possible way: countries left gold one at a time, devalued to grab a larger share
of a shrinking volume of world trade, raised tariffs in retaliation, and imposed
exchange controls that turned commerce into a series of bilateral barter deals.
Capital fled across borders at the first sign of trouble and made every crisis
worse. Everyone at the hotel agreed that this pattern had helped produce both the
Depression and the political catastrophe that followed. The disagreement was
about the remedy.

## Keynes, White, and the Creditor's Advantage

The British delegation was led by John Maynard Keynes, by then a famous man in
visibly failing health. His proposal was radical and elegant. He wanted an
International Clearing Union, a kind of central bank for central banks, which
would keep accounts in a new unit of account he called bancor. Countries running
deficits would be allowed to draw on overdrafts rather than being forced into
immediate austerity. Crucially, countries running persistent surpluses would face
charges on their accumulated balances, pressing them to import more or revalue
upward. Keynes's core insight was that in a world of fixed exchange rates the
burden of adjustment falls entirely on the debtor, who must deflate wages and
demand, while the creditor can simply sit on its reserves. He wanted to make
surpluses uncomfortable too.

The American delegation was led by Harry Dexter White of the Treasury, and the
United States at that moment was the largest creditor on earth, holding a great
majority of the world's monetary gold. White had no interest in a scheme that
would automatically penalize the country in surplus, and no intention of handing
an international body the power to create unlimited overdrafts denominated in a
currency nobody controlled. His plan was more modest: a stabilization fund with
finite resources, subscribed by members in proportion to quotas, from which
countries could draw limited amounts to ride out temporary difficulties. The
American plan won, as it was always going to. Keynes secured concessions on the
size of the fund and on the freedom of members to change their parities, but the
architecture was White's.

## The Rules as Written

The system that emerged had a simple hierarchy. The dollar was fixed to gold at
thirty-five dollars an ounce, a rate inherited from the 1930s. Every other member
currency was fixed to the dollar. Those pegs were not immovable: a country facing
what the Articles called fundamental disequilibrium could change its par value,
with the Fund's agreement for larger moves. This was the compromise at the heart
of the thing — the discipline of fixed rates without the brutality of the gold
standard, an escape hatch for countries whose costs had genuinely drifted out of
line.

The second pillar was less often noticed and mattered just as much. Members were
explicitly permitted to control movements of capital across their borders.
Current-account transactions — paying for imports, remitting profits — were meant
to be free, but speculative money was to be fenced in. This was deliberate. Both
Keynes and White believed that free capital flows and fixed exchange rates and
independent domestic policy could not all coexist, and of the three they were
willing to sacrifice the first. Governments that had just promised their
electorates full employment needed to be able to run low interest rates without
watching money flee. The whole postwar settlement in Western Europe — nationalized
industries, expanding social insurance, wage bargaining — depended on that fence.

Two institutions came out of the conference: the International Monetary Fund, to
police the par values and lend to members in difficulty, and the International
Bank for Reconstruction and Development, aimed first at rebuilding Europe and
later at financing development. A third leg was planned, an International Trade
Organization, but the treaty establishing it died in the United States Congress.
What survived was the interim tariff-cutting agreement signed at Geneva in 1947,
the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, which quietly became the permanent
framework for trade liberalization for the next half century.

## The Decade It Did Not Work

For most of its first decade the system existed largely on paper. Europe emerged
from the war needing American food, coal, machinery, and cotton, and had almost
nothing to sell in return. The resulting shortage of dollars was so severe that
the currency convertibility the Articles required was simply postponed year after
year. Britain tried to make sterling convertible in 1947 under pressure from
Washington and had to abandon the attempt within weeks as reserves drained away.

What actually held Western Europe together in those years was not the Fund but
American aid and a regional clearing mechanism. The Marshall Plan supplied dollars
as grants rather than loans, and the European Payments Union let member countries
settle their mutual trade multilaterally without needing scarce dollars for every
transaction. Only at the end of the 1950s did the major European currencies become
convertible for current-account purposes. The Bretton Woods system as its designers
imagined it therefore ran properly for a little over a decade — roughly from the
end of the 1950s to the end of the 1960s.

## Triffin's Trap

The flaw was identified while the system was still working. Robert Triffin, a
Belgian economist teaching in the United States, pointed out in the early 1960s
that the arrangement contained a contradiction it could not grow out of. World
trade was expanding and needed a growing stock of reserves, and those reserves were
dollars. But dollars entered foreign hands only through American deficits. The
longer the system supplied the liquidity it needed, the larger the pile of foreign
dollar claims became relative to the gold sitting in American vaults — and the more
obvious it became that the promise to redeem at thirty-five dollars an ounce could
not be honoured if everyone asked at once. Stop the deficits and world trade would
strangle for want of reserves; continue them and confidence would eventually break.

Through the 1960s the managers of the system improvised. Central banks formed a
pool to sell gold in London and hold the market price near the official one; it
collapsed in 1968 and a two-tier market emerged, official and private. The Fund
created a synthetic reserve asset, the Special Drawing Right, as a substitute for
gold that never grew large enough to matter. Meanwhile American spending on the
Vietnam War and on domestic programmes pushed prices up, and the dollar became
visibly overvalued against a revived Germany and Japan. France under de Gaulle made
a point of converting dollars into gold and said publicly what the arrangement gave
the United States: the ability to run deficits in its own currency indefinitely.

The end came quickly. In August 1971, after a run on the gold window, Richard Nixon
announced from Camp David that the United States would no longer redeem dollars for
gold, and imposed a temporary import surcharge to force a realignment. An attempt to
rebuild fixed rates at new parities was patched together in Washington that December
and held for barely a year. By March 1973 the major currencies were floating against
each other, and they have floated ever since. A conference in Jamaica a few years
later amended the Articles to legalize what had already happened.

## What Outlived the Rules

The institutions survived their original purpose by reinventing themselves. With no
par values left to police, the Fund became a crisis lender and enforcer of policy
conditions, first for developing countries in the debt crises of the 1980s, later
for post-communist states and for eurozone members. The Bank became a development
agency. The trade agreement kept cutting tariffs and eventually turned into the
World Trade Organization.

Stranger still, the dollar's position did not weaken when its link to gold was cut.
It strengthened. Oil was priced in dollars, central banks kept accumulating them,
and the deepest and most liquid financial markets on earth were American. The
arrangement that emerged after 1973 was in one sense the opposite of what the
delegates in New Hampshire designed: floating rates, no anchor in metal, and capital
moving across borders in volumes that would have appalled Keynes. In another sense
it preserved the core of White's achievement intact. The world still settles its
accounts in the currency of a single country, and it does so now on nothing but
habit, market depth, and the absence of anywhere else to go.
