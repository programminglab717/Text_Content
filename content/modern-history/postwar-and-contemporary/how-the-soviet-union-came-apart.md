# How the Soviet Union Came Apart

On the evening of the twenty-fifth of December 1991, Mikhail Gorbachev sat at a
desk in the Kremlin and read a short statement announcing that he was ceasing his
activities as president of a country that had, by that point, already stopped
existing in law. The pen he was handed did not work and he borrowed another. A
little while later the red flag with the hammer and sickle came down from the
Senate dome and the Russian tricolour went up. There was no ceremony, no successor
taking an oath, and no crowd. One of the two superpowers of the twentieth century
was wound up by administrative notice in the middle of a working week.

That absence of drama is the thing most worth explaining. The Soviet Union was not
defeated in a war. It was not overthrown by a revolutionary crowd storming the
Kremlin. Its armed forces remained intact, its nuclear arsenal remained loaded, and
its security services were never dismantled. It dissolved because the people
running its constituent parts decided it should, and because by then nobody with
the means to stop them was willing to try.

## An Economy That Could Not Bend

The system's economic troubles were old and structurally rooted rather than
sudden. A planned economy allocates by quantity targets rather than prices, which
means it has no reliable way of learning what things actually cost or what people
actually want. Enterprises hoarded labour and materials because supply was
unreliable. Quality was whatever passed inspection. Managers had every incentive to
understate capacity so that next year's target would be easier. None of this
prevented the rapid industrialization of a peasant country or the building of a
formidable military, but it made the system very bad at the thing that mattered in
the second half of the century: continuous innovation in consumer goods and
information technology.

Two circumstances masked the decline for a decade. The oil shocks of the 1970s
turned Soviet Siberian production into a vast hard-currency windfall, which paid
for imported grain and imported machinery and postponed every difficult decision.
And the leadership had become immobile. Leonid Brezhnev's later years were an
exercise in not deciding anything, and after his death in 1982 the Politburo buried
two more elderly general secretaries within three years. When oil prices fell
sharply in the middle of the 1980s, the windfall went away and the postponed
decisions all arrived at once.

## Two Reforms That Worked Against Each Other

Gorbachev took over in 1985 at fifty-four, a generation younger than the men around
him, convinced that the system was salvageable through better management and
renewed discipline. His early measures were conventional: a drive to accelerate
growth, a campaign against alcohol that cut consumption somewhat and gutted a major
source of state revenue, and personnel changes. When acceleration produced nothing,
he moved to deeper restructuring.

The economic reforms of 1987 and 1988 gave enterprises autonomy over what they
produced and allowed cooperatives — small private businesses in everything but
name. The effect was to dismantle plan discipline without building a market to
replace it. Firms were freed from their obligations but not from their subsidies.
They raised wages, dropped unprofitable cheap goods, and diverted output to the new
cooperative channels where prices were unregulated. The state budget, already
strained, went deeply into deficit and was financed by printing money. Because
official prices stayed fixed, the extra roubles showed up not as inflation but as
empty shelves. By 1990 a citizen of the world's second industrial power was queuing
for soap and cigarettes. Whatever legitimacy the system retained had rested on
gradual improvement in daily life, and that was now visibly running backward.

## Glasnost and the Loss of the Story

The political opening was meant to enlist the intelligentsia against entrenched
bureaucratic resistance. It did that, and then kept going. Chernobyl in 1986 was the
turning point: the initial silence and the evacuation delays were so indefensible
that Gorbachev concluded secrecy itself was part of what he was fighting.

Within two years the press was publishing things that had been unpublishable for
sixty years. Solzhenitsyn appeared in Soviet journals. The scale of Stalin's terror
was documented by historians and by an organization of volunteers collecting the
names of the dead. In 1989 the government finally admitted that the secret protocols
to the pact with Nazi Germany had existed — an admission with immediate
consequences, because it meant that the incorporation of Estonia, Latvia, and
Lithuania had been the product of a deal with Hitler. That summer roughly two
million people joined hands in a human chain running the length of the three Baltic
republics on the pact's anniversary.

The same year, elections with some genuine competition produced a Congress of
People's Deputies whose sessions were broadcast live. Factory production reportedly
suffered because people watched instead of working. They saw deputies attack the KGB
by name, heard Andrei Sakharov argue with the chair, and watched Boris Yeltsin — a
Politburo member humiliated and expelled two years earlier — return as a popular
tribune. A state built on the claim that there was only one legitimate account of
reality had surrendered its monopoly on the story, and no new story arrived to
replace it.

## Losing the Outer Empire, Then the Inner One

Gorbachev had told the Eastern European leaderships privately that Soviet troops
would not be used to keep them in power. In 1989 they discovered he meant it. Poland
held partly free elections in June that its communists lost humiliatingly. Hungary
opened its border with Austria, which drained East Germany of citizens through a
side door. The Berlin Wall was opened in November after a confused press conference,
Czechoslovakia's government fell within weeks, and Nicolae Ceaușescu was shot on
Christmas Day. Moscow did nothing, and everyone inside the Soviet Union watched.

The Soviet federal structure then turned out to be a loaded weapon. The union was
formally a federation of national republics, each with defined borders, a capital, a
flag, an academy of sciences, a party organization, and a titular nationality with
its own language and grievances. These were the institutional shells of states, kept
empty by party discipline. Once the party's monopoly was removed from the
constitution in 1990, they filled up fast. Armenians and Azerbaijanis were already
fighting over Nagorno-Karabakh, and the centre proved unable to impose a settlement.
Lithuania declared independence outright in the spring of 1990. A crackdown by
special forces in Vilnius the following January killed more than a dozen civilians
at the television tower and achieved nothing except confirming that force would be
used indecisively.

The decisive defection was Russia's. Yeltsin, elected chairman of the Russian
parliament in 1990 and then president of Russia by direct popular vote in 1991,
discovered that the republic containing half the union's population and most of its
territory could be used as a battering ram against the union itself. Russia declared
that its own laws took precedence over Soviet ones. Other republics followed, and
the resulting confusion — two sets of laws, two tax authorities claiming the same
revenues — became known as the war of laws. Yeltsin also had something Gorbachev
never acquired: a mandate from voters. Gorbachev held office by appointment of party
and congress, and the gap in legitimacy mattered enormously in the year that
followed.

## Three Days in August

Gorbachev spent 1991 negotiating a new union treaty that would have devolved most
powers to the republics while keeping a common defence, currency, and foreign
policy. It was to be signed on the twentieth of August. On the nineteenth, a group
of senior officials — the vice-president, the KGB chairman, the defence and interior
ministers, the prime minister — announced a state of emergency, placed Gorbachev
under house arrest at his holiday residence in Crimea, and sent tanks into Moscow.

The coup was astonishingly badly executed. Its leaders did not arrest Yeltsin, did
not shut down communications, and could not agree to order troops to fire. At their
televised press conference the vice-president's hands shook visibly. Yeltsin walked
out of the Russian parliament building, climbed onto a tank, and read a denunciation
of the plotters to the crowd and the cameras. Units began defecting. Three young men
died in a tunnel confrontation. By the twenty-first it was over, and the plotters
were flying to Crimea to beg Gorbachev's forgiveness.

He returned to a country that no longer had any use for him. The party was suspended
in Russia within days and its property seized. Republics that had been negotiating
autonomy now declared independence outright. On the first of December, Ukraine held a
referendum in which over ninety percent voted to leave, and that settled it — no
plausible union could be assembled without Ukraine. A week later Yeltsin met the
leaders of Ukraine and Belarus at a hunting lodge in the Belovezha Forest, where they
declared that the union founded in 1922 was ceasing to exist and announced a loose
commonwealth in its place. Most of the remaining republics signed on at Alma-Ata
later in the month.

Fifteen new states inherited borders that Soviet administrators had drawn for
internal convenience, with minorities stranded on the wrong sides of them and
industrial supply chains cut by international frontiers. Almost every armed conflict
in the region over the following three decades has run along one of those lines.
