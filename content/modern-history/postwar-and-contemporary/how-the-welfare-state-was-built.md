# How the Welfare State Was Built

In the winter of 1942 people queued outside government bookshops in London to buy
a white paper. It was six hundred pages of actuarial argument about social
insurance contributions, written by a civil servant named William Beveridge, and
it sold in the hundreds of thousands. Copies circulated among troops in North
Africa. The government that had commissioned it was visibly uncomfortable with
how popular it had become. Very few official documents have ever been read that
way, and the reason this one was has less to do with its prose than with what it
promised: that the accidents of a life — illness, unemployment, old age, the
death of a wage earner — would no longer be left to fall where they fell.

The welfare state is usually described as something governments do for poor
people. That description is wrong in a way that obscures almost everything
interesting about it. The bulk of what rich democracies spend on social policy
does not move money from the rich to the poor at all. It moves money from a
person's working years to the same person's childhood, illness, and old age.
Pensions and health care dominate the budgets; unemployment benefit and
means-tested assistance are usually small by comparison. The welfare state is
mainly a machine for smoothing income across a lifetime, and its remarkable
political durability follows directly from that. Almost everyone expects to draw
on it.

## Bismarck's Calculation

The first recognizably modern social insurance was not built by socialists or
liberals. It was built in the 1880s by Otto von Bismarck, who had spent the
previous years trying to suppress the German Social Democrats by law and found
that it did not work. If the state could not outlaw the workers' movement, it
could try to make itself the more reliable provider. Germany introduced health
insurance for workers, then accident insurance paid for by employers, then old-age
and invalidity pensions, all within a decade.

The form mattered as much as the content. These were contributory insurance
schemes, funded by deductions from wages and payments by employers, administered
through occupational funds. A worker who drew on them was not receiving charity.
He was collecting on a claim he had paid for, and the distinction was understood
by everyone. The contrast with the English Poor Law was total. The Poor Law
reform of 1834 had been built on the principle of less eligibility: relief had to
be made worse than the worst available employment, and it was delivered through
the workhouse precisely so that the experience would be humiliating enough to
deter all but the desperate. One system treated need as a risk to be pooled; the
other treated it as a moral failure to be punished.

That divide has never really closed. Every welfare state since has mixed two
logics. Social insurance is contributory, more or less universal within its
category, paid as of right, and politically almost impossible to cut because the
people receiving it believe they earned it. Means-tested assistance is targeted at
those with least, which makes it far cheaper per pound of poverty relieved, and
far more fragile. Targeted programmes carry stigma, which depresses take-up among
exactly the people they are meant to reach. They require intrusive administration
to verify need. And because their beneficiaries are few, poor, and less likely to
vote, they have no constituency to defend them when budgets tighten. Programmes
for the poor alone have a persistent tendency to become poor programmes.

## The Postwar Moment

Beveridge named five giants standing in the way of reconstruction: want, disease,
ignorance, squalor, and idleness. His proposal was to attack want through a single
unified scheme of social insurance covering the whole population, flat-rate
contributions for flat-rate benefits, with a residual safety net beneath it for
those who somehow fell through. He was explicit that this could only work if three
other things were in place: a health service free at the point of use, allowances
for children, and government commitment to maintaining full employment. An
insurance fund cannot survive mass unemployment, because the claims arrive exactly
when the contributions stop.

Most of it was legislated within a few years of the war ending. Family allowances
came first, then a comprehensive National Insurance Act, then the abolition of the
Poor Law and its replacement with national assistance, and in the summer of 1948
the National Health Service, which nationalized the hospitals outright and made
treatment free to anyone who needed it. Aneurin Bevan, the minister who pushed it
through against the opposition of much of the medical profession, famously settled
the consultants by letting them keep private practice — he said he had stuffed
their mouths with gold.

Britain was not alone, and in some ways not first. France built out its social
security system in the same period, Germany rebuilt Bismarck's on a broader base,
and the Scandinavian countries were already some way down a different road. The
Swedish Social Democrats had been campaigning since the late 1920s on the idea of
the folkhem, the people's home: a country run like a good household, where nobody
is treated as a favoured child or a stepchild. That image did a great deal of work.
It implied services for everyone rather than relief for the unfortunate.

## Three Ways of Doing It

The rich democracies ended up with systems that look superficially similar and
behave quite differently, and it is worth being precise about the differences.

The Anglo-American model leans on means testing and leaves a large space for
private provision. The United States is the extreme case: a contributory pension
system established in the 1930s that is genuinely universal and genuinely popular,
health coverage for the old and for the very poor added in the 1960s, and for
everyone else an employer-based health insurance system that exists largely because
wartime wage controls made fringe benefits the only legal way to compete for
workers. The result is a country that spends heavily on health and still leaves
people uninsured.

The continental model keeps Bismarck's shape. Entitlements are tied to occupation
and earnings, funds are segmented by industry or profession, and benefits are
designed to preserve a household's accustomed standard of living rather than to
equalize. These systems are generous in cash and traditionally thin in services,
which reflects an assumption that a wife at home would do the caring. They also
load their costs onto payroll, which makes employing people expensive.

The Nordic model is the outlier. Benefits are universal and residence-based rather
than earned, funded out of general taxation at rates the other groups would find
politically impossible, and a very large share of the money goes to services —
childcare, elder care, training — rather than cash transfers. Those services are
what allowed women to enter paid employment in large numbers, which in turn
produced the tax base to pay for them. The system is expensive and coherent: it
only works because nearly everyone works.

## Where the Strain Comes From

None of these designs anticipated the world they now operate in. They were built
around a specific and historically brief arrangement: a male breadwinner in stable
industrial employment for forty years, a wife doing unpaid care work, a short
retirement, and a workforce growing faster than the population of pensioners. Every
element of that has shifted. Retirement now lasts decades rather than years.
Careers are interrupted, self-employed, or broken across borders in ways that
contributory records handle badly. The risks that push a modern household into
difficulty — raising children alone, caring for a parent with dementia, having a
skill made obsolete in middle age — are not the risks these schemes were written
to insure.

Cost pressure is structural rather than political. Health care and education are
labour-intensive services whose productivity cannot rise at the pace of
manufacturing, so their relative price climbs decade after decade even if nothing
is wasted. A country that wants the same quantity of care must therefore devote a
growing share of its output to it. Meanwhile the ratio of contributors to
beneficiaries deteriorates as birth rates fall and longevity rises, which is
arithmetic, not ideology.

What is striking is how little of this has produced outright dismantling. Even
governments elected on promises to roll the welfare state back have mostly found
it easier to change parameters than to abolish programmes: nudging the retirement
age upward, altering the index used to uprate benefits, tightening the conditions
attached to unemployment payments, contracting delivery out. Pensioners vote.
Hospital closures are visible. The constituencies created by universal programmes
turn out to be their most effective defence, which is exactly what Bismarck had
in mind, though not on behalf of the people he ended up serving.

The deepest change these systems made was not to the distribution of income but to
the shape of a life. Before them, old age for most working people meant working
until you could not, and then depending on children or the parish. Retirement as
an expected, funded, named stage of existence is an invention of the last century,
and it was legislated into being by actuaries.
