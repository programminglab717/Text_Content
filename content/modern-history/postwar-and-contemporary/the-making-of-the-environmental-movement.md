# The Making of the Environmental Movement

On a Sunday morning in June 1969 an oil slick on the Cuyahoga River caught
fire in Cleveland, damaged two railway trestles, and was put out within half
an hour. It was not even a remarkable fire by the standards of that river,
which had burned repeatedly since the nineteenth century. The 1952 blaze had
been far worse. When a national magazine ran the story weeks later, the
photograph it used was from the earlier fire, because nobody had thought the
1969 one worth photographing.

That detail is the whole story in miniature. The pollution was old. What was
new was that a national audience was suddenly prepared to be scandalized by
it. Within a year the United States had an Environmental Protection Agency,
and within four it had the core of the regulatory apparatus that still
governs American air and water. Something had changed in the audience, not in
the river.

## Conservation Was Not Environmentalism

There had been a movement concerned with land for the better part of a
century, but it was a different movement with different instincts. American
conservation in the late nineteenth century was about setting things aside:
Yellowstone in 1872, then a series of parks and forest reserves, then the
Sierra Club in 1892. Its arguments were aesthetic and spiritual on one wing
and managerial on the other, and the two wings fell out spectacularly over
the damming of the Hetch Hetchy valley in Yosemite to supply water to San
Francisco. John Muir thought the valley sacred. Gifford Pinchot thought
forests and rivers were crops to be harvested wisely, for the greatest good
of the greatest number over the longest time. Pinchot won the dam. The split
between preservation and wise use never fully healed, and it still surfaces
whenever a wind farm is proposed on a scenic ridge.

The Dust Bowl of the 1930s added a second strand: soil, erosion, watersheds,
the recognition that agricultural practice could ruin a region within a
decade. And in 1949 Aldo Leopold's posthumous *A Sand County Almanac*
proposed something more radical than either wing had managed, a land ethic in
which soil, water and animals had standing of their own rather than value
only as resources. The book sold modestly for years and then became a
founding text for people who had never heard of Leopold when he was alive.

What none of this addressed was the problem that would define the postwar
decades: not the loss of wild places but the contamination of ordinary ones.

## Silent Spring and the Invisible Contaminant

Rachel Carson was an established writer on the sea when she published *Silent
Spring* in 1962. Her subject was synthetic pesticides, principally DDT, and
her argument was more precise than the caricature of it that circulated at
the time. She did not claim that all pesticides should be banned. She claimed
that compounds designed to be persistent and fat-soluble do not stay where
they are sprayed, that they concentrate as they move up food chains, and that
an industry and a government enthusiastically distributing them had done
almost nothing to find out what they did over the long run.

The chemical industry's response was ferocious and personal, questioning her
credentials, her sex, her lack of a doctorate, her politics. It failed,
partly because the biological argument held up and partly because the public
had already been trained by a decade of argument about fallout from
atmospheric nuclear testing. Strontium-90 in milk had taught an enormous
number of people a genuinely new idea: that something you cannot see, smell
or taste can arrive in your kitchen from a great distance and accumulate in
your children's bones. Carson wrote into an audience that already possessed
the concept.

This is the hinge on which environmentalism turned. Conservation asked people
to care about places they might visit once. The new argument asked them to
care about their own bodies, their own tap water, the air in the street
outside. It converted a minority enthusiasm into a mass constituency, and it
made pollution a matter of public health rather than of taste.

## The Legislating Decade

The first Earth Day, in April 1970, was organized loosely around an idea of
Senator Gaylord Nelson's: a nationwide teach-in modelled on the anti-war
teach-ins, with no central programme, which let millions of people
participate in whatever way made sense locally. Its scale startled the
politicians who had ignored the issue, and the legislative response over the
next several years was extraordinary in both speed and ambition. The
Environmental Protection Agency was assembled in 1970 out of pieces of half a
dozen existing bodies. The Clean Air Act was rewritten that same year with
health-based standards and deadlines that industry insisted were impossible.
The Clean Water Act followed in 1972, the Endangered Species Act in 1973. The
National Environmental Policy Act required federal agencies to produce
environmental impact statements, which quietly did more than any of the
others by giving citizens a procedural handle and a courtroom.

None of this was uniquely American. Japan had been through a sequence of
industrial poisonings whose names became shorthand for the whole category —
the mercury poisoning at Minamata, the cadmium disease in the Jinzu river
basin — and the resulting lawsuits and citizen campaigns pushed the
government into creating an environment agency in 1971. Britain, whose Clean
Air Acts had followed the deadly London smog of December 1952, was dealing
with acid deposition complaints from Scandinavia. West Germany's
forest-dieback alarm in the early 1980s helped put a Green party into a
national parliament, an institutional innovation that spread across Europe.
Sweden proposed and hosted the first United Nations conference on the human
environment, in Stockholm in 1972, which produced the UN Environment
Programme and, more importantly, the first sustained argument between rich
and poor countries about who had caused the damage and who was being asked to
pay for it. Indira Gandhi put the poor countries' case bluntly at Stockholm:
poverty was itself the worst polluter.

## Direct Action, and Movements Elsewhere

The organizations founded around 1970 had a different temperament from the
older societies. A small group in Vancouver chartered a boat in 1971 to sail
toward a planned American nuclear test at Amchitka in the Aleutians,
reasoning that a witness in the way is more persuasive than a petition. They
never reached the island; the test went ahead; the campaign nonetheless
helped end testing there, and the group became Greenpeace. Its method — put
bodies and cameras where the damage is happening, and let the pictures do the
arguing — was learned partly from the civil rights movement and proved
exportable to whaling ships, sealing grounds and industrial outfalls.

The same decades produced movements in the global South that had nothing to
do with recreation or wilderness and everything to do with subsistence. In
the Himalayan foothills, villagers in the Chipko actions placed themselves
between commercial loggers and the forests their households depended on for
fuel, fodder and protection from landslides, a tactic with deep roots in
Indian protest. In Kenya, Wangari Maathai's Green Belt Movement organized
rural women to plant trees, which turned out to be simultaneously an
ecological project, an income project and a challenge to a government that
understood perfectly well what an organized rural constituency meant. In
Brazil, rubber tappers led by Chico Mendes argued not for an empty Amazon but
for extractive reserves where people already living in the forest could keep
working it; Mendes was murdered in 1988 by a rancher's family, and the
reserves were created afterwards. These campaigns are often filed under
environmentalism as an afterthought. They were closer to the centre of what
the word would come to mean than the wilderness tradition was.

## The Arguments Inside

By the 1980s the movement in the rich countries had won enough to start
fighting with itself. One fault line was class and race. In 1978 residents of
Love Canal, a neighbourhood in Niagara Falls built over a buried chemical
dump, organized under Lois Gibbs and forced a federal evacuation, which led
directly to the Superfund programme for contaminated sites. In 1982,
protesters in a poor and largely Black county in North Carolina lay down in
the road to block trucks carrying PCB-laced soil to a new landfill. They
lost, but the campaign crystallized a claim that has shaped the field since:
that the burden of contamination is distributed along the same lines as
everything else, and that a movement led by affluent people concerned with
birds and canyons had not much noticed.

The other fault line was professionalization. Groups that began with
volunteers and lawsuits acquired staff, membership departments and offices
near legislatures. That bought real influence and real competence, and it
also put distance between the organizations and the people whose
neighbourhoods were at issue, which is roughly the point at which the phrase
"jobs versus the environment" became a reliable political weapon against them.

There is a final irony in the early record. The movement's clearest victories
were against pollution you could see, smell or photograph — rivers on fire,
brown air over Los Angeles, dead lakes in Ontario, a thinning ozone layer
whose repair under the 1987 Montreal Protocol remains the most complete
success of international environmental diplomacy. Each of those problems had
a visible symptom, an identifiable set of emitters and, usually, a substitute
technology. Carbon dioxide has none of these. It is odourless, it comes from
essentially everything, and its damage arrives decades after its release,
distributed among people who did not emit it. The movement's founding
instinct — show them the fire on the river — does not transfer.
