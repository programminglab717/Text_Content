# The Transatlantic Slave Trade

The surviving paperwork of the trade is almost entirely accounting. Muster rolls,
insurance policies, bills of lading, invoices for iron bars and Indian cotton
cloth, ledgers ruled into columns for men, women, boys and girls, with deaths
entered as a reduction in stock. A voyage that carried three hundred human beings
across an ocean leaves behind the name of the ship, the name of its master, the
name of its owners, the tonnage, the port of clearance, and a number. The people
themselves are almost never named. That asymmetry is not an accident of
preservation. It is what the system was built to do, and any honest account of it
has to work against the grain of its own sources.

The scale is now reasonably well established, because the ledgers were kept so
carefully and because a long collaborative effort has assembled records of tens
of thousands of individual voyages. Somewhere around twelve and a half million
people were embarked from Africa between the mid-fifteenth century and the
1860s. Somewhat over ten million survived the crossing to be landed in the
Americas. The gap is the Middle Passage.

## Where the Ships Went

The geography confounds most people's assumptions. Close to half of all
disembarkations were in Brazil, overwhelmingly to the sugar country of Bahia and
Pernambuco and later to the coffee districts of the southeast and the mines of
Minas Gerais. Another very large share went to the Caribbean — the British and
French sugar islands above all, and the Spanish colonies, with Cuba absorbing
enormous numbers late in the era. The mainland colonies that became the United
States received under five per cent of the total: a few hundred thousand people
directly from Africa. The enormous African-descended population of the
nineteenth-century United States was the result of natural increase, not of
continued importation, and that single demographic fact shaped almost everything
about how slavery in North America differed from slavery in the sugar colonies.

It differed because sugar killed people faster than they could be born. Cane
cultivation and processing, with its gang labour in the field, its harvest
crushing schedule, and its boiling houses run through the night, combined with
malnutrition, tropical disease and the near-impossibility of raising children
under those conditions, meant that Caribbean and Brazilian slave populations did
not reproduce themselves. They shrank annually, and planters replaced the
shortfall by purchase. The trade was, from the planters' point of view, a
continuous resupply of a consumable input.

The familiar triangle — manufactures to Africa, captives to the Americas,
sugar to Europe — describes some voyages and misdescribes many others. A great
many ships ran directly between Brazil and Angola, carrying Brazilian rum and
tobacco south and east and returning with captives, a bilateral circuit
independent of Lisbon. The Portuguese were in the business earliest, with
fifteenth-century voyages to the Upper Guinea coast and a plantation model
developed on São Tomé that was then transplanted across the Atlantic. The Dutch,
English and French entered in force in the seventeenth century, initially through
chartered monopoly companies and then, more productively for the trade, through
the private merchants who broke those monopolies. The eighteenth century was the
peak. Illegal shipments to Brazil and Cuba continued for decades after the trade
was outlawed.

## How People Were Taken

Europeans very rarely captured people inland themselves. Malaria and yellow
fever made deep penetration lethal for them, and it was unnecessary: African
states and merchants controlled the supply. Captives came from warfare between
African polities, from organized raiding, from judicial sentences and debt
seizure, and from kidnapping. Some were war prisoners in a conventional sense;
many were simply seized. They were marched to the coast, sometimes hundreds of
miles, yoked or coffled, in journeys that killed a substantial number before any
ship was reached, and held in barracoons or in the stone forts at places like
Elmina, Cape Coast, Ouidah, Bonny, Luanda and Benguela.

The goods that bought them were specific and demanded. Indian cotton textiles in
particular quantities and patterns, European woollens and linens, iron and copper
bars, brassware, alcohol, tobacco, cowrie shells shipped from the Maldives as
currency, and firearms. The firearms matter to the historical argument. A coastal
state that sold captives obtained guns; guns made further raiding more effective;
effective raiding produced more captives. Not every region was drawn into that
cycle, and some polities resisted or regulated participation, but where it took
hold it was self-reinforcing and extremely hard to exit, since a neighbour who
refused to trade simply became a target.

## The Crossing

A slave ship was fitted with a temporary deck built into the hold, dividing the
space so that people could be laid in two tiers with insufficient headroom to sit
upright. Men were normally shackled in pairs at wrist and ankle and kept below
except during brief periods on deck; women and children were less often ironed
and consequently more exposed to sexual violence from the crew. Crossings took
roughly one to three months depending on route, season and luck with the wind.

The deaths came mainly from dysentery — the flux — and from dehydration,
compounded by shipboard smallpox, measles and ophthalmia, and by the simple
effects of confinement in heat and filth. Mortality averaged around fifteen per
cent across the earlier centuries and fell over time to something below ten, not
because conditions became humane but because voyages got faster, provisioning
improved, and captains learned that a dead captive earns nothing. Where a ship
was becalmed or its water ran short, the figure could be catastrophic. Suicide by
jumping overboard was common enough that ships rigged netting along the sides,
and force-feeding with a mouth screw was standard equipment.

Resistance was constant and it was armed. Insurrections are recorded on something
like one voyage in ten, most often in the first days while land was still in
sight, and the possibility shaped the entire design of the vessel: the barricade
across the deck, the swivel guns pointed inward at the waist, the size of crews
relative to cargo. Successful revolts were rare and the reprisals were public and
extreme. But the constant threat of them raised costs measurably, and historians
have argued that insurrection and its suppression reduced the volume of the trade
by a meaningful margin.

The Zong case of 1781 is worth knowing precisely because it was a lawsuit rather
than a criminal prosecution. The crew threw more than a hundred and thirty living
people overboard, and the resulting litigation in England concerned whether the
underwriters were liable for the loss of cargo. No one was charged with murder.
The commercial logic was not hidden behind the law; it was the law.

## Arrival, Profit, and Argument

On arrival, captives were held for a period of "refreshment" to improve their
appearance, oiled, sometimes dyed or treated to conceal illness, and then sold at
auction or by "scramble," in which buyers rushed a yard at a signal and seized
whom they could. The first year or two in the Americas — planters called it
seasoning — killed a further large fraction through unfamiliar disease, overwork
and despair. Families formed at the coast or aboard ship were routinely broken at
the point of sale, deliberately, to inhibit organization.

Whether the profits of the trade financed the Industrial Revolution has been
argued for the better part of a century, since Eric Williams put the case in
*Capitalism and Slavery*. The strong version, in which slaving voyages generated
the direct capital for industrial investment, has not survived scrutiny: returns
on individual voyages were volatile and on average respectable rather than
spectacular. The weaker and more durable version is harder to dismiss. Slavery and
the slave trade were embedded in the whole commercial apparatus — the marine
insurance market, the bill-of-exchange networks, the shipbuilding and provisioning
industries, the sugar refineries, the ports of Liverpool, Nantes, Bristol and
Rio, the demand for the Lancashire and Indian cloth that paid for captives. Those
connections are documented in detail and were not marginal.

The effects on Africa were regionally uneven and, in the most affected areas,
severe. The trade drew disproportionately on men of working age, distorting sex
ratios and labour supply in the regions of heaviest export; it rewarded
predatory state-building and militarized political competition; and it made
insecurity a permanent feature of life across wide interiors. Some states grew
rich and powerful on it. The populations they raided did not.

Abolition came through a convergence: the sustained testimony and writing of
formerly enslaved people such as Olaudah Equiano and Quobna Ottobah Cugoano,
which put first-person evidence into British political argument; organized
campaigning by Quakers and evangelicals that invented much of the repertoire of
modern activism, including the consumer boycott; and, decisively, the Haitian
Revolution, which from 1791 destroyed the richest colony in the Caribbean and
established that the enslaved could take and hold a country. Britain and the
United States prohibited the trade in 1807 and 1808. Britain then spent decades
patrolling the African coast to suppress a traffic it had recently dominated,
with mixed results and mixed motives. Slavery itself outlasted the trade: through
emancipation in the British colonies in the 1830s, through the American Civil
War, and in Brazil until 1888, the last country in the Americas to end it.
