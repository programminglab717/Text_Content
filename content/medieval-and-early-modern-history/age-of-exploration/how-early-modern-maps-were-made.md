# How Early Modern Maps Were Made

Hold a portolan chart up to the light and you can see the animal in it. The
vellum keeps the shape of the skin it came from, the neck often trimmed into a
flap at one end, and across it runs a web of fine ruled lines radiating from
circles spaced around the margin. Those lines are not decoration. They are
compass bearings, sixteen or thirty-two of them from each node, and a pilot
working a chart like this laid a straightedge between his port of departure and
his destination, slid it to the nearest parallel rhumb, and read off the wind
direction he needed to hold. There is no grid of latitude and longitude on such
a chart. There is usually no interior at all — the land behind the coast is
blank or filled with flags and kings, because nobody sailing to Chios cared what
was inland of Smyrna.

What is startling about the Mediterranean portolans is how good they are. The
coastlines are accurate enough that modern overlays match them closely, centuries
before anyone could fix a longitude at sea. They were built from accumulated
practice: bearings taken by compass between headlands, distances estimated by
elapsed sailing time, and thousands of corrections folded in by generations of
copyists in Majorca, Genoa, and Venice. The method breaks down as soon as you
leave a body of water whose shores you know intimately. Push out into the
Atlantic and the accumulated bearings stop closing.

## Two Inheritances, One Argument

The chartmakers' craft was one tradition. The other arrived from the library. In
the first years of the fifteenth century a Greek manuscript of Ptolemy's
*Geography* reached Florence and was translated into Latin, and with it came an
idea that the practical chartmakers had never needed: that every place on earth
has a pair of coordinates, and that a map is a projection of a sphere onto a
plane according to a stated rule. Ptolemy supplied a gazetteer of thousands of
such coordinates and two workable conical projections. Printed editions from the
1470s onward put his world map in front of anyone who could afford a book.

It was also wrong in consequential ways. Ptolemy had the earth too small and
Asia extending too far east, which is precisely the error that made Columbus's
proposal look arithmetically survivable to Columbus and absurd to the Portuguese
experts who rejected it. He enclosed the Indian Ocean with a southern landmass
joining Africa to Asia, which is why the Portuguese rounding of the Cape was a
theoretical result as much as a navigational one. For most of the sixteenth
century European cartography consisted of grafting fresh reports onto this
inherited skeleton and arguing about which parts of the skeleton to snap.

## What a Navigator Could Actually Measure

Latitude was the solved problem. The altitude of the noon sun above the horizon,
corrected for the sun's declination on that date using printed tables, gives
latitude directly; so does the Pole Star, with a small correction for its offset
from the true pole. The instruments were crude and got slowly better: the
mariner's astrolabe, a heavy cast ring hung from a thumb ring so the ship's
motion mattered less; the cross-staff, sighted with the sun in your eye, which
ruined eyes; and eventually the backstaff, which let the observer turn away from
the sun and read its shadow instead. On a steady deck a competent pilot could
get within a fraction of a degree.

Longitude was not solved for another two hundred years. In the meantime,
navigation east and west was done by dead reckoning: a course held on the
compass, a speed estimated by heaving a weighted log over the stern and counting
knots in the line as a sand glass ran out, and a running tally kept on a traverse
board or in a logbook. Errors compounded relentlessly. Currents that nobody could
measure pushed ships sideways for weeks. Magnetic variation — the difference
between compass north and true north — changed with position and, infuriatingly,
with time, and its discovery as a variable rather than an instrument defect was
one of the genuine advances of the period. The standard practice on an Atlantic
crossing was to sail to the latitude of the destination well offshore and then
run down that parallel until land appeared, accepting a longer route in exchange
for a bearing you could trust.

All of this fed back into the charts. A returning pilot brought a rutter, a
written sailing direction recording headlands, soundings, tidal streams and
courses steered, and often a sketch of what a coast looks like from seaward,
which is far more useful for a landfall than a plan view. Compilers turned that
material into coastline.

## Mercator's Fix and the Print Shops

Charts drawn as plain squares — equal degrees of latitude and longitude — get
progressively more wrong as you go poleward, because the meridians converge on a
globe and do not on the paper. A course laid off as a straight line on such a
chart is not the course a helmsman steering a fixed compass bearing will
actually sail. Gerardus Mercator's 1569 world map solved this by stretching the
spacing of the parallels in step with the stretching of the meridians, so that at
every point the scale distortion is the same in both directions. The consequence
is that a line of constant compass bearing becomes a straight line on the sheet.
The cost is that areas near the poles balloon, which is why generations of
schoolchildren have absorbed a Greenland the size of Africa.

Mercator did not publish the mathematics, and seamen were slow to adopt a chart
whose parallels were unevenly spaced for no reason they had been told. The
English mathematician Edward Wright worked out the table of meridional parts
that made the projection constructible by any competent draughtsman, and
published it at the end of the century; from then the sea chart and the
projection converged.

The physical making of a printed map was a division of labour that has no
modern equivalent. A compiler gathered manuscript sources, rutters, older plates
and verbal report, and drew a reconciled original. A draughtsman transferred it
in reverse onto a polished copper plate coated with wax or chalk. An engraver
then cut the lines with a burin, a square-sectioned steel rod pushed rather than
dragged, throwing up a curl of metal ahead of the point; lettering was a separate
speciality, often subcontracted, and so were the cartouches, ships and sea
monsters that a good shop kept as house motifs. The plate was inked, its surface
wiped so that ink remained only in the incisions, and printed under heavy
pressure onto damp paper. Copper is soft, so a plate wore out after a few hundred
good impressions — but it could also be burnished flat in patches and recut,
which is why surviving states of the same plate can be dated by watching a
coastline change while the surrounding decoration stays identical. Colour, when a
buyer wanted it, was applied by hand afterwards, usually by women working in the
shop, and was a separate line on the bill.

Meanwhile the business of maps moved north. Antwerp, and after its sack
Amsterdam, became the center of a trade that ran on copperplate engraving —
finer than woodcut, capable of the hairline lettering and stipple that a
crowded map demands, and re-engravable when new information arrived. Abraham
Ortelius assembled the first work that behaves like a modern atlas: uniform
sheets at uniform sizes, bound in a consistent order, each with text on the
reverse, and, unusually for the time, a list naming the cartographers whose work
he had compiled. Mercator's own atlas, the Hondius and Blaeu houses that followed,
and the pilot books of Lucas Janszoon Waghenaer — so widely copied that English
sailors called any coastal atlas a waggoner — turned maps into a consumer product
for merchants, officials, and wealthy households as much as for navigators.

## The Padrón and the Leak

States understood immediately that a chart was strategic information. Both
Iberian crowns ran a central hydrographic office: the Portuguese Casa da Índia
and the Spanish Casa de Contratación in Seville, where a master chart — the
padrón real — was maintained as the official record of the known world. Pilots
were examined and licensed there, and were required on return to report
discrepancies between their charts and what they had found. A pilot major, a post
held at one point by Amerigo Vespucci and later by Sebastian Cabot, presided over
the corrections. Copies for issue were supposed to be drawn from the master and
from nothing else, and exporting them was a crime.

The system leaked constantly. Foreign agents bought pilots; pilots defected; the
famous planisphere spirited out of Lisbon for the Duke of Ferrara in 1502 shows
a Brazilian coast and an African route that Portugal intended nobody else to
have. And secrecy cut against accuracy: a chart that circulates gets corrected,
while a chart locked in a cabinet fossilizes.

Where information genuinely ran out, mapmakers filled the gap by inference or by
decoration. A vast southern continent balanced the northern landmasses for
reasons that were essentially aesthetic and Aristotelian, and it survived on maps
until Cook sailed through the space where it was supposed to be. A bad report and
a persuasive engraver turned California into an island on reputable maps for the
better part of a century, long after Jesuits had walked the overland route.
Northwest passages opened and closed with each expedition's optimism.

On land, the parallel revolution was triangulation, described in a short
appendix by Gemma Frisius in the 1530s: fix a baseline, measure it carefully
once, then determine every other point by intersecting sightlines rather than by
pacing. It is the method that eventually produced national surveys, and its logic
is the opposite of the portolan's. The portolan accumulates local truths and hopes
they cohere. Triangulation builds a rigid frame first and hangs the detail on it
afterwards. The sea would not permit the second approach until a clock could be
carried across an ocean without losing time, and that is a story of the eighteenth
century, not the sixteenth.
