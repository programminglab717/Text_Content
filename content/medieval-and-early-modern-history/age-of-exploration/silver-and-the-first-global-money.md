# Silver and the First Global Money

There is a mountain in the Bolivian altiplano, nearly conical, rust-coloured,
standing above a city at around four thousand metres where the air is thin enough
that walking uphill is work. The Spanish called it the Cerro Rico, the rich hill,
and for roughly a century after 1545 it produced more silver than anywhere else
on earth. The town at its foot, Potosí, grew from nothing into one of the largest
cities in the world — comparable in population to London or Naples at the time —
in a place that could not feed itself, supplied by mule trains hauling everything
from maize to Chinese silk up from the coast.

What came out of that hill did not mostly stay in Spain. A great deal of it ended
up in China. Tracing why is the clearest way to understand how a genuinely global
economy assembled itself in the sixteenth century, and why silver, rather than
spices or sugar or slaves, was the commodity that closed the circuit.

## Getting the Metal Out

Silver ore is not silver. Potosí's upper deposits were rich enough to smelt in
indigenous Andean wind furnaces — *huayrachinas*, clay chimneys set on ridgelines
and fired by the afternoon wind — but those deposits were worked out within a few
decades, and what remained was lower-grade ore requiring a different technology.

The answer was mercury amalgamation, developed for New Spain's ores in the 1550s
and adapted to Potosí after. Crushed ore was spread in a paved yard, mixed with
salt, copper sulphate and mercury, and trodden by mules or by men for weeks. The
mercury takes up the silver into an amalgam; the amalgam is then washed out,
squeezed through cloth, and heated so that the mercury boils off and leaves
refined metal behind. The process turned marginal ore into profit, and it made the
silver economy dependent on a second mining system entirely: mercury from Almadén
in Spain, and above all from Huancavelica in the Peruvian highlands, a mine whose
conditions were understood as a death sentence even by the standards of the time.
Mercury vapour poisons the nervous system. The men who worked the patios and the
retorts were poisoned slowly; the men who mined the cinnabar were poisoned faster.

Labour at Potosí came from the *mita*, a rotational draft reorganized in the
1570s by Viceroy Toledo out of an older Andean obligation. Communities across an
enormous catchment were required to send a proportion of their adult men each
year to the mines and refineries. They walked for weeks to get there, often with
their families, and a great many did not return — killed by accidents in the
shafts, by silicosis, by cold, by mercury. Villages impoverished themselves
hiring substitutes to avoid sending their own. The wealth that moved through
Seville and Manila was extracted at a cost that is documented in Spanish
administrative records by Spanish officials who were frequently appalled by it and
who kept the system running anyway.

## The Coin That Everyone Would Take

Spain minted its American silver into a coin of consistent weight and fineness:
the eight-real piece, struck at Mexico City, Lima and Potosí, known in English as
the piece of eight and later as the Spanish dollar. Its virtue was reliability.
Rulers everywhere debased their coinage when short of money, and merchants
everywhere therefore distrusted it; the Spanish crown, for all its financial
chaos, largely did not debase the eight-real, because the coin's reputation was
itself an asset and because the metal was arriving in such volume that debasement
was unnecessary.

The result was a currency accepted far beyond any territory Spain governed. It
circulated in the Baltic, in the Levant, in Bengal, in West Africa, and most
consequentially in China, where merchants came to prefer particular mints and
where the coins were often chopped with the marks of the traders who had verified
them. It became the standard of value in the British North American colonies,
which is why the United States adopted the dollar rather than the pound, and why
the fraction "two bits" refers to the eighths a piece of eight was physically cut
into. Mexican silver dollars were still circulating in East Asian ports in the
nineteenth century. It is difficult to name another currency with a comparable
reach before the twentieth century.

## Why China Pulled the Silver In

The demand side is the part usually left out. Ming China had experimented with
paper currency and abandoned it after inflationary collapse; copper coinage was
adequate for small transactions and useless for large ones. Over the course of the
fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, taxes, land rents, and commercial payments
shifted onto silver by weight, and in the later sixteenth century a set of fiscal
reforms — known collectively as the single whip method — consolidated the
empire's tangle of grain levies and labour services into consolidated payments in
silver. An economy of well over a hundred million people, with the most
sophisticated commercial sector in the world, effectively put itself on a silver
standard while producing very little silver domestically.

That demand showed up in the relative price of metals. Across Europe, gold
exchanged for something in the vicinity of twelve times its weight in silver. In
China the ratio was much narrower — for a long period closer to six to one. Silver
was, in plain terms, worth roughly twice as much in Guangzhou as in Seville. Any
merchant able to move bullion from one market to the other and back stood to
double his metal, and the entire structure of early modern intercontinental trade
can be read as an enormous, sustained arbitrage on that gap, one that persisted
for generations because the volumes required to close it were larger than the
shipping of the age could deliver.

Two routes carried it. The familiar one ran east: treasure fleets convoyed from
Veracruz and Portobelo to Seville and later Cádiz, where the crown took its
fifth, and from there the metal dispersed across Europe through the Genoese and
later Dutch bankers who had lent against it, moved on through the Baltic and
Levant trades, and passed to Asia in payment for the pepper, cotton, tea and
porcelain that Europe wanted and for which it had almost nothing Asia wanted in
exchange. The trade imbalance that so exercised eighteenth-century pamphleteers
had been structural for two hundred years.

The second route ran west, and is the reason some historians date the birth of a
truly global economy to a single decade. From 1565 a Spanish pilot's discovery of
the northern return route across the Pacific made a round trip from Mexico to the
Philippines practical, and with the founding of Manila in 1571 the circuit closed:
silver from Acapulco to Manila, Chinese silk, porcelain and worked goods back to
Acapulco, with Chinese junks meeting the galleons at Manila to make the exchange.
For two and a half centuries one or two ships a year made that crossing, and some
of them carried a substantial fraction of a year's Mexican silver output straight
to Asia without ever touching Europe. For the first time, every populated
continent was linked by a continuous exchange of goods paying for each other.

## What All That Money Did

Spain, holding the source, ended the period poorer in relative terms than its
neighbours. The crown spent the revenue on the Habsburg wars — the Netherlands
above all, and the Mediterranean, and the Armada — and spent it faster than it
arrived, borrowing against future fleets at high interest from Genoese and German
bankers and defaulting repeatedly. The silver passed through Castile on its way
to the creditors, financing other countries' manufacturing while Spanish
industry struggled against a high domestic price level. It is the earliest
worked example of a resource windfall crowding out the productive economy.

Prices across Europe rose more or less continuously through the sixteenth
century and into the seventeenth, roughly quadrupling in some series — the
so-called price revolution. Earl Hamilton's classic account attributed it
directly to American bullion swelling the money supply. Later work complicated
that considerably: population recovered strongly after the late-medieval
depression, urbanization advanced, more transactions were monetized, coin was
debased in several states, and the timing of price rises does not track bullion
arrivals as neatly as the simple story requires. Silver was clearly part of the
mechanism. It was not the whole of it.

In China, the dependence cut the other way. An empire that had made silver its
fiscal foundation was exposed to disruptions in a supply it did not control, and
the mid-seventeenth century brought disruption in quantity: declining arrivals,
restrictions on the Manila trade, warfare, harvest failures in a cold climatic
phase, and the collapse of the Ming dynasty. Historians disagree sharply about
how much weight the silver factor carries in that collapse, and the
monocausal version has been picked apart. But the debate itself is the point. By
the middle of the seventeenth century it had become possible to argue seriously
that a slowdown in a Bolivian mine might be felt in the tax receipts of a
province in Jiangnan, and no one could have argued that about anything a century
and a half earlier.

The mountain is still there, tunnelled through until it has visibly slumped,
still worked by cooperatives on ore that would have been waste rock in 1600.
