# The First Circumnavigations

In September 1522 a ship came up the Guadalquivir into Seville with her seams
working, her pumps manned by men too weak to stand a full watch, and eighteen
survivors aboard out of a fleet of five vessels and something over two hundred
and fifty men. She was the Victoria, the smallest but one of the ships that had
sailed three years earlier, and she had done what no vessel had done before: she
had gone out west and come home from the east. By one account the survivors went
ashore barefoot, in shirts, carrying candles, to give thanks at a shrine before
they did anything else.

The man who had conceived the voyage was not aboard. Ferdinand Magellan had been
dead for sixteen months, killed in knee-deep water off a small island in the
Philippines, and the captain who brought the ship home, Juan Sebastián Elcano,
had been one of the mutineers who tried to depose him off Patagonia. The first
circumnavigation was not a triumph in the way the phrase suggests. It was the
residue of a catastrophe.

## The Bet

Magellan was Portuguese, had served in India and at the taking of Malacca, and
had fallen out badly enough with his own king to take his proposal to Spain. The
proposal rested on a legal argument as much as a geographical one. The Treaty of
Tordesillas had drawn a line down the Atlantic dividing Portuguese and Spanish
spheres, but a line on a globe has a far side, and nobody knew where the
antimeridian fell in the eastern hemisphere. Magellan argued that the Spice
Islands — the clove islands of Maluku, the most valuable real estate in the
world per acre — lay in the Spanish half. If a passage could be found through or
around the Americas, Spain could reach them without sailing past a single
Portuguese fort.

Everything depended on two unknowns. The first was whether a strait existed at
all; the estuaries of the South American coast had a way of looking like
passages for a few days and then turning fresh and shallow. The second was how
wide the ocean beyond it was, and here the expedition was working from estimates
that were catastrophically wrong. The best guesses put a modest sea between
America and Asia. Five ships sailed from Sanlúcar in September 1519 provisioned
for a voyage that nobody could size correctly.

## The Strait

The fleet probed the coast south of Brazil, spent days investigating the Río de
la Plata before its water turned sweet, and by autumn in the southern hemisphere
was far enough south that Magellan put in to winter at a bleak harbour he named
Puerto San Julián. There the Spanish captains, who had never accepted a
Portuguese commander, rose against him. Magellan put the mutiny down with more
nerve than force, executed one captain, left another marooned on the shore with
a priest, and kept the fleet together. One ship, the Santiago, was wrecked while
scouting the coast; her crew walked back overland.

In October 1520 they found the opening. The passage was nothing like a clean
channel: a maze of dead-end inlets, tide-scoured narrows, and mountain walls
with williwaws coming down them, and it took over a month to work through. Part
way in, the San Antonio — the largest ship, carrying a disproportionate share of
the provisions — put about in the night and ran for Spain. Magellan pressed on
with three, and in late November came out into open water that was, for the
moment, so unthreatening after the strait that he called it the peaceful sea.

## The Pacific

The Pacific crossing is the part of the voyage that still reads as barely
survivable. They ran west and northwest for something close to a hundred days
without finding a single inhabited island to reprovision at, threading by sheer
bad luck through the emptiest water in the largest ocean on earth. Antonio
Pigafetta, an Italian gentleman who had joined the expedition as a supernumerary
and kept the account that gives us most of what we know, described eating
biscuit reduced to powder and swarming with maggots, drinking yellow water,
stripping the leather chafing-gear off the yards and soaking it for days to make
it chewable, and paying good money for rats. Scurvy did what scurvy does: gums
swelling over the teeth, old wounds reopening, men dying in numbers.

They made a landfall in the Marianas, took on food after a violent scuffle over
a stolen boat, and pushed on to the islands that would later be called the
Philippines. There Pigafetta recorded a moment worth more than any of the
geography. Magellan's slave and interpreter, a man from the Malay world called
Enrique, spoke to people in a canoe and was understood. The voyage had reached
the far side of the known world from the wrong direction, and the proof of it was
a conversation.

Magellan then made a choice that historians have argued about ever since. He
allied with a local ruler at Cebu, accepted mass baptisms, and agreed to punish
a chief on the neighbouring island of Mactan who would not submit. He crossed at
dawn with a small force, could not bring the boats close over the reef, and
waded ashore into a much larger body of fighters under a leader remembered as
Lapulapu. He was killed in the shallows covering his men's retreat. Within days
the alliance at Cebu turned, a number of the surviving officers were killed at a
banquet, and the expedition fled with too few men to sail three ships. They
burned the Concepción and went on with two.

## Cloves, and a Missing Day

They reached Tidore in the Moluccas in November 1521 and were received
courteously, because the local sultan had his own reasons for wanting an
alternative to the Portuguese. Both ships loaded cloves. Then the Trinidad was
found to be leaking badly, and the two captains split the risk: Trinidad would
be repaired and attempt to sail back east across the Pacific to Spanish America,
while the Victoria would run west, straight through Portuguese waters, and try
for home.

The Trinidad's attempt failed. Beating east against the trades, she was driven
back, and her surviving crew were taken by the Portuguese; only a handful ever
saw Europe again. Elcano's Victoria crossed the Indian Ocean without touching
any port where she could be seized, rounded the Cape of Good Hope in heavy
weather with her crew dying again of scurvy, and was finally forced by hunger to
put in at the Cape Verde Islands under a false story about coming from the
Americas. The Portuguese there detained more than a dozen of her men.

It was at Cape Verde that the expedition made its final discovery, and it is the
one that delights people most. The ship's careful daily record said one date;
the islanders said another, a day later. They had lost a day by sailing
westward with the sun, gaining a fraction of a rotation on each circuit of the
globe, and no one had anticipated it. The theoretical point had been discussed
by scholars, but the Victoria's log was the first physical demonstration that a
calendar is a function of where you have been.

The cloves in her hold were worth a great deal — enough, it is often said, to
have covered the cost of the entire expedition, which says more about the price
of cloves than about the voyage. Spain's claim to the Moluccas came to nothing
anyway: a follow-up fleet in 1525 ended in wreck and disaster, the problem of
sailing back east across the Pacific stayed unsolved for another forty years,
and in 1529 the Spanish crown, short of money, sold its claim to Portugal for a
substantial cash payment. The Pacific return route was finally worked out in
1565, when a navigator on a voyage from the Philippines ran far north to find
westerly winds and rode them across to the California coast. That track became
the Manila galleon, and it ran for two and a half centuries.

## The Ones Who Came After

The second circumnavigation waited more than fifty years and had a different
purpose. Francis Drake sailed from Plymouth in 1577 nominally on a trading
venture and actually on a raid. He wintered in the same Patagonian bay where
Magellan had faced his mutiny, tried and executed a gentleman of his own company
there, and made a point of the parallel. He came through the strait, ran north
up a Pacific coast where no Spanish port expected an enemy, took a treasure ship
so heavily laden that the transfer of silver took days, careened somewhere on
the California coast, then crossed the Pacific, nearly lost his ship on a shoal
in the East Indies, loaded spices, and came home in 1580 with a cargo whose
value embarrassed a queen who had to decide whether to disavow him. She knighted
him on his own deck instead. Drake was the first commander to complete the
circuit and live.

Thomas Cavendish made the third circumnavigation within the decade, deliberately
imitating Drake and doing rather less well out of it. The Dutch followed:
Olivier van Noort at the turn of the century, and then in 1616 Jacob Le Maire
and Willem Schouten, who suspected that Tierra del Fuego was an island and found
out by sailing around its southern tip, naming the cape after their home town of
Hoorn. Their reward for proving there was a route into the Pacific that bypassed
the strait was to have their ship confiscated in Java by their own countrymen,
on the grounds that the passage they had just discovered could not possibly
exist and they must therefore have trespassed on somebody's monopoly.

Which leaves Enrique, the interpreter. He had been taken west from the Malay
world to Europe years before, and he came east again with Magellan until he
heard his own language spoken from a canoe. After the killings at Cebu he
disappears from the record. Whether he ever stood again on the ground he had
been taken from — whether the first human being to go all the way around was a
slave whose name we barely have — is a question the sources cannot answer.
