# The Spice Trade

A ripe nutmeg looks like a small yellow apricot until it splits along a seam and
opens. Inside is a glossy brown seed wrapped in a crimson net of aril, and the
two are sold as different spices: the seed as nutmeg, the lacy red covering,
dried to brittle orange, as mace. Until the end of the eighteenth century the
tree that produces them grew in one place on earth — a cluster of small volcanic
islands in the Banda Sea, so compact that a sailing ship could pass the whole
group in a day. Cloves were nearly as concentrated, native to a handful of
islands off the west coast of Halmahera, a few hundred miles north.

Almost everything strange about the spice trade follows from that fact. Here
were commodities that grew wild on a few square miles of volcanic soil, kept
well, weighed almost nothing relative to their value, and were wanted from
Guangzhou to Bruges. A cargo that could be carried by one small ship could make
a merchant's fortune. The distance between the grower's price and the consumer's
price was enormous, which meant that every party along the route had a powerful
motive to control a link in it, and eventually that somebody with cannon would
try to control the whole thing.

## What the Spices Actually Were

Most of the great spices are plant defences: aromatic compounds a tree or vine
evolved to discourage being eaten, which humans then acquired a taste for. The
useful part varies considerably.

- **Cloves** are flower buds, picked just before they open and dried until they
  harden and darken.
- **Pepper** is the fruit of a climbing vine, picked green and sun-dried until
  the skin wrinkles black; white pepper is the same berry soaked until the outer
  layer can be rubbed away.
- **Cinnamon** is inner bark, stripped from the coppiced shoots of a tree,
  scraped, and left to curl into quills as it dries.
- **Ginger and turmeric** are rhizomes, dug rather than picked.

That last difference mattered commercially. A rhizome travels as a living plant
and roots wherever the climate allows, which is why ginger spread early to other
tropical regions and never commanded the prices of nutmeg. So did the
distinction between true cinnamon, grown in Sri Lanka, and the coarser cassia
bark from mainland Southeast Asia and southern China, which was cheaper and
constantly sold as the real thing. Europe had a market for near-substitutes:
grains of paradise from the West African coast, long pepper from India, and
saffron, the one great spice Europeans grew themselves, which is expensive for
the plainest of reasons — it is hand-plucked stigmas, and it takes a very large
number of flowers to make an ounce.

## The Chain

Nothing about the route was direct, and until the sixteenth century no single
merchant travelled its whole length. The Bandanese grew nutmeg on islands too
small and too volcanic to feed themselves, so they traded spice for rice, sago,
cloth, and ironware brought by Javanese, Bugis, and Malay shipping. Those
traders carried the cargo west to entrepôts, above all to Malacca, sitting on
the strait where the South China Sea meets the Indian Ocean and where the timing
of two monsoon systems made a natural waiting room.

From there the spices moved with the monsoon across the Bay of Bengal and the
Arabian Sea, in the hands of Gujarati, Tamil, Malay, Persian, and Arab merchants
who had worked these routes for centuries with a sophistication that later
European accounts consistently underestimated. Some of the cargo went to
Chinese markets, which had been buying cloves since antiquity. Some accumulated
at the great Indian ports of the Malabar and Gujarat coasts, where it joined
pepper grown locally.

Westward from India the route forked. One branch ran up the Red Sea to Jeddah,
was transhipped, and went overland by caravan to Cairo and down the Nile to
Alexandria. The other ran up the Persian Gulf to Basra and overland to Aleppo
and the Levantine ports. At the Mediterranean end, Venetian galleys did most of
the carrying into Europe, and Venice grew rich as the last link, redistributing
north through the Alpine passes and the Rhine.

Every transfer added a markup, a customs duty, a bribe, or a loss, and the
compounding was spectacular. The price of cloves in Maluku and the price of
cloves in a London apothecary's shop were related only distantly, by way of a
dozen intermediaries none of whom knew what the others charged. Europeans
understood that they were buying at the end of a very long pipe, and had only
vague ideas about where the other end was. That ignorance was itself a traded
commodity: stories about where cinnamon came from — gathered from the nests of
enormous birds, in one persistent version — were good for margins.

## What They Were For

The most repeated explanation is the wrong one. Spices were not used to disguise
rotting meat. Anyone who could afford cloves could afford fresh meat, and
preservation was done with salt, smoke, drying, and cold cellars, all of which
were cheap. The real demand had three strands.

The first was medicine. European physicians worked within a humoral framework in
which foods and drugs carried qualities — hot, cold, moist, dry — and imported
spices were classified as hot and dry, which made them the standard correctives
for a long list of complaints. Apothecaries sold them by weight alongside
opium, rhubarb, and theriac. Nutmeg was carried in pomanders against plague air.
Much of the pepper landed at Venice went out again through pharmacies.

The second was cuisine, and it was a genuinely spiced cuisine rather than a
disguised one. Medieval and Renaissance cooking at the upper end used ginger,
grains of paradise, cinnamon, and sugar — itself a spice in this scheme — in
sauces built on bread and vinegar, producing flavour profiles closer to modern
Persian or north Indian food than to anything that later became French.

The third was display. A dish that announced its expense in aroma before anyone
tasted it did exactly what a host wanted at a feast, and the fashion moved down
the social scale as prices fell, at which point the aristocracy lost interest.
The heavily spiced style went out of favour in seventeenth-century France for
reasons that were partly about taste and partly about the fact that ordinary
people could now afford pepper.

## Trying to Own the Source

When Portuguese ships came around the Cape at the end of the fifteenth century,
they arrived at the far end of a system that had functioned for a thousand years
without them, and the strategy they settled on was to tax it rather than replace
it. Afonso de Albuquerque took Goa, then Malacca, then Hormuz at the mouth of
the Gulf, with the aim of holding the chokepoints. Portuguese authorities issued
passes called cartazes, which other people's ships were required to buy, and
which committed them to calling at Portuguese-controlled ports and paying duty
there. It was a protection racket with a legal vocabulary.

It never worked completely. The Portuguese failed to take Aden and could not
seal the Red Sea, and within a generation spice was flowing again to Cairo and
Venice in quantity. Their production base was never secure either, because
cloves and nutmeg were grown by people with their own politics — the rival
sultanates of Ternate and Tidore played European powers against each other for
decades with considerable skill.

Control of the source, when it finally came, was achieved by the Dutch and
achieved with a thoroughness that is hard to read about. Nutmeg was reduced to a
single administered crop in the Banda Islands after a campaign in 1621 in which
the Bandanese population was killed, enslaved, or driven off, and the groves
were reassigned to Dutch planters worked by enslaved labour. Cloves were
concentrated by force on Ambon, and fleets of rowed boats were sent out annually
for the specific purpose of cutting down clove trees growing anywhere else in
the archipelago. Cinnamon in Sri Lanka was taken over from the Portuguese and
administered as a state harvest. For a while this produced the purest monopoly
in the history of trade, with production deliberately limited and surplus stock
burned in Amsterdam to hold the price.

## The Premium Evaporates

A monopoly held by force over a plant that can be carried in a pot has a
predictable weakness. Through the eighteenth century, French agents — the most
persistent of them an administrator improbably named Pierre Poivre — smuggled
clove and nutmeg seedlings out of the archipelago and established them on
Mauritius and Réunion, from where they spread to other tropical colonies. The
British, during the wars at the turn of the nineteenth century, occupied the
Moluccas and moved nutmeg stock to Penang and eventually to the Caribbean, which
is how Grenada came to put a nutmeg on its flag. Cloves ended up dominated by
Zanzibar and Pemba, thousands of miles from Halmahera.

Pepper had already gone the same way, spreading from Malabar to Sumatra, Java,
and later Indochina until it was a bulk commodity shipped by anyone with hold
space. What had been the most valuable cargo in the world became an ordinary
grocery. The trees still grow on the Banda Islands, and the harvest is still
split and dried in the same two parts, red and brown, on mats in front of the
houses. The islands themselves are a long, awkward journey from anywhere, which
was once the entire point.
