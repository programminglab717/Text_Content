# The Spanish Conquest of the Americas

On 8 November 1519 a few hundred Spaniards walked down a stone causeway several
miles long into Tenochtitlan, a city built on an island in a lake, larger than
any city in Spain, fed by chinampa gardens and a freshwater aqueduct, organized
around a precinct of pyramids and a market that one of them later wrote had no
equal in Europe. Twenty-one months later the same city was rubble, its canals
choked with bodies, its surviving population evacuating past Spanish and
Tlaxcalteca lines. Understanding how that happened requires setting aside both
of the stories usually told about it: that a handful of Europeans with steel and
horses overthrew an empire, and that disease alone did the work while the
Spaniards watched.

## The Caribbean Rehearsal

Nothing about the mainland conquests makes sense without the twenty-five years
that preceded them. Columbus's settlements on Hispaniola established the pattern
that would be exported: a demand for gold backed by force, a system of tribute
quotas imposed on Taíno communities, and then, when the gold proved thin and the
quotas unpayable, the distribution of whole villages to individual Spaniards as
labour. The *encomienda* — a grant of the right to demand labour and tribute
from named communities, in exchange for a nominal obligation to protect and
Christianize them — was legally not slavery and functionally close to it.

Within a generation the Taíno population of the Greater Antilles had essentially
ceased to exist as an independent society, destroyed by a combination of
epidemic disease, forced labour in placer mining, the disruption of subsistence
agriculture, and outright killing. The survivors were absorbed or fled. The men
who sailed for Mexico and Peru were not innocents encountering a new world; they
were veterans of this system, and many were in debt and looking for the grant of
people that would make them gentlemen.

The legal apparatus grew alongside it. The *requerimiento*, a document read aloud
in Spanish to communities who could not understand it, informed them of papal
donation and Castilian sovereignty and stated that any resulting war and
enslavement would be their own fault. It was frequently read to empty beaches or
from shipboard. Its absurdity was noticed at the time.

## Mexico

Hernán Cortés left Cuba in 1519 in defiance of the governor who had commissioned
and then cancelled the expedition, which meant his legal position depended on
producing a spectacular result. On the coast he acquired the single most
important asset of the campaign: a Nahua woman given to the Spaniards as a slave,
known as Malintzin or doña Marina, who spoke both Nahuatl and Maya and who,
working initially with a shipwrecked Spaniard fluent in Maya, made negotiation
possible. Conquest through an interpreter is a different thing from conquest by
force alone. It permits alliances.

And alliances were the decisive factor. The Triple Alliance centered on
Tenochtitlan ruled through tribute extracted from subject provinces, and the
resentment was real and available. The Totonacs of Cempoala came over early.
Tlaxcala, an independent confederation that had resisted incorporation for
generations, fought the Spaniards hard for weeks and then, having taken their
measure, allied with them — and supplied the tens of thousands of soldiers,
porters and provisions without which the campaign is inconceivable. From the
Tlaxcalteca side this was not surrender but a strategic bet against an old enemy.
The massacre of the nobility at Cholula, which Spanish accounts justify as
pre-empting an ambush, was carried out with Tlaxcalteca participation and served
Tlaxcalteca ends.

The events in Tenochtitlan ran through a hostage situation — Moctezuma held in
his own palace while Cortés governed through him — that collapsed while Cortés
was away on the coast fighting off a rival Spanish force sent to arrest him. His
deputy ordered a massacre of unarmed celebrants at the Toxcatl festival in the
temple precinct; the city rose; Moctezuma died in circumstances the sources
disagree about; and the Spaniards were driven out across the causeways at night
with heavy losses, dragging loot that drowned many of them.

What followed that retreat mattered more than any battle. Smallpox, introduced
to the mainland by the relief force from Cuba, reached the Valley of Mexico in
1520 and burned through a population with no prior exposure. It killed the
successor ruler Cuitláhuac after a reign of weeks. It killed commanders,
priests, farmers and the people who would have brought in the harvest, and it did
so at a moment when the alliance needed to be reconstituted under new leadership.
Cortés returned in 1521 with reinforcements, a far larger indigenous army, and
thirteen brigantines built in sections at Tlaxcala and carried over the mountains
to be assembled on the lake, which gave him control of the water and let him cut
the causeways and the aqueduct. The siege lasted roughly three months. Cuauhtémoc
surrendered in August 1521 after his people had been reduced to drinking lake
water and eating whatever remained.

## Peru

Francisco Pizarro's expedition arrived in the Andes in 1532 with fewer men than
Cortés had commanded, into a polity vastly larger and more centralized: roads,
storehouses, a standing administrative apparatus, and a system of labour tribute
reaching from Ecuador to Chile. It should have been impossible. It was not,
largely because the Spaniards arrived at the end of a succession war.

Epidemic disease had preceded them by several years, moving overland ahead of any
European, and had killed the ruler Huayna Capac and his designated heir. The
resulting contest between his sons Atahualpa and Huáscar had just been settled by
force, leaving the empire's elites divided, provincial loyalties raw, and a
victorious ruler consolidating his position at Cajamarca with an army camped
outside the town.

The ambush at Cajamarca in November 1532 is the hinge. Pizarro invited Atahualpa
into the square, concealed horsemen and gunners in the surrounding buildings, and
at a signal attacked a ceremonial retinue that had come without weapons. They
killed a great many of them in the enclosed space and took Atahualpa alive.
Holding the ruler paralyzed a system built around him. Atahualpa offered to fill
a room with gold and twice over with silver; the Spaniards accepted, collected
the ransom from temples and palaces across the Andes, melted it into ingots, and
then tried and executed him anyway in 1533.

Even then the conquest was not a single event. The Spaniards ruled through
puppet rulers, one of whom, Manco Inca, escaped and raised a general revolt in
1536 that besieged Cuzco and nearly succeeded. A rump Inca state survived in the
forested country at Vilcabamba until 1572. Meanwhile the conquerors fell on each
other in a series of civil wars — Pizarro against Almagro, then their heirs, then
Gonzalo Pizarro in open rebellion against a crown attempting to curb the
encomienda — that killed most of the leading conquistadors, Francisco Pizarro
included, and that the crown ultimately won.

## What Killed the Population

The demographic collapse across the Americas in the sixteenth century was among
the largest in recorded history. Its scale is disputed, because pre-contact
population estimates vary widely and the evidence is fragmentary, but the
direction is not in doubt: in the core areas of Mexico and Peru, populations fell
by something on the order of nine-tenths over roughly a century, with the
Caribbean islands effectively depopulated far faster.

Smallpox, measles, typhus and influenza arriving in succession into populations
with no acquired immunity account for the largest share. But treating epidemics
as a natural disaster separate from the conquest misreads how epidemics work.
People who are conscripted into mine labour, marched long distances, resettled
into congregated towns for administrative convenience, stripped of stored food by
tribute demands, and prevented by war and labour drafts from planting at the
right time, die of infections that better-fed and undisturbed communities
survive. Famine followed the disruption of agriculture. Birth rates fell with the
breakup of households. The epidemics and the exploitation were not two stories.

Silver made the system permanent. The strike at Potosí in 1545 and at Zacatecas
soon after turned the Americas from a source of plunder into a source of revenue,
and Viceroy Toledo in the 1570s reorganized Peru around it — resettling
communities into new towns, and repurposing the Andean rotational labour
obligation into the *mita*, a draft that sent men from a huge catchment to work
in the mines and refineries at Potosí. Men went for a year and often did not come
back. Whole villages bankrupted themselves hiring substitutes.

## Law, Argument, and Aftermath

Spain conducted an unusually loud public argument with itself about all of this.
Dominicans in Hispaniola denounced the encomenderos from the pulpit as early as
1511. Bartolomé de las Casas, a former encomendero turned friar, spent decades
pressing the crown, and his campaigning helped produce the New Laws of 1542,
which barred new encomiendas and ordered existing ones to lapse. The colonists
revolted, and the crown backed down on the key provisions. In 1550 and 1551 the
crown convened a formal debate at Valladolid between Las Casas and Juan Ginés de
Sepúlveda over whether the indigenous peoples of the Americas could be justly
subjected by force. No decisive ruling issued.

What the legal machinery did produce, unintentionally, was a forum. Indigenous
communities and nobles learned to litigate — over land, over tribute assessments,
over abusive officials — and Spanish American archives are full of their
petitions, often written in Nahuatl or Quechua-influenced Spanish by native
notaries. They lost frequently and won sometimes. Meanwhile the conquest's frontier
kept receding without ever closing: the Chichimeca wars in the north ground on for
decades, the Mapuche held the country south of the Bío-Bío against Spain for three
centuries, and the last independent Maya polity in the Petén was not taken until
1697. The map that Europe drew of a conquered continent was, for a very long time,
a claim rather than a description.
