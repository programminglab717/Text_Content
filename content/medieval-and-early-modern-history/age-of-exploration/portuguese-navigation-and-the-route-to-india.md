# Portuguese Navigation and the Route to India

Somewhere on the dry coast of what is now Namibia, in the mid-1480s, a
Portuguese crew manhandled a pillar of limestone out of a ship's hold, rowed it
ashore through surf, and set it upright on a headland. The stone had been cut in
Lisbon and carried thousands of miles as ballast with a purpose. It bore the
royal arms, a cross, and an inscription naming the king and the year. The
Portuguese called these markers padrões, and they planted them at the farthest
point of each voyage, so that the next captain would know exactly where the
known world ended and his own work began.

That habit says most of what matters about how Portugal reached India. There was
no single voyage of discovery, no leap into the unknown. There was a program:
decades of incremental probing down an unfamiliar coast, each expedition
starting where the last one stopped, with the results written down, charted, and
fed back into the next attempt. The hard part was never courage. The hard part
was the wind.

## The Wind That Would Not Let You Home

The Atlantic off northwest Africa is a one-way street. The northeast trades blow
steadily toward the equator, and the Canary Current runs the same direction
along the shore. A ship sailing south from Lisbon has an easy time of it. A ship
trying to return has the wind and the current in its teeth, and a square-rigged
vessel of the early fifteenth century could not beat to windward for weeks on
end against both.

This is the real content of the famous terror of Cape Bojador, a low sandy
projection on the Saharan coast that mariners for years declined to pass. The
stories about boiling seas and a green sea of darkness were the folklore that
grew around a practical fact: past that point, with a following wind pushing you
deeper into the trades, getting home became a genuinely open question. When Gil
Eanes finally rounded Bojador in 1434 and came back, he did it by refusing to
hug the coast on the return. He stood out into the open ocean, away from land,
until he found wind he could use.

That manoeuvre, elaborated over the following decades, became the single most
valuable piece of knowledge Portugal possessed. The Portuguese called it the
volta do mar — the turn of the sea. Instead of clawing back up the African
shore, a homeward-bound ship sailed northwest into the middle of the Atlantic,
losing sight of land for weeks, until it reached the latitude of the Azores and
picked up the prevailing westerlies that would carry it home. It is
counterintuitive to the point of feeling wrong. To get to Lisbon from Guinea you
first sail away from Lisbon, for a very long time, into empty water, on faith in
a wind you cannot see yet.

Sailors learned this pattern in the Atlantic islands. Madeira was settled in the
1420s, the Azores over the following two decades, and the traffic back and forth
to those colonies taught a generation of pilots that the ocean's winds were
organized into great rotating systems. Once you believe the North Atlantic turns
like a wheel, it is a short step to suspecting the South Atlantic does too, in
the opposite sense. That suspicion is what eventually made the route to India
possible.

## A Ship That Could Come Back

The caravel is often described as the vessel that made exploration possible,
which overstates it slightly, but only slightly. It was a small ship — many were
under a hundred tons — with a shallow draft that let it work close inshore and
enter river mouths, and it carried lateen sails: tall triangular canvas on
steeply angled yards, borrowed from Mediterranean and Arab practice. A lateen
rig points much closer to the wind than a square rig. For a coast where the
return leg is a windward problem, that is the whole game.

Caravels were also miserable. They were wet, cramped, had almost no hold space,
and could not carry provisions for a long voyage or trade goods in quantity.
Crews lived on deck. As voyages lengthened, the Portuguese compromised: the
caravela redonda carried square sails on the forward masts for running downwind
and kept lateens aft for handling, and for the India voyage itself the fleets
were built around larger round-hulled naus, with a caravel or two along for
scouting and a storeship to be emptied and broken up when its cargo was gone.

## Latitude Without a Pole Star

Coastal sailing works by recognition — a headland, a river bar, the colour of
the water over a bank. Open-ocean sailing needs numbers. The Portuguese built
their navigation on a single measurable quantity: the height of a celestial body
above the horizon, which gives latitude.

Near home this was easy. The Pole Star sits close to the celestial pole, so its
altitude is roughly your latitude, and pilots carried simple rules for
correcting the small error depending on the orientation of the stars that circle
it. But as the fleets pushed south, Polaris sank toward the horizon, became
useless in haze, and around the equator vanished altogether. The Southern Cross
helped with orientation but offered no equivalent shortcut.

The answer was the sun. If you measure the sun's altitude at local noon, and you
know its declination for that date — how far north or south of the celestial
equator it stands — you can work out your latitude anywhere on earth. That
requires astronomical tables of real accuracy, and Portugal obtained them,
drawing on the work of the astronomer Abraham Zacuto, whose almanac was adapted
into a seaman's handbook of rules and declination values. The resulting manual
told a pilot, in plain steps, what to add and what to subtract. It turned a
problem in spherical astronomy into arithmetic a ship's officer could do.

The instruments were crude and the Portuguese knew it. A quadrant hung from a
thumb and sighted along an edge, or a heavy brass ring called a mariner's
astrolabe with a rotating sight bar, gave readings that a rolling deck could
ruin. Crews went ashore whenever they could, hung a large wooden astrolabe from
a tripod on a beach, and took the noon sight standing on solid ground. Latitude
they could get within a degree or so. Longitude they could not get at all. East
and west position stayed a matter of dead reckoning — course held, time elapsed,
speed estimated by eye and long experience — and it stayed that way, with all
its accumulated error, for centuries.

## The Cape, and the Monsoon Beyond It

Bartolomeu Dias sailed in 1487 with two caravels and a storeship. Off the
southwest African coast he met a gale, ran south under shortened sail for days
out of sight of land, and when he finally turned east to find the shore again
there was no shore. He had been blown around the bottom of the continent without
seeing it. Steering north, he made landfall in a bay well up the eastern side.
His crews, exhausted and frightened, refused to continue. He pushed on to a
river mouth a little further along, planted his padrão, and turned back — and
only on the return did he actually see the great cape he had bypassed in the
storm. He is said to have named it for the storms. The king, who understood what
the discovery meant, renamed it the Cape of Good Hope.

Nearly a decade passed before Portugal used what Dias had found. When Vasco da
Gama left Lisbon in July 1497 with four ships, the long delay showed in his
sailing plan, which was nothing like Dias's cautious coasting. Da Gama went
southwest from the Cape Verde Islands into the open South Atlantic, swung wide
in an enormous arc that took him nearly to the coast of Brazil, and then let the
southern westerlies carry him east toward the Cape. He was something like three
months out of sight of land — an open-water passage longer than anything
recorded before — and he emerged almost exactly where he intended. That arc, the
volta do mar applied to a whole ocean, was the true navigational achievement of
the voyage.

Beyond the Cape the problem changed character entirely. The Indian Ocean was not
unknown water; it was somebody else's well-used sea, crossed for centuries by
Arab, Gujarati, Persian, and Swahili shipping that worked the monsoon like a
timetable — northeast in winter, southwest in summer, with a narrow window for
each crossing and a long wait if you missed it. At Malindi on the Kenyan coast
da Gama took aboard a pilot who knew that system, and with the summer monsoon
behind him reached Calicut on the Malabar coast in under a month.

What happened there was less impressive. The gifts he had brought — cloth,
sugar, honey, hats, washbasins — were the sort of thing that impressed a West
African ruler and insulted a south Indian one. The Zamorin's court, accustomed
to merchants trading gold and fine textiles, treated the Portuguese as shabby
newcomers. Da Gama bought a modest cargo of pepper and left, and then made his
worst mistake: he sailed for home against the monsoon, out of season. The
crossing that had taken weeks took months. Scurvy took much of the crew, one
ship had to be burned for want of hands, and barely a third of the men who had
left Lisbon came back.

They came back with pepper, though, and with something worth more. Within two
years Pedro Álvares Cabral was taking a larger fleet out along the same Atlantic
arc — swinging so far west that he struck the coast of Brazil on the way — and
within a decade the Portuguese were fortifying Indian Ocean ports and issuing
passes to other people's ships. The charts, meanwhile, leaked: an agent of the
Duke of Ferrara got a copy of the royal planisphere out of Lisbon in 1502, and
the shape of a world stitched together by wind systems began circulating in
courts that had not paid for it. The cargo that mattered was never the pepper.
It was the discovery that the way to a place is frequently not toward it.
