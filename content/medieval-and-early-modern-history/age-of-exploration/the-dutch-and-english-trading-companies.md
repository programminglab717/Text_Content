# The Dutch and English Trading Companies

On the last day of 1600, Elizabeth I put her name to a charter giving a group of
London merchants the sole English right to trade east of the Cape of Good Hope.
The grant cost the treasury nothing. She was not investing; she was handing over
a licence to exclude other Englishmen, which is the cheapest thing a government
can give away and one of the most valuable things a merchant can receive. Less
than two years later the States General of the Dutch Republic did something
similar but considerably stranger, and the difference between the two documents
shaped the next two centuries.

What both were responding to was a problem of arithmetic. A voyage to the East
Indies took two or three years, tied up a fortune in ships, crew, provisions,
and silver, and had a real chance of returning nothing at all because the ship
sank, the crew died, or a Portuguese squadron took the cargo. No individual
merchant could absorb that risk repeatedly. The obvious answer was to pool
money across many investors, and the less obvious answer — the one that
eventually mattered more — was to pool it permanently.

## Two Charters, Two Structures

The English company began as a series of separate ventures under one legal
umbrella. Investors subscribed to a particular voyage, and when the ships came
home the cargo was sold, the accounts closed, and the capital returned along
with any profit. Subscribe again next year if you liked the result. This is
transparent and easy to audit and it has one crippling defect: nobody wants to
pay for a fort, a warehouse, or a permanent staff out of a fund that is going to
be wound up in three years. Terminable stocks kept the English trade in a
hand-to-mouth condition for decades, and a permanent joint stock only arrived in
the 1650s.

The Dutch went the other way immediately, and did so because the government
forced them to. Half a dozen competing Dutch companies had sprung up in the
1590s and were bidding against each other in Asian ports, driving up purchase
prices and driving down sale prices at home, while separately failing to fight
the Portuguese. In 1602 the States General merged them into the United East
India Company — the VOC — under a charter that ran twenty-one years and was
repeatedly renewed. The subscription raised over six million guilders, far
beyond anything previously assembled for a commercial purpose.

The merger left visible seams. The VOC kept six regional chambers — Amsterdam,
Zeeland, Delft, Rotterdam, Hoorn, Enkhuizen — each fitting out its own ships and
keeping its own books, with Amsterdam dominant. They were governed by a board
of seventeen directors, the Heeren XVII, in which Amsterdam held eight seats and
the presiding vote rotated so that the largest chamber could not quite control
everything by itself. It was a federal company for a federal republic.

## The Share That Could Be Sold

The consequential clause was almost an accident of administration. Because the
VOC's capital was not to be repaid at the end of a voyage, an investor who
wanted out had to find someone to buy his position. Transfers were recorded in
the chamber's books in front of two directors, and a market in those transfers
developed within a few years, conducted in the open near the Amsterdam exchange.

A tradable claim on an ongoing enterprise behaves differently from a share in a
voyage. Its price moves on news — a fleet sighted, a war rumoured, a director's
remark repeated — and almost at once the market developed the full apparatus of
speculation. Forward contracts appeared. Options appeared. In 1609 a syndicate
led by a disgruntled former director sold VOC stock he did not own, intending to
buy it back cheaper after talking the price down, and the authorities responded
with what is generally reckoned the first regulation against short selling.
Within a lifetime an Amsterdam writer could publish a dialogue describing the
exchange's manoeuvres with the weary irony of a man who had lost money in all of
them.

## Companies That Could Declare War

Both charters contained powers no modern corporation has. The VOC could build
forts, maintain soldiers and warships, appoint governors, conclude treaties with
Asian rulers, coin money, and wage war — all east of the Cape, all in its own
name, all under an oath its officers swore to the company. The English charter
was initially narrower and grew in the same direction. Europeans were working
with a convenient doctrine that peace treaties signed in Europe did not
necessarily extend "beyond the line," which meant that ships of two nations at
peace at home could fight each other in the Indian Ocean without anyone
considering it a war.

The intellectual defence of this was produced to order. When a Dutch captain
seized a rich Portuguese carrack in the Strait of Singapore in 1603, the
legality of the prize was disputed, and the company retained a young lawyer
named Hugo Grotius to argue the case. What he produced, in a chapter published
separately as Mare Liberum, was the argument that the sea cannot be owned and
that trade is a natural right no power may monopolize — a principle of
international law that arrived in the world as a commercial brief on behalf of a
company whose entire business model was monopoly.

The company acted accordingly. Jan Pieterszoon Coen established a headquarters
at Batavia on Java in 1619 and pursued a policy of controlling production at the
source rather than competing for cargo in open markets. English factors who got
in the way in the spice islands were tortured and executed at Amboyna in 1623,
an episode the English complained about for generations, and which had the
practical effect of pushing the English company out of the spice trade
altogether.

## Cloth, Tea, and the Accidental Empire

That expulsion turned out to be the best thing that ever happened to the English
company. Shut out of cloves, it concentrated on India, where it had established
a foothold at Surat and sent an ambassador to the Mughal court to negotiate
trading privileges — as a supplicant, since the emperor commanded resources that
made the visitors look like a modest delegation from a damp island.

What the English found in India was cotton cloth. Indian weavers produced
lightweight printed and painted fabrics at a quality and price European
producers could not approach, and demand in England became a craze: calico,
chintz, muslin. The trade grew large enough that English wool and silk
interests got Parliament to ban the wearing of imported printed cotton, which
did not stop the trade so much as redirect it into re-export and eventually into
domestic imitation — the mechanized spinning of the following century was an
attempt to compete with Indian cloth. The company also bought saltpetre, which
was gunpowder, and increasingly tea from China, which was paid for in silver
until an alternative was found in Indian opium.

Trade of that scale needed secure bases, and the company acquired them: a fort
on the Coromandel coast, an island harbour on the west coast that arrived in the
English crown as part of a Portuguese dowry and was handed on to the company for
a token rent, and a settlement on the Hooghly that became Calcutta. Each was a
walled compound with warehouses, a garrison, and a court, and each was
technically held under the sufferance of a local power.

When Mughal authority fragmented, that arrangement inverted. The company found
itself intervening in Bengali succession disputes, won a battle at Plassey in
1757 largely by arranging the defection of the opposing commander, and in 1765
obtained the diwani — the right to collect the land revenue of Bengal and its
neighbouring provinces. A trading firm had become a tax authority over tens of
millions of people. It was not well suited to it. A catastrophic famine followed
within five years, and the company's own finances collapsed so badly by 1772
that it had to be rescued by the British government, which took the opportunity
to attach conditions. Parliamentary supervision arrived in stages, the
monopolies were stripped away in the early nineteenth century, and after the
rebellion of 1857 the crown simply took the whole apparatus over.

## The Dutch Decline

The VOC's ending was slower and less dramatic. Employees on modest salaries
thousands of miles from oversight traded on their own account, and by the
eighteenth century private trade was a large part of the real economy of the
company's ports. Dividends were paid handsomely and regularly, sometimes out of
borrowing rather than profit, because the directors represented a Dutch
investing public that expected them. Costs of garrisons and shipping rose while
spice margins fell, and the company was slow to move into the bulk goods — tea,
coffee, textiles — where the growth now was. A war with Britain in the 1780s
wrecked its shipping, and at the end of the century the charter was allowed to
lapse and the state assumed the debts. The territories stayed, and became the
Dutch East Indies.

The word the companies used for their overseas posts was factory, which had
nothing to do with manufacturing. A factory was where the factor lived — the
agent who bought and sold on behalf of distant principals, kept the books, and
made himself agreeable to the local authorities. The word later drifted to mean
a building where things are made, and it is hard to think of a neater record of
what these organizations did: they went out as agents for other people's money
and came back, where they came back at all, as something else entirely.
