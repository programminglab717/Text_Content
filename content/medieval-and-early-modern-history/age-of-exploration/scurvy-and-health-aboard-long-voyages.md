# Scurvy and Health Aboard Long Voyages

The first sign was usually not the gums. It was lassitude — a heaviness that
men mistook for laziness in each other, a reluctance to go aloft, a tendency to
sit down. Then the skin at the base of the hair follicles began to pucker and
bruise, particularly on the legs, in small purple points that ran together into
blotches. The gums swelled, went spongy, and bled at a touch; teeth loosened in
sockets that no longer held them. Old wounds that had healed years earlier came
open again along their original lines, and old broken bones separated at the
callus. Men who had seemed merely tired dropped dead when hauled upright to work
the capstan, because the heart's connective tissue had been failing along with
everything else.

That last detail is the key to the whole disease. Scurvy is not an infection or
a poisoning; it is the collapse of collagen. Building collagen requires
hydroxylation of proline and lysine residues, and the enzymes that do that work
need ascorbic acid to keep their iron in the reduced state. Almost every mammal
makes its own ascorbic acid in the liver. Primates, guinea pigs and a few other
lineages carry a broken copy of the final enzyme in the pathway, so we must eat
it. A body stores perhaps a few weeks' comfortable surplus and a couple of
months' grim reserve. Deprive it longer and the scaffolding of blood vessels,
skin, tendon and bone quietly stops being repaired.

## A Disease Invented by Logistics

Scurvy was rare on land in ordinary times, and rare at sea on the coastal and
Mediterranean routes that dominated European shipping for centuries, because
nobody went more than a few weeks without something green. It became a
catastrophe when voyages became long. The Portuguese runs to India, the Spanish
Pacific crossings, and then the whole apparatus of oceanic trade and exploration
created something new: a closed nutritional system, sealed for months, stocked
entirely with food chosen for the fact that it would not spoil.

Ship's biscuit was flour and water baked to a stone and baked again, edible for
years if kept dry, which it never entirely was. Salt beef and salt pork sat in
casks of brine that leached out what little the meat had to begin with. Dried
peas, oatmeal, cheese hard enough to be carved into buttons, beer while it
lasted and spirits after, and water in wooden casks that went green and then
went foul and then, oddly, cleared again as the microbial succession ran its
course. It is a ration engineered for shelf life, and ascorbic acid is one of
the least stable things in food: destroyed by heat, by prolonged storage, by
oxidation, by contact with copper.

The arithmetic was therefore brutally simple. Around six to eight weeks out from
the last fresh provisioning, in a crew that had been eating poorly ashore to
begin with, men began to sicken. Magellan's survivors described gums grown over
their teeth so that they could not eat. Vasco da Gama lost a large portion of his
company on the Indian Ocean legs. Two centuries later, Anson's squadron, sent
round the world to raid Spanish possessions, lost the great majority of its men,
the overwhelming share of them to scurvy rather than to any Spaniard — a disaster
conspicuous enough, and reported publicly enough, to force the Royal Navy to
treat the disease as a strategic problem rather than an occupational nuisance.

## Why the Cure Kept Getting Lost

The exasperating thing about the history is that the remedy was found repeatedly
and repeatedly mislaid. Dutch East India crews planted vegetable gardens at the
Cape for exactly this reason. Richard Hawkins recommended oranges and lemons from
his own experience in the 1590s. Captains along the Atlantic coasts knew that
spruce beer, scurvy grass, cresses and fresh onions answered. The knowledge
existed. It simply could not stabilize, because there was no theory that made it
make sense.

Every available framework explained scurvy as the presence of something rather
than the absence of something. It was putrefaction in the blood, and so alkaline
remedies should answer. It was the damp and foul air of the lower deck, and so
ventilation and smoking the decks with brimstone and vinegar should answer. It
was the salt in the diet, or blocked perspiration, or idleness, or homesickness —
and each of these theories had supporting evidence, because voyages that produced
scurvy also produced damp, salt meat, idleness and despair. Remedies that did
nothing at all appeared to work whenever a ship made port, since landfall
supplied both the proposed cure and, incidentally, fresh food. Remedies that
genuinely worked failed on the next voyage because the juice had been boiled
down to a syrup for storage, or stored in copper, or kept two years.

James Lind's trial aboard HMS *Salisbury* in 1747 is famous because of its shape:
twelve scorbutic sailors, kept on the same diet and quarters, divided into six
pairs, each pair given a different candidate treatment — cider, elixir of
vitriol, vinegar, seawater, a paste of garlic and spices, and two oranges and a
lemon a day. The citrus pair recovered so quickly that one was fit for duty
inside a week. It is one of the earliest controlled therapeutic comparisons on
record. But Lind did not draw the modern conclusion from it. He kept a
putrefaction theory, buried the trial in a long and cautious treatise, and
recommended concentrating citrus juice by heating it into a storable "rob" —
which destroys most of the vitamin. He had the answer and the wrong reason, and
the wrong reason wrecked the answer.

James Cook's voyages muddied the water further in the other direction. Cook lost
almost nobody to scurvy across years of Pacific sailing, and the Admiralty took
notice. His actual methods were a bundle: relentless provisioning with whatever
was fresh at every island and anchorage, sauerkraut carried in quantity and
issued by rule, portable soup, insistence on clean bedding, dry decks and washed
clothing, and a willingness to flog men for refusing unfamiliar food. Sauerkraut
does retain some vitamin C. But Cook publicly credited wort of malt, which
contains none, and the Admiralty accordingly invested in malt. Success attributed
to the wrong component is worse than failure, because it forecloses the question.

## Institutional Memory, Finally

What ended scurvy in the British service was not a discovery but an
administration. Gilbert Blane, physician to the fleet and well connected enough
to be heard, pushed for a daily issue of lemon juice preserved with a little
spirit, provisioned as a standing item rather than a medicine dispensed when men
were already ill. From the mid-1790s the Royal Navy issued it as routine, and
within a few years scurvy essentially disappeared from a fleet that was then
keeping squadrons at sea continuously for years on blockade duty. The strategic
consequences were considerable: a navy that can stay on station indefinitely is a
different instrument from one that must come home to recover.

Even then the knowledge was fragile. When the service later switched from
Mediterranean lemons to West Indian limes — cheaper, imperially sourced, and
lower in ascorbic acid to begin with — and then began pumping the juice through
copper tubing and storing it in clear glass, the protection quietly failed. That
failure, combined with a revival of theories blaming tainted meat, is why polar
expeditions in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries were still
losing men to a disease the Navy had beaten a hundred years before.

## The Rest of the Sick List

Scurvy dominates the retelling because it is specific to long voyages, but it was
rarely the leading killer in any given year. Typhus, carried by body lice and
thriving wherever men slept in the same unwashed clothes in crowded tiers of
hammocks, arrived aboard with newly pressed or recruited men and could gut a
ship's company before it cleared soundings. Dysentery — the flux — spread through
fouled water and heads, and killed steadily. Typhoid, smallpox and measles came
aboard with passengers. On the African and Caribbean stations, yellow fever and
malaria produced mortality that dwarfed everything else; European crews in the
slave trade died at rates comparable to, and in some phases exceeding, the rates
among the captives they carried, which is an indictment of the trade rather than
a mitigation of it.

The physical environment made all of it worse. The lower deck of a warship in
bad weather was closed up, unlit, and streaming with condensation. Bilge water
stood under the ballast, stinking. Men slept in fourteen inches of allotted
hammock width, in shifts. Reformers of the later eighteenth century attacked this
with practical engineering as much as with medicine: windsails to force air below,
Hales's mechanical ventilators, stoves to dry the decks, an insistence on washing
and airing bedding, purpose-built sick berths sited forward under the forecastle
where there was light and air, and quarantine of new drafts before they joined the
ship's company. Much of this was justified by miasma theory, which was wrong about
causation and right about practice. Dry, ventilated, clean-clothed crews with
enough water got less typhus and less dysentery, whatever anyone believed about
the air.

What the whole episode leaves behind is less a story of heroic discovery than one
about the difficulty of holding on to a fact. The cure for scurvy was known,
written down, printed, and forgotten, several times, by literate and intelligent
people with every incentive to remember it.
