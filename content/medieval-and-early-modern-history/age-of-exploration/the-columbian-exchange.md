# The Columbian Exchange

Take three dishes that seem to belong absolutely to the places that make them: a
Sichuan stir-fry built on chilies, a Neapolitan tomato sauce, an Irish potato
field. None of those plants existed in the Old World before the end of the
fifteenth century. Every one of them arrived on a ship, was treated with
suspicion for decades, and then embedded itself so thoroughly in a local cuisine
or a local economy that the memory of its arrival wore away.

The historian Alfred Crosby gave this two-way biological traffic a name in the
early 1970s, and the phrase stuck because it captured something that political
history had been missing. The consequential thing about 1492 was not the
transfer of sovereignty over some islands. It was that two sets of living
things, separated since the last ice age had closed the land bridge, were
suddenly reconnected — plants, animals, and pathogens — and the reconnection ran
in both directions but not on anything like equal terms.

## What Went East

The American contribution to Old World agriculture was mostly calories and
flavour. Maize, potatoes, sweet potatoes, cassava, beans, squash, peanuts,
tomatoes, chilies, cacao, vanilla, pineapple, and tobacco all crossed within a
century or so of contact, and several of them turned out to be exactly the crops
that marginal land had been waiting for.

Maize grows where wheat struggles. It took hold in the Mediterranean, in the
Balkans, in West Africa, and in the uplands of southern China, and it did so
partly because it produced a decent yield on hillsides and thin soils that had
previously supported almost nothing. Cassava did the same job in drier and
poorer African country, with a catch: the more productive bitter varieties carry
enough cyanogenic compounds to kill, and they are edible only after grating,
soaking, pressing, and cooking. The processing knowledge travelled and was
elaborated, and cassava became a staple across a broad belt of Africa. Sweet
potatoes reached China through the Philippines late in the sixteenth century —
Chinese accounts credit a merchant with carrying vines home from Luzon — and
became famine insurance in provinces where rice paddies were already fully
subscribed.

Chilies moved fastest of all, because they were light, dried well, grew
almost anywhere warm, and answered a demand that already existed. Portuguese
ships carried them to West Africa, to the Malabar coast, and on to Southeast
Asia within a few decades, where cooks who had been paying for imported black
pepper found they could grow a sharper heat in a kitchen garden. Within a
century chilies had worked their way into the cooking of Sichuan and Hunan, into
Korean fermented pastes, into Hungarian paprika fields and the stews of the
Balkans and North Africa. The tomato, by contrast, dawdled. It was grown
ornamentally in Italy for generations and regarded as dubious food before it
became the base of a sauce, and northern Europeans stayed wary of it longer
still.

The potato took longer and mattered most. It arrived in Europe as a curiosity,
grown in botanical gardens, distrusted because it belonged to the nightshade
family and produced nothing recognizable above ground. Adoption came slowly
through the eighteenth century, pushed along by agronomists and by rulers who
had noticed that an acre of potatoes fed more people than an acre of rye and
could not be burned by an army in the way a standing grain crop could. Northern
Europe's population grew on it. Ireland grew on it most of all, and when a
water-mould from the Americas reached a countryside dependent on a narrow range
of potato varieties in the 1840s, the exchange delivered its cruellest lesson
about the risk carried in the same parcel as the gift.

## What Went West

Traffic in the other direction was heavier and more organized, because it was
deliberate colonial policy. Wheat, barley, rye, rice, chickpeas, olives, grapes,
citrus, bananas, coffee, and above all sugarcane crossed the Atlantic in
purposeful shipments, along with the livestock that European agriculture could
not function without: cattle, horses, pigs, sheep, goats, chickens.

The animals changed American landscapes faster than the crops did. Released into
grasslands with no comparable large grazers and few predators willing to take
them on, cattle and horses multiplied at rates that astonished the people who
turned them loose. Pigs driven along as walking rations for exploring
expeditions escaped, went feral, and rooted their way through woodlands and
gardens. In central Mexico, sheep and cattle grazed valley systems that had
carried dense irrigated cultivation and helped turn them into scrub within a few
generations.

Horses had the most striking second life. Escaped and traded north from Spanish
settlements, they reached the Great Plains in numbers during the seventeenth
century, and peoples who had hunted bison on foot rebuilt their societies around
mounted hunting and raiding within a couple of generations. The Comanche,
Lakota, Cheyenne, and Blackfeet cultures that nineteenth-century Americans
treated as timelessly ancient were in fact recent, dynamic responses to an
imported animal. It is a useful correction to any tidy story about Europeans
acting and everyone else receiving.

Sugar was the darkest transfer. Cane is a crop of tropical lowlands that demands
enormous, brutal, coordinated labour at harvest and in the boiling house, and
the plantation complex worked out in the Atlantic islands was transplanted to
Brazil and the Caribbean at scale. The demographic collapse of Native American
populations and the unsuitability of European indentured labour to the disease
environment produced the answer that shaped four centuries of Atlantic history:
enslaved Africans, shipped in millions. Human beings were part of the exchange,
and so were the diseases they carried with them. Yellow fever and the
falciparum malaria parasite established themselves in American tropics, and by
killing newcomers at ferocious rates they helped lock in the plantation system's
logic.

Tobacco went east as a drug rather than a food, and it went fast. Within
decades of contact it was being smoked, chewed, and taken as snuff from London
to Istanbul to Japan, condemned by physicians and rulers who tried bans and
taxes and mostly discovered that they had acquired a reliable revenue stream
instead. It also became the first American export crop to make a colony viable:
Virginia, which had nearly failed, survived on it, and in surviving it
established the pattern of a plantation staple grown for a distant market by
labour that was coerced first by indenture and then by slavery.

## Why the Diseases Ran One Way

The epidemiological side of the exchange was catastrophically lopsided.
Smallpox, measles, influenza, typhus, whooping cough, diphtheria, and mumps
moved west and devastated societies that had never met them. Almost nothing
moved east with comparable force. Syphilis is the one serious candidate, and its
origins remain genuinely contested — skeletal evidence has been read both ways,
and the case is not closed.

The asymmetry is not mysterious. Most human crowd diseases began as animal
diseases, jumping from domesticated herds kept in close, continuous contact with
dense human populations. Eurasia had cattle, pigs, horses, sheep, goats,
chickens, and ducks, and thousands of years of cities and caravan routes to
circulate the pathogens that emerged from them until they became endemic
childhood illnesses among survivors. The Americas had far fewer domesticable
herd animals — llamas and alpacas in the Andes, guinea pigs, turkeys, muscovy
ducks — and nothing like the same crowded, continent-spanning traffic in
livestock.

So Old World populations arrived carrying a pathogen load they had partial
immunity to, and met populations with no prior exposure at all. The conquests
that follow in the textbooks look different once this is in view. Smallpox
reached central Mexico during the campaign against Tenochtitlan, killing
through the besieged city while the fighting went on.

Epidemics frequently ran ahead of the colonizers themselves, travelling along
indigenous trade networks, so that European arrivals in many regions
encountered societies already reeling from an invisible event they could not
explain. Estimates of pre-contact American population vary widely enough that
the arguments are still live, but the direction and scale of the collapse are
not in dispute: most of the population of two continents, within a century or
so of first contact.

## Weeds, Worms, and Second-Order Effects

The organisms nobody intended to move may have reshaped as much as the ones
people chose. Dandelion, plantain, and a variety of European grasses spread
through disturbed colonial ground so reliably that Native peoples named them
after the newcomers whose footsteps they followed. Rats came ashore from every
ship. Honeybees were introduced for wax and honey and their swarms outran
settlement, arriving in country no colonist had seen.

Earthworms are the quiet example. Glaciation had scoured native earthworms from
the forests of the northern part of North America, and those woodlands had
developed over thousands of years around a thick, slowly decomposing layer of
leaf litter. European worms, arriving in ships' ballast and root balls,
consumed that layer. Understory plants adapted to a deep duff have been losing
ground ever since, in forests that look untouched.

Some proposed consequences reach further still. One hypothesis holds that the
depopulation of the Americas allowed enormous areas of farmland to revert to
forest, drawing down atmospheric carbon dioxide enough to contribute to the cold
decades of the Little Ice Age. The mechanism is plausible and the magnitude is
argued over, which is roughly the state of any claim that tries to connect
mortality on one continent to the temperature of the whole planet.

What the exchange was not is a trade. A trade implies parties who negotiate.
This was a collision of biotas with a human catastrophe embedded in it, and its
gains and costs landed on entirely different people. Europe and Asia got
calories that supported population growth for centuries. Africa got maize and
cassava, and the slave trade. The Americas got wheat and cattle and an
extinction-level demographic event. The one Andean gift that eventually came
back around was cinchona bark: the quinine that let nineteenth-century Europeans
survive long enough in West Africa to colonize it. Even the medicine kept the
books unbalanced.
