# Timbuktu and the Empire of Mali

Timbuktu sits a few miles north of the Niger, at the point where the river,
having run northeast out of the well-watered savanna, makes its great bend and
turns away southeast again. That geography is the whole explanation for the
city. A canoe coming downstream from Djenné with grain, dried fish and kola
could get no closer to the desert; a camel coming south out of the Sahara with
salt could get no closer to the river. Somewhere in between, goods had to be
lifted off one and loaded onto the other, and wherever that happens for long
enough, a town grows. The Tuareg who used the spot as a seasonal camp in the
twelfth century were not founding anything. They were parking near water.

What turned that transfer point into a name Europeans would spend centuries
mispronouncing and misimagining was the empire that came to control it, and the
peculiar industry the city developed once it was rich: the copying, selling,
teaching and hoarding of books.

## Sundiata's Inheritance

The Mali empire begins, in the telling of the griots who have transmitted the
story for something like eight hundred years, with a crippled boy. Sundiata
Keita, son of a minor Malinke king, unable to walk until adolescence, exiled,
then returning to lead a coalition against Sumanguru of Sosso and beating him at
Kirina around the middle of the 1230s. The epic is not a chronicle and it is
not trying to be; it is a charter, a statement about who owes loyalty to whom
and why, recited by professional keepers of memory whose families still hold the
office. But the outline is corroborated by the Arabic sources, and the political
result is not in doubt. Within a few generations the Malinke rulers controlled a
belt of West Africa running from the Atlantic coast to beyond the Niger bend,
including the goldfields of Bambuk and Bure and the salt road that crossed the
desert to Morocco.

Mali was not a centralized state in the European sense. It was a paramount
kingship over a mass of provinces, tributary kings, garrison towns and merchant
communities, held together by tribute, by marriage, by an army of considerable
size, and by the mansa's control of the long-distance trade that made everyone
in the system richer. The mansa took the nuggets and let the gold dust
circulate — a policy North African writers noted with interest, since it kept
the price up and the supply predictable. Gold left the empire north across the
sand. Copper, cloth, horses, books and salt came back.

## The Pilgrimage

In 1324 Mansa Musa went to Mecca. The journey is the single best-documented
episode in the empire's history, because he stopped in Cairo on the way and the
Egyptians never got over it. He travelled with an enormous retinue, distributed
gold to officials, to the poor, to anyone who presented themselves, and bought
whatever caught his eye at prices that suggested he had no clear idea what
things cost. Writers in Cairo a decade later recorded that his spending had
pushed down the value of gold in the city's markets and that it had still not
fully recovered. Whether or not the depression was as severe as they said, the
episode fixed Mali in the Mediterranean imagination as a place of absurd
wealth. Within a few decades Musa was appearing on European maps as a crowned
king holding a gold nugget, which is roughly how a fourteenth-century Catalan
mapmaker chose to say "the money comes from over here."

The pilgrimage mattered inside Mali too. Musa came home with architects,
scholars and jurists, among them the Andalusian poet and builder Abu Ishaq
al-Sahili, who is credited with work at Timbuktu and Gao. Mosques were built or
rebuilt, including the great mud-brick congregational mosque at Timbuktu whose
walls are replastered communally every year. The mansa's public commitment to
Islam gave the merchant and scholarly classes an unambiguous position at court,
without displacing the older ritual obligations that bound the Malinke countryside
to its rulers. When Ibn Battuta arrived in the 1350s, under Musa's brother
Sulayman, he found a kingdom where the roads were safe, children were made to
memorize the Qur'an, and Friday prayer was so crowded you had to send a servant
ahead with a mat to hold your place. He also found, to his visible irritation,
that women moved freely and unveiled, that courtiers threw dust on their heads
when the king spoke to them, and that the food was not to his taste.

## Books as Property

Timbuktu's scholarly life grew out of its commercial life and never entirely
separated from it. The teachers were often merchants, or merchants' sons, or
supported by merchant kin; a manuscript was both an object of study and a
durable asset, like a house or a bolt of imported cloth. What people call the
university of Sankore was not an institution with a rector and a registry. It
was a mosque quarter in which a number of independent scholars held circles —
you read a particular book with a particular teacher, and when you had finished
he wrote you a certificate saying so, and that chain of certificates was your
qualification. Law, Qur'anic commentary, grammar, logic, astronomy, medicine,
poetry and the properties of plants were all taught. Copying was a trade with
its own workshops, and imported paper from North Africa was one of the more
valuable things a caravan could bring.

The manuscripts themselves are physical objects with habits of their own. Most
are unbound — loose leaves kept between tooled leather covers and slipped into a
satchel, which is one reason so many survived being moved in a hurry. The script
is a distinctively West African hand, rounder and heavier than the Maghribi
styles it grew from, written with reed pens in soot-based ink with headings and
vowel marks picked out in red and yellow. Margins fill up with the notes of
successive owners: corrections, cross-references, a teacher's gloss, the price
paid, the name of the man who copied it and how long it took him. A fair number
are not scholarship at all but the residue of ordinary life — contracts, letters
about a debt, an inventory, a judge's ruling on an inheritance dispute — and
those are often the most informative, because nobody was writing them for
posterity.

The scale of it is easy to underestimate because so much was private. The
libraries were family libraries, passed down and split among heirs, kept in
trunks and in wall niches. The most famous of Timbuktu's scholars, Ahmad Baba,
who was born in the 1550s into a learned family of Sanhaja origin, is said to
have complained that his own collection of some sixteen hundred volumes was the
smallest in his family. He wrote on law, on biography, on the theological limits
of enslavement — arguing that being a non-Muslim, not being black, was the only
ground the law recognized, and naming the Muslim peoples of the Sudan who could
not lawfully be enslaved at all. Two seventeenth-century chronicles written in
the city, the Tarikh al-Sudan and the Tarikh al-Fattash, remain the backbone of
what historians know about the region's politics; they are local history written
by insiders with opinions, which is exactly what makes them useful.

The city that held all this was built of mud. Sun-dried brick and banco
plaster, walls that taper as they rise, bundles of palm wood left protruding
from the surfaces as permanent scaffolding for the replastering, flat roofs
reached by internal stairs. Three mosques anchored three quarters — the big
congregational Djinguereber in the west, Sankore in the north, Sidi Yahya
between them — and the houses of the merchant families, two-storeyed and turned
inward around a courtyard, filled the ground between. It was never a large city
by the standards of Cairo or Fez, and in the dry season it must have looked like
a cluster of earth-coloured blocks on an earth-coloured plain. Visitors who
arrived later with the gold legend in their heads were reliably disappointed,
which says more about the legend than the place.

## Songhay, Morocco, and the End of the Boom

Mali's hold on the Niger bend slipped during the fifteenth century. Timbuktu
passed briefly to the Tuareg and then, in the 1460s, to Sunni Ali of Songhay, a
ruler whose treatment of the city's scholars earned him a permanently hostile
press in the chronicles they later wrote. Under Askia Muhammad, who seized power
in the 1490s and made his own spectacular pilgrimage, Songhay became the
dominant power of the middle Niger, and Timbuktu's scholarly establishment
recovered its standing and its endowments. Gao was the political capital. Djenné
handled the trade of the inland delta. Timbuktu kept the books.

What ended it came from the north, and it came for the gold. In 1591 the
Moroccan sultan Ahmad al-Mansur sent an army across the Sahara — a crossing that
killed a large fraction of the men who set out — under a commander named Judar.
At Tondibi, near Gao, a few thousand troops with arquebuses broke a Songhay army
many times their size. Firearms had crossed the desert before, but never in
disciplined formation, and cavalry and spearmen could not close the distance.

The conquest did not pay. The invaders held the river towns, established a
soldier caste known as the Arma who ruled Timbuktu for generations and
eventually stopped taking orders from Morocco altogether, but the gold they had
come for was already draining away: Portuguese ships had been buying Akan gold
on the Atlantic coast for more than a century, and the forest merchants no
longer needed the desert. Meanwhile the countryside came apart into small
warring polities, and the security that had made long-distance trade profitable
went with it. In 1593 the occupiers arrested the scholars of Timbuktu on
suspicion of disloyalty and deported many of them to Marrakesh, Ahmad Baba among
them. He taught there for a dozen years, was eventually permitted to go home,
and is supposed to have said that he had lost his library on the way out.

The books mostly stayed, though, in the families, in the trunks, for four more
centuries — through French colonial collecting, through drought and migration,
through the moment in 2012 when armed occupiers took the city and the people who
owned the manuscripts moved tens of thousands of them out in footlockers, by
road and by river, to Bamako. The habit that made Timbuktu a name in the first
place turned out to be the most portable thing it had.
