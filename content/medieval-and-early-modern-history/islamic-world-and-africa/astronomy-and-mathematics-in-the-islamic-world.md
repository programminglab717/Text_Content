# Astronomy and Mathematics in the Islamic World

Five times a day, in every city from Fez to Samarkand, somebody had to know
what time it was and which way to face. Neither question has an easy answer.
Prayer times are defined by the sun's position — dawn twilight, true noon, the
moment a vertical stick's shadow exceeds its noon length by the object's own
height, sunset, the end of dusk — and those moments shift with the date and
with how far north you live. The direction of prayer is worse. It is the
direction of Mecca along the shortest path over a spherical earth, which is not
the direction you would get by drawing a straight line on a flat map, and it
changes with every city. Get it wrong by a few degrees and nobody notices; get
it wrong by forty and the mosque is built facing the wrong way for good.

These are not the only reasons the exact sciences flourished in the Islamic
world, but they are unusually concrete ones, and they explain a good deal about
the shape that work took. It was heavily observational, heavily computational,
obsessed with tables and instruments, and it treated spherical trigonometry —
a subject that barely existed in usable form before — as a central discipline
rather than an appendix to astronomy.

## Building the Machinery of Calculation

Start with numbers. Arabic astronomers inherited two counting systems and used
both. From Greek practice came sexagesimal fractions, base sixty, which is why
we still divide degrees into minutes and seconds; it is excellent for angles
and awkward for everything else. From India came decimal place-value notation
with a zero, described in Arabic in the early ninth century by al-Khwarizmi in
a short book that survives only in Latin translation. The Latin version begins
with a mangled form of his name, and that word — algorism — became the European
term for calculating with these numerals, and eventually, much later and with a
shifted meaning, our word algorithm.

The same author wrote the book that gave us algebra. Its title promises
al-jabr and al-muqabala, roughly restoration and balancing: the two operations
you perform on an equation to tidy it, moving a subtracted quantity to the
other side and cancelling like terms. Because negative numbers were not
admitted, every equation had to be written with positive coefficients, which
meant there was no single quadratic formula but six distinct cases, each with
its own recipe. Al-Khwarizmi then proves each recipe geometrically, drawing a
square and literally completing it with rectangles. No symbols appear anywhere.
The whole book is prose — a square and ten of its roots equal thirty-nine
units — and it is still unmistakably algebra, because the object of study is
the unknown itself and the legal moves you may make on it.

Later mathematicians pushed hard on this. Al-Karaji and his successor
al-Samaw'al worked out an arithmetic of polynomials, treating expressions in
powers of the unknown as objects you could add, multiply and divide by long
division, and they tabulated binomial coefficients in the triangular array that
Europe would later name after Pascal. Al-Samaw'al proved results by a chain of
reasoning that runs from a case to its successor, an early form of induction.
Omar Khayyam, better known outside mathematics for his quatrains, produced a
systematic treatment of cubic equations by intersecting a circle with a
parabola or two conics with each other, reading off the root as the abscissa of
the crossing point. He knew he had not solved them by radicals and said
plainly that he expected someone eventually would.

## Trigonometry, and the Qibla Problem

The Greeks did their astronomy with chords — the straight line joining the ends
of an arc. Indian astronomers had switched to the half-chord, which is our
sine, and the Arabic writers took it over along with a transliteration of the
Sanskrit word. Centuries later a Latin translator, meeting the consonant
skeleton of that transliteration and reading it as an ordinary Arabic word
meaning fold or bay, rendered it with the Latin for a fold of cloth: sinus. The
name we use for the fundamental function of trigonometry is a misreading, and
it has stuck for eight hundred years.

To the sine were added the cosine, the tangent and cotangent — these two
arising naturally from shadow lengths, the ratio of a gnomon to the shadow it
throws — and the secant and cosecant. Between the ninth and eleventh centuries
astronomers assembled the identities relating them and the theorems that let
you solve a triangle drawn on the surface of a sphere: the rule of four
quantities, the spherical law of sines, the general theorem for a right-angled
spherical triangle. Abu al-Wafa in Baghdad computed sine tables of formidable
accuracy at small intervals, a task that involves finding the sine of one
degree, which cannot be done by bisection and angle-addition alone. Nasir
al-Din al-Tusi eventually wrote trigonometry up as a subject in its own right,
independent of the astronomy it had been invented to serve.

All of it converges on the qibla. If you know the latitude and longitude of
your town and of Mecca, finding the direction is a problem in spherical
triangles: the pole, your position, and Mecca form a triangle whose sides you
can compute and whose angle at your position is the answer. The mathematics was
settled by the eleventh century. The hard part was the longitudes, which
required either simultaneous observation of a lunar eclipse from two places or
careful accumulation of road distances — and al-Biruni, who worked on this for
much of his life, also used a spherical-trigonometric argument to measure the
size of the earth from the angle by which the horizon dips when seen from a
known mountain height.

## Observatories and the Correction of Ptolemy

Ptolemy's astronomy arrived in Arabic complete and was immediately checked.
That is the crucial fact. Al-Battani, observing in northern Mesopotamia around
the turn of the tenth century, found the solar apogee had moved since Ptolemy's
time — a real effect Ptolemy had missed — and revised the length of the year
and the rate of precession. Ibn al-Haytham, a generation or so later, wrote a
book whose title translates as Doubts Concerning Ptolemy, arguing that the
Ptolemaic models could not correspond to any physically real arrangement of
spheres. Observation was institutionalized: the observatory founded at Maragha
in northwestern Iran in the thirteenth century, and the one Ulugh Beg built at
Samarkand in the fifteenth, were staffed research establishments with large
masonry instruments, permanent astronomers, and multi-year programs.

The intellectual objection that drove the Maragha astronomers was not
empirical but foundational. Ptolemy had accounted for the varying speed of a
planet by inventing the equant — a point offset from the center of the
deferent about which the motion is uniform. This works numerically and violates
the premise that celestial motion is uniform about the centers of real spheres.
So al-Tusi, al-Urdi, al-Shirazi and later Ibn al-Shatir set about rebuilding
planetary theory using only combinations of uniformly rotating circles. Al-Tusi
devised the elegant device now called the Tusi couple: a small circle rolling
inside a circle of twice its diameter, which converts two circular motions into
a straight-line oscillation. With that and a lemma of al-Urdi's, the equant
could be replaced.

Ibn al-Shatir, who worked in the fourteenth century as the timekeeper of the
great mosque in Damascus — a salaried post, concerned with prayer schedules and
the calendar — produced models for the sun, moon and planets free of the equant
and with the earth still at the center. When Copernicus's planetary devices are
written out, several of them are mathematically identical to Ibn al-Shatir's.
Whether and how Copernicus learned of them is still argued over; that the
technical apparatus of the first heliocentric astronomy was assembled in
Damascus and Maragha is not.

## Tables, Instruments, and the Last Great Computer

The characteristic product of all this was not a treatise but a zij: a handbook
of astronomical tables with instructions, giving mean motions, equations,
eclipse predictions, star positions and geographical coordinates, computed for
a particular meridian and epoch. Hundreds were compiled. A working astronomer
or astrologer did not rederive the theory; he looked things up and interpolated,
and the quality of a zij was judged by how well its predictions held after
twenty years.

The instruments were the other half. The astrolabe, inherited from late
antiquity and refined into a portable brass analogue computer, solves by
rotation what would otherwise take an hour of spherical trigonometry: it tells
you the time from the altitude of a star, the altitude of the sun at any hour,
the direction of a celestial object. Its one flaw is that its plate works for a
single latitude, which is why Andalusi makers designed universal projections
usable anywhere, and why quadrants with printed trigonometric grids became the
working tool of mosque timekeepers.

The high point of the computational tradition came at Samarkand, where
al-Kashi found the sine of one degree by setting up a cubic equation and
solving it by iteration to a precision no one had approached, and computed the
ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter far past any conceivable
practical need — correct to sixteen decimal places, at a time when European
arithmetic was still struggling out of Roman numerals. He also wrote
systematically on decimal fractions. He was doing it, he explained, so that the
circumference of a circle the size of the known universe could be calculated to
within the width of a hair.
