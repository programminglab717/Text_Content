# The Swahili Coast and the Monsoon

Twice a year the wind over the western Indian Ocean reverses. From roughly
November to March it blows out of the northeast, pushed by cold high pressure
over Asia; from April to September it swings around and comes hard out of the
southwest, wet and strong, as the continent heats and sucks air across the
equator. Sailors on the East African coast call the first kaskazi and the second
kusi, and for something like two thousand years those two words governed the
calendar of every port from Mogadishu to Kilwa. A ship from Gujarat or Oman or
the Persian Gulf rode the northeast wind down in a few weeks, spent months in
harbour while the weather was wrong, and went home on the southwest. You could
not hurry it. You could only decide where to be when the wind changed.

That enforced pause is why the Swahili coast exists in the form it does. A
trading system where visitors have to stay for half a year produces something
quite different from one where they unload and leave: it produces
intermarriage, resident foreign quarters, creole vocabulary, credit
relationships that outlast a single voyage, and a local elite whose wealth comes
from brokerage rather than production.

## Towns of Coral, Lime, and Mangrove

The stone towns run in a broken string down some three thousand kilometres of
shoreline and offshore islands: Mogadishu and Barawa in the north, then Pate,
Lamu, Malindi, Mombasa, Pemba, Zanzibar, Mafia, Kilwa, the Comoros, and
scattered settlements as far as northern Madagascar and the Mozambique channel.
They share a building tradition so consistent that a mason from one could have
worked in any of the others. Walls were built of coral rag — rough lumps of
fossil reef quarried from raised beaches — laid in a mortar of lime burnt from
coral in wood-fired kilns and plastered smooth. For anything that needed
carving, the builders used porites, a fine-grained live coral cut from the reef
while still soft enough to shape and left to harden in the air, which is why
mosque mihrabs and tomb panels along this coast carry crisp geometric and floral
work in a material that would be impossible to cut once set.

Roofs were the constraint. The available timber was mangrove poles from the
creeks, straight and rot-resistant but short, which capped the span of a room at
something like two and a half metres. So the houses grew as sequences of long
narrow parallel galleries, one behind another, deepening away from the street,
with the most private rooms furthest in. Plaster niches in tiers — zidaka — held
lamps, books and imported porcelain, displayed against the wall like a ledger of
the household's connections. Outside, the dead were marked with pillar tombs:
square plinths with a tall column rising from them, sometimes set with a Chinese
bowl in the plaster, a form found nowhere else in the Islamic world.

## Kilwa and the Gold Road

The southern end of the coast worked differently from the northern, because of
the wind again. South of about Cape Delgado the monsoon weakens and the timing
gets awkward: a ship from Arabia or India could not reliably reach the
Mozambique coast and return within a single season. Somebody had to break the
journey. Kilwa Kisiwani, on an island in a sheltered bay in the south of modern
Tanzania, made itself that somebody, and grew rich on it from roughly the
thirteenth century.

What came up from the south was gold, brought to the coast at Sofala from the
plateau country between the Zambezi and the Limpopo, along with ivory, copper
and animal skins. Kilwa took control of that transshipment and skimmed it. Its
sultans struck their own copper coinage, unusual for the region, with rhyming
sobriquets rather than dates. They built a congregational mosque whose later
phase is roofed in a forest of domes and barrel vaults on coral piers, and a
palace on the cliff at Husuni Kubwa with an audience court, a sunken octagonal
bathing pool and a range of storerooms suggesting the sultan was also the
biggest warehouse-keeper in town. Ibn Battuta, who stopped there in the 1330s,
called it one of the finest and most handsomely built towns he had seen, and
noted approvingly that its ruler raided inland for slaves and gave away a great
deal of what he took.

The imports found in the ground confirm the reach: Chinese stoneware and
celadon, blue-and-white porcelain, glazed wares from the Persian Gulf, glass
beads by the tens of thousands. The traffic ran both directions in occasional
spectacular ways. When a Ming fleet under Zheng He worked the coast in the early
fifteenth century, envoys went back to China, and a giraffe sent from Malindi
reached the imperial court, where it was received with some excitement as a
sign of heavenly approval.

## Sewn Ships and Unexpected Cargoes

The vessels were as distinctive as the buildings. Western Indian Ocean
shipwrights built hulls without nails, stitching the planks edge to edge with
coir cord passed through drilled holes and caulked with fibre and fish or
vegetable oil. Arab and later European observers found this alarming and said
so; the shipwrights pointed out, reasonably, that a sewn hull flexes instead of
splitting when it grounds on a reef, and that a torn seam can be restitched on a
beach with materials growing behind it. The coastal type known as the mtepe,
with a square matting sail and a prow carved to suggest a camel's head, survived
on this coast into the twentieth century alongside the nailed, lateen-rigged
craft that Europeans lumped together as dhows.

What those hulls carried out was not only gold and ivory. One of the steadiest
exports was mangrove poles, cut in the creeks and shipped north to the Persian
Gulf and southern Arabia, where there was money, stone and almost no timber; a
great many roofs in Gulf towns rested for centuries on East African wood. Ivory
went east rather than north in bulk, because African tusks are larger and
softer-grained than Asian ones and Indian carvers preferred them. Add ambergris
picked off the beaches, tortoiseshell, rhinoceros horn, leopard skins, copal
resin dug from the ground, and iron — Arabic geographers describe iron from the
Sofala hinterland being shipped to India, where smiths valued it for making
blades. Against that came cloth above all, Indian cotton in quantities that
dominated every cargo list, plus beads, porcelain, metalware, dates and, in the
later centuries, firearms.

## A Language That Keeps the Receipts

Kiswahili is a Bantu language. That was disputed for a long time by people who
heard the Arabic vocabulary and the Islamic culture and assumed the whole
civilization had been planted from Arabia — a reading the coastal elites
themselves encouraged, since many towns preserved origin traditions tracing
their founding dynasties to Shiraz in Persia. Grammar settles the question: the
noun-class system, the verb morphology and the core vocabulary sit squarely
within the northeast coastal Bantu group, alongside neighbouring mainland
languages. The name itself comes from the Arabic for coast, sawahil, the
inhabitants of the shore.

Archaeology settled the rest. Excavations at early sites in the Lamu
archipelago and elsewhere found villages of African farmers, fishers and
ironworkers from around the eighth century, trading modestly with the wider
ocean, whose small timber-and-thatch mosques were rebuilt on the same spot in
coral stone as the settlements grew. There is no horizon of foreign conquest, no
imported town plan. What happened was that local people became Muslim, married
into the visiting merchant families, adopted the writing system, and ran the
business — and then, quite reasonably, claimed prestigious ancestry the way
elites everywhere do. The loanwords record the actual traffic: Arabic for law,
commerce and religion; Persian for a scattering of maritime and household terms;
Hindi and Gujarati for goods and weights; later Portuguese for a handful of
things the Portuguese introduced, including the word for table and the word for
prison, which came from their word for church, after the building they used as
one.

## Portuguese Guns, Omani Sails

Vasco da Gama came up this coast in 1498, was received badly at Mombasa and well
at Malindi, and picked up a pilot who took him across to India. What followed
was not exploration but extraction. Within a decade Portuguese fleets had
bombarded and sacked Kilwa and Mombasa, demanded tribute from the rest, and set
about diverting the Sofala gold into their own hands. They were never numerous
enough to govern the coast; they were numerous enough to break it. Kilwa never
recovered its position. At Mombasa the Portuguese eventually built Fort Jesus in
the 1590s, a bastioned Italianate work planted directly on the coral above the
harbour entrance, which changed hands repeatedly and is still there.

The long siege of that fort by Omani forces at the end of the seventeenth
century — a blockade that dragged on the better part of three years before the
garrison fell — marks the handover. Oman replaced Portugal as the outside power
in the north, and in the nineteenth century the Busaidi sultan Sayyid Said moved
his court from Muscat to Zanzibar outright, correctly judging that the money was
now in Africa. He planted cloves on Zanzibar and Pemba with slave labour,
licensed caravans that pushed hundreds of kilometres inland for ivory and
captives, and turned the island into the commercial capital of the region. The
trade that had run on monsoon timing for a millennium was, by then, being
reorganized around plantation agriculture and steamships, and it drew in the
British navy, the abolition treaties, and eventually the partition of the
mainland behind it.

The physical coast absorbed all of this without losing its grammar. Walk through
Lamu or Stone Town now and the sequence is the same one a fifteenth-century
merchant would have recognized: the harbour front, the customs house, the mosque,
the narrow shaded lanes running back between high plastered walls, the carved
door with its brass studs, the inner courtyard where the family lives out of
sight of the street. The wind still turns in November.
