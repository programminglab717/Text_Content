# How the Trans-Saharan Caravan Trade Worked

At Taghaza, in the middle of the western Sahara, the houses were built of salt.
Ibn Battuta, who passed through in the 1350s, described slabs cut from the
ground and stacked into walls, roofed with camel hide, in a place with no trees,
no crops and nothing to drink but brackish water hauled up from below. Nobody
lived there voluntarily. Enslaved labourers quarried the deposit, caravans
brought them food from a month's journey away, and the entire settlement existed
because a commodity worthless in Morocco was worth a great deal eight hundred
kilometres to the south.

That asymmetry is the engine of the whole system. The Sahara is not a barrier
between two halves of a continent so much as a very expensive sea, and like a
sea it rewards anyone who can move goods across it at acceptable risk. The
technology and the institutions that made that possible took centuries to
assemble, and once assembled they held for the better part of a millennium.

## The Animal That Made It Possible

The dromedary reached North Africa from the east in the early centuries of the
common era and was in general use for desert transport by late antiquity. It is
not a magical creature and it is often described badly. A camel does not store
water in its hump; the hump is fat, which is fuel. What the animal does have is
a set of unglamorous adaptations that compound: it tolerates a swing in body
temperature that would kill a horse, excretes very concentrated urine, loses
water from its tissues rather than only from the blood so that circulation keeps
working as it dehydrates, and can rehydrate enormously in a few minutes at a
well. It eats thorn scrub and dry stalks that would starve an ox. Loaded with
roughly its share of a hundred and fifty kilograms or so, it walks perhaps forty
kilometres a day, day after day, and in cool weather it can go several days
between waterings.

Horses and oxen could not do this. Wheeled vehicles could not do it either, over
dunes and stone pavement with no road maintenance for a month in any direction.
Camels made the Sahara crossable for ordinary freight, and the timing of their
spread roughly matches the point at which north–south trade stops being a
trickle of luxuries and starts being an economy.

## Salt Going South, Gold Coming North

The southern demand for salt was not a matter of taste. In a hot climate where
people sweat heavily and the staple diet is grain, salt is a physiological
requirement, and much of the West African savanna has almost no local source.
The Sahara, by contrast, is littered with the dried-out floors of ancient lakes
and seas. Taghaza, and later Taoudenni when Taghaza was abandoned, supplied the
western routes; the oases of Kawar, Bilma above all, supplied the road to Lake
Chad. Slabs were cut to a standard size, two to a camel, roped on either flank,
and at the southern end they were traded on, broken up, and moved by donkey and
canoe deep into the forest zone.

Coming the other way was gold. It was mined by communities along the upper
Senegal and Falémé rivers in Bambuk, at Bure on the upper Niger, and eventually
in the Akan country in the forests of what is now Ghana — alluvial gold, washed
out of gravels, and shaft-mined where the seams were reachable. The producers
sold to intermediaries and did not welcome visitors; medieval Arabic writers
repeat stories of a silent trade in which one party leaves goods, withdraws, and
returns to find gold set beside them, nobody ever meeting face to face. That
story was already old when Herodotus told a version of it, and it is best read
as what outsiders said about a supply chain whose upstream end was deliberately
closed to them. Something close to it may have happened at some frontiers. It
certainly was not how the bulk of the metal moved.

The exchange rates were not, as the old textbook line has it, gold for salt
weight for weight. But they were good enough that for centuries the mints of
Kairouan, Sijilmasa, Cairo and eventually Italy and Iberia ran substantially on
metal that had come up out of West African river gravels.

## Routes, Wells, and the Places in Between

A trans-Saharan route is really a chain of wells. The western road ran from
Sijilmasa, in the Tafilalt oases on the Moroccan side of the Atlas, south
through Taghaza to Walata or Awdaghust and thence to the Niger towns. A central
road connected Tunisia and Tripoli through Ghadames and the Fezzan to Kawar and
the Lake Chad basin, serving Kanem and later Bornu. In the east the Forty Days
Road linked Darfur to the Nile at Asyut. Each had a worst stretch — on the
western route, a waterless run of several days where there is nothing at all,
not even grazing — and each had a season. You crossed in winter, because summer
heat multiplies water consumption and kills animals.

At both ends of the desert, and at the oases in between, there were towns whose
only function was the trade. Sijilmasa grew rich enough to strike its own coins
and to attract the attention of every dynasty in Morocco. Awdaghust was worth
sacking. The eleventh-century geographer al-Bakri, working from travellers'
reports, described the capital of Ghana as two settlements a few miles apart:
the king's town, with its sacred grove and its royal court, and the merchants'
town, built of stone and wood, with a dozen mosques. That arrangement is the
whole social history of the trade in miniature. Islam travelled with the
merchants, settled first among them, was adopted by rulers who found literate
administrators and useful foreign contacts attractive, and worked its way
outward from there over generations rather than arriving by conquest.

## Running a Caravan

A large caravan was a temporary corporation. Merchants pooled their goods and
hired the specialists: guides who knew the wells and could navigate by stars and
dune alignment, cameleers, armed escorts, and in the western desert a runner
sent ahead from the last town to carry word that a particular party was coming
and needed water brought out to meet it. Passage through tribal territory was
bought with payments that outsiders called extortion and the recipients called
rent for guaranteed safe conduct, which is what it was. The risk was real: the
sources record caravans lost entire when a guide died or a well was found dry.

Credit and law did the rest. A merchant in Fez could consign goods to a partner
in Walata under contracts recognized by Maliki jurists at both ends, and a
dispute could be heard by a qadi in either place, applying the same body of law
and much the same commentaries. Family networks spread deliberately: the
Wangara or Juula merchants of the Sahel planted relatives in towns across
hundreds of kilometres, so that every node of the trade was staffed by someone
with a reputation to protect and kin to answer to. It is a system built almost
entirely out of trust that has been given teeth, since there was no state that
could enforce a contract across the desert.

## What Else Was Loaded

Gold and salt dominate the story, but the manifests were more varied. North to
south came copper, which West Africa lacked and valued highly; brass and
horses, sold to Sahelian cavalry states at ruinous prices; fine cloth, glass
beads, dates, paper and books. South to north came ivory, gum arabic, kola nuts,
hides, ostrich feathers, and enslaved people. That last traffic ran for a
thousand years and more, moving captives from the Sahel into North Africa, Egypt
and the Ottoman lands, and unlike the later Atlantic trade it took a heavy
majority of women and children into domestic and concubine servitude, leaving
fewer visible descendant communities. The desert crossing itself killed people
in numbers nobody recorded. Cowrie shells — brought from the Maldives through
Egypt — went south in bulk and became the small change of West African markets
for centuries, a currency that could not be counterfeited locally because no one
had a Maldivian lagoon.

## Why the Routes Faded, and Why They Did Not

Portuguese caravels reaching the Gold Coast in the later fifteenth century began
buying Akan gold on the beach, paying in cloth, brass and shells, and offering
the producers a shorter route to the same goods. The Atlantic did to the Sahara
what the Cape route was simultaneously doing to the overland spice trade: it did
not close anything, it just made the old crossing the more expensive option for
the highest-value commodity. Moroccan invasions of the Niger bend at the end of
the sixteenth century wrecked the political order at the southern terminus.
Later, colonial borders and railways redirected commerce toward the coasts on
purpose.

Yet the caravans did not stop. Salt still had to reach the Sahel, and until
lorries could cross the sand reliably — well into the twentieth century — camels
were still the cheapest way to move it. The azalai out of Timbuktu to the
Taoudenni pans, hundreds of animals walking two weeks north to load slabs cut by
hand and two weeks back, ran essentially unchanged within living memory, and has
not entirely ended even now. It is the last working piece of a machine that once
moved the gold supply of the Mediterranean world.
