# Fatimid Cairo: A City Built for a Caliph

In the summer of 969 a general named Jawhar, a Sicilian by origin and a slave
soldier by career, marched into Egypt at the head of an army and took it
without much of a fight. Egypt was ripe: its Abbasid-appointed governors had
lost control, the Nile floods had failed, and there was famine. Jawhar's master,
the caliph al-Mu'izz, ruled from Ifriqiya in what is now Tunisia and claimed
something more ambitious than a province. The Fatimids were Isma'ili Shi'is who
held that the leadership of all Muslims belonged by divine designation to the
descendants of the Prophet through his daughter Fatima and his cousin Ali, and
that their own caliph was that living imam. Egypt was not the goal. Egypt was
the base from which the rest would be taken.

So Jawhar did not move into Fustat, the old garrison town on the Nile that had
been Egypt's capital since the Arab conquest three centuries earlier. He paced
out a new rectangular enclosure a short distance north of it and began building
walls. The foundation was accompanied, as such things were, by an astrologically
chosen moment, and the city took a name associated with the planet Mars — al-
Qahira, the Victorious, which European tongues would eventually flatten into
Cairo.

## A Royal Enclosure, Not a Town

What went up inside those walls was not a city in the ordinary sense. It was a
compound for a dynasty: two enormous palaces facing each other across a parade
ground, barracks for the regiments, quarters organized by the ethnic origin of
the troops — Kutama Berbers, Turks, Armenians, Sudanese — treasuries,
workshops, and a congregational mosque, al-Azhar, finished within a few years
of the conquest. Ordinary Egyptians did not live there. They lived in Fustat,
which remained the commercial city, a dense warren of tall apartment blocks
where the glass, lustre pottery, carved rock crystal and linen that made
Egypt's reputation were actually produced. Cairo was the government. Fustat was
the economy.

The distinction mattered because the Fatimids were a small sectarian elite
governing a population that was overwhelmingly not Isma'ili and in large part
not Muslim at all. Egypt in the tenth century still had an enormous Coptic
Christian population and a substantial Jewish one, and the Muslims among whom
the Fatimids settled were mostly Sunni. The regime handled this with a
pragmatism that is one of its more interesting features. Christians and Jews
served in the bureaucracy at high levels — the fiscal administration in
particular relied on Coptic scribes — and Sunni judges continued to operate
alongside Isma'ili ones. The dynasty's distinctive doctrines were taught
through a parallel structure of sessions of wisdom, and the missionary
organization that trained agents for work in Iraq, Iran and Yemen was a state
department in its own right. The caliph was, in a sense, running two
enterprises at once: an Egyptian government that had to be broadly acceptable,
and a clandestine international movement aimed at replacing every other Muslim
ruler.

## Ceremony as Government

Fatimid legitimacy could not rest on conquest alone, and the dynasty invested
heavily in making itself visible in a particular, carefully staged way. The
caliph appeared in processions on the great occasions, mounted, under a
parasol, robed in fabrics woven in the state workshops with inscriptions naming
him. Those inscribed textiles — tiraz — were both ceremonial gifts and portable
propaganda; fragments of them survive in collections all over the world,
because Egypt's dry soil preserves cloth.

The ritual calendar was denser than in Sunni states because it included Shi'i
observances: the commemoration of Ashura, and the feast of Ghadir Khumm,
marking the occasion on which the Prophet was held to have designated Ali. But
the most important ceremonies were the ones that everybody in Egypt cared about
regardless of creed. The Nile rose in late summer, and the level it reached
determined whether the coming year meant surplus or starvation. A measuring
column on the island of Roda, built a century before the Fatimids arrived and
maintained by them, was read daily and the figures cried through the streets.
When the river reached the mark that meant a good flood, the caliph went in
procession to cut the dam and let water into the canal that irrigated the
capital's hinterland. A regime that presided over the flood had a claim on the
year's harvest and on the gratitude that came with it.

Money was the other pillar. The Fatimid gold dinar was struck to a high and
consistently maintained standard, and it circulated far beyond Egypt because
merchants trusted it. That trust rested on gold coming across the Sahara and on
Egypt's position astride the trade between the Indian Ocean and the
Mediterranean. The Fatimids deliberately pulled that traffic away from the
Persian Gulf and up the Red Sea, where ships unloaded at Aydhab and goods went
overland to the Nile and downriver to Alexandria. Pepper, and later sugar,
paid for the army.

We know an extraordinary amount about how this commerce actually worked,
because of an accident of religious practice. Jewish law forbids the
destruction of documents that might bear the name of God, so the community in
Fustat deposited worn-out paper in a storeroom of its synagogue and left it
there. The Cairo Geniza, opened to scholars at the end of the nineteenth
century, yielded hundreds of thousands of fragments: business letters,
partnership contracts, marriage settlements, court records, shopping lists,
complaints about business partners in Tunisia and India. It is the closest
thing the medieval Mediterranean has to a database of ordinary economic life,
and it describes a world in which a merchant in Fustat routinely had capital
working in Sicily, Aden and Spain.

## The Reign That Went Wrong, and the One That Nearly Ended Everything

The dynasty produced one ruler about whom it is impossible to write soberly.
Al-Hakim, who came to the throne as a child at the end of the tenth century,
issued decrees of startling severity and inconsistency: he banned certain foods,
forbade women to leave their houses and ordered shoemakers to stop making shoes
for them, ordered the city to conduct its business at night, humiliated and
persecuted Christians and Jews, and had the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in
Jerusalem demolished — an act that resonated in Europe for generations. He also
founded a house of learning in Cairo with a library and public lectures, and he
brought the physicist Ibn al-Haytham to Egypt to dam the Nile. Ibn al-Haytham
saw the site, understood the project was impossible, and — by the traditional
account — feigned madness until the caliph died, spending the confinement
writing the book on optics that would dismantle Greek theories of vision and
establish that light travels from objects to the eye rather than the reverse.
Al-Hakim rode out toward the hills east of the city one night in 1021 and did
not come back. Only his donkey and bloodstained clothes were found. Some of his
followers concluded that he had not died but withdrawn, and from that belief the
Druze religion took shape.

The near-fatal crisis came two generations later under al-Mustansir, when the
floods failed repeatedly and the army's factions turned on each other. The
famine of those years is described in terms that sound like the end of the
world: the palace treasuries emptied and their contents sold off, the great
library dispersed, people eating animals in the street, the caliph reduced to
dependence on charity. In 1074 al-Mustansir summoned Badr al-Jamali, an
Armenian commander governing Acre, who arrived with his own troops, killed the
rival generals, and restored order at the price of taking all effective power
himself. From then on the Fatimid caliphs were mostly figureheads and the
military viziers ruled. Badr rebuilt Cairo's walls in stone, and three of his
gates still stand — Bab al-Futuh and Bab al-Nasr in the north, Bab Zuwayla in
the south — massive, competent fortification work with distinctly Syrian and
Armenian detailing.

## What Is Left

The Fatimids never got the rest of the Muslim world. A succession dispute in
1094 split the movement, and the branch that rejected the Cairo line went on to
become the Nizaris of Alamut. The Crusader states appeared on the Egyptian
frontier a few years later. In 1171 Saladin, sent to Egypt as an officer in a
Syrian army and installed as vizier, simply had the Friday sermon read in the
name of the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad instead of the Fatimid one, and the
two-century-old caliphate ended without a battle. He built a citadel on the
Muqattam spur, tore down the palaces, and the closed royal city opened up and
filled in with houses and shops.

The street that once ran between the two palaces is still there, still the
spine of the old quarter, still called by a name that means Between the Two
Palaces. The mosque of al-Aqmar, built in the early twelfth century, shows the
moment the city stopped being a parade ground: its facade is angled to meet the
street line while the prayer hall inside turns toward Mecca, the first building
in Cairo known to make that compromise, which is what architects do when the
street matters more than the plan.
