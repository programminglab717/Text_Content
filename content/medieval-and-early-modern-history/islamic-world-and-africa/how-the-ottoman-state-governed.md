# How the Ottoman State Governed

Suppose a village in Rumelia has a complaint. The local cavalryman who collects
its taxes has demanded a levy nobody recognizes, in a year when the harvest was
bad. What happens next is not obvious to anyone raised on European models of
the period, because there is no lord to appeal to over his head and no estate
court to hear the case. What the villagers do is go to the judge in the market
town, have a petition written, and send it to Istanbul. And a plausible outcome
is that some months later a sealed order arrives from the imperial council
addressed jointly to that judge and to the district governor, quoting the
villagers' complaint back at them, stating what the register says the lawful
dues are, and instructing that the excess be returned.

This is not evidence of a benign state. It is evidence of a particular theory
of government, written down repeatedly in Ottoman political literature as a
circle: there is no sovereignty without an army, no army without revenue, no
revenue without prosperous subjects, and no prosperous subjects without
justice. Squeezing a village past its capacity was not a moral failing so much
as an accounting error, and the machinery existed to catch it because the
center had a strong interest in peasants staying on their land and paying.

## Two Kinds of People

The fundamental division in Ottoman society was not between noble and commoner.
It was between the askeri and the reaya. The askeri — the term originally means
military — were those in the sultan's service, and they were exempt from
ordinary taxation. The reaya, literally the flock, were everybody else:
peasants, artisans, merchants, Muslim and Christian and Jewish alike, whose
function was to produce and to pay.

The askeri divided into three branches that a career could run along. Men of
the sword held military and executive commands. Men of the pen staffed the
scribal bureaucracy, kept the registers and drafted the correspondence, a
profession learned by apprenticeship in the offices themselves. Men of learning
had gone through the medrese system, and they supplied the judges, the
professors and the jurists.

Crossing this was an institution with no parallel elsewhere. Periodically,
agents went into the Christian villages of the Balkans and Anatolia and
selected boys — the devshirme, the levy. They were taken from their families,
converted to Islam, taught Turkish, and sorted. The great majority were
assigned to Turkish farming households for years of labor and language before
being enrolled in the infantry corps known as the janissaries. A minority, the
most promising, went into the palace school, where they spent a decade or more
in a graded curriculum of languages, Islamic sciences, horsemanship, archery
and a craft, under a discipline that contemporaries found extraordinary. Out of
that school came the provincial governors, the admirals and most of the grand
viziers of the empire's strongest period.

Legally all of these men were slaves of the sultan. Their property reverted to
him at death, and he could execute them without trial. What that status
actually meant in practice was the absence of an alternative power base: a
grand vizier born in a Bosnian or Albanian village had no lineage, no
inherited estates and no clients independent of the ruler who had made him.
The empire's highest offices were, for two centuries, closed to the sons of the
Turkish and Muslim aristocracy and open to the sons of Balkan peasants, which
is a strange arrangement and was meant to be.

## The Land, and Who Really Owned It

Most agricultural land was miri — state land. The peasant household held it by
a title that was hereditary and could not be taken away as long as the land was
cultivated and the dues paid; leave it fallow for a few years and the claim
lapsed. The standard unit of assessment was a farm of a size a household could
work with a pair of oxen, and the tax system was built around that household
as its basic object.

The revenues from a cluster of such farms were not collected by the treasury.
They were assigned, as a timar, to a cavalryman — a sipahi — who was entitled to
take specified dues from specified villages and obliged in return to appear on
campaign each season, mounted and armed, bringing a number of retainers
proportional to the size of his grant. He did not own the land, could not
dispose of it, had no judicial authority over the people on it, and could lose
the assignment for failing to show up. The distinction from European feudalism
is real and worth holding onto: the sipahi was a salaried officer paid in the
right to collect, not a lord with a domain. He could not try a peasant, could
not compel labor services of the manorial kind, and could not prevent his
villagers from taking him to court.

To make any of this work, the state had to know what was there. So it
conducted cadastral surveys — sending commissioners into a newly conquered or
newly reorganized province to record, village by village, the heads of
households, the adult males, the widows, the cultivated fields, the mills,
vineyards and orchards, and the revenue each yielded. The results were bound
into registers, copies kept in the capital and the province, and revised every
generation or so. Almost no other early modern state produced documentation at
this level of granularity, and the archives that survive in Istanbul are
consequently one of the richest bodies of pre-modern social data anywhere.

## Judge and Governor, Watching Each Other

A province was headed by a governor-general, divided into districts under
military governors, and those districts were further divided into judicial
circuits. The judicial circuit had a kadi, appointed from the capital, rotated
after a short term, and paid by fees.

The kadi's job was far wider than adjudication. He registered sales, marriages,
divorces, manumissions, loans and inheritances; supervised the guilds and the
market, including the official ceiling prices set on staple goods; oversaw
pious endowments; certified the accounts of construction projects; and served
as the local representative of the imperial council, receiving its orders and
being responsible for seeing them carried out. His court register — a bound
volume recording every transaction in sequence — is the single best source on
provincial life, and thousands of them survive.

The crucial structural point is that the kadi and the district governor were
separate appointments answering separately to Istanbul, and neither could give
the other orders. The governor had the troops and could arrest; he could not
sentence. The kadi could sentence; he had no force of his own. An imperial
order about a local matter typically went to both, which meant each was a
witness to the other's compliance. Subjects understood this and used it,
petitioning against governors in the kadi's court and sometimes against kadis
through the governor. The system leaked and was abused constantly, but it was
designed by people who assumed abuse.

Law itself came in two streams. Sharia, interpreted through the Hanafi school,
governed personal status, contract and much criminal law, and its highest
authority was the chief jurist of Istanbul, whose opinions on points of law
carried great weight. Alongside it ran kanun — the sultan's own statute,
covering taxation, land tenure, the timar system, market regulation and
administrative penalties, issued as codes for particular provinces and
eventually compiled generally. The two were not supposed to conflict, and the
great jurist who served as chief mufti under Suleiman spent much of his career
demonstrating in careful legal reasoning that the Ottoman land regime could be
squared with the requirements of the sacred law. That this took sustained
intellectual effort tells you it was not self-evident.

Religious communities managed their own internal affairs through their own
clergy and courts, and the state dealt with recognized leaders — the Greek
patriarch, the Armenian patriarch, the leaders of Jewish congregations. The
tidy empire-wide institution called the millet system belongs more to the
eighteenth and nineteenth centuries than to the sixteenth; earlier arrangements
were local, varied, and worked out case by case. Non-Muslims paid a head tax,
faced restrictions that were enforced unevenly, and appeared in kadi courts
constantly by choice, because Islamic court procedure was often more favorable
to them in commercial matters than their own.

## What Changed

The classical arrangement described above was already coming apart in the
seventeenth century, and not because of decadence. Warfare shifted toward
infantry with firearms and long sieges, and a cavalryman supported by village
revenues was becoming the wrong kind of soldier. The state needed cash — to pay
musketeers, to pay for artillery — so it began converting timars into tax farms
auctioned to the highest bidder, and by the end of the century into life-term
farms sold for a large down payment. That put revenue collection in the hands
of contractors and investors, and it created, in the provinces, exactly the
class of entrenched local notables that the timar system had been designed to
prevent.

The janissaries meanwhile became something else entirely. Recruitment by levy
lapsed, the corps opened to Muslim-born volunteers, membership became
hereditary and tradable, and janissaries took up trades in the towns where they
were garrisoned. By the eighteenth century they were less an army than an
urban interest group with weapons, capable of vetoing reforms and of deposing
sultans. A state that had been remarkable for the reach of its center into the
countryside spent the next century and a half negotiating with the provincial
magnates it had accidentally created, and when the reformers finally moved
against them, they had to destroy their own infantry first.
