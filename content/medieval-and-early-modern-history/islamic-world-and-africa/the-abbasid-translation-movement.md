# The Abbasid Translation Movement

Sometime in the middle of the ninth century, a physician named Hunayn ibn
Ishaq sat down to account for his working life. He had been asked by a patron
which of Galen's medical books existed in Syriac and Arabic, who had rendered
them, and how well. His answer runs to more than a hundred entries, and it is
one of the most revealing documents to survive from the period, because Hunayn
does not merely list titles. He explains that he went to Syria, to upper
Mesopotamia, to Palestine and to Alexandria hunting for manuscripts of a single
treatise, that he found only half of one in Damascus and the rest years later,
that an earlier translator had worked from a corrupt copy and produced nonsense
in consequence, and that he himself had revised his own youthful versions once
better Greek exemplars turned up. This is the voice of a professional textual
scholar, and he was being paid extremely well to be one.

Hunayn was a Nestorian Christian from al-Hira, an Arab and a native Syriac
speaker who had learned Greek deliberately, as an adult, because the work
demanded it. He was one node in an enterprise that ran for roughly two
centuries and moved almost the entire scientific and philosophical inheritance
of the Greek world into Arabic — along with a substantial body of Persian and
Sanskrit material. Nothing quite like it had happened before, and the reasons
it happened when it did have less to do with a sudden appetite for Aristotle
than with the political needs of a new empire and the mundane fact that books
had become cheap.

## A Capital That Needed Its Own Past

The Abbasids took power in 750 after a revolution that began in Khurasan, in
the Iranian east, and they moved the center of gravity of the Islamic world
with them. Baghdad, founded by the caliph al-Mansur in 762 on the Tigris, sat
close to the ruins of Ctesiphon, the old Sasanian capital. The new dynasty
governed through a bureaucracy staffed heavily by Persian secretarial families
who carried with them an inherited conception of what a legitimate empire
looked like. Part of that conception was the idea that the ancient sciences had
originally belonged to Iran, had been scattered by Alexander's conquest, and
were now being recovered by the rightful heirs of the Persian kings. Whether or
not anyone fully believed this, it gave the collection and translation of
foreign books the character of state business rather than private curiosity.

Astrology reinforced the point. The Abbasids used horoscopes to date the
founding of Baghdad and to argue that their rule was written into the
configuration of the heavens, and astrology in the serious sense required
Ptolemy, which required Euclid, which required a working technical vocabulary
in Arabic for things Arabic had never needed to say. The same logic pulled in
medicine, since caliphs and viziers wanted competent physicians; arithmetic and
geometry, since the state surveyed land, divided inheritances according to a
complicated Quranic scheme, and audited tax revenue; and eventually logic and
metaphysics, because theological disputes between Muslims, Christians, and
others were increasingly conducted with Greek argumentative tools and nobody
wanted to be the side that could not use them.

The other precondition was paper. The technique came from Central Asia in the
eighth century, and by the end of it Baghdad had paper mills. Parchment is
livestock; papyrus is an Egyptian monopoly crop. Paper is rags and water, and
it made the copying of long technical books an ordinary expense rather than an
aristocratic extravagance. The translation movement produced a book market, and
the book market in turn paid for more translation.

## Who Paid, and Who Worked

The caliphs are the famous patrons — al-Mansur, Harun al-Rashid, and above all
al-Ma'mun, whose reign in the early ninth century later tradition associated
with the Bayt al-Hikma, the House of Wisdom. That institution has grown in the
retelling. The evidence suggests something more modest than a research academy:
a palace library, probably continuing a Sasanian model, where texts were stored
and some translation and astronomical work was organized. The heavy lifting
happened elsewhere, in private households, and it was funded by a much broader
class of people than the caliphal court.

The three Banu Musa brothers, sons of a former highwayman turned astronomer,
were wealthy engineers and geometers who kept translators on retainer and sent
agents to Byzantine territory to buy manuscripts. Viziers, generals, physicians
and merchants commissioned books because owning and understanding them had
become a mark of standing. Hunayn reports fees that made translation a
lucrative career. Around this demand grew a workshop culture: Hunayn's son
Ishaq, his nephew Hubaysh, and a circle of assistants divided labor, checked
each other, and built the Arabic technical lexicon piece by piece, sometimes by
borrowing a Greek word outright, more often by stretching an existing Arabic
root to carry a new philosophical load.

Thabit ibn Qurra came from a different community again — the Sabians of Harran,
a pagan group in upper Mesopotamia with their own astral religion and their own
reasons to value Greek learning. He was a first-rate mathematician who did not
merely translate Euclid, Archimedes and Apollonius but corrected and extended
them. This is the pattern worth noticing. The best translators were rarely
copyists. They were practitioners who understood the argument they were
rendering, which is why so many of them appear a generation later as authors of
original work.

## How a Book Was Actually Moved

The craft itself changed over the period, and the change is visible in the
surviving versions. The earliest translators tended to work word by word,
reaching for an Arabic equivalent of each Greek term in sequence and
transliterating whatever defeated them. The results are often barely
intelligible, because Greek builds meaning through case endings and
participles that Arabic syntax cannot mirror. The mature method, associated
with Hunayn's circle, worked at the level of the sentence and the argument:
read the passage, establish what it asserts, then say that in Arabic. It
required the translator to understand the science, which is precisely why
physicians translated medicine and geometers translated geometry.

Much of the traffic went through Syriac, the literary Aramaic of the eastern
Christian churches, which had been absorbing Greek philosophy and medicine
since late antiquity and already possessed a vocabulary for it. A treatise
might be rendered from Greek into Syriac for one patron and from that Syriac
into Arabic for another, and some texts passed through the chain twice as
better manuscripts surfaced. Hunayn is explicit about this, and equally
explicit that he regarded a translation from a single unchecked copy as
provisional work. He describes assembling several Greek manuscripts of a
treatise, comparing them line by line to produce a sound text, and only then
translating — editorial practice that would not be systematized in Europe for
many centuries.

Words had to be invented as they went. Arabic had no settled way to say
substance, accident, quality, potentiality, syllogism, or premise. Sometimes a
Greek term was simply naturalized, as with the words for philosophy, geometry
and music. More often an ordinary Arabic root was conscripted and narrowed
until it could bear a technical weight, and rival translators made rival
choices, so that for a generation the same concept circulated under two or
three names before one won. The philosophical Arabic that Avicenna would write
in two centuries later was not a natural growth. It was engineered, by
committee, under deadline.

## What Was Taken, and What Was Left

The selection was frankly utilitarian, and its gaps are as informative as its
contents. Into Arabic went Aristotle nearly entire, Galen in bulk, Hippocrates,
Euclid's Elements, Ptolemy's great astronomical synthesis — known ever after by
a version of its Greek nickname, the Almagest — Archimedes, Apollonius on conic
sections, Dioscorides on medicinal plants, Hero and Philo on mechanics. Out of
the Neoplatonic tradition came paraphrases of Plotinus and Proclus, some of
which circulated under Aristotle's name and gave Arabic philosophy a
distinctively Neoplatonic Aristotle that shaped it for centuries.

What did not cross over is striking. No Homer. No Greek tragedy, no comedy, no
Thucydides or Herodotus, no Demosthenes. Arabic already had a magnificent
poetic tradition and a strong sense of its own rhetorical standards, and it had
no use for Greek literature. Nobody was translating for the sake of
civilizational homage; they were translating what they intended to use.

Greek was not the only source. Sanskrit astronomical works reached Baghdad in
the later eighth century and were worked up into Arabic, bringing with them
Indian computational methods and the decimal numerals that would eventually
travel to Europe under an Arabic name. From Middle Persian came administrative
literature, dynastic history, and Ibn al-Muqaffa's Arabic version of the animal
fables known as Kalila wa-Dimna — itself a Persian rendering of a Sanskrit
original, and a book that would go on to be translated into Hebrew, Latin,
Spanish and most European languages, carrying a set of stories from India to
the Atlantic without anyone in between quite knowing where they had started.

## Why It Stopped

The movement wound down in the tenth and eleventh centuries, and the reason was
not persecution or hostility. It was completion, in two senses. The stock of
useful foreign material had largely been processed. And the Arabic-language
sciences had outgrown their sources. When a scholar could read al-Khwarizmi on
calculation, Thabit on the geometry of solids, al-Battani's revised
astronomical parameters, or, a little later, Ibn al-Haytham's dismantling of
Greek theories of vision, there was less reason to keep combing Byzantine
monasteries for another Greek codex. The translators had put themselves out of
business by succeeding.

What they had built, though, kept working. Arabic became for several centuries
the language in which the exact sciences were done, from Samarkand to Córdoba,
and the technical vocabulary Hunayn's generation hammered out is still audible
in the languages that borrowed from it. When scholars in twelfth-century
Toledo and Sicily began turning these books into Latin, they were mostly not
recovering Greek texts directly. They were translating Arabic — Arabic Ptolemy,
Arabic Aristotle, Arabic Galen, thick with Arabic commentary — and Latin Europe
inherited the reading list that a handful of Baghdadi households had assembled
three hundred years earlier, along with their judgments about what in the
ancient world had been worth the trouble of keeping.
