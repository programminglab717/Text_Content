# al-Andalus and the Making of a Legend

Walk into the Great Mosque of Córdoba today and the first thing you register is
not the arches but the columns, and the fact that they do not match. They are
spoils — Roman shafts, Visigothic capitals, marble of different colors and
diameters, gathered from ruined buildings across the peninsula and North Africa
and pressed into service. They are also, most of them, too short. The builders
of the 780s wanted height and had columns that would not give it, so they set a
pier on top of each capital and threw a second tier of arches above the first,
striping the voussoirs in alternating brick and pale stone. The solution is
structural improvisation. The effect is one of the most disorienting interiors
in the world, a forest that recedes in every direction with no nave to organize
it and no obvious end.

That building is a decent emblem for al-Andalus generally: something assembled
out of inherited material by people in a hurry, which then turned out to be
more original than anything designed from scratch. It is also an emblem for how
the place gets remembered, since the cathedral now sitting in the middle of it
was dropped there in the sixteenth century, and no account of the mosque can
avoid becoming an argument about what happened afterwards.

## An Accident That Lasted Eight Centuries

The conquest itself was almost casual. In 711 a Berber commander named Tariq
crossed from North Africa with a modest force, met the Visigothic king
Roderic's army and destroyed it, and within a few years most of the peninsula
had fallen — not through systematic campaigning but through a cascade of local
surrenders by Visigothic lords who negotiated terms and kept their estates. The
Pyrenees checked the advance; so did the mountains of the north, where a
Christian remnant survived in Asturias and would, over centuries, become the
kingdoms that eventually took everything back.

What made al-Andalus a distinct political world was a family disaster
elsewhere. When the Abbasids overthrew the Umayyad caliphs in Syria in 750 and
hunted down the dynasty, one young prince escaped, crossed North Africa as a
fugitive, and in 756 established himself in Córdoba. Abd al-Rahman I's
successors ruled as emirs for nearly two centuries, and in 929 Abd al-Rahman
III, having spent decades subduing rebellious provincial lords, declared
himself caliph outright. Córdoba under his rule and that of his son al-Hakam II
became one of the largest cities in Europe, with paved and lit streets, running
water, public baths, and a library whose catalogue alone was said to run to
many volumes. Outside the city the caliph built Madinat al-Zahra, an entire
terraced palace-city of marble and ivory and water channels, which was looted
and burned within seventy years of its completion and then simply lost — its
location argued over until archaeologists began uncovering it in the twentieth
century.

The caliphate collapsed in civil war in the early eleventh century and shattered
into the taifas, a couple of dozen small kingdoms in perpetual competition.
Politically this was a humiliation; culturally it was a boom, because every
petty ruler in Seville, Zaragoza, Badajoz and Toledo wanted the poets and
astronomers that his rivals had. Toledo under its taifa kings produced the
astronomical tables of al-Zarqali, which were still being used in Europe three
centuries later. It was also the first great city to fall, taken by Castile in
1085 — the event that brought the Almoravids over from Morocco, and after them
the Almohads, austere reformist regimes that stopped the Christian advance for
a while and made life considerably harder for everyone who was not their kind
of Muslim.

## The Argument About Convivencia

The Spanish word convivencia — living together — has been doing heavy political
work since it was popularized in the twentieth century, and it deserves to be
handled carefully. The claim in its strong form, that al-Andalus was a haven of
interfaith tolerance, is not sustainable. The legal framework was one of
protected subordination. Christians and Jews kept their religion, their courts
and their communal leaders, and paid a tax for the privilege; they were barred
from certain offices in theory, subject to restrictions on building and display
that were enforced erratically, and always juridically beneath Muslims. There
were pogroms — a mob killed the Jewish vizier of Granada and much of the Jewish
quarter with him in the eleventh century — and there were episodes of outright
persecution, particularly under the Almohads, who gave Jews and Christians the
choice of conversion or exile and thereby drove out, among many others, the
family of the boy who would become Maimonides.

But the deflationary account is not right either, and it tends to be argued by
people with their own stake in the outcome. What actually happened over
centuries was a slow, thorough mixing that no ideology mandated. Arabic became
the prestige language, and there is a famous ninth-century complaint from a
Christian in Córdoba that young Christians read Arabic poetry with pleasure and
can no longer write decent Latin. Converts to Islam — over generations, most of
the population — brought their families, cuisine, festivals and habits with
them. Jewish courtiers served Muslim rulers as viziers, diplomats and
physicians, and Hebrew poetry was rebuilt on Arabic metrical models in what is
still regarded as its golden age. Christian kings in the north hired Muslim
masons, wore Andalusi silks, and had their tomb effigies carved under Islamic
influence. Mudejar architecture — brick, horseshoe arches, geometric
woodwork — was produced by Muslim craftsmen for Christian patrons for centuries
after the conquest of the cities they lived in.

The honest description is neither paradise nor lie. It is a hierarchical
society in which three religious communities lived in close, continuous,
frequently uncomfortable contact, in which the boundaries were legally firm and
socially porous, and in which the ordinary condition was neither harmony nor
massacre but negotiation.

## What the Place Actually Produced

The intellectual output was not an ornament on the political history; it was
the peninsula's most durable export. Al-Zahrawi, working near Córdoba around
the year 1000, compiled a vast medical encyclopedia whose surgical volume
illustrated its instruments — scalpels, forceps, lithotomy tools, cautery
irons — and described procedures for lithotomy, cauterization, the setting of
fractures and the ligature of vessels. That volume was translated into Latin
and remained a surgical standard in Europe for something like five hundred
years.

Ibn Hazm of Córdoba wrote both a rigorous and combative work of comparative
religion and a book about love, The Ring of the Dove, which is candid,
psychologically acute and still readable. Ibn Tufayl wrote a philosophical
novel about a child raised alone on an island who reasons his way to knowledge
of the natural world and of God, a book that found readers in Europe well into
the eighteenth century. Ibn Rushd of Córdoba — Averroes to Latin readers — was
a working judge and physician who spent decades writing commentaries on
Aristotle of three different lengths, aimed at three different audiences, in an
attempt to recover what Aristotle had actually argued from underneath centuries
of Neoplatonic accretion. His commentaries were condemned by some Muslim
authorities and embraced by Latin scholastics with an enthusiasm that produced
a durable heresy; Dante puts him in Limbo with the virtuous pagans. Ibn Arabi,
born in Murcia, left for the east and became the most influential mystical
writer in the history of Islam.

Then there is the ordinary stuff, which may matter more. Andalusi agriculture
worked with irrigation systems of real sophistication — gravity channels,
water-lifting wheels, allocation rules enforced by local tribunals, some of
which survived into modern Valencia — and with crops that came west along
Islamic trade routes: rice, sugarcane, cotton, citrus, hard wheat for pasta,
eggplant, spinach, artichokes. Spanish absorbed several thousand Arabic words,
and they are concentrated exactly where you would expect: irrigation,
architecture, administration, textiles, food. A Spaniard who says ojalá is
saying, without hearing it, may God will it.

## The Long Ending

Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212 broke Almohad power, and the thirteenth century saw
the great cities go one after another — Córdoba, Valencia, Seville. What
survived was the Nasrid emirate of Granada, wedged into the mountains of the
southeast, which lasted another two and a half centuries by paying tribute,
playing Castilian factions against each other, and being difficult to invade.
It built the Alhambra: a fortress and palace complex whose interiors dissolve
solid wall into stalactite vaulting and carved stucco, with Arabic verse
running along the walls describing the building you are standing in. Granada
fell in January 1492.

The Muslims who stayed were promised their religion and lost it within a
decade, then were forced to convert, then were forbidden their language,
clothing and baths, then rebelled, then were dispersed, and finally, in the
early seventeenth century, were expelled wholesale — hundreds of thousands of
people, most of them born in Spain to families that had been there for
generations, shipped to a North Africa where they were often regarded as
foreigners. The mosque in Córdoba had by then been a cathedral for centuries.
The columns are still mismatched.
