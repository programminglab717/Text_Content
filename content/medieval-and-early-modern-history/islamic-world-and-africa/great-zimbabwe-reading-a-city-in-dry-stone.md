# Great Zimbabwe: Reading a City in Dry Stone

Near the top of the outer wall of the Great Enclosure, high enough that you have
to tilt your head back, a band of granite blocks is set at alternating angles to
make a chevron pattern that runs for tens of metres around the curve. It is
purely decorative. It is also the hardest part of the wall to build, laid last
and highest, in a structure that already represents an enormous quantity of
quarried and carried stone, and it tells you something the guidebooks sometimes
miss: the people who built this were not solving a problem. They were finishing
a statement.

There are no mortar, no windows, no arches, no straight lines to speak of. The
walls curve. They taper as they rise, battered inward, and where two runs meet
they do not form a corner but round into each other. Entrances are gaps with
rounded jambs, some of them fitted with steps and drainage channels. The outer
wall of the Great Enclosure runs roughly a quarter of a kilometre around,
reaches about eleven metres at its tallest and something like five metres thick
at the base, and contains, by any estimate, several hundred thousand blocks —
all of them set by hand, without mortar, in courses level enough that they have
stood for six hundred years of rain.

## What Is Actually There

Great Zimbabwe is not a castle and it was not primarily a fortress. It sprawls
over several hundred hectares of a granite valley in the southeastern uplands of
modern Zimbabwe, and it has three broad components. On a steep-sided hill there
is a complex of walls woven between and on top of natural boulders, enclosing
small spaces — the oldest built part of the site, and, on the evidence of what
was found in it, the ritual and probably residential core of the ruling lineage.
Below in the valley is the Great Enclosure, the great oval whose outer wall
carries the chevron, containing a narrower passage between two walls that leads
to a solid conical tower about ten metres high, built like a grain bin and
serving no structural purpose anyone has identified. Spread across the valley
floor between and around these are dozens of smaller walled enclosures.

Almost everything people actually lived in has disappeared, because it was not
made of stone. The houses were daga — puddled and moulded earth, often with
gravel and anthill soil, built up in curved walls, floored and plastered and
sometimes burnished, with thatch roofs and moulded benches and hearths inside.
The stone walls were mostly not the buildings; they were the property lines,
screens that divided one household compound from the next and kept some people
from seeing what others were doing. In other words the site should be imagined
not as a ruin of empty courts but as a dense town of round plastered houses,
with the stonework running between them like hedges, and a population in the
thousands at its height — perhaps ten thousand or more if the surrounding
settlement is counted, which would have made it one of the larger places in
Africa south of the equator.

## Granite That Splits Itself

The building material explains the building technique. The country rock is a
coarse granite that weathers in a characteristic way: the domed outcrops shed
curved sheets from their surfaces as the rock expands and contracts, a process
geologists call exfoliation and everyone else recognizes as onion-skin peeling.
Those sheets break into slabs of very consistent thickness. Builders could
collect them off the hillsides, split them further along the natural planes by
heating with fire and dousing with water, and dress them roughly into blocks
already close to a usable module.

That is why the walls are coursed the way they are. You do not need mortar if
your stones are flat-bedded, roughly uniform, and stacked in a wall thick enough
that the mass does the work; the two faces are laid carefully and the middle is
filled with rubble. The quality of the coursing varies across the site and
archaeologists have long used it as a rough chronology — the ragged early work
of the hill, the very regular later work of the Great Enclosure with its evenly
sized blocks and true horizontal beds. Skill accumulated. So did the ability to
mobilize labour, which is the more interesting variable.

## Cattle, Gold, and Things from Far Away

The wealth that paid for this came from two directions. The first was cattle.
The plateau grasslands are good grazing and largely free of tsetse fly, and
faunal remains from the site are overwhelmingly cattle — a striking proportion
of them slaughtered at the prime age for beef rather than kept for milk and
draught, the signature of a place that was being fed with other people's
animals. Cattle in this society were wealth, bridewealth, tribute and political
obligation all at once. A ruler with a great many of them had claims on a great
many people.

The second was long-distance trade. The plateau held gold in quartz reefs and in
river gravels, and it produced ivory and copper; these moved east to the coast,
to Sofala and on to Kilwa, and entered the Indian Ocean system described in the
previous paragraph of any book about the Swahili towns. What came back is in the
ground at Great Zimbabwe: Chinese celadon and stoneware, glazed pottery from the
Persian Gulf, glass beads in quantity, fragments of coral, an iron spoon,
Arabic-inscribed coins struck at Kilwa. Nothing about these imports suggests
foreign residents or foreign control. They are the ordinary residue of a ruling
class that could afford exotic things, arriving through intermediaries, in
exchange for commodities dug and hunted by the people the rulers taxed.

Among the objects made on site are the carved soapstone birds, eight or so of
them, each perched on a column more than a metre tall, stylized creatures with
lips instead of beaks and five-toed feet instead of talons. They were found in
and around the hill complex. Nobody knows what they represented — messengers,
ancestors, a lineage emblem — and the guesses are guesses. They were removed
piecemeal by nineteenth-century visitors and dispersed; pieces have since been
recovered and returned, and the bird now appears on the national flag.

## The Ophir Delusion

In 1871 a German geologist named Karl Mauch reached the site and concluded that
it had been built by Phoenicians, and that the hill complex was a copy of
Solomon's temple built for the Queen of Sheba. The story told of his reasoning
is that he compared a sliver of wood taken from a lintel with his own pencil,
decided both were cedar, and reasoned back to Lebanon. The wood was local.

What followed was worse than bad reasoning. The idea that the ruins were the
biblical Ophir was enormously attractive to a settler society then in the
process of taking the country, because a foreign origin meant the stonework was
not evidence of African statehood. Treasure hunters worked the site with the
approval of the chartered company that governed the territory, melting down gold
objects. A man named Richard Hall was appointed curator and proceeded to strip
metres of occupation deposit off the floors of the enclosures to reach what he
believed was the older, non-African level beneath, destroying in the process the
stratigraphy that would have dated everything.

Professional archaeology corrected the record early and was ignored. David
Randall-MacIver excavated in the first decade of the twentieth century and
reported that the site was medieval and African. Gertrude Caton-Thompson
returned in the late 1920s with more rigorous methods and reached the same
conclusion, independently and unambiguously. Radiocarbon dating later fixed the
main building phases between roughly the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries. The
Rhodesian government of the 1960s and 1970s nonetheless pressured museum staff
and guidebook writers to present foreign origin as an open question; archaeologists
who refused lost their jobs or left the country. It is one of the clearer cases
on record of a state deciding what the past would be permitted to say.

## Why It Emptied

Great Zimbabwe was largely abandoned by the later fifteenth century, before any
European set eyes on it, and the reasons are probably mundane and cumulative. A
concentrated population of that size in one valley consumes firewood, exhausts
soils and overgrazes pasture in widening rings until the cost of living there
exceeds the benefit. Trade routes shifted too: the gold and ivory frontier moved
north toward the Zambezi, where the Mutapa state rose, and west, where Khami
became the centre of a successor kingdom whose builders kept the stone tradition
and used it differently, facing terraced platforms with decorated walls rather
than enclosing space.

The tradition did not die with the city. There are hundreds of smaller stone
enclosures scattered across the plateau, built by the same Shona-speaking
societies over several centuries, and the word itself — dzimbabwe, houses of
stone — is the ordinary name for such a place. The country took its name from
one of them in 1980. Standing at the foot of the chevron wall, what is hardest
to hold onto is not who built it, a question that was only ever difficult for
people who wanted it to be, but the ordinary fact that somebody up there was
laying a decorative course at eleven metres and had to get the angles right.
