# The Safavids and the Making of Shi'i Iran

In the summer of 1501 a boy of about fourteen rode into Tabriz at the head of a
few thousand Turkmen cavalry and had himself proclaimed shah. His name was
Ismail, he had spent much of his childhood hidden from assassins in the
mountains south of the Caspian, and one of his first acts as ruler was to order
that the Friday sermon be preached in the names of the twelve Shi'i imams. The
city was overwhelmingly Sunni. The population of the wider plateau he claimed to
rule was almost entirely Sunni. Contemporary accounts say he stationed men with
drawn swords in the mosque in case anyone objected. Within a century the
territory he founded would be one of the most thoroughly Shi'i societies on
earth, and it has stayed that way ever since — which makes Ismail's proclamation
one of the more consequential things ever done by a teenager.

## From a Sufi Lodge in Ardabil

The Safavid family did not begin as kings. They began as the hereditary masters
of a Sufi order centred on the shrine of Safi al-Din, a pious ascetic who died
in Ardabil in the early fourteenth century and whose tomb became a place of
pilgrimage and endowment. For generations his descendants presided over a
respectable, quietist brotherhood. Then, over the fifteenth century, the order
changed character. It acquired a militant edge, an increasingly extravagant
theology about the spiritual status of its leaders, and — crucially — a
following among the Turkmen tribes of eastern Anatolia, Syria and Azerbaijan.

Those tribesmen were the Qizilbash, the "red heads," named for the crimson caps
with twelve gores they wore to signal devotion to the twelve imams. They were
not ordinary soldiers and they did not fight for pay. They believed their
master was something more than a man, and they charged accordingly. Ismail
wrote poetry in Turkish under the pen name Khata'i in which he spoke of himself
in language that edged past sainthood into the frankly divine, and his followers
took it literally. Conventional Shi'i jurists would have found the whole
arrangement appalling. It was also, for about thirteen years, militarily
unstoppable. Ismail took Tabriz, then Baghdad, then Khurasan, and in 1510 he
destroyed the Uzbek ruler Muhammad Shaybani near Merv and reportedly had his
skull mounted as a drinking cup.

## Chaldiran and What It Cost

The limit arrived in 1514 on the plain of Chaldiran, in the far northwest, where
the Ottoman sultan Selim I met the Safavid army with janissary infantry carrying
firearms and a line of field guns chained together to stop cavalry riding
through. The Qizilbash charged into it. They were cut apart, Ismail was wounded,
and Selim occupied Tabriz — briefly, because his own troops refused to winter so
far from home.

The battle settled several things at once. It fixed an Ottoman–Safavid frontier
that would stay roughly in place, contested and bloody, for two centuries. It
cost the Safavids the Anatolian recruiting ground that had supplied their most
fervent followers. And it broke something in Ismail himself: the invincible
master of the order had been beaten in the field, and the sources describe him
afterward as withdrawn, given to hunting and drink, leaving government to his
officials until his death a decade later. The mystique that had built the state
turned out to be a poor foundation for running one.

What replaced it, slowly, was religion of a much more institutional kind. The
dynasty imported Arabic-speaking Twelver jurists, particularly from the Jabal
Amil region of what is now Lebanon and from Bahrain, and installed them with
salaries, endowed land and judicial authority. Seminaries were funded. Shrines
at Mashhad and Qom were enlarged and given the revenues of whole villages. The
mourning rituals of Muharram were encouraged in public. Conversion of the
general population was not instantaneous and in places it was brutal, but by the
seventeenth century a scholarly Shi'i establishment existed in Iran that had not
existed before, with its own hierarchy, its own wealth, and eventually enough
confidence to be a rival power rather than a royal instrument.

## Shah Abbas Rebuilds the Machine

The reign that made the empire durable belonged to Ismail's great-grandson.
Abbas I came to the throne in 1588, at seventeen, inheriting a state that had
spent a decade tearing itself apart in Qizilbash faction fights while Ottomans
took the west and Uzbeks raided Khurasan. He began by signing a humiliating
peace with the Ottomans to buy time, and spent the time rebuilding his army from
the ground up.

The central move was to stop depending on the tribes. Abbas expanded a corps of
ghulams — soldiers and administrators drawn from Georgian, Armenian and
Circassian households, many taken as captives in Caucasian campaigns, raised at
court, converted, and promoted on loyalty to the shah alone. He paid them from
the treasury, gave them muskets and artillery, and made provinces that had been
Qizilbash fiefs into crown land whose revenues fed the new force. Georgians
rose to the highest offices; Allahverdi Khan, born a Christian slave, ended as
commander-in-chief and governor of Fars. Two English adventurers, the Shirley
brothers, turned up at court and helped with the guns, a detail European writers
have always enjoyed more than it probably deserves. Once the instrument was
ready, Abbas used it: the Uzbeks were driven out of Khurasan, Baghdad and the
Caucasian provinces were retaken, and in 1622 a joint operation with English
East India Company ships expelled the Portuguese from Hormuz.

## Isfahan, and the Business of Silk

Around the end of the 1590s Abbas moved his capital south to Isfahan, on the
Zayandeh Rud, and built there one of the most deliberate pieces of urban design
of the early modern world. Its centre is a rectangle roughly half a kilometre
long, the Naqsh-e Jahan, laid out as a maidan for polo, parades and markets, its
stone goalposts still standing at either end. On one long side is the Ali Qapu,
a tall pavilion with a columned terrace from which the court watched what
happened below, and whose upper music room is carved into plaster niches shaped
like bottles and lutes. Opposite it, the small Sheikh Lotfollah mosque — no
minaret, no courtyard, built as a private oratory for the royal household, its
dome the colour of unbaked clay shot through with a pattern like a peacock's
tail. At the south end the great congregational mosque, whose entrance portal
faces the square but whose whole body is swung some forty-five degrees behind it
to face Mecca, a geometrical sleight of hand you only notice once someone points
it out. At the north end, an arched gateway into a covered bazaar that runs for
kilometres. A long tree-lined avenue, the Chahar Bagh, was cut southwest across
the river, crossed by a bridge of thirty-three arches with walkways built into
its thickness.

Across the water Abbas settled a population of Armenians he had forcibly moved
from Julfa on the Aras during a scorched-earth retreat before the Ottomans. The
transfer was a catastrophe for the people moved, and it was also a calculated
investment: the Julfan Armenians were merchants with correspondents from Aleppo
and Venice to Surat and Manila, and the shah wanted the silk of Gilan and
Mazandaran sold through them rather than through Ottoman middlemen. He made the
export of raw silk a royal monopoly and pushed it out by sea through the Gulf as
well as overland. New Julfa got its own churches, its own headman, and a degree
of autonomy unusual for a non-Muslim community anywhere at the time.

The wealth shows in what survives from the period: carpets woven with silk and
metal thread for the court, tile revetments in cobalt and turquoise and a
particular acid yellow, and a school of painting in which Riza Abbasi and his
followers abandoned the crowded epic page for single languid figures — a youth
with a wine cup, a dervish, a European visitor in a strange hat — drawn with a
line that thickens and thins like a held breath. Isfahan in this era acquired
the rhyming boast that it was half the world.

## The Slow Unravelling

The system had a structural flaw that Abbas himself made worse. Fearing his own
sons, he had one killed and others blinded, and the old practice of sending
princes to govern provinces — a rough apprenticeship in warfare and
administration — gave way to raising them inside the harem under the eyes of
eunuchs and their mothers. Heirs arrived on the throne with no experience of
anything. Some were able anyway. Others were not, and the court factions that
had produced them governed in their place.

Meanwhile the clerical establishment the dynasty had built grew less
comfortable with its founders' mystical inheritance. Scholars of the late
seventeenth century, Muhammad Baqir Majlisi foremost among them, pressed
successfully for the suppression of Sufi orders and philosophical speculation —
this in a capital that had produced Mir Damad and Mulla Sadra, whose metaphysics
remains taught. Pressure also fell on Sunnis, Armenians, Zoroastrians and Jews
in ways that made peripheral provinces resentful.

The end came from one of those peripheries. Ghilzai Afghans around Kandahar,
Sunni and badly misgoverned, revolted, and in 1722 an army under Mahmud crossed
the plateau, beat a much larger Safavid force outside the capital, and settled
in to besiege Isfahan. The city held for months and then starved — the accounts
of that winter are terrible — and Shah Sultan Husayn rode out and handed over
his crown. Safavid claimants persisted for a while as puppets, and a Khurasani
soldier named Nader would soon drive the Afghans out and crown himself, but the
dynasty as a functioning state was finished after a little over two centuries.
What it had made was not: the borders of modern Iran and the religious identity
of the people inside them both date, in their essentials, from that morning in
the mosque at Tabriz.
