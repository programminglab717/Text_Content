# What the Medieval Town Was Selling

The most valuable thing produced inside a medieval town was not cloth, or nails,
or barrels of salted herring. It was a legal condition. Outside the walls, a man
belonged to a lord, owed him labour, could not marry off his daughter without
paying for the privilege, and answered in a court where the lord's steward
presided. Inside, a burgess held his plot for a money rent, could sell it, could
sue and be sued before officers his neighbours had chosen, and in many places
enjoyed a rule that made the whole difference: a runaway who lived in the town
unclaimed for a year and a day became free. A German proverb later put it that
town air makes free. The proverb was describing a bargain, and the bargain is
what the town had to sell.

That bargain had to be bought from somebody. Charters were granted by kings,
bishops, counts and abbots, generally in return for cash or political support,
and they were haggled over item by item: exemption from tolls at the lord's
bridges, the right to hold a market on a named day, a court of the burgesses'
own, permission to collect the town's own dues and pay a fixed annual sum to the
lord instead of being taxed at his discretion. In northern Italy and Flanders
the towns did not always wait to be given anything. Citizens swore associations
— communes — and extracted recognition afterwards, sometimes at the cost of long
and vicious quarrels with the bishops or counts who thought they already
governed the place. The Flemish cloth towns took that argument as far as the
battlefield, and in 1302 at Courtrai an army largely of townsmen on foot
destroyed a French force of mounted nobility, an outcome nobody had expected and
which nobody in a castle forgot.

## The Guild as a Kind of Citizenship

Within the town, the institution that turned a trade into a standing was the
guild. Two rather different things go by that name. The merchant guild of an
English borough was close to being the town's governing body: the men who held
the guild merchant controlled the right to trade at wholesale, and in many
places the guild's hall and the town hall were the same building. Craft guilds —
the misteries, from the word for a trade, not for anything mysterious —
organised a single occupation: the bakers, the goldsmiths, the fullers, the
saddlers, each with its own ordinances, its own officers, and its own chapel or
altar in a parish church.

The craft guild's structure was an apprenticeship ladder. A boy was bound to a
master for a term of years, commonly around seven, during which the master owed
him food, lodging, instruction and discipline, and owned his labour. Coming out
the far end made him a journeyman — a man paid by the day, free to work for
wages and move between shops. Becoming a master meant paying an entry fine,
proving competence, and in the later Middle Ages producing a masterpiece to the
satisfaction of the wardens: a joiner's dovetailed chest, a locksmith's lock with
its key, made to a specified standard at the candidate's own expense.

What the master then acquired was not just permission to work but membership in
something that behaved simultaneously as a trade association, a regulator, a
burial club and a religious confraternity. The guild paid for masses for dead
members, supported those too sick to work, dowered daughters in hard cases,
marched as a body at civic festivals, and in English towns took charge of whole
plays in the cycles performed at Corpus Christi. The assignments were pointedly
apt: in York the shipwrights built Noah's ark on a pageant wagon in front of the
crowd. Belonging to a craft was one of the ways a townsman was a person in the
eyes of his neighbours.

## Regulation, and Whose Interest It Served

Guild ordinances read like an argument about quality, and part of the time that
is what they were. Searchers or wardens had the right to enter workshops and
inspect: to check that leather was properly tanned, that gold reached the
assayed standard, that cloth held the statutory width and length on the tenter,
that bread was not adulterated or short-weight. A defective product was seized,
sometimes publicly destroyed, and the maker fined. Work by candlelight was
frequently forbidden outright on the stated grounds that shoddy work is easier to
hide at night — a rule that also happened to cap the working day and keep the
industrious from outpacing the rest.

That double character runs through nearly everything the guilds did. Limits on
how many apprentices a master might keep protected boys from being used as cheap
unskilled hands; they also stopped any one shop from growing large enough to
dominate the trade. Bans on poaching another master's customers or calling out to
passers-by in front of a rival's stall enforced a certain civility; they also
suppressed competition on price and service. Rules requiring raw materials
bought at the common market to be shared out among members at cost prevented
cornering; they also kept anybody from building an advantage out of better
purchasing. The guild's honest instinct was that a craft was a common possession
with a reputation to protect, and that unrestrained rivalry among its members
would end by debasing the product and impoverishing everyone in it. The
consequence, intended or not, was an economy in which output per shop was capped
by agreement.

## The People Outside the Arrangement

It is easy to romanticise all this, and the people best placed to see through it
were the ones the arrangement did not cover. Journeymen were the first. Where
mastership was open, a journeyman was a master in waiting and had little reason
to organise against the men he expected to join. Where it closed, he was a
permanent employee, and journeymen across Europe began forming associations of
their own — sworn brotherhoods with their own boxes, feasts and funerals, which
the masters' guilds regarded with deep suspicion because they were also
instruments for withdrawing labour. Strikes over wages and hours appear in the
records of the cloth towns well before the end of the Middle Ages.

Beneath them were the workers in trades that had no guild at all. Carding and
combing wool, the wet and unpleasant preparatory stages of cloth-making, were
done by people with no organisation and no political voice in towns whose whole
prosperity rested on cloth. In Florence in 1378 those workers, the Ciompi,
revolted and briefly forced their way into the government, demanding guilds of
their own; within a few years the old families had taken the city back and the
concession was cancelled.

Women's position was uneven and generally worsening. Brewing was widely women's
work, and silk-working in London and Paris was largely in women's hands; the
register of Paris trades compiled in the thirteenth century records crafts whose
membership was female. Widows very often carried on a dead husband's shop, kept
his apprentices, and voted in the guild in some towns. But formal apprenticeship
was mostly reserved for boys, and as guilds tightened in the fourteenth and
fifteenth centuries many wrote women out except as masters' widows, and then
limited even that to a few years of grace.

The final way around the guilds was simply to leave. Merchants with capital
discovered that spinning and weaving could be put out to village households
beyond the wardens' jurisdiction, supplying the raw wool and collecting the yarn
and cloth, paying by the piece and answering to no ordinance. In England the
mechanised fulling mill, which needed falling water, had already pulled part of
the cloth industry into the countryside. The towns fought this in the courts and
occasionally with violence, and lost slowly.

## What the Walls Were Worth

None of which cancels what the towns achieved. They built the first western
institutions since antiquity in which ordinary working people governed
themselves, kept written accounts, ran courts that enforced commercial contracts
between strangers, and maintained the roads, walls, clocks, water supplies and
schools out of common funds. The merchants of the German Hanse, from Bruges to
Novgorod, made a trading system out of nothing more coercive than agreement
between towns and the collective threat of boycott. The fairs of Champagne,
where Italians met Flemings under the count's protection and safe conduct, did
the same for the overland routes and produced credit instruments the whole of
Europe later used.

For a peasant looking at a town gate, the calculation was concrete. Inside were
rents instead of labour services, a court that recorded your title, a trade you
might enter if you could be bound to it, and a real chance of dying of plague in
a crowded parish. It was still, for a great many people, worth the walk.
