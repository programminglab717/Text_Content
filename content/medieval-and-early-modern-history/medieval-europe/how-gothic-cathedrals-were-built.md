# How Gothic Cathedrals Were Built

Stand in the nave at Amiens and look up. The vault is roughly forty metres
above the floor, which is about the height of a twelve-storey building, and it
is held there by nothing but shaped stone resting on shaped stone. There is no
steel, no reinforcement, no adhesive worth the name. The mortar between the
blocks is lime and sand, and lime mortar is weak enough that a mason can chip it
out with a hand tool centuries later. What holds the building up is geometry and
weight, arranged so precisely that the whole structure is permanently falling
and permanently catching itself.

Masonry has one great strength and one great weakness. Squeeze a block of
limestone and it will take an enormous load before it crushes. Pull it apart, or
bend it, and it fails at a small fraction of that. Every problem in building a
cathedral follows from this asymmetry. A stone lintel over a wide opening bends
under its own weight, cracks on the underside, and comes down. An arch avoids
the problem entirely by turning bending into compression: each wedge-shaped
voussoir pushes on its neighbours, and the load travels around the curve as pure
squeeze. The catch is that the load does not arrive at the ground going
straight down. It arrives leaning outward, and that outward push — thrust — is
what actually determines the shape of a Gothic church.

## Three Devices and What They Solve

The pointed arch is usually named first, and it is often explained badly. Its
real virtue is not that it is taller or more dramatic, though it is both. It is
that a pointed arch can span two different widths and still rise to the same
height, because the builder can simply change the radius of the two struck
curves. Round arches cannot do this: a semicircle's height is locked to half its
span. In a vaulted bay that is rectangular rather than square — and most of them
are — the round-arched builder has to fudge something, stilting the narrow
arches on stubs or domeing up the middle of the vault. With pointed arches every
arc in the bay can be brought to a common crown. The steeper profile has a
second benefit: it directs more of the load downward and less of it outward,
which is to say it reduces thrust.

The rib vault gathers that thrust. Instead of a continuous shell of masonry
pressing outward along the whole length of the wall, the ribs form a skeleton
that carries the main lines of force down to four points at the corners of each
bay. The panels of the web between them are thin — sometimes only a single
course of light stone — and mostly carry themselves. This concentration is what
frees the wall. If the load lands at four points, the material between those
points is no longer structural, and can be glass.

The flying buttress catches the thrust where it emerges from the vault and walks
it down to the ground outside the building. A half-arch leaps from the upper
wall over the aisle roof to a heavy pier, and that pier is weighted at the top
with a pinnacle — not decoration, or not only decoration, but ballast. Adding
vertical load at the head of a buttress tilts the combined line of force inside
the masonry, keeping it within the middle of the pier where the stone stays in
compression and the joints stay closed. Medieval masons did not have a
mathematical theory of the thrust line. They had something nearly as good: two
centuries of accumulated judgement about which shapes stayed up and which ones
did not, and a willingness to overbuild wherever they were unsure.

## Designing Without Arithmetic

No medieval master mason worked in decimals, and almost none of them used
anything we would recognise as calculation. They designed with a compass, a
straightedge, and a set of proportional rules that let complicated shapes be
generated from a simple starting figure. A square is drawn; a second square is
inscribed within it at forty-five degrees; the smaller square's side becomes the
next dimension in the sequence, and the ratio between successive steps is the
diagonal of a square to its side. Set out this way, a plan can be scaled up or
down on site without a single number changing hands. German lodges called the
method by the square, ad quadratum; some churches were laid out by the triangle
instead.

Full-size detail was drawn directly on plaster tracing floors, some of which
survive scratched into the upper chambers at York and Wells. From those
life-size drawings the mason cut wooden or metal templates, and the templates
went down to the banker — the low bench where a stonecutter shapes a block — so
that every voussoir in a given arch could be produced to a standard profile by
different hands in different years. Masons cut identifying marks into their
finished stones, partly for payment, and those marks are still legible on the
back faces of blocks all over Europe. The sketchbook of Villard de Honnecourt,
a thirteenth-century Picard who travelled to look at buildings, gives a rare
glimpse of the mentality: plans of Reims, a device for keeping a lamp upright, a
sawmill driven by water, a lion drawn, he insists, from life.

## The Site

A cathedral began at a quarry, and the quarry often mattered more than the
architect. Transport by cart was so expensive that hauling stone twenty miles
overland could cost more than cutting it, which is why so many great churches
sit near good rivers. Blocks were roughed out at the quarry face to avoid
carrying waste, then finished on site. Timber was the other great consumption:
not only the roof trusses above the vault, but the centering — the temporary
wooden formwork on which every arch and vault was built and which held the
stones in place until the keystone locked them and the mortar took its initial
set. Centering was reused from bay to bay as the work marched along, which is
part of why Gothic churches are so relentlessly modular.

Lifting was done with treadwheel cranes, great hollow drums walked by one or two
men, geared to a rope drum. One of them still hangs in the roof space at
Salisbury. Stones were gripped by lewises — tapered iron wedges dropped into a
dovetailed socket cut in the top of the block, which tighten as they take
weight. The work was seasonal. Lime mortar cures slowly and freezing destroys it
before it has gained strength, so the season closed in autumn, the unfinished
wall heads were capped with straw and dung to shed water, and the masons either
went home or moved into the lodge to cut stock for spring.

A cathedral's workforce was smaller than most people imagine — usually a few
dozen skilled men at a time rather than armies of labourers — but it persisted
for decades. That continuity lived in the lodge, the shed against the building
where the masons kept their tools, their templates, their tracing floor and
their customs. Master masons moved between projects and carried their solutions
with them. William of Sens brought the new French manner to Canterbury after the
choir burned in 1174, and when he fell from the scaffolding some years later he
tried for a while to direct the work from his bed.

## Glass, and the Argument For It

The wall having been made unnecessary, it was filled with coloured glass held in
lead. Medieval pot-metal glass was coloured in the melt by metal oxides —
cobalt for blue, copper and iron for greens, gold or copper for the difficult
reds, which were often made as a thin red layer fused onto clear glass because
the full-thickness colour came out nearly black. Pieces were cut, painted with
an iron-oxide and ground-glass paint for faces and drapery lines, fired to fuse
the paint, then joined with H-sectioned lead cames and cemented. Panels were
tied with copper wire to iron armatures bolted across the opening, because a
window of any size cannot carry its own weight in lead alone.

Abbot Suger, rebuilding the east end of Saint-Denis outside Paris in the 1140s,
left an account of why he wanted so much of it. He was working from a
Neoplatonic idea then circulating in French schools: that material light is an
image of divine light, and that a mind contemplating the one can be carried
toward the other. Whether the masons shared the theology is unknowable. But the
patrons who paid did, and it justified the extraordinary expense of a building
whose upper walls were mostly holes.

The limits were found by experiment. Choirs grew taller through the thirteenth
century — Chartres after the fire of 1194, then Reims, then Amiens, then
Beauvais, whose vault was pushed past forty-eight metres and came down in 1284.
The cathedral was never completed. Its choir and transept were patched with
extra piers and stand today without a nave, which is an unnerving thing to walk
into, and a reminder that the people who built these things were working at the
edge of what they knew, one bay at a time, in the reasonable expectation that
they would not live to see the roof go on.
