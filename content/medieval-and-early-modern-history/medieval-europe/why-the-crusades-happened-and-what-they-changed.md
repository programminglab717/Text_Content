# Why the Crusades Happened and What They Changed

There is no reliable text of the speech that started it. Pope Urban II preached
at Clermont in the Auvergne in November 1095, in a field outside the town
because the crowd would not fit in the church, and five chroniclers later wrote
down what he said. All five were writing after the expedition had succeeded, all
five disagree on the substance, and at least three of them were not present. The
famous cry from the crowd — God wills it — may be genuine or may be a literary
flourish added once everyone knew how the story ended. What can be established
is the offer, because it turns up in the council's surviving canons: whoever
went to Jerusalem to free the church of God, out of devotion rather than for
money or honour, would have the journey counted in place of all penance.

That offer was the innovation, and it is worth pausing on, because it inverted
something the Church had taught for centuries. Killing was a sin requiring
penance. Now a specific kind of killing, in a specific place, against specific
enemies, was itself the penance. A knight whose profession had guaranteed him
years of fasting and pilgrimage could discharge the whole account by doing the
thing he already did well, in a different postcode. The response was far larger
than anyone in the papal curia expected.

## What Urban Thought He Was Doing

The immediate trigger was an embassy. The Byzantine emperor Alexios Komnenos,
having lost most of Anatolia to Turkish groups after the collapse of his eastern
frontier, sent envoys west asking for mercenaries — professional soldiers, paid,
under his command, of the kind Byzantium had hired for generations. He did not
ask for an armed migration of western Europe, and when one arrived he spent a
difficult year extracting oaths from its leaders and getting them across the
Bosphorus before they ate his capital's food supply.

Urban had his own reasons. The papacy was some decades into a struggle with the
German emperors over who appointed bishops, and a pope who could summon the
knighthood of Latin Christendom to a cause of his own choosing was a different
kind of actor. The Church had also been trying, through the Peace of God
movement, to constrain a warrior class that was terrorising its own countryside.
Exporting that violence to a distant frontier solved two problems at once. And
beneath all of it lay pilgrimage, which was enormously popular and had become
harder and more dangerous as Anatolia fell apart.

## The One That Worked

The first wave was not the knights. Preachers, Peter the Hermit foremost among
them, gathered large bodies of ordinary people who left immediately, months
ahead of the organised contingents, and marched east with no logistics and no
military leadership. Before they left the Rhineland, bands associated with this
movement attacked the Jewish communities of Speyer, Worms, Mainz and Cologne,
demanding conversion and killing those who refused — the worst violence against
Jews in western Europe before the fourteenth century, carried out over the
protests, and occasionally the physical resistance, of local bishops. The
logic, as far as it can be reconstructed, was that it made no sense to travel
two thousand miles to fight the enemies of Christ while his enemies lived next
door. That reasoning would recur in every subsequent crusade.

The popular contingents were destroyed in Anatolia within weeks of crossing. The
princes' armies that followed took Nicaea in 1097, won a hard battle at
Dorylaeum, crossed Anatolia in summer heat that killed most of their horses, and
sat down in front of Antioch for eight months. They took it by treachery in June
1098 and were immediately besieged inside it by a relieving army, starving,
until the discovery of a lance said to be the one that pierced Christ's side
gave them enough conviction to march out and win. Jerusalem fell in July 1099
after a five-week siege, and the sack that followed was severe even by the
standards of the age.

The expedition succeeded for reasons that had little to do with western
prowess. The Seljuk domains had fractured after the death of their sultan in
1092 into competing principalities that would not cooperate; Fatimid Egypt was
a rival to the Seljuks, not an ally, and had itself just taken Jerusalem from
them. The crusaders walked into a political vacuum at the one moment it existed.
No later crusade ever found that condition again.

## Two Centuries of Holding On

Four states emerged: Edessa, Antioch, Tripoli, and the Kingdom of Jerusalem. They
were narrow strips of coast and hill country with a small Frankish ruling class
governing a mostly Muslim and eastern-Christian population, dependent on
seaborne reinforcement, and permanently short of manpower. Their answer was
stone and institutions. The great castles — Krak des Chevaliers is the intact
example — let a garrison of a few hundred control a district. The military
orders, the Templars founded around 1119 to protect pilgrims and the
Hospitallers growing out of a pilgrim infirmary, produced something western
Europe had never had: permanent, disciplined, celibate standing regiments funded
by estates across Christendom.

It was not enough. Edessa fell in 1144, prompting a second expedition that
achieved nothing except a bungled siege of Damascus, a city that had been more
or less friendly. Over the following decades Muslim Syria and Egypt were
unified, a process completed by Saladin, and unification was decisive. At Hattin
in 1187 he destroyed the kingdom's field army in a single July day by cutting it
off from water, and Jerusalem surrendered within months. The Third Crusade
brought the most powerful monarchs in Europe: Frederick Barbarossa drowned
crossing a river in Anatolia and his army dissolved, Philip of France went home
early, and Richard of England retook the coast, twice advanced on Jerusalem and
twice judged that he could not hold it if he took it. He negotiated pilgrim
access instead, which was arguably the most that force could achieve.

What followed was a long sequence of diminishing returns. The Fifth Crusade
bogged down in the Nile delta. Frederick II recovered Jerusalem in 1229 by
negotiation while excommunicate, which satisfied nobody and lasted fifteen
years. Louis IX of France went twice, was captured and ransomed in Egypt the
first time and died of disease in Tunisia the second. Acre, the last mainland
stronghold, fell to the Mamluks in 1291.

## The Idea Escapes the Levant

Crusading long outlived its original target, because the mechanism — a papal
declaration, a vow, a cloth cross, an indulgence — could be attached to almost
any war the Church wished to sponsor. It was applied to the Iberian peninsula,
where an existing frontier conflict was retrofitted into holy war. It was
applied along the Baltic, where the Teutonic Knights, having lost their purpose
in Syria, spent two centuries conquering and converting Prussia and Livonia and
built a state doing it. It was applied against heretics in southern France in a
campaign that broke the independent culture of Languedoc, and against the
papacy's political enemies in Italy.

And in 1204 it was applied, catastrophically, to Constantinople. The Fourth
Crusade had contracted with Venice for a fleet far larger than the numbers who
actually turned up could pay for. Indebted and stranded, the army took on a
Byzantine claimant's offer of money and men, sailed to the capital to install
him, found he could not pay either, and stormed and sacked the greatest
Christian city in the world. The Latin Empire that resulted lasted little more
than half a century; the damage to Byzantium was permanent, and so was the
memory, which hardened the schism between the Orthodox and Latin churches into
something no negotiation could later close.

## What It Actually Left

A durable myth holds that the crusades opened Europe to eastern goods and
learning. Mediterranean trade was already brisk before 1095 and continued
straight through the fighting; Venetian and Genoese merchants dealt with Egypt
during crusades aimed at Egypt. Arabic science and philosophy reached the west
overwhelmingly through Spain and Sicily, translated by scholars working in
libraries the Latins inherited intact, not through Syrian battlefields.

The real consequences were mostly internal to Europe. The papacy built the first
serious system of international taxation to pay for these expeditions, and the
machinery of vows, dispensations and indulgences it developed became an ordinary
instrument of church finance — one whose abuse Martin Luther would take as his
opening grievance four centuries later. Anti-Jewish violence became a recurring
feature of western Christian society. And a vocabulary of sanctified war entered
European politics and stayed there.

The word itself is a late arrival. Contemporaries spoke of taking the cross, of
a journey or a pilgrimage; crusade as a noun is early modern, and the standard
Arabic term for these wars, the wars of the cross, came into general use only in
the nineteenth century, largely through translations of European histories.
Which means that the version of these events that circulates now — a clash of
civilisations running continuously from Clermont to the present — was assembled
by nineteenth-century writers on both sides, working from each other, several
hundred years after the last Frankish garrison sailed out of Acre.
