# Castles and the Craft of Siege

Walk into the gatehouse of a serious thirteenth-century castle and look up. In
the vaulted passage between the outer and inner doors there are holes in the
ceiling — plain, square, unornamented. Above them is a chamber where men could
stand with crossbows, rocks, or buckets of something unpleasant. Ahead is a
second portcullis, and behind you a first one that has just come down. The
passage is not an entrance. It is a box designed so that the people inside it
can be killed at leisure by people who are in no danger at all. Everything a
castle knows about war is compressed into that short corridor.

It helps to remember that a castle was not primarily a barracks. It was the
fortified residence of a lord, and it did the ordinary work of lordship: it
housed a household, stored the rents that came in as grain and wool, held a
court, imprisoned people, and announced by its bulk who owned the surrounding
countryside. Garrisons in peacetime were small — a constable, a porter, a few
sergeants, a chaplain. The point of the architecture was that a handful of men
could hold the place long enough for a field army to arrive, or long enough that
besieging it was not worth the months it would cost.

## From Spade Work to Stone

The Normans built their first castles in England out of earth and timber because
earth and timber are fast. A motte was a conical mound thrown up from the ditch
dug round it, topped with a timber tower; a bailey was an adjoining enclosure
ringed by a ditch and a palisade, holding hall, stables, kitchen, and smithy.
Several hundred labourers could raise one in a few weeks, which is exactly what
an occupying minority needed. Timber burns, but it is easy to repair, and plenty
of these castles stayed in wood for a century.

Stone came in where money and permanence justified it, first as a great tower —
the keep, or donjon. The White Tower in London and the keeps at Rochester and
Dover show the type at its most confident: walls three or four metres thick at
the base, a well shaft inside, the entrance raised to first-floor level and
reached by a stair against the outer face, and a forebuilding to make that stair
its own small fortress. A keep was a last redoubt and a statement, but it had a
weakness, and besiegers found it. Square corners are the easiest thing in
masonry to undermine, and a tower is a trap once the enemy is in the bailey.

The answer, worked out over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries and refined by
what crusading engineers saw in Syria, was to stop relying on a single strong
point and push the strength outward into the enceinte. Curtain walls got
projecting towers, spaced so that archers in each could shoot along the face of
the wall between them and leave no dead ground at its foot. Towers became round
or D-shaped, since a curve has no corner to be levered out and deflects
projectiles better. Wall-heads grew timber hoardings in wartime — galleries
overhanging the parapet with gaps in the floor — and where a builder could
afford it these were rebuilt permanently in stone as machicolations. Arrow loops
narrowed outside and splayed inside so a crossbowman could cover a wide arc
through a slot barely a hand's width across.

At the top of the development sit the concentric castles: a low outer ring of
walls entirely enclosed by a higher inner ring, so that defenders on the inner
wall shoot clean over the heads of those on the outer, and an attacker who
carries the first line finds himself in a narrow lane with fire coming from both
sides. Krak des Chevaliers is the famous crusader example. In Wales, Edward I's
campaign of castle-building after his conquest produced Harlech and Beaumaris,
laid out under the direction of a Savoyard master of works, James of St George,
with the classic great gatehouse doing the job the old keep used to do.

## What a Besieger Could Actually Do

Storming a castle outright — escalade, with ladders, at dawn, hoping surprise
carries the wall before the garrison is awake — was cheap if it worked and
catastrophic if it did not. Most commanders tried it only against slack
garrisons or after bombardment had opened a breach.

The reliable method was to sit down and wait. A besieging army dug its own lines,
cut the water supply where it could, and starved the place out. This was slow,
and it punished the besieger nearly as much as the besieged: camps of thousands
of men without sanitation, fouled water, and a countryside eaten bare within a
few weeks' radius. More medieval soldiers died of dysentery outside walls than
were killed by anything thrown over them. Sieges were races between the
garrison's stores and the besieging army's health, money, and patience.

Mining was the great wall-killer before gunpowder. Sappers under cover of a
timber shed — a sow, or a cat — dug a gallery to the base of a wall or tower,
propping the excavation with heavy timbers as they hollowed out the foundation.
Then they filled the gallery with brushwood and fat and set it alight. When the
props burned through, the wall above dropped into the void. When King John
besieged Rochester in 1215 he sent for forty pigs of the sort least good for
eating, and used their fat to fire the mine beneath the keep's south-east
corner; the corner came down, and when the tower was rebuilt afterwards that
corner was made round. Defenders answered by listening for digging, by
countermining and fighting underground in the dark, and above all by building on
living rock, where no mine can go. Castles perched on crags — Stirling, Edinburgh
— were sited for exactly that reason.

Then there were the engines. Torsion and lever-arm machines had been throwing
stones since antiquity, but the counterweight trebuchet, which appeared in the
Mediterranean world in the twelfth century and spread quickly, was something
else: a beam pivoted on a tall frame with a multi-tonne box of earth or lead on
the short arm and a sling on the long one, capable of putting a stone weighing
as much as a man into a wall repeatedly and accurately enough to chew a hole in
the same spot. Great engines were named, and their reputations mattered. At
Stirling in 1304 Edward I had a machine called Warwolf assembled over weeks, and
when the garrison offered to surrender before it was finished he refused to
accept until he had seen it work. Trebuchets also threw things other than
stones: dead horses, dung, the severed heads of messengers, all with a clear
understanding of what disease and morale could do.

Rams and bores worked under a penthouse against gates and wall bases; belfries —
mobile timber towers — were rolled up filled ditches to overtop a parapet, which
is why ditches were kept deep and wet and why defenders fought so hard over the
slow business of filling them.

## Sieges Had Rules

For all the machinery, a siege was as much a legal event as a military one. A
besieger formally summoned the place to surrender. A garrison that yielded at
once kept its lives and often its property, and its commander kept his honour
because he had been given no chance of relief. A place that resisted and was
then taken by storm was, by a widely accepted convention, forfeit — the garrison
could be killed and the town plundered, and commanders invoked this openly to
frighten defenders into terms.

Between those extremes lay the compromise that resolved a large share of real
sieges: an agreement that the garrison would surrender on a named day unless a
relieving army had appeared by then, with hostages given as security. Both sides
got something. The besieger avoided the casualties of an assault; the defender
discharged his duty to his lord, since he had held out until relief demonstrably
failed to come. Commanders who surrendered without such a formality could be
tried for it.

## The Thing That Ended It

Gunpowder artillery did not make castles obsolete overnight — early bombards
were slow, dangerous to their crews, and difficult to drag through mud. But
powder improved, iron and bronze casting improved, trunnions and wheeled
carriages made guns mobile, and by the middle of the fifteenth century a royal
train of artillery could do in days what mining had done in months. When Charles
VII of France reconquered Normandy and Gascony in the years around 1450, his
master gunners, the brothers Jean and Gaspard Bureau, reduced English-held
strongholds at a pace that astonished everyone. In 1453 enormous guns helped
bring down the land walls of Constantinople, which had turned back attackers for
a thousand years.

The reply came out of Italy: walls built low, thick, and backed with earth so
that shot buried itself instead of shattering masonry, with angled bastions
projecting at the corners so that every stretch of ditch could be swept by
flanking gunfire. The logic of the gatehouse passage had not changed at all.
Only the scale had, and the new works spread outward and downward until
fortresses looked less like buildings than like geometry drawn on the ground.
