# Who the Vikings Actually Were

The helmet is the place to start, because the helmet is wrong. Scandinavians of
the ninth and tenth centuries did not wear horns on their heads. Archaeologists
have recovered exactly one reasonably complete helmet from the whole Viking age
— an iron cap with a spectacle-shaped face guard, found at Gjermundbu in Norway
— and it has no horns, nor does any contemporary carving or coin. The horns
arrive in the nineteenth century, partly through Romantic painting and
decisively through a costume designer named Carl Emil Doepler, who put them on
the singers in the first Bayreuth production of Wagner's *Ring*. They stuck
because they suited a story that Europe wanted to tell about its own barbarous
infancy.

Strip them off and the people underneath get more interesting, not less. The
Norse were not a nation and did not call themselves Vikings; *víkingr* in Old
Norse names an activity, something a man goes off and does for a season, the way
one might go whaling. Most people in Denmark, Norway and Sweden never did it.
They farmed thin soil, kept cattle, fished, and argued about boundaries. What
made the minority who did go raiding so consequential was a piece of technology
and an unusually good read of their neighbours' weaknesses.

## The Ship

A Norse ship was built shell-first, which is the opposite of how a modern
wooden boat is made. The keel was laid, then the planks were fastened to it and
to each other, each overlapping the one below and riveted through with iron
nails clenched over roves on the inside. Only once the hull existed as a shell
were the frames dropped in and lashed or trenailed to it. This is clinker
construction, and it produces a hull that is astonishingly light for its
strength and — crucially — flexible. A Norse hull works in a seaway rather than
resisting it.

The planks themselves were not sawn. Oak trunks were split radially with wedges,
like slicing a cake, so that each plank followed the grain instead of cutting
across it. Radially split oak is stronger, more rot-resistant and far more
elastic than sawn board, and it can be worked down to a couple of centimetres
thick. Seams were caulked with tarred animal hair or wool. The whole thing was
then powered by a single square sail on a short mast, a technology Scandinavians
seem to have adopted only in the eighth century — late, by Mediterranean
standards, and the timing is suggestive.

What the design bought them was reach combined with access. A longship drew
perhaps a metre of water. It could cross open sea, then run up a river that no
other seagoing vessel could enter, then be dragged ashore on any beach without a
harbour. Cargo versions — the broader, deeper *knarr*, sailed rather than rowed
— carried the trade. The five vessels scuttled in Roskilde Fjord to block a
channel, raised in the 1960s and now in a museum at Roskilde, include both
kinds; one of the warships was built from Irish oak near Dublin, which says
something about how far the shipwrights' world extended.

Navigation was done without instruments. Sailors worked by known landmarks,
soundings, the behaviour of birds and whales, the colour of the water, and
latitude sailing — running north or south along a coast until the noon sun sat
at the familiar height, then turning and holding that latitude east or west. The
much-discussed sunstone, a crystal supposedly used to find the sun through
overcast, remains a hypothesis with no archaeological confirmation.

## Raiding as a Business Model

The attack on the monastery at Lindisfarne in 793 shocked the literate classes
of Christian Europe, and the Northumbrian scholar Alcuin, writing from
Charlemagne's court, said so in letters that still carry the sound of genuine
disbelief. But the shock was about the target, not the violence. Early medieval
Europe was extremely violent. What was new was that somebody had noticed that
monasteries were warehouses of portable wealth — silver, gold, fine textiles,
and people who could be sold — sited for contemplative isolation on undefended
coasts and islands, with no garrison and no plausible retaliation.

For a generation the raids were seasonal and small: a few ships, a summer, home
before winter. Then the pattern escalated in a way that reads like any
successful enterprise scaling up. Fleets overwintered rather than sailing home,
first on offshore islands, later in fortified camps inland. Raiding became
extortion, as English and Frankish rulers discovered they could buy a season's
peace with silver, and the silver then financed larger fleets. By the 860s the
force the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle calls the Great Heathen Army was not raiding at
all; it was conquering, dividing land among its followers, and putting kings on
thrones. Three of the four English kingdoms fell. Only Wessex under Alfred
survived, and it did so by paying, fortifying and eventually fighting the
invaders to a negotiated line across the middle of the country.

## The Silver Road East

The raiding is the half of the story everyone knows. The other half ran the
opposite direction and was probably worth more. Swedish traders pushed east
across the Baltic and into the river systems of what is now Russia and Ukraine,
portaging between watersheds, working down the Volga toward the Caspian and the
Dnieper toward the Black Sea. At the far end were the Abbasid Caliphate and
Byzantium, the richest markets in the world.

They sold what the north had: furs, walrus ivory, amber, honey, wax, iron,
whetstones, and slaves in very large numbers. They took payment in silver. The
scale is visible in the ground. Scandinavian soil, and the island of Gotland
above all, has produced tens of thousands of Islamic silver dirhams in buried
hoards, coins struck in Samarkand and Baghdad and carried two thousand miles
north. When the eastern silver supply faltered in the late tenth century, the
whole northern economy felt it.

These eastern Norse, the Rus, gave their name to a state. An Arab envoy named
Ibn Fadlan met a party of them on the Volga in the 920s and wrote a description
that is unsparing and invaluable — their tattoos, their hygiene, their trading
prayers, and a chieftain's ship funeral he watched in detail. Further south,
Norse warriors became a standing institution of the Byzantine army: the
Varangian Guard, the emperor's personal corps, in which a Norwegian named Harald
Sigurdsson served for years before going home to become king and, in 1066, to
die in Yorkshire trying to take England.

## Farms, Assemblies and Law

Wherever they went in numbers, they settled. Place-name evidence across northern
and eastern England is thick with Norse elements, and the legal vocabulary
followed: *law* itself is a Norse loan, as are *riding*, *wapentake*, *husband*
and *sky*. In 911 a Frankish king bought off a Norse war-band by giving its
leader Rollo the lower Seine, and within a century and a half those settlers,
speaking French and calling themselves Normans, had taken England, Sicily and
southern Italy.

The North Atlantic was emptier and the settlements there were purer. Iceland was
colonised from about the 870s by families who brought livestock, law and a
determination not to be ruled by a king. What they built instead was an assembly
— the Althing, meeting annually on the lava plain at Thingvellir, where a
law-speaker recited a third of the legal code from memory each year and disputes
were argued before an assembly of free farmers. It was not egalitarian; it was
dominated by chieftains and rested on an underclass of slaves and dependants.
But it functioned for three centuries without any executive power at all, and it
converted the island to Christianity around the year 1000 by putting the
question to a vote.

From Iceland the chain continued. Erik the Red led settlers to Greenland in the
980s, and Greenland Norse reached North America: the site at L'Anse aux Meadows
in Newfoundland is unmistakably Norse, a small cluster of turf buildings with an
iron smithy, and tree-ring evidence points to wood being felled there in 1021.
They did not stay. A few dozen people at the end of a supply line thousands of
miles long, facing a populous and unwelcoming continent, had no prospects, and
the sagas written down two centuries later remember the attempt as a short and
bloody failure.

## How It Ended

Nothing dramatic ended the Viking age. The raiding stopped being profitable as
its targets adapted — Frankish bridges across rivers, English burhs, standing
fleets — and it stopped being necessary as Scandinavian kings built states that
could tax their own populations instead. Christianity arrived through the same
channels as the trade, and by the 960s Harald Bluetooth was carving into a
runestone at Jelling the claim that he had won all Denmark and Norway and made
the Danes Christian. The men who had once sailed to plunder monasteries began
endowing them.

The image that persists — the horns, the berserk fury, the lone sail on a grey
horizon — belongs to the nineteenth century, to nationalists looking for
ancestors and opera houses looking for costumes. The people themselves left a
different record: the shape of a hull, a trail of foreign silver, a parliament
on a lava field, and a great many ordinary words still in daily English use.
