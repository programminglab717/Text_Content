# Inside a Benedictine House

In the ruined abbeys of northern England there is usually a doorway high in the
wall of the south transept, opening onto nothing. It was the top of the night
stair. Below it, where the steps came down into the church, the stone treads of
surviving examples are worn into shallow bowls, because for four or five
centuries a line of men in wool habits came down them in the dark, at about two
in the morning, every night, to sing psalms. That is the single hardest fact to
hold about medieval monasticism. Not the architecture or the wealth or the
libraries, but the fact that the central activity of these enormous
establishments was getting up in the middle of the night, and that they did it
for generation after generation without the option of a lie-in.

The instruction book was the Rule written by Benedict of Nursia in Italy in the
sixth century — some seventy-odd short chapters covering everything from the
election of an abbot to what to do about a monk who breaks a kitchen bowl.
Benedict called it a rule for beginners, and its temper is its most striking
quality: it is moderate, practical, and suspicious of heroics. Earlier monastic
traditions had been full of feats of endurance, hermits on pillars, fasts that
killed. Benedict's Rule assumes ordinary men of mixed capacity who will live
together for the rest of their lives, and it legislates accordingly, with
allowances for the young, the old, the sick and the slow, and a standing warning
to the abbot not to push the strong so hard that the weak run away.

Its three distinctive promises were stability, obedience, and conversion of
life. Stability mattered more than it sounds. A Benedictine did not merely
become a monk; he became a monk of this house, bound to this community and this
valley, with these particular brothers, until he died and was buried in the
chapter house floor or the cemetery outside the east end.

## The Shape of the Day

The day was built around the Divine Office, eight gatherings in the church
spaced through the twenty-four hours. Vigils in the deep night, Lauds at first
light, then Prime, Terce, Sext and None through the working day, Vespers in the
evening and Compline before bed. Between them the Rule prescribed manual labour
and sacred reading, and it expected the whole psalter — all hundred and fifty
psalms — to be worked through in the course of every week, which monks
accordingly knew by heart. Nothing was to be preferred to this work of God. A
monk coming late to the night office stood apart where everyone could see him.

Around that skeleton the day filled in. After Vigils, in winter, there were
hours of darkness for reading before Lauds. After the morning chapter meeting
came work: in the garden, the kitchen, the scriptorium, the accounts. Meals were
taken in the refectory in silence while one brother read aloud from a pulpit set
into the wall, and because talking was not allowed, monastic communities
developed sign languages of a few hundred gestures for the salt, the bread, the
book, the abbot. There were one or two meals a day depending on the season, no
meat from four-footed animals except for the sick — a restriction that most
later houses got round by building a separate dining room, the misericord, where
the rule technically did not apply and where a substantial proportion of the
community could be found eating beef on any given day. A measure of wine or ale
was allowed, with the Rule observing drily that monks really ought not to drink
at all but that this was not an argument worth having.

## The Plan as an Argument

The buildings followed the day so exactly that you can reconstruct the life from
the footings. A ninth-century drawing preserved at the abbey of St Gall lays out
an ideal monastery in astonishing detail — church, cloister, workshops, gardens,
brewery, infirmary, a separate house for distinguished guests, and a bed count.
No monastery was ever built exactly to it, and nearly every monastery built in
the following six hundred years looks like a variation on it.

The cloister was the hinge: a square of covered walks around an open garth,
attached to the flank of the church. Along one side ran the church wall, with
the cloister walk nearest it used for reading, sometimes fitted with carrels —
small timber study booths against the windows. On another side lay the chapter
house, where the community met each morning to hear a chapter of the Rule read
out, transact business, hear the confession of faults and administer discipline.
On a third, the refectory, with a lavabo or washing trough by its door because
Benedict required hands to be washed before meals. Above one range was the
dorter, the long communal dormitory with the night stair at its far end dropping
straight into the church, and beyond it the reredorter, built over a drain fed
by a diverted stream. The infirmary, the abbot's lodging and the guest house
stood outside the square, because the sick, the powerful and the passing all
disturb the rhythm and were kept at one remove from it.

Offices were distributed among the brethren and the titles turn up constantly in
records. The cellarer handled supplies and estates and was told by the Rule to be
like a father to the community. The sacrist kept the church, the vessels, the
lights and the bells, which meant he kept time. The precentor ran the chant and
the books. The almoner dealt with the poor at the gate, the infirmarer with the
sick, the hosteller with guests, who under the Rule were to be received as
Christ himself — an obligation that in practice meant feeding and stabling a
stream of travellers, at considerable cost, because monasteries stood where the
roads were.

Who ended up in the dorter varied with the century. In the early Middle Ages
many monks arrived as children, given by their families as oblates and raised
inside the walls by a master of novices, with no memory of any other life; the
practice faded after the eleventh century in favour of adult entry and a
probationary year during which the Rule was read to the newcomer three times and
he was invited, more than once, to leave. Women's houses followed the same
pattern with the same Office and a comparable plan, though they were generally
fewer, poorer and more tightly enclosed, and depended on priests from outside to
say mass. Some were formidable institutions in their own right, ruled by
abbesses drawn from great families and holding lands and courts; Hildegard of
Bingen, who governed her community on the Rhine in the twelfth century and wrote
music, medicine and visionary theology, is the best known of a type that was not
as rare as later centuries assumed.

## Where the Money Came From, and What It Did

Monasteries were endowed by the laity, in land, tithes, churches, mills and
rents, and the endowment was not a donation without expectation. A founder
bought prayer. Monks prayed for the souls of their benefactors by name, in
perpetuity, kept lists of them, and buried them in the church; for a warrior
aristocracy with a great deal on its conscience, a community of celibate men
singing on your behalf until the end of the world was a serious investment. Some
houses grew immensely rich on it, and wealth was the standing problem of
monastic history, because the same endowment that freed a community to pray also
gave it estates to manage, tenants to discipline and lawsuits to fight.

The reform cycle turned on this repeatedly. Cluny, founded in Burgundy in the
early tenth century and answerable to Rome rather than to any local lord,
escaped lay interference and expanded the liturgy until the singing filled
nearly the whole day, financed by a vast network of dependent priories and
housed in a church that was for centuries the largest in the West. A hundred and
fifty years later the Cistercians recoiled from exactly that: they went out to
remote valleys, stripped their churches of carving and coloured glass, cut the
liturgy back, and insisted on returning to manual labour. To make that possible
while still farming large estates, they took in lay brothers — the conversi —
illiterate men under vows who did the heavy work and lived in granges out on the
land. Within a few generations the Cistercians of Yorkshire and Burgundy were
among the most efficient agricultural producers in Europe, selling wool clips
forward to Italian and Flemish merchants, and the whole argument about wealth
had begun again.

Learning was a by-product rather than the purpose, but the by-product mattered.
Because the Office required books and because reading was a duty, monasteries
copied, and for several centuries in the early Middle Ages they were nearly the
only institutions in the West that did. Parchment was prepared from skins,
ruled, and written in the clear separated minuscule developed under Carolingian
patronage; a good deal of Latin literature survives because some monk was set to
copy it in a cold room. Libraries were small by later standards — a few hundred
volumes was a great collection — and books were chained, lent against security,
and catalogued.

By the thirteenth century monasticism was no longer the only serious option for
a religious life, and probably no longer the most vital. The friars — Dominicans
and Franciscans — went into the towns, preached, begged, taught in the
universities, and attracted the donations and the ambitious recruits. Many
Benedictine houses drifted into a comfortable middle age: fewer monks than the
buildings were designed for, revenues leased out, corrodies sold to laymen who
bought board and lodging for life with a lump sum and then inconveniently
declined to die. When Henry VIII dissolved the English monasteries in the space
of four years in the 1530s, some were genuinely decayed and some were not, and
the lead came off the roofs of all of them.

What is left, in most cases, is the ground plan: the square of the cloister, the
drain of the reredorter, the doorway high in the transept wall.
