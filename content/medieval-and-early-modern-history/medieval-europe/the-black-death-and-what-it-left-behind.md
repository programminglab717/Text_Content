# The Black Death and What It Left Behind

In the autumn of 1347 a small fleet of Genoese galleys put in at Messina, on the
northeastern tip of Sicily. The ships had come up from the Black Sea, where
Italian merchants kept trading colonies at the western end of the caravan
routes, and by the time they tied up the crews were in a condition that the
Sicilian chronicler Michele da Piazza described with some care: swellings at the
groin and armpit, black blotches on the skin, blood coughed onto the deck, death
within days. The harbour authorities ordered the ships out again. It made no
difference. Whatever the sailors carried was already ashore, and within months
it was in Genoa, Venice, Marseille and Pisa, and from there it moved inland
along every road that carried grain and cloth and pilgrims.

The pandemic that followed took about four years to cross the continent and
killed, by most reckonings, somewhere between a third and a half of the people
living in it. Some towns lost less. Some lost far more, to the point that they
were never reoccupied and survive only as green humps in a field. Nothing else
in the recorded history of Europe compresses that much mortality into that short
a span.

## The Road West

The disease had been moving for years before anyone in the west knew it
existed. It appears to have travelled out of Central Asia along the trade
corridors that the Mongol peace had made passable, reaching the Crimea by the
mid-1340s. The most famous story of its arrival there comes from Gabriele de'
Mussi, a notary from Piacenza who wrote that a Mongol army besieging the Genoese
port of Caffa, its own ranks dying, catapulted corpses over the walls and thus
infected the defenders, who fled home by sea. De' Mussi was not there, and
historians treat the detail with caution; the siege and the flight are real
enough, and that is the part that matters.

From Sicily and the Italian ports the spread was rapid but not uniform. It went
fastest by water, leaping hundreds of miles in a week when a grain ship made
port, then crawled outward from each landing at something closer to walking
pace. It reached Florence and Siena in the spring of 1348, Paris that summer,
the English south coast at Melcombe in Dorset around the same time, London by
autumn. In 1349 it worked through Germany, Ireland and Scandinavia; a ship
drifting with a dead crew is said to have brought it to Norway. By 1351 it had
reached Russia, having effectively encircled the continent. Only a few pockets —
parts of the Pyrenees, some of Poland, the Milanese contado where the Visconti
enforced a brutal cordon — came through comparatively lightly.

## What It Actually Was

Ancient DNA recovered from the dental pulp of skeletons in plague cemeteries,
including the emergency burial ground at East Smithfield in London, has settled
the long argument about the pathogen: the Black Death was caused by *Yersinia
pestis*, the same bacterium that causes plague today. What remains genuinely
unsettled is how it moved so fast.

The classic model is the rat and the flea. *Y. pestis* lives in rodent
populations; when an infected rat dies, its fleas seek a new host, and a bite
from an infected flea introduces the bacterium into the lymphatic system. It
multiplies in the nearest lymph node, which swells into the hard, agonising
lump the Latin sources called a bubo. Untreated bubonic plague kills a large
fraction of those who catch it, usually within a week. If the infection reaches
the lungs, the victim develops pneumonic plague and becomes contagious directly,
by coughing, to anyone nearby — a form that is faster and very nearly always
fatal. If it floods the bloodstream, death can come before any bubo appears at
all, which accounts for the medieval reports of people going to bed apparently
well and being dead by morning.

The difficulty is that the rat-and-flea route is slow. Modern plague outbreaks
of this kind creep. The fourteenth-century pandemic did not creep; it tore
through dense towns in a matter of weeks, and it did so in northern winters when
rat fleas should have been dormant. Several researchers now argue that human
ectoparasites — the body louse and the human flea — carried the infection
person to person, and that pneumonic transmission did more work than the older
model allowed. It is likely that all these routes operated at once, in
proportions that varied with climate, housing and season.

## What People Did

Medieval medicine was not stupid; it was working from the wrong first
principles. Trained physicians followed Galen, and Galen understood disease as
an imbalance of bodily humours, aggravated by corrupted air. In October 1348 the
medical faculty of the University of Paris, asked by the king for an
explanation, produced a formal report tracing the outbreak to a conjunction of
Saturn, Jupiter and Mars three years earlier, which had drawn up poisonous
vapours from the earth and the seas. Within that framework the recommended
responses were rational: burn aromatic woods, avoid bathing (which opened the
pores), avoid damp low ground, bleed the patient to restore balance, and above
all leave. The rich left. Boccaccio opens the *Decameron* with ten young
Florentines withdrawing to a villa in the hills to tell each other stories while
the city dies below them, and that fiction describes exactly what wealthy
families did.

Other responses were worse. Bands of flagellants moved through Germany and the
Low Countries, whipping themselves in ritual processions of thirty-three and a
half days, one for each year of Christ's life, offering their pain as expiation
for a sin they believed had brought the punishment. They attracted large crowds,
began attacking clergy, and were condemned by Clement VI in 1349. In the same
months the accusation spread that Jews had poisoned the wells. Confessions
extracted under torture in Savoy circulated northward ahead of the disease
itself, and communities in Strasbourg, Basel, Mainz, Cologne and dozens of
smaller places were burned or expelled, often by townsmen who owed them money
and sometimes before the plague had even arrived. Clement VI issued bulls
pointing out that Jews were dying of the disease at the same rate as everyone
else and that killing them was murder. He was widely ignored.

The most consequential response was administrative. Italian city governments,
having no theory of contagion but plenty of observation, began controlling
movement. Ragusa, the merchant republic at modern Dubrovnik, decreed in 1377
that arrivals from infected places must wait thirty days on a designated island
before entering the city. Venice and others extended the period to forty days —
*quaranta giorni*, which is where the word quarantine comes from. Health boards,
bills of mortality, plague hospitals and cordons around infected districts all
grew out of this period, and they are the ancestors of public health as a
function of the state.

## The Century After

The immediate economic result was a labour market turned upside down. Land,
which had been scarce and expensive relative to people, was suddenly abundant;
people, who had been cheap, were suddenly the constraint. Surviving peasants
could bargain, move, or simply walk to a manor offering better terms. Landlords
and governments tried to legislate the change out of existence. England's
Ordinance of Labourers in 1349 and the Statute of Labourers in 1351 froze wages
at pre-plague levels and made it an offence to refuse work or to leave a
holding. The statutes were enforced energetically and failed steadily; real
wages rose anyway across the second half of the century, and the grievances
generated by enforcement fed directly into the rising of 1381.

In western Europe the long consequence was the erosion of serfdom. Labour
services were commuted to rent, rents fell, and villeinage withered over several
generations into forms of tenancy that were, if not free, at least negotiable.
East of the Elbe the outcome ran the other way: landlords with political power
and thin populations responded by tying peasants down harder, and the so-called
second serfdom took hold in exactly the period when the western version was
dissolving. Land use shifted too. Arable that no longer had hands to work it
went to pasture, and England in particular leaned into wool, with all that
followed for its cloth towns.

The plague did not go away. It returned in 1361, hitting children hardest
because they had no exposure to the first wave, and thereafter came back roughly
every decade or two for three hundred years. Europe's population did not regain
its early fourteenth-century level until well into the sixteenth century. The
long familiarity shows in the art: skeletons leading popes and emperors by the
hand in the danse macabre, tomb effigies carved as rotting corpses, the
insistence in sermon and woodcut that death was neither distant nor
discriminating.

The last great western outbreaks were separated by long intervals — London in
1665, Marseille in 1720 — and by then the sanitary apparatus invented in
fourteenth-century Italy was sophisticated enough to contain what it could not
cure. The bacterium itself never left. It still circulates in rodent
populations on several continents, causing a few thousand human cases a year,
and it responds well to antibiotics if it is caught early enough, which is a
narrow margin on which to rest a great deal of comfort.
