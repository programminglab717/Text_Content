# The Byzantine Empire and How It Survived

When Liudprand of Cremona was received at the imperial palace in Constantinople
in the middle of the tenth century, he was brought before a throne flanked by
gilded mechanical lions that beat their tails and roared, beneath a gilded tree
in which mechanical birds sang. Having made his prostration, he looked up to
find that the emperor and the throne had risen towards the ceiling and were now
looking down at him, robed differently. Liudprand, an Italian bishop with a
sharp tongue and a general disdain for Greeks, recorded all this without
admitting to being impressed, which is how one can tell that he was.

The performance was not vanity. It was a budget line. An empire that spent most
of its long life with fewer soldiers than its enemies had learned to invest
heavily in the impression of inexhaustible power, because impressing a visiting
envoy was cheaper than fighting his king. That instinct — solve it with money,
ceremony, engineering or paperwork before solving it with an army — runs through
the whole history of the eastern Roman state, and it is a large part of why that
state outlasted its western half by a thousand years.

## A Problem of Names

Nobody in Constantinople ever described themselves as Byzantine. They were
*Rhomaioi* — Romans — and they meant it literally, not as a claim to
inheritance. Their state had no founding moment because it had never stopped:
the senate, the consulship, the law, the taxes and the emperorship ran
continuously from Augustus through Constantine's dedication of the new capital
in 330 and onward past the collapse of imperial authority in the west. Their
neighbours agreed. Arabs and Turks called their land Rum for centuries.

The word Byzantine was applied retrospectively, in the sixteenth century, by the
German scholar Hieronymus Wolf, who needed a label for the Greek historical
texts he was editing and took it from Byzantion, the old colonial town on the
site. Later writers found it useful for a different reason: it let them treat
the eastern empire as something other than Rome, and therefore as a decadent
sideshow rather than the main line of descent. Gibbon's verdict — a long
monotonous story of weakness and misery — shaped English-language opinion for
two centuries and is now very hard to defend, given that the subject of the
verdict was a state which kept a functioning bureaucracy, a gold coinage and a
codified legal system running through the entire period that western Europe
spent without any of the three.

## The City

Almost everything about the empire's endurance comes back to the site. The
capital sits on a triangular promontory with water on two sides and a
magnificent deepwater inlet, the Golden Horn, that could be closed by a chain
across its mouth. That left one landward approach, and in the fifth century,
under Theodosius II, it was sealed off by a system of fortification that has few
equals anywhere.

The Theodosian Walls were not a wall but a graded obstacle in depth: a broad
brick-lined moat, then a low outer terrace, then an outer wall with its own
towers, then a second terrace, then the great inner wall, five metres thick and
twelve high, studded with nearly a hundred towers. An attacker who took the
first line found himself in a killing ground overlooked from above by the
second. When an earthquake knocked long stretches of it down in 447, with Attila
moving in the Balkans, the city's factions rebuilt it in a matter of months. It
was breached by an enemy exactly once in a thousand years.

A fortress is only as good as its supply, so the city's engineers built the
other half of the system underground. Aqueducts brought water from the Thracian
hills across more than a hundred kilometres of channel and arcade; inside the
walls, that water filled open reservoirs and hundreds of roofed cisterns, of
which the largest, built under Justinian, is a forest of over three hundred
recycled columns holding up a brick roof over an artificial lake. A besieging
army could cut the aqueducts. It could not empty the cisterns.

## The Great Contraction

The empire's worst century was the seventh, and the crisis reshaped it into
something quite different from the late Roman state Justinian had ruled.
Justinian himself had pushed the other way: in the 530s and 540s his generals
took North Africa back from the Vandals and fought a long, ruinous war to
recover Italy from the Goths, while at home he codified Roman law into the
compilation that would later re-enter western Europe and underlie most
continental legal systems, and rebuilt the burnt cathedral of Hagia Sophia with
a dome that his own contemporaries struggled to describe. The bill arrived
almost at once. Plague swept the Mediterranean from 541, the reconquered
provinces were expensive to hold, and the treasury never recovered.

Then came a generation of catastrophe. Persia overran Syria, Egypt and
Palestine. Heraclius spent years rebuilding an army, marched it deep into
Mesopotamia and won the war outright in the 620s — and within a decade of that
victory, both exhausted empires were confronted by Arab armies out of the south.
At Yarmouk in 636 the eastern field army was destroyed. Syria, Palestine, Egypt
and eventually North Africa were gone permanently, taking with them the grain
supply, the richest tax base and roughly two-thirds of the empire's territory.

What survived did so by restructuring. Latin gave way to Greek as the language
of government. The old provincial administration was replaced by military
districts, the themes, in which soldiers were settled on land and supported
themselves from it — an army that cost the treasury far less because it was also
a peasantry. And around the 670s a Syrian refugee named Kallinikos is credited
with bringing to the capital the incendiary weapon the sources call liquid fire,
pumped through bronze siphons in the bows of warships, burning on the surface of
the water and almost impossible to extinguish. It broke Arab fleets in two
enormous sieges of the capital, and its composition was so closely held that it
was eventually lost altogether. A petroleum base is nearly certain; beyond that
nobody knows.

## Instruments

The empire fought when it had to and preferred not to. Its actual toolkit was
stranger and more effective. Gold, first: the solidus struck by Constantine held
its weight and fineness for something like seven centuries, which made it the
reserve currency of the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean, and gave emperors a
subsidy weapon that could turn one enemy against another without a soldier
leaving barracks. A tenth-century treatise attributed to Constantine VII walks
his son through the steppe peoples north of the Black Sea, explaining which of
them can be paid to attack which others.

Second, information. The state kept archives, ran a courier system, maintained
an office that briefed on foreign rulers' genealogies and grievances, and trained
a civil service through a functioning higher education in Greek rhetoric and
law. Third, religion. Missions carried Orthodox Christianity and a purpose-built
Slavic alphabet north through Moravia and Bulgaria, and in the late tenth
century to Kyiv, creating a cultural sphere that outlived the empire by many
centuries and still shapes the map of eastern Europe.

The internal disputes were correspondingly theological and correspondingly
violent. For a century and more, beginning under Leo III, the empire tore itself
over whether images of Christ and the saints were legitimate aids to worship or
forbidden idols. Iconoclasm was imperial policy twice, reversed twice, and its
final defeat in 843 is still commemorated in the Orthodox calendar. A great deal
of earlier religious art was destroyed in the process, which is why the mosaics
we admire in Byzantine churches are so overwhelmingly later work.

## Two Endings

The wound that proved fatal was not obviously fatal when it opened. At Manzikert
in 1071 a Turkish army defeated and captured the emperor, but the real damage
came afterwards, when rival claimants stripped Anatolia of troops to fight each
other and Turkish groups simply moved into the vacuum. Anatolia had been the
empire's manpower and its food. Within twenty years an emperor was writing west
for mercenaries, and what arrived instead was the First Crusade.

The Fourth Crusade arrived in 1204, diverted by debt and Venetian interest, and
did what no foreign enemy had managed: it got inside the walls, from the Golden
Horn, and sacked the city for three days. The empire fragmented into successor
states, one of which recovered the capital in 1261 — but it recovered a
half-empty ruin with a wrecked economy, hemmed in by Italian merchants who now
controlled its trade and paid it no customs.

The last two centuries were a long managed decline, punctuated by
extraordinary cultural production and increasingly desperate diplomacy,
including a church union with Rome that its own population rejected. In the
spring of 1453 Mehmed II brought an artillery train built around enormous
bronze bombards cast for him by a Hungarian gunfounder, and the walls that had
held for a thousand years were reduced to rubble over seven weeks. The last
emperor was killed somewhere in the final assault, and his body was never
identified. Scholars who had already been drifting to Italy with their
manuscripts kept going, and the Greek texts they carried fed directly into what
Italy was then beginning to call a rebirth.
