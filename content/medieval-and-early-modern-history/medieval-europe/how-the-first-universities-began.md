# How the First Universities Began

At Bologna in the thirteenth century a professor of law who wanted to leave town
for a few days had to put down a deposit first, as security that he would come
back. If he began his lecture late, he was fined. If he ran past the hour, the
students walked out — and if he skipped a difficult passage of the text, or
failed to cover the syllabus he had contracted to cover by the date agreed, he
was fined for that too. The fines were levied by officers the students elected
from among themselves, and paid out of a sum the professor had lodged with a
banker at the start of the year.

This was not a prank or a peculiarity. It followed from what a university
originally was. The word universitas meant a corporation: a sworn association of
people with a common interest, the same word used for a guild of bakers or
shoemakers. At Bologna the corporation was one of students, and the teachers
were the hired help. Elsewhere the corporation was one of masters, and the
students were apprentices. Either way, the institution was a body of persons
with legal privileges, not a place with buildings, and for a long time most of
them owned no property at all. They met in hired rooms and rented churches. That
weightlessness turned out to be their strongest weapon.

## Two Models, from Two Kinds of Need

Bologna grew out of the revival of Roman law. In the late eleventh and early
twelfth centuries scholars in northern Italy went back to Justinian's compilation
— in particular the Digest, an enormous and difficult anthology of classical
jurists that had been almost unused in the West for centuries — and started
teaching it systematically, annotating the text with glosses that were
themselves copied and taught. Irnerius is the name attached to the beginning of
this. At almost the same time, a teacher known as Gratian assembled the
contradictory canons of church law into a single work that sorted the
inconsistencies out by argument, and canon law became a subject one could study
as a discipline rather than a heap of precedents.

Lawyers were what a Europe of expanding governments, church courts, communes and
commerce suddenly needed. Students came to Bologna from everywhere, often in
their twenties, often already holding a benefice or a post, and they arrived as
foreigners in a city whose law made a foreigner liable for the debts of any
countryman. So they organised for protection, in two great associations
according to whether they came from north or south of the Alps, elected rectors,
and used the one lever they had: collectively they were the city's principal
industry, and they could take it somewhere else. The threat of secession
extracted from Bologna the guarantees that made student power real.

Paris went the other way. Its schools grew around the cathedral of Notre-Dame,
where the bishop's chancellor granted the licence to teach and where, by the
early twelfth century, the pull of famous lecturers in logic and theology —
Abelard the most magnetic and the most trouble — had drawn an international
crowd of students into a quarter of the city. The masters formed a guild to
defend themselves against the chancellor's control over who could teach, won
royal protection after a violent episode between students and townsmen around
1200, and obtained written statutes a few years later. Paris became the model for
northern Europe: a corporation of masters, organised into faculties, with the
arts masters far the most numerous and theology the senior subject. Oxford took
shape in the same decades, and Cambridge began in part as a colony of Oxford
scholars who left after a lethal quarrel with the townspeople.

## What Was Taught, and How

Everyone began in arts, often at fourteen or fifteen. The old scheme of seven
liberal arts — grammar, rhetoric and logic, then arithmetic, geometry, astronomy
and music — described the ground, but in practice logic swallowed the
curriculum. Then came the higher faculties: theology, canon law, civil law,
medicine. A university did not need all of them, and each developed its
speciality. Bologna was law. Paris was theology. Montpellier and, earlier,
Salerno were the places to learn medicine.

The intellectual shock that shaped the whole thing was the arrival of Aristotle.
Over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, translators working from Arabic in
Spain and from Greek in Sicily and Constantinople put nearly the whole Aristotelian
corpus into Latin, along with the commentaries of Muslim and Jewish
philosophers who had been thinking about it for generations. This was not a few
new books. It was a complete, coherent, pagan account of physics, biology,
psychology, ethics and metaphysics arriving all at once in a Christian
university, and it did not obviously fit. Paris banned lecturing on the natural
philosophy for a time early in the thirteenth century. Within forty years the
banned books were the required syllabus, and the work of showing how far
Aristotle could be reconciled with Christian doctrine — and where he could not —
occupied the best minds of the age. The bishop of Paris was still issuing
condemnations of propositions taught in the arts faculty in the 1270s.

The teaching methods were shaped by the scarcity of books and by the conviction
that truth emerges from structured conflict. In a lecture the master read the
set text aloud and glossed it, since students could not all own copies. Alongside
this ran the disputation, which was the real engine. A question was posed in a
form that admitted a yes or no. Arguments were marshalled for the wrong answer
first, in their strongest form; then the master gave his determination; then he
answered each of the opening arguments in turn. The written works of the
scholastics preserve this shape exactly, which is why a page of Aquinas reads
like the minutes of an argument rather than an essay. Twice a year at Paris a
master could hold a quodlibetal disputation, in which anyone present could ask
anything at all, and he had to handle it on his feet.

Books themselves were organised into a system. Stationers licensed by the
university kept approved master copies — exemplars — broken into quires called
peciae, which were rented out one at a time to be copied, so that several scribes
could work on one book simultaneously and errors could be traced back to the
official text. Certain works became fixed apparatus: Peter Lombard's collection
of theological sentences, on which every candidate in theology had to lecture;
Gratian's compilation; Justinian with the standard gloss.

## Degrees, Nations, and the Right to Walk Out

The degree structure was an apprenticeship, like any guild's. A student who had
attended the required courses and passed examination became a bachelor, which
carried the right and the obligation to teach some elementary classes under
supervision. The licence to teach came from the chancellor and was a formal legal
permission. Mastership itself came by inception, a public ceremony in which the
candidate gave his first lecture, was invested with the cap and the book and the
ring, and was received into the company of masters, usually followed by a feast
he had to pay for and which the statutes kept trying to make less extravagant.
Master, doctor and professor all originally meant the same thing: a qualified
member of the teaching corporation.

Students were grouped into nations by rough geographical origin, each with
elected proctors, its own funds, feast days and patron saints, and a capacity for
factional violence that the authorities never fully contained. Scholars counted
as clerics, which put them under church jurisdiction rather than the town's, gave
them the protection of the tonsure, and exempted them from a number of ordinary
civil burdens. Universities also claimed the right to control the rents students
paid, through joint boards of assessors, and the price of food and drink.

None of this made relations with the host city easy. Town-and-gown fighting was
endemic and occasionally became a massacre; the riot that began in an Oxford
tavern on St Scholastica's Day in 1355 killed scores of people and ended with
the town formally humiliated for centuries afterwards. But the decisive weapon
remained the strike. Because a university was a corporation and not a campus, it
could simply stop teaching and leave. Paris dispersed for two years in the 1220s
after the authorities killed students in a riot, and came back with a papal bull
confirming the masters' rights to their own statutes and to suspend lectures when
wronged. Oxford's dispersals seeded other schools. Cities learned that the
scholars were worth conceding to.

The colleges arrived later and almost incidentally — endowed halls of residence
founded by bishops, kings and royal confessors to keep poor students housed, fed
and supervised. At Paris the best known was set up in the thirteenth century by
Robert de Sorbon, chaplain to Louis IX, for students of theology. At Oxford and
Cambridge the colleges gradually absorbed the teaching itself and eventually
became the thing people meant by the university, which was not how it started.

By the fourteenth century rulers had grasped that a university was an asset, and
began founding them deliberately by charter — Prague, Kraków, Vienna,
Heidelberg, and a long list after them — to supply trained administrators and
lawyers, and to keep their own subjects from having to study abroad. What
travelled with those foundations was a package still recognisable now: faculties
and degrees, lectures and examinations, a governing body of teachers, gowns and
hoods, and a shared language of instruction that let a master from Scotland
lecture in Bologna to an audience from Poland without anyone thinking it
remarkable.
