# The Manor and the Open Field

Fly over the English Midlands on a low winter afternoon and you can still read
the medieval field system in the grass. Long parallel corrugations run across
pasture that has not been ploughed since the seventeenth century — ridges a few
metres wide with shallow furrows between them, often curving into a shallow S at
either end. That curve is the signature of an ox team. Eight beasts hitched in
line cannot turn on a sixpence, so the ploughman began swinging them left before
he reached the headland, and season after season the soil piled up along that
arc. The ridges are the strips of the open fields, and the villagers who made
them lived inside an arrangement of land, labour and obligation that historians
call manorialism.

The word is tidier than the thing. There was no standard manor. In parts of
Kent and East Anglia, peasants held land in severalty and paid money rents from
an early date; in Wales and the Pennines and the Alpine valleys, pastoral
farming produced something quite different; in northern Italy the villages that
mattered were the ones swallowed by towns. The classic manor — a lord's home
farm worked by the compulsory labour of unfree tenants who also farmed strips
of their own — was a northern French and English Midland pattern, strongest
between roughly the ninth and the thirteenth centuries. But where it did take
hold it organised nearly everything about a person's life: what they ate, whom
they could marry, where they ground their corn, and how much of the week
belonged to somebody else.

## Why the Fields Were Shaped That Way

The heavy plough drove the layout. A light scratch-plough of the Mediterranean
kind breaks the surface and does little else; it suits thin, dry soils and is
usually pulled by a pair of oxen and worked crosswise in square plots. The
northern plough was a different machine — a coulter to cut vertically, a share
to cut horizontally, and above all a mouldboard to turn the sliced sod over. It
could bite into the wet, heavy clays of northern Europe, bury weeds, and throw
up the ridges that drained water into the furrows. It also took six or eight
oxen to pull, which almost nobody owned outright.

So the plough team was pooled. One household contributed two oxen, another one,
another the plough itself, and the resulting team worked its way round the
village fields in an agreed order. Pooling the traction made it sensible to
pool the land as well, and the great fields were divided into furlongs — blocks
of a convenient ploughing length — and each furlong into strips, with a given
family's strips scattered across the whole territory rather than gathered in one
place. The scattering looks absurd to modern eyes and wasted an enormous amount
of walking. It also spread risk: frost hollows, light gravel, heavy clay and the
good loam were shared out, so no household was ruined by a single bad patch.

A full peasant holding in England was a virgate or yardland, conventionally
reckoned at about thirty acres though the real figure moved around from village
to village; in the Danelaw the equivalent unit was the bovate or oxgang,
notionally the land one ox could plough in a season. Many families held a half
or a quarter of that, and at the bottom of the village were cottagers with a
garden, a pig, and the need to work for wages.

Because everyone's strips lay intermixed, nobody could choose their own
rotation. The village had to farm in unison. Under a two-field system half the
arable lay fallow each year; the three-field system that spread across much of
northern Europe after the ninth century put one field under winter wheat or rye,
one under spring barley, oats, peas or beans, and left only a third idle. That
change alone lifted the sown acreage by a sixth and added legumes that fixed
nitrogen and fed both people and horses. Yields were still poor by any later
standard — three or four grains harvested for each grain sown was respectable,
and a sizeable part of the crop had to be set aside as next year's seed before
anyone ate.

## Two Kinds of Land, Two Kinds of People

A manor was split between the demesne, which the lord farmed for his own
household and for sale, and the tenant land, held by peasants in return for
rent. The genius of the system, from the lord's side, was that the rent could be
paid in work on the demesne. A villein holding a virgate might owe week-work —
two or three days on the lord's land every week of the year — plus boon-work at
the pinch points of the calendar, when the hay had to come in before rain or the
corn had to be reaped. Boon days were often sweetened with a meal, and the
quality of that meal was remembered and argued about: the wet boon where ale was
provided, the dry boon where it was not.

Villein status was hereditary and carried a set of humiliating incidents that
mattered less as cash than as markers of unfreedom. Merchet was a payment for
permission to marry a daughter off, particularly outside the manor. Heriot took
the best beast from a dead tenant's holding before the heir took it over.
Tallage could in theory be levied at the lord's will. Villeins could not leave
without permission, could not sue their lord in the royal courts, and could be
pursued and brought back if they ran. Alongside them lived free tenants who
owed money rent and little or no labour, sokemen with their own peculiar
obligations, and household servants who lived in. A single village might contain
all of them, and the line between free and unfree was exactly the line the
manorial court spent most of its energy policing.

Then there were the monopolies — banalities, in the French term. The lord's mill
was the only mill, and the miller took a fixed proportion of the flour as
multure, which is why handmills kept being confiscated and why villagers kept
buying new ones. In some places the lord's oven baked the village's bread and
his press handled its grapes, each for a cut.

## Running the Place

Very few lords ran their own manors. A great estate employed a steward who rode
a circuit of the properties and held the courts; each manor had a bailiff, often
an outsider on a salary; and below him a reeve, who was usually a villein
elected or pressed by his own neighbours, responsible for seeing that the work
got done and for answering to the auditors when it did not. The reeve's position
was genuinely uncomfortable — he was accountable to the lord for the shortfall
and had to live in the village all year with the people he chivvied. Alongside
him a hayward watched the hedges and fences, and impounded straying animals in
the pound until their owners paid.

The manorial court met every few weeks in the hall or under a tree. It handled
transfers of land, admitted heirs, fined people for letting pigs into the
standing corn, for brewing weak ale, for drawing blood, for failing to turn up
to boon work. Its decisions were made by the suitors — the tenants themselves —
who were asked to declare the custom of the manor, and custom was the whole
point. It was not written law, but it was not arbitrary either. It was the
remembered practice of the village, and a tenant who could persuade his
neighbours that something had always been done a certain way had a real defence
against a lord who wanted it done differently. The court rolls that survive in
their thousands are the best window we have into how ordinary medieval people
argued.

## What Pulled It Apart

Even before the fourteenth century, the classic labour-rent manor was eroding.
Where markets were good and coin was plentiful, lords found it simpler to
commute labour services into money and hire wage workers at harvest, and many
demesnes were leased out whole to a farmer who paid a fixed sum and took the
risk. A rising population up to about 1300 made land dear and labour cheap,
which suited landlords; then the famines of the second decade of the fourteenth
century and the plague at mid-century inverted the terms overnight. With a third
or more of the population dead, holdings stood empty and survivors could bargain
or simply walk to a manor that offered better terms.

Lords in England and elsewhere tried to legislate the old world back into
existence, capping wages and forbidding tenants to leave, and the attempt helped
provoke the revolt of 1381, whose rebels demanded the end of villeinage in
plain terms. They did not get a charter, but they got the drift of events.
Serfdom in England dissolved over the following century into copyhold — tenure
by a copy of the court roll entry, still customary, no longer personally unfree.
East of the Elbe the same shock produced the opposite result, as landlords with
more coercive power and fewer towns tightened their grip and built the harsher
serfdom that lasted into the nineteenth century.

The open fields themselves outlived manorialism by centuries in many villages,
and were finally undone not by plague but by enclosure, hedge by hedge and act
by act, until the strips were consolidated into the rectangular fields that
still cover most of lowland England — with the old ridges surviving only under
grass, where no later plough has gone.
