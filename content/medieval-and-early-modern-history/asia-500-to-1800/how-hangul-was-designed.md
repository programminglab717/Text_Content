# How Hangul Was Designed

Say the Korean letter ㄱ out loud. To make that sound, the back of your tongue
humps up and touches the soft palate, sealing the throat for an instant before
releasing. Now look at the letter again. It is a right angle: a horizontal line
running back, then a vertical drop. That is a picture of what your tongue just
did — a side view of the tongue root rising and closing off the passage. The
letter is a diagram of its own articulation.

No other writing system in regular use was designed this way, and for nearly
five hundred years nobody knew it was true. The alphabet was promulgated in
Korea in the middle of the fifteenth century, and the commentary explaining the
reasoning behind the shapes dropped out of circulation. Scholars in the
nineteenth and early twentieth centuries proposed that the letters derived from
Mongol script, from ancient seal characters, from the lattice patterns of paper
windows. Then, in 1940, a copy of the original explanatory volume turned up in a
house in the southeast of the country, and the guessing stopped. The letters
meant what they looked like.

## The Problem It Was Built to Solve

Korea in the early Joseon period was a Neo-Confucian state that took the Chinese
classical tradition with complete seriousness. Government business, scholarship,
poetry of the respectable kind, the civil service examinations that sorted the
aristocratic yangban class — all of it ran in literary Chinese, a language
nobody in Korea spoke.

Korean and Chinese are not merely different languages; they are structurally
mismatched in ways that make one a poor vehicle for the other. Chinese is
analytic, with grammatical relations largely carried by word order. Korean is
agglutinative: verbs take strings of suffixes marking tense, mood, negation, and
several grades of politeness, and nouns take particles marking their role in the
sentence. Chinese characters can record Korean vocabulary items but have no
natural way to write a four-syllable verb ending. Korean scribes had developed
workarounds for centuries, pressing characters into service for their sound
values to notate grammatical particles, or inserting abbreviated reading marks
into classical texts. These systems worked, more or less, for clerks who already
knew thousands of characters. They did nothing for anyone else.

That was the stated motive. The preface attached to the new alphabet makes the
argument plainly: the speech of this country differs from that of China, the
characters do not fit it, and ordinary people with something to say are unable
to write it down. The king who signed that statement, Sejong, presided over a
reign full of similar projects — standardized rain gauges distributed to the
provinces, water clocks and astronomical instruments, expanded metal type — all
pointed at getting useful knowledge to circulate. Whether he personally designed
the letters or supervised scholars who did has been argued over, but the
contemporary record credits him directly, and the design has an internal
consistency that suggests one mind.

## Letters Shaped Like Mouths

The consonants start from five basic shapes, each derived from the organ that
produces its sound.

ㄱ is the tongue root at the soft palate, as described. ㄴ shows the tip of the
tongue curling up to touch the ridge behind the upper teeth: a line up, then
forward. ㅁ is the outline of the mouth, a square. ㅅ is the shape of a tooth,
for the hissing sound made by driving air past it. ㅇ is a circle, the outline of
the throat, used for the sound at the end of the English word "sing."

The rest of the consonant inventory is generated from those five by adding
strokes, and the addition is not decorative. A stroke means more breath. From ㄴ
comes ㄷ, which is the same tongue position with full closure, and from ㄷ comes
ㅌ, the same again with aspiration — a puff of air on release. From ㅁ comes ㅂ
and then ㅍ; from ㅅ comes ㅈ and then ㅊ; from ㄱ comes ㅋ. Sounds that are
related in the mouth are related on the page, in a visible, ordered progression.
Korean also has a third series, tense consonants produced with a stiffened
larynx, and these are written by simply doubling the letter: ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ, ㅆ, ㅉ.

Two letters sit slightly outside the scheme. ㄹ, the liquid that sounds like an
r between vowels and an l at the end of a syllable, and one now-obsolete sign
were treated in the original commentary as a separate category, derived from the
tongue and tooth shapes but with an added stroke that marks them as something
other than a straightforward increase in breath. The designers knew they were
exceptions and said so, which is itself a sign of how deliberate the rest of it
was.

The result is a script that encodes phonological features, not just phonemes,
which is why linguists sometimes classify it as featural rather than simply
alphabetic. A learner who grasps the principle does not memorize fourteen
unrelated consonant signs. They memorize five pictures and two rules.

## Heaven, Earth, and a Standing Person

The vowels were built on a different logic, drawn from the cosmology that
educated Koreans of the period used to explain everything else. Three primitive
marks: a round dot for heaven, a flat horizontal line for earth, an upright
vertical line for the standing human being.

Combining the dot with the two lines produces the basic vowels. A dot above the
horizontal gives ㅗ; below it, ㅜ. A dot to the right of the vertical gives ㅏ; to
the left, ㅓ. In printing the dot quickly flattened into a short stroke, which is
how the letters look today, but the derivation is exactly that. Adding a second
stroke produces the y-glide series: ㅛ, ㅠ, ㅑ, ㅕ.

The placement was not arbitrary either. Korean at the time had vowel harmony,
with vowels sorted into two classes that could not freely mix within a word.
Dots placed above or to the right marked one class — the bright or yang set —
and dots below or to the left marked the other. The geometry carried grammatical
information. Vowel harmony has since weakened in the modern language, surviving
mainly in onomatopoeia and certain verb endings, but the visual sorting remains
embedded in the letter shapes.

The original set had twenty-eight letters. Four have since fallen out of use,
including the pure heaven-dot vowel, whose sound merged with others over the
following centuries; twenty-four remain in standard use today.

## Why the Letters Stack

Hangul is not written in a running line like the Latin alphabet. Letters are
grouped into square syllable blocks: an initial consonant, a medial vowel, and
optionally a final consonant, assembled into a single glyph of uniform size.

The arrangement inside the block follows the shape of the vowel. If the vowel is
vertical, like ㅏ, the initial consonant sits to its left. If the vowel is
horizontal, like ㅗ, the consonant sits on top of it. A final consonant, called
the batchim, goes underneath the whole arrangement. So the consonant ㅎ with the
vowel ㅏ and the final ㄴ yields 한; with ㄱ and ㅡ and ㄹ it yields 글.

There are two good reasons for this. One is linguistic: Korean phonology is
strongly syllable-based, with rules about what can occupy a final position and
how a final consonant resyllabifies when the next syllable begins with a vowel.
Writing by the syllable makes those patterns visible. The other is typographic
and political. Chinese characters occupy uniform squares. By packaging itself
into squares of the same dimensions, the new alphabet could be set in the same
columns and the same grid, mixed freely into a Chinese text to gloss a
pronunciation or supply a grammatical ending. It did not demand that anyone
abandon the existing written culture. It offered to slot into it.

## The Objections, and the Long Wait

That accommodation did not save it from resistance. Within months of the
announcement, a senior scholar of the royal research academy submitted a memorial
against the project, and its arguments are worth noting because they were
sincere. Only barbarian peoples — Mongols, Jurchens, Tibetans, Japanese — had
scripts of their own; to adopt one was to step outside the civilized world.
Korea had served the Chinese cultural order for centuries and it was improper to
introduce such a thing without consultation. Existing clerical notation was
adequate for practical needs. And, revealingly, a script so easy to learn would
let people pick up statutes and precedents without the years of classical study
that were supposed to make a person fit to judge anything.

Sejong pushed it through anyway and had it demonstrated in use: a long dynastic
eulogy, Buddhist texts commissioned after his queen's death, a standardized
dictionary of Chinese character readings.

The alphabet also had a use its opponents had not anticipated. Because it could
notate sounds precisely, it became an instrument for studying other languages.
Scholars used it to fix the readings of Chinese characters and to argue about
what the correct pronunciations ought to be, and the government bureau that
trained interpreters used it to transcribe Chinese, Mongolian, Manchu, and
Japanese in its teaching manuals. Those transcriptions are now valuable evidence
for historical linguists reconstructing how all of those languages sounded
several centuries ago.

But the yangban kept writing Chinese, decade after decade,
because the examinations were in Chinese and careers ran through the
examinations. The vernacular script acquired the dismissive name eonmun, the
vulgar writing, and later the sneer of being women's script. Early in the
sixteenth century a king who found anonymous criticism of himself posted in it
banned its study outright, though the prohibition did not hold for long.

What kept it alive was everything outside the examination track. Women wrote
letters and memoirs in it. Court ladies kept records in it. Popular fiction
circulated in it, read aloud and rented out by the chapter. Practical manuals for
farming and medicine used it to reach people who would never read a classic.
Nineteenth-century missionaries seized on it immediately as the obvious way to
put scripture into Korean hands.

Official status came only at the very end of the Joseon dynasty, when reforms in
the 1890s made Korean the language of government documents. The name hangul —
the great script — was coined in the early twentieth century by linguists
working to standardize it, at precisely the moment Korean itself was coming
under pressure from Japanese colonial rule, which is part of why the alphabet
carries the nationalist charge it still has. The document that explained the
letters was found during those same colonial years, hidden in a private house.
