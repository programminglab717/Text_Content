# The Foreign Quarter of Tang Chang'an

At dusk a drum began in the gate towers of Chang'an and rolled out over the
city, hundreds of beats, and by the time it stopped the gates of every walled
ward had swung shut. Anyone caught in the avenues after that was liable to be
beaten by the patrols. The city was built to be locked: a rectangle of rammed
earth walls enclosing a grid of more than a hundred residential wards, each with
its own wall and its own gate, laid out by Sui engineers and inherited by the
Tang more or less intact. It looks, on a map, like a machine for control. It
was also, for roughly two centuries, the most polyglot place on earth.

The contradiction was not accidental. A city that counts and confines its
residents can afford to let a great many strangers in. Chang'an's two great
markets — the Eastern Market serving the aristocratic quarters, the Western
Market serving everyone else — were themselves walled enclosures opened and
closed by drum and gong, with officials assigned to inspect weights, register
brokers, and fix prices. Inside the Western Market a Persian could sell
gemstones, a Sogdian could take deposits and lend at interest, and a Chinese
customer could buy grape wine, pepper, glassware, or a trained dancing girl
from Tashkent. The state watched all of it and taxed some of it, and mostly let
it run.

## The Sogdians

The commerce of the overland routes ran, more than on any other group, on the
Sogdians — the merchants of Samarkand, Bukhara, Tashkent, and the other oasis
cities of Transoxiana. They had been trading east since before the Tang, and
their diaspora was organized: family firms, chains of resident colonies strung
out along the desert routes through Turfan, Dunhuang, and the Gansu corridor,
letters of credit and consignment arrangements, sons apprenticed abroad. In
Chinese records their names give them away. Sogdians in China took surnames
derived from their home cities, so that a man surnamed Kang traced his family
to Samarkand, An to Bukhara, Shi to Tashkent, and so on down the list. Those
surnames turn up in tax registers, tomb inscriptions, army rosters, and
eventually in the highest ranks of the Tang military.

What they carried mattered less than what they made possible. Silk moved west,
of course, but so did paper, ceramics, and lacquer; horses moved east, along
with furs, aromatics, medicinal drugs, ammoniac salt, and slaves. Sogdian
became a lingua franca of the routes, written in an alphabet that the Uighurs
borrowed, and that the Mongols would later borrow from the Uighurs. The tomb
figurines that fill museum cases today — the tricolor glazed camels with their
loads slung over a saddle frame, the grooms with deep-set eyes and thick beards,
the musicians perched cross-legged on a camel's back — are Chinese objects made
by Chinese potters for Chinese tombs, and they show that a well-off family in
the eighth century thought a caravan was a natural thing to take into the
afterlife.

## Borrowed Music, Borrowed Clothes

Tang taste ran west for most of the dynasty's first half. The court maintained
ensembles of music categorized by origin, with separate repertoires from Kucha,
Samarkand, Bukhara, India, and Korea, each with its own instruments and its own
costumed dancers. The pipa, the lute that would become one of the most Chinese
of instruments, came in from Central Asia along with the players who knew how to
handle it. So did the whirling dance the Chinese called the Sogdian spin, so
fast that poets kept reaching for images of wind and snow to describe it.

Fashion followed. Women of good family in the capital wore Iranian-style
riding coats with narrow sleeves, boots, and soft caps; they rode astride, and
they played polo, a game that arrived from the west and became an aristocratic
obsession, played by emperors and by teams of palace women. Grape wine was
pressed in the capital after the Tang took the Turfan oases and brought the
vine stock and the technique back with them. Bread baked in a tandoor-style
oven, sprinkled with sesame, was sold on the street as a foreign cake. By the
mid-eighth century moralists were complaining in verse that the capital had
gone barbarian in its dress, its cosmetics, and its songs — which is the usual
sign that a fashion has stopped being exotic and become ordinary.

Religion moved along the same roads. Buddhism was long since naturalized, but
it was still being replenished: the monk Xuanzang walked to India and back in
the 630s and 640s, returned with crates of Sanskrit texts, and spent the rest
of his life translating them, with a brick pagoda built in the southern part of
the city to house the collection. Other faiths arrived as communities rather
than doctrines. Sogdian Zoroastrians maintained fire temples under a state-
recognized headman. Manichaeans, long suspect, got official tolerance after the
Uighur khaghan adopted the religion and the Tang court found itself badly in
need of Uighur cavalry. Christians of the Church of the East, working east from
Persia, established a presence early enough that a stele raised in the late
eighth century recorded the arrival of their first missionary under the second
Tang emperor and listed a century and a half of imperial favors. Muslim
merchants settled at the southern ports and at Yangzhou on the Yangzi, forming
resident communities with their own judges and their own quarters.

## The Empire as a Magnet

None of this was purely commercial. The Tang had beaten the Eastern Turks in
the early seventh century, and Taizong took from the steppe leaders the title
of Heavenly Khaghan alongside his Chinese one — a dual sovereignty that made
the court a destination for Inner Asian nobles, hostages, envoys, and exiles.
Garrison towns and protectorates ran out through the Tarim Basin. The armies
that held them were commanded, increasingly, by generals of non-Chinese
descent, on the sound bureaucratic logic that such men had no civil faction
behind them and owed everything to the throne.

Students came too, and they came to stay. Japanese embassies crossed at
intervals with scholars, monks, and craftsmen aboard, and the capital they
brought home in their heads was reproduced in the grid plans of Nara and later
Kyoto, down to the north-centered palace and the numbered avenues. Two of the
monks who sailed with an early-ninth-century mission returned to found the
Tendai and Shingon schools that would shape Japanese Buddhism for a thousand
years. The most remarkable of the Japanese visitors, Abe no Nakamaro, sat the
Chinese examinations, passed, took a Chinese name, and served the Tang state as
an official for decades; his one attempt to sail home was driven off course to
the far south, and he came back to Chang'an and died there, mourned in poems by
friends who were among the best poets of the age.

## What Ended It

The break is usually dated to the rebellion that erupted in 755 under An
Lushan, a frontier commander of Sogdian and Turkic parentage who held three
military commissions at once and used them. The rebellion lasted eight years,
depopulated the north China plain, forced the emperor out of the capital, and
was put down only with Uighur help that the Uighurs then charged for, in an
extortionate exchange of worn-out horses for good silk that ran for decades.
The dynasty survived another century and a half, but it survived as a weaker
thing, with a shrunken tax base and provinces it did not fully control.

Cosmopolitanism did not end at once, but its terms changed. Foreigners who had
lived in the capital on government stipends — envoys whose missions had no route
home, hostages nobody had reclaimed — were audited and struck off the rolls in
the late eighth century when a minister discovered how many of them there were
and how long the treasury had been feeding them. The overland routes were cut
when Tibet took the Gansu corridor, and the Tarim garrisons were left to wither.
In the 840s a thoroughgoing suppression of Buddhism, undertaken partly for
fiscal reasons, swept up the smaller foreign religions with it; the Manichaean,
Zoroastrian, and Christian communities in the interior never recovered. Late in
the ninth century rebels sacked Guangzhou and slaughtered a large part of its
foreign merchant population, in numbers that Arabic accounts report and no one
can verify.

The trade itself did not stop; it moved into the water. A ninth-century Arab
vessel wrecked off a small Indonesian island and found more than a thousand
years later was carrying tens of thousands of mass-produced painted bowls from
a kiln complex in Hunan, packed in storage jars — a cargo that shows a Chinese
industry already making wares to foreign taste for a shipping route that ran
from the Persian Gulf to the South China Sea. That route would carry far more
than the camel caravans ever did. What it would not do is deposit all of it in
one walled city, drum-locked at nightfall, where a Sogdian banker, a Japanese
examination graduate, a Tibetan hostage, and a Kuchean lutenist could all be
found within a few blocks of each other.
