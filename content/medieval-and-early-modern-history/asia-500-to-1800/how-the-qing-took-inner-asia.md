# How the Qing Took Inner Asia

The hardest part of fighting on the steppe was never the fighting. It was the
grain. A nomad army lives off its herds and moves with them; an army marching
out of China carries its food, and every camel and cart that carries food also
eats. Beyond a certain distance from the last supply depot, the column consumes
more than it can transport and the campaign ends whether or not the enemy has
been found. For two thousand years this arithmetic protected the steppe from
sedentary empires. Han and Tang armies could win battles in Mongolia and could
not stay. The Ming built walls instead.

The Qing broke the arithmetic. Over roughly a century of campaigning they
established forward granaries, organized camel trains and cart convoys on a
scale nobody had attempted, contracted with merchants to move supplies for
profit, planted military colonies to grow grain closer to the front, and
absorbed the cost out of a tax base that could bear it. By the 1750s they could
put an army in the Ili valley, thousands of kilometers from the nearest Chinese
farmland, and keep it there. That capability, more than any battlefield
innovation, is why the map of China today includes Xinjiang, Tibet, and
Mongolia's southern half.

## A Frontier Dynasty

The people who did this did not start out as a Chinese dynasty, and never
entirely became one. The Manchus emerged from the Jurchen populations of the
forests and river valleys northeast of the Ming frontier — farmers, hunters,
and traders in ginseng, furs, and pearls, organized in lineages and loosely
supervised by Ming officials who preferred them divided. Nurhaci, a chieftain
who had begun as a Ming client, spent three decades unifying them by war and
marriage, and in the process invented the institution that would carry the
conquest: the banners.

A banner was a hybrid of army unit, household registry, and hereditary estate.
Every family in the system belonged to a company; companies made up banners;
banners mobilized for war, held land, and passed membership down through the
generations. Crucially, the structure cut across tribal lines, and it expanded
by absorption. Surrendered Mongols were enrolled in Mongol banners, and Chinese
defectors from the Liaodong frontier in Chinese banners — the last group
bringing with them the cannon founders and artillerymen the Manchus needed to
take walled cities.

Nurhaci's son Hong Taiji built the rest of the state. He cultivated marriage
alliances with the Khorchin Mongols, who became the dynasty's in-laws for
generations. He defeated the Chakhar Mongols and took from the son of their last
khan the seal that had belonged to the Yuan, then made sure everyone in the
steppe knew he had it: a claim to Chinggisid succession delivered in the
grammar of steppe politics. In 1636 he renamed the state Qing and took an
imperial title. He was preparing to conquer China, but he was already ruling a
multiethnic empire before a single Manchu soldier crossed the Great Wall.

The crossing, when it came in 1644, was opportunistic. A rebel army took Beijing
and the last Ming emperor hanged himself on the hill behind the palace. The Ming
general holding the pass at Shanhaiguan, caught between the rebels and the Qing,
chose the Qing and opened the gate. The regent Dorgon entered Beijing claiming
to avenge the Ming rather than replace it, and then replaced it. The conquest of
the south took another two decades, and it was brutal — the order that all men
adopt the Manchu shaved forehead and queue on pain of death provoked resistance
that was answered with the sack of cities. A last serious internal challenge
came in the 1670s when three great Chinese frontier commanders revolted, and it
took eight years to put down. Taiwan, held by a sea-going regime descended from
the Ming loyalist Zheng Chenggong, fell in 1683 and was annexed for the first
time.

## The Zunghar War

Only then did the Qing turn to the problem that would define them. In western
Mongolia and the Ili valley, the Zunghars had assembled the last great steppe
confederation: Oirat Mongols with Tibetan Buddhist legitimacy, Russian and
Central Asian firearms, their own agriculture and mining, and ambitions across
the whole of Inner Asia. When their khan Galdan drove the Khalkha Mongols of
the eastern steppe out of their pastures, the Khalkha nobles fled south and
submitted to the Kangxi emperor in exchange for protection. That submission gave
the Qing both a claim and a war.

Kangxi took the field himself, more than once, with columns converging across
the Gobi on supply lines that nearly failed, and in 1696 caught and shattered
Galdan's army at Jao Modo. Galdan died the following year, but the Zunghar state
did not; it reconstituted itself and fought the Qing for another sixty years,
seizing Lhasa in 1717 and forcing a Qing expedition that installed a new Dalai
Lama in 1720 and left resident imperial commissioners in Tibet permanently.

Diplomacy did as much as the cavalry. Treaties with Russia — negotiated at
Nerchinsk in 1689 through Jesuit interpreters in Latin, and extended at Kyakhta
a generation later — fixed a northern boundary, regulated caravan trade, and
provided that neither empire would shelter the other's fugitives. What that
meant in practice was that the Zunghars could not play the two powers against
each other or retreat into Russian territory. The steppe was being closed from
both ends at once.

The end came under Qianlong in the 1750s, when a Zunghar succession struggle
produced a claimant who invited Qing intervention and then revolted against it.
The response was explicitly exterminatory: the emperor's orders directed that
the Zunghar people be destroyed as a people, and between the campaigns and the
smallpox that swept the survivors, they effectively were. The emptied land was
resettled with Manchu and Mongol garrisons, Muslim farmers from the oases, exiles
and convicts, and Chinese merchants. A whole banner population from Manchuria
was marched across the continent to garrison the Ili and is still there. Two
years later the Qing took the oasis cities of the Tarim Basin as well, and the
combined territory was named Xinjiang, the new dominion.

## The Limits of the Machine

Qianlong advertised the result as the Ten Great Campaigns, a tally carved onto
stelae and celebrated in commissioned battle prints engraved, at his request, in
Paris. The list flatters. Alongside the Zunghar and Tarim conquests it counts an
invasion of Burma that lost more men to malaria than to the enemy and ended in a
face-saving agreement, an intervention in Vietnam that installed a client and
then watched him swept out within weeks, and two wars against small hill
chiefdoms in western Sichuan whose stone tower fortresses absorbed years of
siege and a quantity of silver that would have paid for a steppe campaign
several times over. The empire's reach had limits, and they showed up wherever
the terrain was wet, forested, or vertical — precisely the conditions under
which the Qing logistical machine, built for dry grassland and long sightlines,
did not work.

## One Emperor, Several Thrones

The Qing did not govern this empire as an extension of China, and that is the
most instructive thing about it. Inner Asia was administered not by the boards
that ran the provinces but by a separate institution, the Lifanyuan, which
handled Mongol nobility, Tibetan clergy, and, for a long time, relations with
Russia. Mongols were governed under their own legal code through their own
princes, whose ranks and pastures the court confirmed and whose sons married
into the imperial house. Muslim Altishahr was left largely to local officials
under military supervision. Tibet kept the Dalai Lama's government, watched by
imperial commissioners.

The emperor himself was several people at once, quite deliberately. To Chinese
subjects he was the Son of Heaven, sponsor of the examinations, patron of a
colossal imperial library project. To Mongols he was khan, heir to the Yuan seal
and the hunting rituals of the steppe. To Tibetans and to Mongol Buddhists he
was a lay patron in the old priest-patron relationship and, in commissioned
paintings, an emanation of the bodhisattva Manjushri, with a Tibetan hierarch at
court as his preceptor. At the summer capital north of the wall, where the court
went to hunt and to receive Inner Asian visitors away from the constraints of
Beijing ritual, Qianlong built temples modeled on Tibetan originals, including a
version of the Potala, and raised stelae inscribed in four scripts — Chinese,
Manchu, Mongolian, and Tibetan — so that each audience could read the same claim
in its own language. Sensitive Inner Asian business was conducted in Manchu,
which kept it out of the hands of Chinese officials entirely.

Holding all of this together required insisting that the Manchus remain Manchu.
Emperors issued edict after edict about riding, archery, the Manchu language,
and frugality, which tells you how hard the drift was running the other way.
Banner garrisons lived in walled quarters inside major cities, forbidden to
enter trade or farm, supported by stipends that inflation steadily eroded.

Meanwhile the empire filled up. Peace, new land, and American crops — maize,
sweet potatoes, peanuts, tobacco — that grew where rice and wheat would not
pushed settlement into hills and uplands, and the population roughly doubled
over the eighteenth century. The same growth produced landless migrants,
deforested slopes, silted rivers, and a state whose revenue base had been fixed
by an early promise never to raise the land tax. When a millenarian rebellion
broke out in the mountains where three provinces met at the very end of the
century, it took nearly a decade and an enormous sum to suppress, and it
revealed that the banner armies which had conquered Inner Asia could no longer
handle a peasant insurgency. The territory acquired in the great campaigns was
permanent. The machine that had acquired it was not.
