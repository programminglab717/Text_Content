# The Samurai Who Never Fought

In the middle of the eighteenth century, a samurai of middling rank serving a
domain on the Japan Sea coast would spend most of his working life the way a
clerk does. He checked rice tallies coming in from villages, copied
correspondence in a careful hand, sat through meetings about the order of
precedence at a ceremony scheduled for the following spring. He wore two
swords, a long one and a short one, and by law nobody outside his class could
wear both. He had almost certainly never drawn either in anger. Neither had his
father. In many cases, neither had his great-grandfather.

This is the central strangeness of Tokugawa Japan: a society organized from top
to bottom around a hereditary warrior caste that had no wars to fight. The
fighting stopped with brutal suddenness. Tokugawa Ieyasu won the decisive field
battle at Sekigahara in 1600, finished off the last serious rival coalition at
Osaka in 1615, and after the suppression of the Shimabara rebellion in the late
1630s — an uprising of peasants and Christians in Kyushu, crushed with siege
works rather than swordsmanship — organized violence essentially vanished from
the Japanese islands for two centuries. The warrior class did not vanish with
it. It was maintained, at public expense, at something like five or six percent
of the population counting families, for two hundred and fifty years of peace.

## Pulled Off the Land

The samurai of the warring decades had been, in many cases, armed landholders
who farmed when they were not campaigning. That arrangement ended before the
Tokugawa came to power. Toyotomi Hideyoshi's sword hunt of the 1580s disarmed
the villages, and his cadastral surveys fixed who was a cultivator and who was a
retainer. The consequence, elaborated under the Tokugawa, was that samurai were
pulled off the land entirely and resettled in castle towns around their lord's
keep. They no longer drew income from particular fields they knew. They drew a
stipend, measured in koku of rice — nominally the amount needed to feed one
person for a year — paid out of the domain's collected revenue.

Cutting the warrior's tie to any specific piece of ground made rebellion much
harder to organize, which was precisely the point. It also turned the samurai
into a salaried employee of a hereditary bureaucracy, and a salaried employee
whose salary was fixed by his family's rank rather than by anything he did.
Promotion existed but was slow and constrained by birth; a low-ranking man of
real ability could be given responsibility, but rarely the income that
corresponded to it. What the domain actually needed, increasingly, was
administrators: tax assessors, granary supervisors, inspectors, judges of
village disputes, engineers for flood control and land reclamation. The warrior
class supplied them, because there was nobody else.

## The Rice Problem

A stipend paid in rice sounds secure until you notice that a samurai does not
live on rice alone. He needs cloth, oil, lacquerware, paper, medicine, sake,
tuition for his sons, and the gifts that social obligation demanded at a dozen
points in the calendar. All of that had to be bought with money, which meant
converting rice into cash, which meant dealing with merchants. In Edo the
brokers who handled the conversion, the fudasashi, became notorious; in Osaka,
the rice exchange at Dojima developed forward trading in warehouse receipts
early in the eighteenth century, one of the first futures markets anywhere.

The arithmetic worked against the warrior in two directions at once. Rice
production rose over the Tokugawa period as land was reclaimed and yields
improved, which tended to depress the price of rice relative to manufactured
goods. Meanwhile the consumer economy of the castle towns expanded, and
standards of appropriate consumption rose with it. A stipend that had been
adequate in 1650 bought less of what a samurai household was expected to display
in 1750. Retainers borrowed against next year's stipend, then the year after
that. Domains, squeezed themselves, responded by "borrowing" from their own
retainers — which is to say withholding a portion of stipends, sometimes half,
sometimes indefinitely.

At the bottom of the warrior class the pressure showed as visible poverty. The
lowest ranks in a large city — men with tiny hereditary allowances, foot
soldiers whose ancestors had carried spears — took on by-employments that would
have been unthinkable for their status and were tolerated because everyone knew
why. Edo households of this sort assembled paper umbrellas, pasted lanterns,
raised goldfish and crickets for sale, cultivated ornamental plants, did piece
sewing. They kept the swords and the right to a surname, and they sold insects.

The Confucian ordering of society placed the warrior at the top and the merchant
at the bottom, below peasants and artisans, on the reasoning that merchants
produce nothing and profit from the labor of others. The ordering was a
statement of values, not a description of who was comfortable. By the
eighteenth century it was an open joke that the men at the bottom of the
hierarchy held the notes of the men at the top.

Falling out of the system entirely was worse. A retainer whose lord was
disgraced, or whose domain was dissolved for failing to produce an heir, became
a ronin — a masterless man, still legally a samurai, with no income and almost
no prospect of being taken on elsewhere, since domains were not expanding their
rolls. The first decades of Tokugawa rule produced tens of thousands of them as
the losing side at Sekigahara was dismantled, and they were a genuine security
problem until the shogunate softened the succession rules that had been creating
them. Many drifted into teaching, medicine, or the fencing academies. Some
became the scholars and writers whose books the settled samurai read.

## Sankin Kotai

The financial pressure on the domains themselves had a deliberate architecture.
Every daimyo was required to maintain a residence in Edo and to alternate his
own presence between Edo and his domain, generally in yearly cycles, while his
wife and heir stayed permanently in the capital as hostages in everything but
name. Travel meant a procession sized to match one's rank, moving along
maintained highways with fixed post stations, with hundreds or in the largest
cases thousands of retainers, all of them eating and lodging at the lord's
expense. The system consumed a substantial share of many domains' budgets.

It also did more to shape Japan than any deliberate program of nation-building
could have. It created a genuinely national road network and a national market
for provisions along it. It made Edo one of the largest cities in the world,
stuffed with samurai on rotation who had money to spend and time to spend it —
the audience for kabuki, for woodblock prints, for the pleasure districts, for
the publishing trade. And it produced a class of men who had seen the capital,
compared their own domain's customs to those of a dozen others encountered on
the road, and could no longer think of their province as the whole world.

## Inventing the Warrior in Retrospect

What is a warrior for, when there is no fighting? The question was taken
seriously. Yamaga Soko, writing in the seventeenth century, argued that the
samurai's justification was moral: he consumed without producing, and therefore
owed society the example of disciplined, upright conduct that everyone beneath
him could model. This was a workable ideology for an administrative class, and
it fit comfortably with the Neo-Confucian learning that domain schools
increasingly taught.

The nostalgic alternative is best known through the Hagakure, dictated in the
early eighteenth century by Yamamoto Tsunetomo, a retainer of the Nabeshima
domain who had been forbidden to follow his lord in death and became a monk
instead. Its famous insistence that the way of the warrior is found in dying was
the complaint of a man who felt the world had gone soft, and it circulated in
manuscript within one domain rather than being widely read. Its enormous later
fame belongs mostly to the twentieth century. The word bushido itself was rare
before the modern period; the systematic code that foreigners now associate with
the samurai was assembled largely in peacetime and popularized long after the
class it described had been abolished.

Between those two positions sat the Ako incident, which fascinated everyone
because it exposed the contradiction exactly. In 1701 a provincial lord drew his
sword inside Edo Castle against a court official who had, he felt, humiliated
him. He was ordered to commit seppuku and his domain was dissolved. Nearly two
years later, forty-seven of his now-masterless retainers killed the official and
turned themselves in. They had behaved as loyal vassals were supposed to behave
and as subjects of a law-governed state were forbidden to behave. The government
resolved the problem by allowing them the honorable death of seppuku rather than
executing them as criminals, and the theatres turned the affair into the most
durable melodrama in the Japanese repertoire — staged, to evade censorship,
under the names of historical figures from an earlier century.

## The Class Dissolves

Martial training did not disappear. Fencing schools proliferated, and by the
late Tokugawa period practice with bamboo staves and protective armor let men
spar at full speed, which the older forms-based training had not. But whether
any of it amounted to military capability was untested, and firearms — used in
mass at Nagashino in the sixteenth century — had lost prestige along with the
tactical conditions that made them decisive. When American warships arrived in
the 1850s and the coastal domains inventoried their defenses, what they found
was alarming.

The unraveling took fifteen years, and it was driven by samurai: young, often
low-ranking men from Satsuma and Choshu who concluded that the shogunate could
not defend the country and that the whole domainal order had to go with it. The
government they built abolished the domains in 1871, converted hereditary
stipends into bonds, opened the military to conscription, and in 1876 forbade
the wearing of swords. When Saigo Takamori led the last samurai revolt in
Satsuma the following year, the force that defeated him was an army of
conscripted commoners — peasants and townsmen, the people Hideyoshi had disarmed
three centuries earlier.
