# The Mongol Empire and the Century It Made

A courier leaves a post station somewhere in the Gansu corridor with a tablet of
inscribed silver hanging at his chest, bells on his belt so the station ahead
hears him coming and has a fresh horse saddled before he arrives. He rides it
until it is spent, swaps, and rides on. The stations sit roughly a day's travel
apart, stocked with mounts, food, and fodder by the local population, who are
taxed for the privilege. On this system a message could cross Eurasia in weeks
rather than seasons, and an order given in Mongolia could reach an army in
Persia while it still meant something. The relay network was not the most
dramatic Mongol invention, but it may have been the most consequential: it is
the reason a confederation of pastoralists could be administered as an empire at
all.

The empire began with a man who spent his youth on the losing side of steppe
politics. Temüjin was the son of a minor chief poisoned by rivals; the family
was abandoned by its clan and lived for years on roots and marmots. He was at
various points a captive in a wooden collar and a client of a more powerful
khan. What he learned from all of it, and applied ruthlessly once he had the
power to, was that steppe confederations collapsed because they were built out
of tribes, and tribes defected.

## Breaking the Tribes

At an assembly on the Onon River in 1206, after two decades of fighting that
had ended with every major steppe people either destroyed or absorbed, he was
proclaimed Chinggis Khan. The army he then organized was structured in
multiples of ten: squads of ten, companies of a hundred, thousands, and the
ten-thousand-strong tümen. Men from defeated tribes were distributed across
units rather than kept together, so that a Naiman fought beside Tatars and
Kereits under a commander who owed his rank to Chinggis. Moving from one unit
to another without orders was a capital offense. Units were collectively liable
for each other's conduct in battle, and a squad that abandoned a comrade could
be executed entire.

Around the khan stood the keshig, a guard of thousands drawn substantially from
the sons of his commanders — simultaneously a bodyguard, a household staff, a
hostage pool, and a school from which governors and generals emerged. Promotion
ran on demonstrated competence to a degree that was unusual anywhere in the
thirteenth century. Jebe, one of the two best field commanders of the early
campaigns, had entered Mongol service after shooting Temüjin's horse from under
him on the other side of a battle. Subutai, the other, came from a forest people
of no standing and, by tradition, a blacksmith's family; he planned campaigns
across three thousand kilometers and lived to direct armies for Chinggis's son.

Administration was improvised out of whatever the conquests turned up. A
captured Uyghur scribe adapted the Uyghur alphabet to write Mongolian, giving
the chancery a script. Chinese, Persian, Khitan, and Turkic officials were
recruited, sometimes at the point of a sword, to count populations and assess
taxes. A body of decrees attributed to Chinggis, the Yasa, was cited for
centuries as the foundation of Mongol law, though no full text survives and
later authors disagree about what was in it. What is clear is that the empire
exempted religious specialists of all faiths from taxation and labor service,
which bought it the cooperation of Buddhist monks, Muslim clerics, Nestorian
priests, and Daoist masters alike, and gave every one of them a reason to write
about the khans with restraint.

## The Conquests

The campaigns began against the Tangut state of Xi Xia and then the Jurchen Jin,
who held north China. Steppe armies had raided walled cities for a thousand
years; what was new was that the Mongols learned to take them. They recruited
Chinese siege engineers, then Muslim ones, and the counterweight trebuchets that
finally broke the long Song defense of Xiangyang in the 1270s were built by
engineers sent from the Mongol court in Iran. Capitulation was rewarded and
resistance was punished with a thoroughness that was meant to be reported:
garrisons massacred, artisans deported, populations driven ahead of the next
army as living shields. The chroniclers' body counts are not usable as figures —
they run to numbers larger than the cities could have held — but the pattern of
abandoned irrigation systems and shrunken towns across Khurasan is real.

That western war began with a provocation. The Khwarazmian governor of Otrar
seized a Mongol-sponsored trade caravan and executed the merchants as spies;
the envoys sent to demand redress were killed as well. Between 1219 and 1221 the
empire of Khwarazm, the dominant power of Central Asia and Iran, ceased to
exist. Bukhara, Samarkand, Urgench, Merv, and Nishapur were taken in succession.
A flying column under Jebe and Subutai pursued the fleeing shah, kept going
around the Caspian, wrecked a Georgian army and then a Rus-Kipchak one, and rode
home having scouted the entire western approach.

The Mongols came back for it. Under Batu and Subutai the Rus principalities were
reduced one at a time in the late 1230s, Kiev falling in 1240, and in the spring
of 1241 two forces crossed into Poland and Hungary within days of each other and
destroyed both royal armies. Then, the next year, they withdrew. The traditional
explanation is the death of the great khan Ögedei and the need to return for the
election; historians have since added the limits of Hungarian pasture for a
cavalry army of that size, a wet winter, and the sheer distance from the center.

A third great campaign, under Chinggis's grandson Hülegü, took the Ismaili
mountain fortresses of northern Iran and then, in 1258, Baghdad. The last
Abbasid caliph was executed, and a line of religious authority five centuries
old ended. Two years later a detachment of that army, left behind when Hülegü
withdrew east on news of another succession crisis, was beaten by the Mamluks of
Egypt at Ain Jalut in Galilee. It was not a catastrophic defeat, but it marked
the edge of the expansion, as Hungary had in the west and as Japan and Java
would in the east.

## Four Empires Out of One

Succession was the structural flaw. There was no rule of primogeniture, only
election by assembly among a family that multiplied fast and disagreed
violently. Each transfer of power was a crisis, and the one after the death of
the great khan Möngke in 1259 broke the empire for good. His brothers Khubilai
and Ariq Böke both claimed the throne and fought a war over it, and by the time
Khubilai had won, unity was a fiction maintained on paper.

Four states emerged. Khubilai's, proclaimed as the Yuan dynasty, finished the
conquest of the Southern Song in 1279 and ruled China from a new capital on the
site of modern Beijing. The Ilkhanate held Iran and Iraq. The Golden Horde took
the steppe north of the Black and Caspian seas and the tribute of the Rus
princes. The Chaghadaids held Central Asia. They fought each other — the
Ilkhanate and the Golden Horde were enemies for generations, the Horde allying
with the Mamluks against their own cousins — and yet the roads between them
stayed open enough for merchants, envoys, and scholars to use.

## What Traveled

The trade was deliberate policy. Mongol nobles put capital into partnerships
with Muslim merchant associations, sharing the profits of long-distance ventures
they financed but did not conduct. Along those routes went silk, porcelain,
horses, slaves, and information. A Persian astronomer arrived at Khubilai's
court with instruments and tables; Chinese medical texts and agricultural
techniques turned up in Iran. Under Ilkhanid patronage the vizier Rashid al-Din
compiled a history that tried to cover the Mongols, the Turks, the Chinese, the
Indians, the Jews, and the Franks in one framework — the first work that can
reasonably be called a world history, and a product of a court that had reason
to think of the world as a single administrative problem. A Turkic Christian
monk born near Khubilai's capital traveled as an Ilkhanid envoy as far as Rome,
Paris, and Bordeaux, and left an account of it. The Ilkhanate even tried, once,
to introduce Chinese-style paper money in Tabriz; the bazaar shut rather than
accept it and the experiment lasted weeks.

Gunpowder moved west along the same corridors. So, most likely, did plague.
Something carried Yersinia pestis out of the rodent reservoirs of Inner Asia in
the 1340s, and it reached the Black Sea ports where Italian merchants loaded
grain and slaves; one Genoese report blames a Mongol besieging army for hurling
its own dead over the walls of Caffa. Within five years it had emptied a third
of Europe, and it hit the Middle East and China as well.

The Yuan fell to rebellion in 1368 and its court retreated to the steppe. But
descent from Chinggis had become the license to rule across half of Asia, and it
outlived every state he founded. Timur, who conquered from Delhi to Anatolia,
never claimed the title of khan because he was not of the line; he ruled through
Chinggisid puppets and married a princess of the house so he could be called
son-in-law. His descendant Babur, founding an empire in India, traced himself to
both men. When the Manchus wanted the Mongols to accept them three centuries
later, what they took possession of, and made sure everyone heard about, was the
seal of the Yuan.
