# The Delhi Sultanate and the Art of Holding On

Stand in the courtyard of the first great mosque built at Delhi by its Muslim
conquerors and the improvisation is right there in the stone. The colonnades
around the court are made of pillars lifted from demolished temples, carved with
figures that have been chiselled away with varying degrees of care, stacked two
deep where a single pillar was not tall enough. The enormous screen of arches
across the prayer hall looks like arches and is not: the masons who built it did
not yet know how to turn a true arch, so they corbelled the openings, stepping
stones inward course by course until they met, and then dressed the result to
imitate the shape their patrons wanted. A conquering regime in a hurry, using
the hands and materials it found, insisting on a form nobody locally could yet
produce.

That is a fair description of the state as well. The sultanate at Delhi lasted a
little over three centuries, ran through five dynasties, and at no point had a
settled rule of succession. It was assembled out of Turkish military slaves,
Persian administrative technique, Indian revenue, and Central Asian cavalry, and
it survived as long as it did less through design than through a series of
violent corrections applied by men who understood that the alternative was being
killed by someone else.

## Founded by Slaves

The conquest itself was an extension of Afghan politics. Muhammad of Ghur pushed
east through the passes in the late twelfth century, broke a Rajput coalition at
Tarain, and left his lieutenants to hold what he had taken. When he died without
a son, the lieutenant holding Delhi, Qutb al-Din Aibak, simply kept it.

Aibak had been a slave. So had many of the men around him, and so were several
of his successors, which is why the first dynasty is conventionally called the
Slave or Mamluk line. The institution behind that word needs explaining, because
it was nothing like plantation slavery. Boys were bought or taken in the Turkish
steppe, raised in a commander's household, trained in horsemanship and archery
and often in letters, converted, and attached to their master by a bond that was
supposed to be stronger than kinship because it replaced kinship. They had no
tribe to defect to and no relatives to favor. A talented one could be manumitted,
given command of a province, and marry his master's daughter. The system produced
formidable soldiers and an insoluble political problem: the loyalty ran to a
person, not an office, so every succession reopened the question.

Iltutmish, Aibak's own slave and son-in-law, did the real work of consolidation
over a long reign — subduing rival Turkish commanders, standardizing a silver
coinage, and obtaining formal investiture from the Abbasid caliph, which gave
him a legitimacy no rival could manufacture. He also nominated his daughter
Raziyya as successor over his sons, judging her the abler. She ruled for a few
years, rode out publicly, dispensed with the veil, and was deposed and killed by
the corps of Turkish nobles who had never accepted the arrangement. That corps,
known as the Forty, spent the following decades making and unmaking sultans
until one of their own, Balban, took the throne and systematically destroyed
them — executing, poisoning, and posting rivals to distant commands, while
surrounding the throne with Persian ceremonial designed to place the sultan
above ordinary men.

## The Mongols at the Door

None of this happened in a vacuum. From the 1220s, when Chinggis Khan's armies
chased a fleeing Khwarazmian prince to the banks of the Indus, the Mongols were
a permanent fact on the northwestern frontier. Lahore and the Punjab were
raided, occupied, lost, and retaken; twice armies reached the outskirts of Delhi
itself.

The pressure shaped everything. It forced the sultans to maintain a large
standing cavalry force and to fortify, which is why Delhi accumulated a series of
walled cities rather than one. It made the northwest a military zone rather than
a settled province. And it delivered, as refugees, a substantial part of the
Persian-speaking scholarly and administrative class of Central Asia and Iran,
whose homelands the Mongols had wrecked. Delhi took in poets, jurists, secretaries,
and Sufi masters, and became within a couple of generations one of the major
centers of Persianate culture anywhere — a secondary consequence of catastrophe
elsewhere, and probably the most durable thing the sultanate produced.

## Alauddin Khalji's Machine

The Khalji takeover at the end of the thirteenth century brought to power a man
who thought about the state as an engineering problem. Alauddin Khalji faced
Mongol invasions in successive years and concluded that he needed a large
standing army paid in cash rather than through land assignments, because
assignments made commanders independent.

A cash army requires that soldiers' pay buy enough to live on, so he fixed
prices. Grain, cloth, horses, cattle, slaves — each had an official rate,
enforced in the capital's markets by inspectors with the power to punish short
weight and hoarding. Grain was procured directly from the countryside and stored
in state granaries to be released when supply tightened. To make the procurement
possible he had the cultivated land measured and set the revenue demand as a
fixed share of the produce, assessed on the measured area rather than negotiated
with intermediaries, which cut the local chiefs who had been skimming the
difference out of the arrangement.

He extended the same suspicion to his own nobility. Drinking was banned, private
gatherings of nobles required permission, marriages between great families needed
his consent, and an intelligence service reported on all of it. Meanwhile his
armies went south. Under the general Malik Kafur, campaigns reached deep into the
peninsula, defeating the Yadavas, the Kakatiyas, the Hoysalas, and pushing as far
as the southern tip. These were not, for the most part, annexations. They were
raids for treasure and tribute, and they established that Delhi could project
force over the whole subcontinent without being able to hold much of it.

## The Tughluq Experiments

The most inventive and least fortunate of the sultans was Muhammad bin Tughluq,
who ruled through the middle decades of the fourteenth century and whose reign
reads like a sequence of intelligent ideas colliding with logistics.

Recognizing that the Deccan conquests could not be governed from a thousand miles
away, he moved his capital to Daulatabad in the south and ordered the elite of
Delhi to move with him. The distance and the march killed people, the new capital
proved as badly placed for holding the north as Delhi had been for holding the
south, and within a few years the court came back. Facing a silver shortage of
the kind then affecting much of Eurasia, he issued token coins in brass and
copper meant to circulate at the value of silver. The idea was sound and was
being used in China with paper. It failed because the mint had no monopoly on the
technology: the tokens were easy to counterfeit, households produced their own,
and the treasury eventually had to redeem heaps of forgeries at face value.

The Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta arrived during this reign and was appointed
judge of Delhi, a post for which he was not especially qualified and which he
held for years. His account is the fullest outside view of the sultanate at its
height: the wealth, the enormous gifts, the elephants, and the sultan's
alarming oscillation between generosity and lethal rage.

His cousin and successor, Firuz Shah, drew the opposite conclusion from all of
it and governed by concession. He stopped campaigning, lowered exactions,
granted revenue assignments on hereditary terms to keep the nobility content,
built canals from the Yamuna and the Sutlej that irrigated a large stretch of
the plain, founded towns, and repaired old monuments — including transporting two
polished stone pillars inscribed by the emperor Ashoka, sixteen centuries
earlier, to his own new city, where nobody could read them. The reign was
comfortable and the army rotted. Within a decade of his death the provinces were
detaching themselves, and in 1398 the armies of Timur came down from the north,
stormed Delhi, massacred and enslaved much of its population, and left with the
craftsmen.

## What Was Left

The sultanate technically continued for another century and a quarter, first
under a family claiming descent from the Prophet and then under Afghan Lodi
kings, but the scale had changed completely. A joke of the period had the emperor
of the world ruling from Delhi as far as the village of Palam, a few miles down
the road. The Lodis were more substantial than that, pushing east and shifting
their base to Agra, but they governed as first among Afghan equals rather than as
autocrats, and when Ibrahim Lodi tried to govern otherwise his own nobles invited
a Timurid adventurer from Kabul to come and replace him. Babur obliged at Panipat
in 1526.

What the sultanate left behind was not a durable state but a template. The
regional kingdoms that broke away — in Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa, Jaunpur, and the
Deccan — kept its Persian chancery practice, its coinage, its revenue vocabulary,
and its architecture, and used them to build local traditions that were often
more interesting than the original. Hindavi, the speech of the Delhi region,
began the long absorption of Persian vocabulary that would eventually produce
Urdu. The Chishti Sufi lodges, which took in anyone and generally refused state
money, established a religious geography that still draws crowds to the shrines
at Ajmer and Delhi. And the arch that the first masons could not build was
thoroughly mastered, which is how the Mughals were able to start where they did.
