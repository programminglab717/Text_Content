# What the Ming Treasure Voyages Were For

In the autumn of 1414 an embassy from Bengal led a giraffe through the gates of
Nanjing. The animal had come out of East Africa by way of an Indian Ocean port
and a diplomatic regifting, and by the time it reached the Ming court the
officials who received it had decided what it was: a qilin, the auspicious beast
that appears when a sage rules. A court painter made a portrait of it, held on a
halter by a groom, its head at the top of a tall vertical scroll. The Yongle
emperor, who had taken the throne from his own nephew in a civil war and spent
the rest of his life demonstrating that heaven approved, is said to have waved
off the congratulations. He kept the giraffe.

That is roughly the right frame for the fleets that Zheng He took into the
Indian Ocean seven times between 1405 and 1433. They are often described as an
aborted age of discovery, a moment when China nearly became a maritime power and
then lost its nerve. That framing imports a set of European purposes — finding
new routes, seizing ports, extracting trade profits for the crown — that the
Ming did not have and did not want. The voyages make far more sense read as what
the giraffe was: a demonstration, conducted at enormous expense, that the
world's rulers recognized where the center of it was.

## The Admiral

Zheng He was born Ma He in Yunnan, in a Muslim family whose men had made the
pilgrimage to Mecca — his father's grave inscription records the title. Yunnan
was the last holdout of Mongol loyalists, and when Ming armies took it in the
1380s the boy was captured, castrated, and assigned as a servant to a young
imperial prince in the north. The prince was Zhu Di. When Zhu Di rebelled
against his nephew and seized the throne, the eunuch had distinguished himself
in the fighting, and he emerged from it with a new surname and the emperor's
trust.

That background is not incidental. Eunuchs were the emperor's personal
instrument, outside the civil bureaucracy and detested by it, useful precisely
because they had no lineage, no examination cohort, and no future apart from
their patron. Every one of the great Ming projects run around the bureaucracy
was run by eunuchs, and the fleets were among the largest.

## The Ships

Chinese accounts describe expeditions of several hundred vessels carrying
something in the range of twenty-five to thirty thousand men: soldiers, sailors,
interpreters, physicians, shipwrights, astrologers, and clerks. The largest
craft, the treasure ships proper, are given dimensions in the sources that imply
hulls well over a hundred meters long, and naval architects have been arguing
about those figures for decades, since a wooden hull of that length carries
serious structural problems. What survives is suggestive rather than decisive: a
rudder post excavated from the mud of the imperial shipyard at Nanjing is large
enough to have hung on a very big ship indeed, whether or not it was as big as
the texts claim.

The navigation was more impressive than the tonnage. The fleets ran on magnetic
compass bearings recorded in written sailing directions, on measured depths and
bottom samples, and on stellar altitudes taken with a set of graduated wooden
boards held at arm's length, the height of a guide star recorded in finger
widths. A chart derived from those voyages survived in a military compendium
compiled two centuries later, a long strip map running from the Yangzi to the
Persian Gulf and the Swahili coast with bearings and star altitudes written
alongside it.

The itineraries repeated with variations: down the coast to Champa, through the
Java Sea, to Palembang in Sumatra, to Malacca, then across to Ceylon and up the
Malabar coast to Calicut, with squadrons detached to Hormuz, Aden, Mogadishu,
and Malindi, and on the last voyage to Jeddah. At Galle in Ceylon the fleet set
up a stone tablet inscribed in three languages — Chinese, Tamil, and Persian —
recording offerings to the Buddha, to a Hindu deity, and to Allah, with each
text making its own pitch to its own audience. Whoever drafted it understood
exactly what the ports of the Indian Ocean looked like.

## Not Conquest, But Not Peaceful Either

The fleets were not trading ventures in the sense that a Portuguese carrack was.
They carried silk, porcelain, and lacquer, but distributed much of it as
imperial gifts; what came back were tribute goods, envoys, and exotica, often
worth less than what went out. Nor were they colonizing expeditions: no
territory was annexed, no permanent garrison was planted, no fortified factory
was built along the model the Europeans would use a century later.

They were, however, armed, and they used force when Ming interests in the sea
lanes were at stake. At Palembang the fleet destroyed the following of a Chinese
pirate chief who had made himself master of the strait, and hauled him back to
Nanjing for execution. In Ceylon a local ruler who moved against the fleet was
defeated and carried off to China, where he was eventually released and sent
home. In northern Sumatra the fleet intervened in a succession dispute and
removed the losing claimant. Malacca, by contrast, was cultivated: Ming
patronage helped turn a small settlement into the dominant entrepôt of the
straits, and its ruler traveled to China in person. The pattern is of a power
policing a corridor and rewarding clients, which is a political program, not a
commercial one.

## The Reasons They Stopped

The voyages ended because the Ming state's attention and money went elsewhere,
and because nothing in the way the state was funded made them pay.

Yongle was simultaneously running a war in Vietnam, five personal campaigns into
Mongolia, the reconstruction of the Grand Canal, and the transfer of the capital
from Nanjing to Beijing. Any one of those was a generational undertaking. The
Vietnam occupation, begun in 1407, turned into two decades of insurgency and was
abandoned after his death at a cost in men and silver that nobody at court had
forgotten. The canal mattered in a quieter way: once it could carry the grain
tribute reliably from the Yangzi to the new northern capital, the state no
longer needed a coastal transport fleet, and one of the standing arguments for
maintaining a deep-water navy went away.

Then there was the structure of Ming revenue. The dynasty taxed land, largely in
grain, and handled foreign trade as a ritual privilege granted to tribute
missions rather than as a customs stream to be maximized. A state financed that
way experiences a fleet as pure expenditure. The Song and Yuan had both drawn
real revenue from maritime commerce, and both had supported large merchant
marines; the early Ming deliberately did the opposite, banning private overseas
trade outright.

Above all, the threat was continental. The Mongols had not gone away. In 1449 an
emperor led an army north, was surrounded at Tumu, and was captured — a
humiliation that fixed strategic priorities for the rest of the dynasty around
the northern frontier, walls, garrisons, and horses. A navy that sailed to
Africa did nothing about that.

The civil bureaucracy supplied the argument and the follow-through. Eunuch-run
prestige projects were exactly what officials most wanted to curtail, and when a
later eunuch proposed reviving the voyages, the ministry could not produce the
archives. The story that a vice-minister had hidden or burned them to kill the
idea circulates in later accounts; whether or not it happened that way, the
records did vanish, and most of what we know comes from stelae, a few
participants' travel books, and fragments preserved elsewhere.

## The Counterfactual Is Wrong

What did not happen is that China withdrew from the sea. The state withdrew from
the sea. Chinese merchants kept sailing, illegally when necessary, in numbers
the bans could not touch. Fujianese and Cantonese shipping ran to Luzon, Java,
Siam, and Nagasaki through the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries; the coastal
piracy that the Ming spent decades fighting was substantially conducted by
Chinese subjects locked out of a trade they intended to carry on anyway. In 1567
the government gave up part of the argument and licensed private trade out of a
Fujian port, and the traffic that was already happening simply became visible.

By then the world economy had arranged itself around Chinese demand rather than
Chinese fleets. China had shifted its taxes and its commerce onto silver and did
not produce enough of it, so silver came — from Japanese mines, and then from
Spanish America across the Pacific to Manila, where Chinese junks met the
galleons and swapped silk and porcelain for bullion that had been dug out of a
mountain in the Andes. A Chinese quarter grew outside Manila's walls large
enough to alarm the Spanish into massacring it more than once. None of that
required a treasure ship.

The voyages are worth remembering for what they were rather than for the empire
they did not found: the largest sustained projection of state power across the
Indian Ocean before the age of steam, mounted by a court that wanted
acknowledgment rather than territory, and dropped the moment the acknowledgment
stopped being worth the grain it cost.
