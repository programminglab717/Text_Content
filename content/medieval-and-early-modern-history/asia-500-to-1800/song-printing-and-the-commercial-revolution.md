# Pear Wood, Ink, and Paper Money

Start with a block of pear wood, sawn along the grain, planed flat, and soaked
and dried so it will not warp. A calligrapher writes out two pages of text on a
sheet of thin paper. The sheet is pasted face down on the block while the paste
is still wet, so the ink transfers and the characters sit on the wood in mirror
image. Then the carver goes to work, cutting away everything that is not a
stroke, leaving the text standing in relief a couple of millimeters high. Water-
based ink is brushed over the raised surface, a sheet of paper is laid down, and
a pad is rubbed across the back. Peel it off and you have a printed leaf, which
gets folded down the middle so the blank side faces in, and sewn with others
into a book.

That is the whole technology, and it is worth pausing on how little of it is
mechanical. There is no press. There is no type. A single worker with inked
blocks and a stack of paper could pull impressions all day, and printers in Song
China routinely did, at rates that contemporaries described in the thousands of
sheets. The expensive part happened before any paper was touched: paying a good
hand to write the text out, and paying a cutter to spend weeks turning that
writing into wood.

## Why Blocks Beat Type

Movable type existed. A Song official named Shen Kuo, a polymath whose notebooks
are one of the best windows into eleventh-century technical practice, described
a craftsman called Bi Sheng making individual characters out of fired clay,
setting them in an iron frame bedded with resin and ash, and melting the bed to
release them afterward. The account is specific enough that it plainly describes
something Shen Kuo had seen or been told about in detail. Wooden and metal type
followed in later centuries. None of it displaced the carved block for a very
long time, and the reason is arithmetic.

A Chinese printer needs thousands of distinct characters to set an ordinary
page, with many duplicates of the common ones — tens of thousands of pieces of
type before a single sheet is printed, plus the labor of sorting and
redistributing them. Against that, the block has three advantages that mattered
more in practice than typesetting speed. It can be stored. A publisher who has
carved a book owns a physical object that can sit in a warehouse for fifty years
and then print another hundred copies when somebody asks. That turns publishing
from a gamble on a print run into a durable asset, and it makes small editions
viable. Second, a block reproduces whatever is on it — a diagram, a map, a
picture of an acupuncture point, a distinctive calligraphic hand — with no extra
apparatus. Third, corrections are local: plug the error, recut the plug, and
reprint.

So the Song became a printing society without becoming a press society. What
poured out of the blocks first was religion and government. The Buddhist canon
was carved in Sichuan in a state project early in the dynasty that ran for
years and consumed a quantity of blocks best described as enormous. The
Directorate of Education issued authoritative editions of the classics and the
histories. Other offices printed medical formularies, agricultural handbooks,
mathematical texts, and the law. Commercial publishers followed fast, with the
booksellers of Jianyang in Fujian supplying the cheap end of the market:
compact, closely printed, sometimes carelessly proofread editions of the things
people actually bought, including examination anthologies, almanacs, household
encyclopedias, ghost stories, and primers. One surviving Song artifact is a
bronze printing plate for a shop advertisement — a needle maker in Jinan,
identified by a picture of a rabbit pounding a mortar, promising good steel and
prompt service to wholesalers. Whoever cut that plate was not thinking about
literature.

## The Examination Machine

Printing and the examinations fed each other. The Song, distrusting the
hereditary aristocracy that the Tang had run on, built recruitment around
competitive written tests, and then built procedures to keep them honest:
candidates' names were sealed, and their papers were recopied by clerks so that
graders could not recognize handwriting. The palace examination, conducted
under the emperor's name, made the emperor personally the patron of every
successful candidate rather than the official who had recommended him.

The result was an enormous nationwide population of men preparing for an
examination most of them would fail. They needed texts — the classics with
approved commentaries, model answers, historical compendia, rhyming
dictionaries — and printing supplied them at prices a modest landowning family
could manage. This is the mechanism by which a degree of literacy spread well
below the level of people who would ever hold office, and by which a genuinely
empire-wide educated class came into being, sharing a canon of books that were
textually identical from Sichuan to the coast. Standardized texts are an
underrated political technology.

## Rice, Coal, and Coins

The intellectual boom sat on an agricultural one. Early-ripening, relatively
drought-tolerant rice varieties associated with Champa in what is now central
Vietnam were introduced and actively propagated by the state, with seed
distributed in the Yangzi region. A crop that matures quickly can be followed by
a second crop in the same year, or grown on hillier and less reliably watered
land. Combined with better water control, iron-shod plows, and manuring, the
effect was more food from the same acreage, and the population of China
probably passed a hundred million for the first time during the Song.

More food meant fewer farmers were needed to feed everyone, which meant cities.
Kaifeng in the north and, after the Jurchen conquest drove the court south,
Hangzhou on the coast became urban concentrations of a scale Europe would not
see for centuries. They also became a different kind of city than Tang Chang'an
had been. The walled ward and the enclosed market broke down. Shops opened
directly onto the streets, night markets ran past the old curfew hours,
restaurants specialized by regional cuisine, and entertainment districts
supported storytellers, puppet theaters, and shadow plays as paid professions.
The famous handscroll of a spring festival along the river, painted for the
court, is mostly a picture of commerce: porters, a wine shop with a tall
decorated gate, camels entering a gate, a grain barge fighting the current under
a wooden bridge while the crowd shouts advice.

Industry grew with it. North China ironworks burned coal, which was abundant
near the ore, and turned out cast iron for plowshares, nails, cooking pots,
tools, coins, and weapons in quantities that modern historians have estimated
from tax records and then argued about ever since. Ceramics went industrial
too, with kiln complexes producing standardized wares in the hundreds of
thousands for domestic sale and export.

All of this needed money, and here the Song ran into a physical problem. Its
currency was bronze coin, strung through square central holes in units of a
thousand, and the state minted it in immense volume — enough that Song coins
turn up in hoards in Japan, Korea, Java, and East Africa, because they were
better money than anything locally available and simply drained out of the
country. Even so there was never enough, and a thousand coins weighs several
kilograms. In Sichuan the situation was worse, since the region used iron
coinage, heavier still per unit of value.

The response came from merchants rather than from the state. Sichuanese deposit
shops began issuing receipts for coin left with them, the receipts started
circulating instead of the coin, and in the 1020s the government took over the
business, licensed it, and began issuing notes itself against a reserve, with
fixed terms of validity and printed anti-counterfeiting devices. Under the
Southern Song, paper notes circulated across the empire, and the government
learned the other half of the lesson: a state that can print money will print
too much of it when a war goes badly, and late Song notes depreciated the way
such notes generally do.

## Ships and the Sea Route

The Southern Song, having lost the north, turned to the water. Superintendencies
of maritime trade at Guangzhou, Quanzhou, and Ningbo registered ships, collected
duties in kind, and took a share of cargo, and the revenue mattered enough that
the court cultivated it deliberately. Customs and monopoly income from salt,
tea, wine, and trade came to rival the land tax, which is not how earlier
Chinese states had been funded.

The ships were good. A thirteenth-century seagoing hull excavated from the mud
of Quanzhou harbor has a keel, a V-shaped section for sailing to windward, and
a hull divided into more than a dozen watertight compartments by transverse
bulkheads, so that a holed ship stayed afloat. Stern-mounted rudders hung on a
post gave control that steering oars could not. And navigation had a new
instrument: Shen Kuo, again, described a magnetized needle floated or suspended
so that it points south, noting carefully that it does not point quite true
south but a little east of it — an observation of magnetic declination made
while European sailors were still centuries from using a compass at all. Within
a few decades a writer describing the Guangzhou trade mentions pilots relying on
the needle when cloud hid the stars.
