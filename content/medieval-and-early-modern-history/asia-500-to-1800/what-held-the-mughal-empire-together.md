# What Held the Mughal Empire Together

When a Mughal emperor travelled, he took the capital with him. The imperial camp
was a canvas city with a fixed plan: the enclosure for the ruler's tents at the
center, the audience hall, the treasury, the bazaars in assigned lanes, the
nobles pitched at distances that corresponded exactly to their rank. To avoid
the delay of striking and raising it, there were two complete sets. While the
court occupied one, the other went ahead by a day's march and was already
standing when the emperor arrived, laid out identically, so that a courtier
could find his way to the audience tent in the dark in a place he had never
been.

The camp is a useful way in, because the empire itself worked on the same
principle. It was not a collection of territories held by hereditary lords who
happened to acknowledge a sovereign. It was a portable apparatus of ranks,
salaries, measurements, and written records, imposed on an enormously varied
subcontinent, and designed so that the people staffing it could be moved around
without the structure changing shape. When it worked, it produced perhaps the
richest state in the world. When the arithmetic underneath it stopped balancing,
it came apart with astonishing speed.

## The Dynasty That Nearly Wasn't

Its founder was a Timurid prince from Ferghana who had spent his youth losing
Samarkand repeatedly and ended up in Kabul because there was nowhere else to go.
Babur came down into the plains in 1526 more as a raider with ambitions than as
an imperial founder, beat the Lodi sultan at Panipat with field artillery and
mobile cavalry, and then had to beat a Rajput confederacy the following year to
keep what he had taken. He left a memoir, written in his Turkish mother tongue,
that is candid about fear, boredom, and the disappointing fruit of Hindustan.

His son Humayun lost the whole thing. An Afghan commander from Bihar, Sher Shah
Suri, drove him out of India entirely and ruled for five years with a
competence that matters more than its brevity suggests: he standardized a silver
coin that became the rupee, rebuilt the trunk road across the north with rest
houses at intervals, and pushed land measurement and cash revenue assessment
further than anyone before him. Humayun clawed his way back from Persian exile
and died within months, falling on his library steps. The empire then passed to a
thirteen-year-old.

What Akbar did over the next half-century was to take the Sur administrative
machinery, extend it, and wrap it in a political settlement that made it
possible for non-Muslims to serve the state as something other than subordinates.
Both halves were necessary. Neither alone would have held.

## Ranks, Not Fiefs

Every imperial officer held a mansab, a numbered rank, expressed as two figures.
The first fixed his personal status and pay. The second specified the number of
cavalrymen he was obliged to maintain and bring when summoned. Rank was granted
by the emperor, raised or cut by the emperor, and did not descend to sons as a
right. There was no hereditary landed aristocracy in the European sense, because
there were no hereditary lands to be aristocratic about.

Officers were paid, usually, not in cash but by assignment of the revenue of a
tract of land — a jagir — sufficient to cover the salary the rank entitled them
to. The crucial detail is that jagirs were rotated. A holder might keep one for
three or four years before being transferred somewhere else entirely, which
prevented him from sinking roots, building a local following, or confusing his
assignment with his property. A portion of the empire's land was held back
entirely, its revenue going straight to the central treasury.

Because the whole system depended on officers actually producing the troops they
were paid for, the court policed the muster. Horses brought for inspection were
branded with an imperial mark so that the same animals could not be borrowed and
presented twice, and descriptive rolls recorded each soldier's features. Fraud
persisted anyway, but the paperwork was serious, and the archive of it — orders,
assignments, audits — is the reason we know as much about this state as we do.

The nobility staffing these ranks was deliberately mixed. Central Asian Turks
who had come with the dynasty, Persians who arrived seeking careers, Indian
Muslims, Afghans, and — after Akbar's alliance with the ruling house of Amber —
Rajput chiefs who served as imperial commanders while retaining their ancestral
territories as a special kind of assignment. No single group could dominate the
others, which was the point. In the provinces the same logic applied: a governor
commanded troops but did not control revenue, which belonged to a separately
appointed official reporting to the center, and each watched the other.

## Measuring the Land

The empire's income came overwhelmingly from agriculture, and the method of
extracting it was, in the core provinces of the north, an exercise in
measurement that took decades to build.

Cultivated land was surveyed with a standardized rod and classified by how
continuously it was farmed — land under crop every year, land left fallow in
rotation, land long abandoned. For each locality, officials compiled what
amounted to a schedule of cash rates: for this crop, in this district, a
demand per unit of area, derived from averaging yields and market prices over a
run of previous years. The peasant's obligation was therefore known in advance
and fixed in money rather than being negotiated at harvest by whoever showed up
with soldiers. Remissions were allowed for genuine crop failure. The share
claimed was heavy — commonly around a third of estimated produce, sometimes more.

Demanding revenue in cash had a consequence the designers understood perfectly
well: it forced cultivators into the market. Peasants had to sell grain to obtain
coin, which deepened rural monetization, supported a dense network of local
market towns, and pushed farmers toward cash crops — cotton, indigo, sugarcane,
opium — wherever the schedules made those attractive. It also meant the state's
revenue was tied to price levels it did not control.

This full apparatus never covered everything. In regions annexed late, in hill
country, in the east, older arrangements persisted, with revenue taken as a share
of the crop or as a lump sum from local chiefs. Everywhere, collection ran
through zamindars — hereditary holders of a right to a cut of the produce of
particular villages, who were not landlords in the modern sense but were
indispensable, and who were perfectly capable of leading armed resistance when
the demand rose too high.

## Peace With Everyone

The political settlement was as engineered as the fiscal one. Akbar abolished the
pilgrimage tax and then the poll tax on non-Muslims, married into Rajput houses
and gave their men high command, sponsored translations of the Sanskrit epics
into Persian, and held long evening debates at his new capital at Fatehpur Sikri
among Muslim jurists, Hindu and Jain scholars, Zoroastrians, and visiting Jesuits
— debates which, by most accounts, left him less rather than more confident in
any single claim to exclusive truth.

His ideologist Abu'l Fazl built this into a theory of sovereignty in which the
emperor stood above all sects as the dispenser of a universal concord, deriving
his authority from a divine light passed down through his line rather than from
the approval of religious scholars. A small circle of nobles took a personal oath
of discipleship to the emperor, which hostile writers then and since have
described as a new religion; it was closer to a court order of loyalty.

Whatever its spiritual content, the politics were sound. An empire whose ruling
house was Muslim and whose population was overwhelmingly not could either govern
by exclusion or by recruitment, and recruitment is cheaper. The settlement held
for roughly a century. Aurangzeb's decision to reimpose the poll tax on
non-Muslims after two decades on the throne was a reversal of it, and although
he continued to employ Hindu officers in large numbers — more, by some counts,
than his predecessors — the signal it sent was not lost on anyone.

## The Arithmetic Fails

The empire's real crisis was less about faith than about jagirs. Expansion
required absorbing the nobles of conquered regions into the rank system, which is
how you buy off resistance. But every new mansab granted was a claim on revenue
that had to be assigned somewhere. As Aurangzeb pushed into the Deccan, annexing
the sultanates of Bijapur and Golconda and spending his last twenty-five years in
the field against Maratha forces who declined to fight the kind of battle he was
equipped to win, the number of claimants grew faster than the assignable revenue.
Officers waited years for jagirs. Those who received them, knowing transfer was
coming, extracted as much as they could as fast as they could, which damaged the
very productivity the system was assessed on. Peasants absconded. Zamindars
armed themselves.

When Aurangzeb died in 1707 after nearly fifty years on the throne, the empire
was at its largest extent and its machinery was seizing. Within a generation the
provinces had become hereditary in practice: the governor of the Deccan founded
his own dynasty, Bengal and Awadh went the same way, and the Marathas extended a
tribute system across much of the subcontinent. A Persian invader carried off the
jewelled throne in 1739. The successor states were often well run and
commercially vigorous — this was not a simple collapse into chaos — but they no
longer sent revenue to Delhi.

The final irony is procedural. In 1765 the East India Company, having won control
of Bengal by arms, took its position there not as a conqueror but as the emperor's
revenue collector, holding the office by imperial grant. The forms of the Mughal
state were still worth having. The state itself was already gone.
