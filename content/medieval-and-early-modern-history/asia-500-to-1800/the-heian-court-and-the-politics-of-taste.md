# The Heian Court and the Politics of Taste

At a festival procession in the capital, the ox-carts of the nobility would be
drawn up along the route with their blinds lowered, and from beneath each blind
the women inside allowed the edges of their sleeves to spill out over the
carriage rail. This was the whole of what spectators saw of them: a stack of
silk edges, eight or ten layers deep, each layer a slightly different color,
graded from the outermost inward. The combinations had names and seasons. Plum
in the first month, wisteria in the fourth, the particular russet-over-green
that meant autumn was getting on. A woman who wore a spring combination in
autumn, or who let one layer sit badly against the next, had told several
hundred people something about herself that she could not take back.

It is easy to read this as decadence, and generations of later Japanese
moralists did exactly that. It is more useful to read it as a functioning
political system. For roughly four centuries after the court settled at
Heian-kyo in the 790s, a few thousand people living inside a single city
governed, or believed they governed, the Japanese islands. They had no standing
army; mass conscription had been abandoned late in the eighth century as more
trouble than it was worth. They had no police force outside the capital worth
the name. What they had was rank, precedent, and an exquisitely calibrated
system for deciding who belonged. Taste was not an ornament on that system. It
was the system's operating language.

## The City and the Rank

The capital was laid out on a Chinese grid, with a great central avenue running
south from the palace enclosure and numbered cross-streets dividing the whole
into wards. The western half was low and damp and never really filled in, so
the city grew lopsided eastward, but the plan mattered more than the reality.
Everything that counted happened within it. To be sent out of the capital was a
punishment, and provincial service, however profitable, was a kind of exile
that court poetry treats with genuine grief.

Rank ordered everything. The court had a graded hierarchy of ranks and offices
inherited from the Chinese model and then bent to Japanese purposes, and the
senior nobles who actually deliberated on affairs of state numbered only a few
dozen men at any time. Rank determined the color of your outer robe, the size of
your residence, the gate you entered, the number of attendants you could bring,
whether you could enter the emperor's private quarters. The examinations that
staffed the Chinese bureaucracy with talent never took root here. Birth did the
work instead, and by the tenth century birth meant, overwhelmingly, the northern
branch of the Fujiwara.

## Rule by Maternal Grandfather

The Fujiwara did not seize the throne, and it is worth being precise about why
they did not need to. Heian marriage was generally uxorilocal: a husband visited
his wife at her own family's residence, and their children were raised there,
among their mother's relatives, under the eye of their maternal grandfather. Now
place an imperial prince in that household. The man who marries a daughter to
the emperor, and whose daughter bears the heir, will raise the next emperor in
his own house. When that boy takes the throne as a child, his grandfather is the
obvious regent.

Two offices formalized it — regent for a child sovereign, and a parallel office
that allowed the same man to keep governing once the sovereign grew up — and the
Fujiwara held them, with interruptions, for generations. The technique's master
was Fujiwara no Michinaga, whose daughters became consorts to a succession of
emperors and who therefore ended up grandfather to several. A rival's diary
records him composing a verse at a banquet comparing his own fortune to the full
moon, unobstructed by any cloud. The diarist wrote it down, one suspects,
without much pleasure.

Encouraging early abdication became part of the method. An emperor who retired
in his thirties could be replaced by an infant, which restored the regency, and
retirement carried real compensations of leisure and religious merit. The system
worked until retired emperors noticed that an abdicated sovereign, free of the
ritual paralysis of the throne and still the head of the imperial house, could
simply govern from his own residence. From the late eleventh century that is
what they did, and Fujiwara dominance ended not through defeat but through being
routed around.

## Government as Ceremony

What did these men actually do? A great deal of their labor went into
ceremonies: annual observances, appointments, purifications, banquets,
accessions. This was not idleness. In a state where legitimacy rested on correct
performance and where no written constitution settled disputes, precedent was
law, and knowing precisely how a rite had been conducted forty years earlier was
genuine expertise. Senior nobles therefore kept meticulous diaries in Chinese,
recording the sequence of events, who stood where, what went wrong. They kept
them so their sons and grandsons would not be humiliated. Several of those
diaries survive and remain the spine of what is known about the period.

Layered over the ceremonial calendar was a dense system of avoidance. Yin-yang
specialists attached to the court calculated which directions were unlucky on
which days, so a man might have to travel to an intermediate house overnight
before approaching his destination from an acceptable angle. Contact with death,
blood, or childbirth brought ritual defilement requiring days of seclusion, and
a defiled official could not enter the palace. Dreams were interpreted. Illness
was addressed with esoteric Buddhist ritual as readily as with medicine. None of
this struck anyone as incompatible with administration; it was administration.

## The Woman's Hand

The court's official prose was Chinese — documents, histories, the diaries just
described, and the Buddhist canon. But alongside the borrowed characters a
phonetic script had developed, formed by cursive abbreviation of characters used
for their sound alone, and because educated men were expected to write in
Chinese, this simpler script became known as the woman's hand.

The consequence is one of the genuinely remarkable accidents in literary
history. Women at the Heian court, writing in a script their own culture
regarded as the lesser one, produced the period's major prose. Murasaki Shikibu
wrote a long novel about a prince's love affairs and his descendants that is
psychologically acute in ways European fiction did not attempt for many
centuries. Sei Shonagon assembled a book of observations, lists, and judgments
so sharp that it still reads as a living voice — things that should be small,
things that make the heart beat faster, people who are insufferable at dawn.
Another woman, known to us only as the mother of a man named Michitsuna, wrote
an unsparing account of two decades as the secondary wife of a powerful husband
who mostly did not come.

We do not know most of their given names. They are recorded by nicknames built
from the court offices held by their fathers or husbands, which tells you a
great deal about the position from which they wrote.

Poetry was the other universal medium, and it was not a leisure activity but the
ordinary currency of relations between people. A thirty-one-syllable verse was
the correct way to open a courtship, apologize, mourn, complain about rain, or
acknowledge a gift. What mattered was not only the words but the paper chosen
for them, its color and weight, the handwriting, and the spray of blossom or
autumn grass the letter was attached to. A reply was expected quickly; delay was
itself a message. The imperially commissioned anthology assembled around the
turn of the tenth century fixed the vocabulary of acceptable allusion, and a
courtier who could not place a quotation was as exposed as one who wore spring
colors in the wrong month.

Judgment of this kind was formalized into competition. Courtiers divided into
left and right teams and matched poems against one another round by round, with
a senior figure appointed to declare winners and give reasons — the reasons
being the point, since they established what counted as good. The same format
was applied to paintings, to arrangements of flowers and rocks, and to blended
incense, where participants compounded their own recipes from imported
aromatics and the assembled company ranked them. Scent was taken as seriously as
color. A person's robes carried a blend that was personal and recognizable,
smoke-cured into the silk over a brazier, so that in a dim room an arriving
visitor might be identified before anyone spoke.

## Outside the Walls

While the capital refined itself, the fiscal base underneath it eroded. The
original system of allotted public land and central taxation gave way to private
estates, commended by their holders to tax-exempt patrons — great noble houses
and temples — in exchange for protection. Provincial governorships remained
lucrative precisely because the gap between what a governor collected and what
he forwarded was his own. Meanwhile local landholding families, including
branches of the imperial line demoted to commoner status and sent to the
provinces, were building armed followings to defend estates and collect what
others would not pay.

The court used these men as enforcers and thought of them as instruments. They
were summoned when a rebellion needed suppressing and dismissed when it was
done. But a succession quarrel in the middle of the twelfth century brought
rival warrior bands into the capital to settle the question by force, and the
leader of the winning side discovered that once inside, there was very little
reason to leave. The war that followed between two warrior houses ended with the
victor establishing his government in a coastal town far to the east, deliberately
out of reach of the capital's atmosphere.

The court was not abolished. It kept its ranks, its ceremonies, its calendar, and
its monopoly on legitimacy for another seven hundred years, conferring titles on
warriors who held the actual power and occasionally starving in fine houses with
leaking roofs. What it had built in those four centuries — the anthologies, the
color vocabulary, the assumption that discrimination in small things reveals the
quality of a person — outlasted every government that succeeded it.
