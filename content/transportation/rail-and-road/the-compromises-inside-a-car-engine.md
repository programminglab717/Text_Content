# The Compromises Inside a Car Engine

A gasoline engine idling in traffic is producing perhaps a tenth of the power it was designed to make, burning fuel to fight against its own closed throttle, and wasting most of what it does burn as heat into the radiator and the exhaust. This is not a defect. It is the normal operating condition of nearly every car engine ever built, and the reasons behind it run right through the design of the machine. An engine is not an optimum. It is a set of compromises that have been argued over for more than a century, and most of the interesting engineering is in the arguments rather than in the basic cycle.

The basic cycle is easy. A piston moving down a cylinder with the intake valve open draws in air and fuel. The valve closes and the piston rises, squeezing that charge into a fraction of its original volume. Near the top, a spark lights it; the flame spreads across the chamber in a few thousandths of a second, pressure spikes, and the expanding gas drives the piston down. On the way back up, the exhaust valve opens and the burnt gas is pushed out. Four strokes, two crankshaft revolutions, one power event. Every cylinder does this in turn, spaced evenly around the rotation so that the crankshaft receives a reasonably steady series of shoves.

## Why Compression Ratio Is Everything, Until It Isn't

The single largest lever on efficiency is how much the charge is squeezed, because what actually extracts work from hot gas is expansion. A charge compressed into a small volume has further to expand, and the further it expands before the exhaust valve opens, the more of its heat becomes motion rather than noise and hot exhaust. Raise the compression ratio and efficiency rises with it.

The limit is knock. As the piston compresses the mixture, the unburnt gas ahead of the advancing flame — the end gas, pressed against the far wall of the chamber — is heated by compression as well as by radiation from the flame. If it gets hot enough for long enough, it ignites on its own, all at once, in a detonation that sends pressure waves ringing across the cylinder at a frequency you can hear as a metallic rattle. Sustained knock hammers pistons and burns holes in them.

Everything in the fight against knock is about giving the end gas less time and less heat. Fuels are blended to resist autoignition, and the octane number on the pump is a measure of exactly that resistance and nothing else — high octane is not more energetic fuel, it is more patient fuel. Combustion chambers are made compact and centrally sparked so the flame has less distance to travel. Modern engines inject gasoline directly into the cylinder partly because the fuel evaporating inside the chamber cools the charge by several tens of degrees, buying back a point or two of compression ratio. And every engine carries a knock sensor, effectively a microphone bolted to the block, listening for that ringing and retarding spark timing the instant it appears — which is how a car that wants premium fuel will run on regular, quietly, at a cost in power it never mentions.

## Breathing

An engine is an air pump, and the fuel is almost incidental to the sizing: how much power it makes depends on how much air it can get into the cylinders and how completely it can clear them. At any given speed, a naturally aspirated engine fills each cylinder with rather less than a full atmosphere's worth of air, and the ratio between what it actually takes in and what the swept volume could hold is its volumetric efficiency.

Improving that is the whole point of camshaft design, and it is where the compromises get sharpest. Gas in an intake runner has inertia; if the intake valve is held open past the bottom of the stroke, a fast-moving column of air keeps ramming charge in after the piston has begun to rise. Open the valves earlier, close them later, and overlap the two events so exhaust flow helps scavenge the cylinder, and the engine breathes beautifully at high speed. The same timing at idle lets exhaust gas wash back through the intake, so the engine runs rough, makes no low-speed torque, and stalls at a traffic light. Fixed camshafts force a designer to pick a range of engine speeds to be good at. Variable valve timing — rotating the camshaft relative to the crank, and on more elaborate systems switching between cam profiles or varying the lift continuously — is an attempt to stop having to choose.

Turbocharging sidesteps the whole problem by forcing air in. A turbine in the exhaust stream drives a compressor on the same shaft, recovering energy that would otherwise leave as pressure and heat, and the engine is fed air at well above atmospheric pressure. Since compressing air heats it, an intercooler sits between compressor and engine to cool the charge back down, because hot air is both less dense and more prone to knock. The cost is a turbine that has to spool up before it delivers anything, and a compression ratio that usually has to be lowered to keep detonation in check.

## The Other Family

A diesel does away with the spark and with most of the knock problem by inverting the sequence. It compresses air alone — hard, often to twice the ratio a gasoline engine would tolerate — until the air in the cylinder is hot enough to ignite fuel on contact. Fuel is then sprayed in at very high pressure near the top of the stroke, and burns as it arrives.

Two consequences follow, and they explain why diesels dominated heavy transport. First, the high compression ratio means high expansion and good efficiency. Second, and less obvious, a diesel does not need a throttle. A gasoline engine has to keep its mixture close to the chemically correct ratio, so to make less power it must admit less air, which means strangling the intake and making the pistons work against a partial vacuum on every induction stroke. That pumping loss is why a gasoline engine is at its worst precisely where cars spend most of their time: light load, low speed. A diesel simply injects less fuel into the same full breath of air and runs lean, so it loses almost nothing at part load.

The bill arrives at the tailpipe. Burning fuel in a lean, hot, oxygen-rich cylinder is an efficient way to produce nitrogen oxides, and burning it as a spray means some of it burns in locally rich pockets and leaves soot. Worse, the two problems trade against each other: measures that cool combustion to suppress nitrogen oxides, such as recirculating exhaust gas back into the intake, tend to increase soot, and vice versa. The modern answer is a chain of aftertreatment — a filter that traps soot and periodically burns it off, plus a catalyst fed with urea solution that converts nitrogen oxides to nitrogen and water — and the expense and fragility of that chain is a large part of why diesel passenger cars fell out of favour.

Gasoline engines get an easier deal only because of a chemical coincidence. A three-way catalyst can oxidise carbon monoxide and unburnt hydrocarbons while simultaneously reducing nitrogen oxides, but only within a very narrow band of air-fuel ratio. So the engine runs an oxygen sensor in the exhaust and a closed feedback loop that trims injection constantly, oscillating slightly rich and slightly lean around the stoichiometric point to keep the catalyst in its window. It also explains why the first minute after a cold start matters so much: the catalyst does nothing until it is hot, so engines run enriched and deliberately retarded to dump heat into it as fast as possible.

## Where the Fuel Actually Goes

Of the energy in a tank of gasoline, a well-designed engine delivers something under a third to the crankshaft at its best operating point, and considerably less than that averaged over real driving. The rest leaves as hot exhaust, as heat into the coolant through the cylinder walls, as friction between rings and bores and in every bearing, and as the pumping work of breathing.

Most of the last three decades of development has been attacks on specific items in that list. Direct injection and variable timing chip away at pumping and knock. Cylinder deactivation shuts down half an engine at cruise so the remaining cylinders work harder and less throttled. Thinner oils and low-tension piston rings reduce friction. Hybrids take a different route entirely: let a battery and motor handle the low-load conditions the engine is worst at, and run the engine only near its efficient region, which is why so many hybrids use an Atkinson-style cycle with late intake valve closing — an arrangement that gives up power density for expansion work, and would feel gutless in a conventional car.

None of it escapes the underlying constraint. A heat engine can only convert the fraction of its heat that corresponds to the temperature drop it can exploit, and the top of that range is fixed by what the materials in the combustion chamber will survive. Every gain has come from working the edges of that limit: expanding further, throttling less, losing less to friction, and wasting less of the first cold minute.
