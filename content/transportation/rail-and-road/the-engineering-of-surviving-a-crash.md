# The Engineering of Surviving a Crash

The most counterintuitive fact in vehicle safety is that a car which comes out of a crash looking wrecked has probably done a better job than one that comes out looking solid. For the first half of the automobile's history the opposite belief prevailed. Heavy, rigid construction seemed obviously protective, and manufacturers advertised it. The trouble is that a rigid car stops very quickly when it hits something, and the people inside do not stop with it.

Every serious collision is really two collisions in sequence. The first is the vehicle against whatever it struck. The second, a few hundredths of a second later, is the occupant against the vehicle's interior — or, if they are restrained, against the restraint. There is arguably a third, when the organs inside a decelerating body meet the skeleton that has already stopped. What injures people is not speed, and not really impact. It is the rate at which velocity is removed, and the rate depends on the distance over which the removal happens.

That gives the whole field its governing idea. If a car strikes a wall at fifty kilometres an hour and deforms by half a metre while doing so, the average deceleration is bounded by simple kinematics. Halve the deformation and the deceleration doubles. Since kinetic energy scales with the square of speed, doubling the impact speed means four times as much energy to absorb over the same crushable length — which is why the difference between a survivable crash and an unsurvivable one is often a matter of a few tens of kilometres an hour, and why speed limits do more for occupant survival than any structural change.

## Crumple Zones Are Not Just Soft Ends

Béla Barényi, working at Mercedes-Benz in the 1950s, is the engineer most associated with turning this principle into a design. His patents describe a car divided into three zones: a rigid passenger cell, with deliberately deformable structures ahead of and behind it. The front end becomes an energy-absorbing device; the compartment becomes a survival space that must not collapse.

Getting the front to crush in a controlled way is harder than making it weak. The longitudinal rails that run forward from the firewall are designed to fold like a concertina, with initiator beads or changes in section that tell the metal exactly where to buckle first, so that crush proceeds progressively from the front backwards rather than the whole assembly jack-knifing sideways and doing nothing. The force-versus-distance curve of that crush is tuned: too soft and the structure is used up before the car stops, leaving a hard impact against the engine block; too stiff and the pulse transmitted to the occupant is brutal.

Modern cars also carry multiple load paths, so that the impact force is split between the main rails, an upper path through the shock towers and A-pillars, and often a lower path under the engine. The powertrain itself is mounted so that it is pushed down and under the passenger compartment rather than through the firewall. Meanwhile the cell around the occupants is made of the strongest steel in the car — hot-stamped boron alloys in the B-pillars, roof rails and door beams, with tensile strengths several times that of ordinary body panel steel. Soft outside, hard inside, and the boundary is deliberate.

## The Belt Does Most of the Work

None of the structure helps an occupant who is not attached to it. The three-point belt, developed by Nils Bohlin at Volvo in the late 1950s, works by coupling the torso and pelvis to the decelerating shell so they experience roughly the same deceleration curve, spread over the strongest bony structures available: the pelvis and the rib cage against the collarbone. Volvo made the patent freely available to other manufacturers, which is the rare case of an industry-wide safety improvement moving quickly.

Two refinements matter enormously and are invisible to the user. The first is the pretensioner, a small pyrotechnic or mechanical device that yanks several centimetres of slack out of the belt in the first milliseconds of a crash, before the occupant has begun to move forward. Slack is deadly: any distance travelled before the belt takes up is distance during which the body is accelerating relative to the car, so it arrives at the restraint already moving.

The second is the load limiter, which does the opposite, and does so on purpose. Once belt tension reaches a designed threshold, a torsion bar in the retractor twists and allows webbing to pay out under controlled force. The occupant is permitted to move forward a little further, which lowers the peak force on the chest — the chest being the thing most likely to fail in an adult, particularly an older one. The belt is not trying to hold you still. It is trying to slow you down over the longest distance available inside the car.

Lap belt geometry has its own failure mode, called submarining, in which the belt rides up off the pelvis onto the abdomen and the occupant slides underneath it. Seat pan shape, anti-submarining ramps built into the cushion, and belt anchorage position all exist to prevent it.

## Airbags Buy Distance

An airbag is often described as a cushion, which undersells what it does. Its function is to give the head and chest a surface to decelerate against that is further forward than the steering wheel and spread over a much larger area, and to do it during the specific window of a few tens of milliseconds when the occupant is moving forward. Inflate too late and it does nothing; inflate too early and it has already begun venting by the time the head arrives. The bag has vent holes precisely so it can absorb energy by expelling gas rather than acting as a trampoline.

Getting the timing right is a sensing problem. Accelerometers feed an algorithm that must distinguish a real frontal crash from a pothole, a curb strike, or a shopping trolley, and commit to firing within a very short window of the first contact — typically well under twenty milliseconds. Once the decision is made a gas generator fills the bag in a small fraction of a second, which is itself violent enough to injure someone sitting too close. That lesson was learned in production during the 1990s, when early aggressive inflators caused serious injuries to children and small adults, and led to multi-stage inflators, occupant weight and position classification, and firmer messaging about where children should sit.

## Why Test Protocols Changed the Cars

Regulatory and consumer test programmes have shaped structures more than almost any internal engineering preference, for the plain reason that a published score moves sales. A flat full-width barrier test loads both rails evenly and rewards a strong, symmetric front end. An offset deformable barrier test, striking only part of the width, loads half the structure with the full mass of the car and is much harder on the passenger cell.

The most instructive example is the small overlap test introduced by the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, which strikes a narrow band at the outer corner — outboard of the main rails entirely. Cars that performed well in every existing test suddenly folded around the barrier, with wheels driven back into the footwell and A-pillars buckling. Manufacturers responded with new structures to catch that corner and, in some designs, features that make the front wheel glance the car sideways away from the obstacle. The test revealed a real-world crash mode that the older protocols simply never loaded.

Measuring injury requires instrumented dummies, which are not mannequins but calibrated mechanical analogues with defined stiffness in the neck, ribs and spine, carrying accelerometers and load cells throughout. Criteria derived from their readings — head acceleration history, chest deflection, femur load, neck tension — stand in for injury risk, and their limitations are well known to the people who use them. A dummy validated against the response of an average adult male does not represent a smaller woman or an elderly passenger, which is a live and legitimate criticism of the whole apparatus.

## The Problem of Everybody Else

Two vehicles colliding share their momentum change, so the lighter one experiences the greater velocity change. Mass disparity between a large SUV and a small hatchback is therefore a direct safety transfer from one occupant to another. Height disparity is worse: if a truck's stiff structure rides above the car's main rails, it bypasses the energy-absorbing hardware entirely and loads the passenger cell directly. Voluntary agreements on bumper and rail height have narrowed the gap, without closing it. Pedestrians face a version of the same problem, and are the reason for bonnet clearance requirements, softer front-end shapes and active hoods that lift on impact.

The current frontier has moved from survival to avoidance. Electronic stability control, which brakes individual wheels to correct a skid, may have prevented more deaths than any structural advance since the seat belt, and automatic emergency braking is following the same path. But the frontal structure cannot be retired, because avoidance systems fail against the crash that arrives from outside their assumptions — at a junction, in fog, at closing speeds no sensor can do anything useful about. The crushable metre ahead of the firewall remains the last argument in the chain.
