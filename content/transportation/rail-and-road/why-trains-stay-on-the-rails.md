# Why Trains Stay on the Rails

Ask most people what keeps a train on the track and they will point at the
flange, the inner lip on each wheel that hangs down past the railhead. It
looks like the answer. It is shaped like the answer. But on a well-maintained
line a train running straight and true barely touches its flanges at all,
and a railway that relied on them would be intolerable — grinding, noisy, and
constantly wearing itself away. Flanges are a backstop, the thing that stops
a bad situation becoming a derailment. The steering is done somewhere else,
by a piece of geometry so simple that it is easy to miss.

Railway wheels are not cylinders. Each tread is machined as a shallow cone,
sloping by something like one in twenty or one in forty, with the larger
diameter on the inside, towards the flange. The two wheels of a wheelset are
pressed onto a common axle and cannot rotate independently of each other.
There is no differential anywhere in the running gear. Those two facts
together — the cone and the rigid axle — are what hold the train on the rails.

## The Cone Does the Steering

Picture a wheelset sitting centrally in the track, each wheel contacting its
rail at the same rolling radius, running perfectly straight. Now nudge it a
centimetre to the right. The right-hand wheel climbs very slightly up its
cone and now contacts the rail on a larger radius; the left-hand wheel slides
down its cone onto a smaller one. Because both are locked to the same axle
they must turn at the same rate, so in one revolution the right-hand wheel
covers more ground than the left. The wheelset yaws to the left and rolls
itself back towards the centre of the track. Push it the other way and the
same mechanism pushes back. It is a self-centring system built entirely out
of the shape of a lathe cut.

The same geometry handles curves, which is the neat part. On a curve the
outer rail is longer than the inner one, and a rigid wheelset has to resolve
that somehow. Centrifugal effect pushes it slightly outward, the outer wheel
finds a larger rolling radius, the inner wheel a smaller one, and the
wheelset steers itself round the bend without anybody asking it to. On gentle
curves this is the whole story: the flanges stay clear and the train takes
the curve on pure rolling contact.

The self-centring is not free of side effects. A restoring force applied to a
mass with inertia does not settle quietly; it overshoots. A wheelset released
off-centre does not creep back to the middle, it weaves — tracing a sinusoid
along the track whose wavelength depends on the wheel radius, the track gauge
and the conicity of the tread. The phenomenon has been understood since the
nineteenth century and the relationship is named after Klingel, who wrote it
down. At low speed the weaving is harmless and damps out. Above a critical
speed it does not damp out. It grows, the wheelset starts slamming flange to
flange, and the ride turns violent. This is hunting, and it is the single
most important constraint on how a bogie is designed.

## The Bogie's Compromise

A modern bogie is a frame carrying two wheelsets, connected to the body
through a second stage of suspension, usually air springs. The connection
between the wheelsets and the frame is where the argument happens. Make the
axles stiff in yaw — hard to twist relative to the frame — and hunting is
suppressed, because a wheelset that cannot yaw cannot oscillate. But a
wheelset that cannot yaw also cannot steer itself into a curve, so it enters
the bend crabbing, slams its leading flange against the outer rail, and
scrubs its way round.

Loosen the yaw restraint and the reverse happens: beautiful curving, and
instability at speed. Every bogie in service is a point chosen on that
trade-off, usually with hydraulic yaw dampers between frame and body to soak
up energy at the frequencies that matter, and often with subtler tricks —
radial steering linkages, or cross-bracing that lets the two wheelsets in a
bogie yaw in opposite directions while resisting them yawing together. Freight
bogies, which have to be cheap and tolerate enormous load variation, lean
towards the crude end and are the reason heavy-haul lines wear their curves
out so quickly.

Track engineers attack the same problem from the other side. Curves are
canted, the outer rail lifted so the resultant of gravity and curving force
points closer to the middle of the track, which keeps the wheelsets from
being pressed hard against the outer rail. Cant is set for a compromise
speed, so a slow freight running on a curve canted for an express leans in
too much and grinds its inner rail, while the express running faster than the
cant allows for — cant deficiency, in the jargon — leans out and loads the
outer one.

## A Contact Patch the Size of a Coin

All of this happens through a contact area no bigger than a small coin. Steel
wheel on steel rail deforms elastically to form an ellipse a centimetre or so
across, and through that patch passes the full weight on the wheel — several
tonnes for a passenger vehicle, considerably more for a loaded freight wagon
— along with every newton of traction and braking.

The pressures involved sit close to the yield strength of the steel, which is
why railheads behave oddly. The surface layer deforms plastically, flows,
work-hardens, and eventually cracks. Rolling contact fatigue on the gauge
corner, where curving wheels press hardest, produces fine crack networks that
grow into the rail and occasionally into a break; a fatal derailment at
Hatfield in England in 2000 came from exactly that mechanism and triggered a
national programme of rail inspection and grinding. Grinding trains now run
routinely, cutting the damaged skin off the railhead before the cracks get
deep, and also restoring the rail's transverse profile so that it meets the
wheel cone where the designer intended.

Wheels get the same treatment in reverse. A tread wears away from its
as-machined cone into a hollow profile shaped by the rails it has been
running on, which changes the effective conicity and therefore the hunting
behaviour, so wheels are turned back to profile on an underfloor lathe long
before they are worn out in any structural sense. A wheel that locks under
braking and slides for a few metres grinds a flat onto the tread; every
revolution thereafter delivers a hammer blow to the rail, and both the flat
and the rail damage it causes are among the loudest things on a railway.

## Why Trains Cannot Stop

The other consequence of steel on steel is that there is almost no grip.
Coefficient of friction between a clean dry wheel and a clean dry rail is
somewhere around a fifth — good enough, but a fraction of what a rubber tyre
manages on asphalt. Wet it, and the figure falls. Contaminate it and the
figure collapses.

Autumn leaves are the notorious case, and they are not a joke. Leaves drawn
under the wheels are crushed and cooked by contact pressure into a thin black
film bonded to the railhead, tough, slick, and startlingly persistent. Trains
lose adhesion braking into stations and slide through them; they lose it
accelerating away and spin their wheels. Railways answer with sanders on the
trains and with railhead treatment trains that blast the film off with
high-pressure water and lay down a sticky adhesion-modifying gel behind them.

Even with good adhesion, the force at the contact patch is not transmitted by
pure rolling. A driven wheel turns very slightly faster than its forward
motion would require, and a braked wheel slightly slower. That small relative
motion is called creep, and the tangential force grows with it, steeply at
first and then flattening off as the contact patch progressively slips. Push
past the top of that creep curve and the wheel spins or slides. Modern
traction control lives on the rising part of the curve deliberately, holding
a percentage or two of creep to extract maximum force, backing off the moment
the slope goes flat.

When a train does come off, the mechanism is usually a flange climbing rather
than a flange failing. The criterion, worked out by the French engineer Nadal
more than a century ago, compares the lateral force pushing a wheel outward
against the vertical force holding it down. If the ratio between them exceeds
roughly what the flange angle and friction can resist, the flange rides up
the face of the rail and over. Everything that reduces the vertical load
makes this easier — a twist in the track that momentarily unweights one
wheel, a poorly loaded wagon, a vehicle bouncing on worn suspension — which
is why track geometry cars measure twist obsessively, and why a derailment
report so often turns out to be about several small things arriving at the
same place at once rather than one large one.
