# What Actually Makes a Railway Fast

The Tokaido Shinkansen opened in October 1964, a few days before the Tokyo
Olympics, and it did something that in retrospect looks obvious and at the
time looked eccentric: it abandoned the existing railway entirely. Japan's
network was built to a narrow gauge of three feet six inches, its curves
tight, its alignment threaded through valleys and towns, and the corridor
between Tokyo and Osaka had simply run out of capacity. Rather than upgrade
it, the national railway built a second railway alongside — standard gauge,
no level crossings, no freight, generous curves, its own electrification and
its own signalling, sharing nothing with the old line but the cities at each
end.

That decision, more than any train, is what high-speed rail means. Speed is
not really a property of rolling stock. It is a property of an alignment. A
train capable of three hundred kilometres per hour on a French high-speed
line becomes a very ordinary train the moment it runs onto a Victorian
main line, because the curves, the signalling and the structures will not let
it do anything else. The expensive, slow, politically difficult part of going
fast is the earthworks.

## Geometry Sets the Price

The binding constraint is lateral acceleration in curves, and it scales
badly. The sideways force a passenger feels goes up with the square of speed,
so doubling the speed on a given curve quadruples it. Railways buy some of
that back with cant, tilting the track so that gravity leans the train into
the bend, but cant is bounded: too much and a train stopped on the curve, or
crawling through it, is uncomfortable and eventually unsafe for freight.
Beyond cant lies cant deficiency, the residual sideways force the passengers
actually feel, and that is limited by what people will tolerate with a coffee
in front of them.

Work the numbers through and a line built for three hundred kilometres per
hour needs curve radii in the range of five to seven kilometres. That is an
almost straight line drawn across a landscape that is rarely obliging. It
means viaducts across valleys that a conventional railway would have followed,
tunnels through hills that a conventional railway would have skirted, and a
route that cuts across the grain of field boundaries, villages and everybody's
property. Vertical geometry matters too: crests and dips need long transitions
or the train pitches unpleasantly, and the aerodynamic loads on a train
cresting a summit at speed are not trivial.

Different countries made different bargains here. France, building the first
TGV line from Paris towards Lyon at the end of the 1970s, accepted steep
gradients — far steeper than classic practice — on the reasoning that a
powerful train arriving at the foot of a bank at close to three hundred
kilometres per hour can trade momentum for height and barely notice. Steep
gradients let the line follow the ground rather than tunnelling through it,
which made it dramatically cheaper. The catch is that such a line is useless
to freight, which cannot climb it. Germany's newer lines were built with
gentler gradients precisely so that freight could use them at night, which
cost more in tunnelling and brought its own problems: mixing 300 km/h
passenger trains with 100 km/h freight on the same track wastes capacity,
because the fast trains spend their time catching the slow ones.

The French also decided, importantly, that the high-speed line did not need
to reach the city centre. TGVs run onto the classic network for the last
stretch into Paris and Lyon at ordinary speeds. That single compromise saved
an enormous amount of urban construction and is a large part of why the French
network grew as fast as it did. The Japanese, whose legacy lines were a
different gauge, had no such option and had to build all the way in.

## What the Train Has to Deal With

At low speed, most of the resistance a train overcomes is mechanical —
rolling resistance at the wheel, friction in bearings. By three hundred
kilometres per hour that is a minor term. Aerodynamic drag rises with the
square of speed and the power needed to overcome it with the cube, which is
why the step from 200 to 300 costs so much more than the step from 100 to 200.
A high-speed trainset is consequently shaped with much more care than its
running gear would suggest: a long tapered nose, flush glazing, skirts over
the bogies, inter-car gaps sealed with fairings, and the pantograph sunk into
a recess with an acoustic shroud around it.

Some of that shaping is not about drag at all. When a train enters a tunnel
at speed it pushes a pressure wave ahead of it down the bore. The wave travels
at the speed of sound, reaches the far portal, and bursts out as a bang loud
enough to alarm anybody living nearby — the tunnel boom, or micro-pressure
wave. The classic fix is a very long, gently tapering nose that builds the
pressure gradually rather than slamming it, plus flared hoods at tunnel
portals and a tunnel cross-section considerably larger than the train
requires for clearance. The engineer Eiji Nakatsu, a keen birdwatcher, is
widely credited with drawing on the kingfisher's bill when shaping one of the
Japanese noses for exactly this problem, which is the rare case of
biomimicry that actually happened rather than being invented afterwards.

Power delivery divides the field. The French approach put the traction in
dedicated power cars at each end, with unpowered coaches between — simple,
keeps the noise and machinery away from passengers, but concentrates weight
and limits total adhesion to four driven axles at each end. The Japanese
approach, later adopted by the German ICE3 family and its relatives, spreads
the traction motors along the train. Distributed power gives lower axle loads,
which is gentler on the track and which matters a great deal in a country
that builds on soft ground and worries about earthquakes; it also gives far
more adhesive weight for acceleration and for regenerative braking. Almost
every new design now distributes the power.

Electrification is not optional at these speeds. A high-speed trainset draws
several megawatts continuously, and the practical way to deliver that through
a single sliding contact is high voltage alternating current — twenty-five
kilovolts at industrial frequency, fed from substations spaced tens of
kilometres apart. The overhead wire itself becomes an engineering problem: the
pantograph excites a travelling wave in the contact wire, and if the train
approaches the speed at which that wave propagates the wire begins to whip and
the contact breaks up. High-speed overhead is therefore tensioned far harder
than ordinary catenary, with tighter tolerances on height and stagger.

## The Rest of the System

Signalling disappears from the lineside, because a driver cannot read a
coloured light that is in view for a second and a half; the authority to
proceed is displayed in the cab. Track is continuously welded with no joints,
laid either on deep ballast with heavy concrete sleepers or on concrete slab,
the latter costing more up front and much less to maintain. Ballast has an
unexpected failure mode at very high speed — stones lifted by the airflow
under the train and thrown against the underframe — which is one reason
Japanese and Chinese lines favour slab.

Then come the hazards that only become visible once the speeds are high
enough. Crosswinds on exposed viaducts. Snow, which on the Tokaido line is
suppressed with sprinklers that wet the ballast so it cannot be picked up.
Earthquakes, answered with seismometers placed ahead of the line that detect
the fast-travelling primary wave and cut traction power before the damaging
wave arrives. Intrusion onto the track, which is why every metre of a
high-speed railway is fenced and every road crosses over or under.

## What It Is Actually For

The case for building all this is narrower than its advocates usually admit
and stronger than its critics allow. High-speed rail wins decisively against
air travel on city pairs where the door-to-door journey lands somewhere around
two to three hours, because the train delivers you to the middle of the city
and the aeroplane does not. Beyond four hours the advantage erodes; below an
hour or so, ordinary rail was probably good enough. It needs dense population
at both ends and preferably something useful in between, because intermediate
stations are what make the economics work and also what slow the service down,
which is why fast lines run a mix of stopping and non-stop patterns.

And the headline speeds are, to a degree, theatre. A French train touched just
over 570 kilometres per hour in 2007, but it did so on a new line with the
voltage raised, the train shortened to three cars between two power units, and
larger wheels fitted — a laboratory result, not a timetable. Commercial
services settle around 300 to 350 because beyond that the energy consumption,
the track wear, the noise and the maintenance windows all start winning the
argument. The interesting engineering has largely moved off the peak speed
and onto reliability, capacity and how quickly a line can be inspected and
handed back at four in the morning.
