# The Working Life of a Steam Locomotive

Long before the first train of the day, someone has to light the fire. A cold locomotive holds several tons of water in a boiler wrapped around an iron firebox, and that water has to be brought from ambient temperature to something near two hundred degrees Celsius under pressure, slowly enough that the thick plates and the hundreds of tubes running through them expand together instead of tearing at their joints. Raising steam properly takes hours. On a busy shed it began in the middle of the night, with a fire of oily rags and wood chips spread thin across the grate, and a man watching the pressure gauge creep up while the whole machine ticked and knocked as its metal grew.

That long warm-up is the first thing to understand about the steam locomotive, because it explains a great deal about why the type eventually lost. But it is also the beginning of a machine whose logic is unusually visible. Almost nothing on a steam locomotive is hidden. You can stand on the platform and watch every major subsystem do its job.

## A Fire in a Box of Water

The boiler is a fire-tube design, and the phrase is literal. Combustion happens in the firebox at the rear, a steel box whose walls, roof and back are all double, with water filling the gap between the inner and outer sheets. Those two sheets are held apart at a fixed distance by hundreds of short threaded stays, because without them the pressure inside would balloon the flat surfaces outward like a bicycle tube. From the front of the firebox, the hot gas does not escape straight up a chimney. It is drawn forward through a nest of tubes — a few hundred of them, running the length of the barrel and surrounded on all sides by water — and only then into the smokebox at the front and out of the stack.

That arrangement puts an enormous amount of heated surface in contact with the water for the size of the vessel, which is why a locomotive boiler can produce steam at a rate that would embarrass a stationary plant of the same weight. It also creates the machine's single most dangerous failure mode. The crown sheet, the flat top of the inner firebox, is cooled only by the water standing above it. Let that level drop while the fire is burning hard, and the sheet overheats, softens, and lets go, dumping the entire contents of the boiler into the firebox to flash into steam at once. Locomotives carried fusible plugs — soft metal cores in the crown sheet designed to melt and squirt water onto the fire as a warning — but the real protection was a crew who watched the water glass more attentively than anything else on the footplate.

## The Loop That Makes It Work

The elegant part of the design, and the part that sets a locomotive apart from a stationary engine, is how the fire is kept burning. There is no forced-draft fan. Instead, the steam that has already done its work in the cylinders is piped into the smokebox and blown upward through the chimney out of a nozzle called the blastpipe. Each jet of exhaust dragging up the stack pulls smokebox pressure slightly below atmospheric, which draws hot gas forward through the tubes, which in turn pulls air up through the firebed from beneath the grate.

The consequence is a self-regulating loop. Work the engine harder and the exhaust beats become stronger and more frequent, the draught intensifies, the fire burns fiercer, and the boiler makes more steam to meet the demand. Shut off and the loop stops with it, which is why a standing locomotive needs a small auxiliary blower — a ring of jets fed directly from the boiler — to keep some draught through the tubes and stop the fire from dying or blowing back through the fire door. The sharp bark you hear from an engine starting a heavy train is the same phenomenon a driver can hear as information: four crisp, evenly spaced beats per revolution means the valves are set correctly and both cylinders are pulling their share.

## Cylinders, Valves and the Art of Notching Up

Steam does not push the pistons in one direction. Each cylinder is double-acting, admitting steam alternately to the front and back of the piston, so every stroke does work. Two cylinders set ninety degrees apart on the crank axle guarantee that at least one of them can always produce turning effort, whatever position the wheels happen to have stopped in.

The valve gear is where the driver's skill lives. A valve — a sliding block or, on later engines, a pair of piston heads on a spindle — uncovers the port to admit steam at the start of the stroke and then closes it again partway through. Everything after that point is expansion: the trapped charge continues to push while its pressure falls, doing work it has already been paid for. The moment of closure is called cut-off, and the reversing lever or screw in the cab controls it continuously, using a linkage such as Stephenson's link motion or the later Walschaerts gear.

Starting a train, the driver runs in full gear, admitting steam for nearly the whole stroke: maximum force, terrible economy. Once the train is rolling he winds the reverser back toward the middle, shortening admission to perhaps a fifth or a quarter of the stroke, and eases the regulator open wider. The engine then breathes deeply and expands the steam hard, which is the difference between a locomotive that makes it over the summit on the coal in its bunker and one that does not. The same mechanism, pushed past centre, reverses the engine.

## Getting the Power to the Rail

All that effort is useless without adhesion. Steel on steel offers roughly a tenth of the grip that a rubber tire has on dry asphalt, so tractive effort is limited not by what the cylinders can produce but by the weight sitting on the driving wheels. This is why locomotives grew coupled wheels — two, three, four or more axles tied together by side rods so that the weight of the whole engine, not just one axle, resists slipping. The Whyte notation that classifies them simply counts wheels from the front: a 4-6-2 has a four-wheel leading truck to guide it into curves at speed, six coupled driving wheels, and a two-wheel trailing truck under the cab so the firebox can spread out wider than the frames.

Coupling rods and pistons bring their own problem. Those masses reciprocate rather than rotate, and balancing them is a compromise with no clean solution: counterweights added to the wheels to smooth the fore-and-aft surging also produce a vertical hammer blow that pounds the rail once per revolution, worse at speed. Track engineers and locomotive engineers argued about the limits for decades, and the argument is one reason why some very fast designs used three or four cylinders, which cancel each other's forces far better than two.

## Water, Coal and the Cost of Both

A locomotive working hard consumes water at a rate that shocks people who have not seen it — tons per hour, far more by weight than coal. Long-distance running therefore meant water stops, and on some main lines it meant troughs laid between the rails so an express could drop a scoop and take on water at speed. Getting that water into a boiler already at high pressure is a small marvel in itself: the injector, a device with no moving parts, uses a cone of steam from the boiler to accelerate a jet of feedwater until its momentum carries it past a check valve into the very vessel the steam came from.

Feeding the fire was harder. Hand firing a large locomotive on a heavy grade is among the most punishing jobs industrial work ever produced, and the largest engines simply exceeded what one man could shovel, which is why mechanical stokers appeared on big American power. Then there was the servicing tail: ash pans to drop, clinker to break out of the grate, char to shovel from the smokebox, and periodic boiler washouts to clear scale, because water left behind its dissolved minerals every time it boiled. A steam locomotive spent a large fraction of its existence being prepared, cleaned and repaired rather than earning money.

Set against a diesel that starts in a minute, runs several days on a tank, and can be driven from the other end of a multiple-unit consist, none of this survived contact with accountants. The thermal efficiency did not help either: even a good superheated locomotive turned only a single-digit percentage of the energy in its coal into work at the drawbar, since most of the heat went up the chimney by the same draught that made the fire burn in the first place.

What kept people attached to them, and keeps preserved engines running on heritage lines, is that the machine tells you what it is doing. Boiler pressure, cut-off, fire condition, the colour at the chimney top, the rhythm of the beats — all of it is legible from the footplate, and none of it is mediated. Drive a steam locomotive badly and it will be visibly, audibly, embarrassingly obvious to everyone standing on the platform.
