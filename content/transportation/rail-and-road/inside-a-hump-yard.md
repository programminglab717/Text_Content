# Inside a Hump Yard

Stand near the crest of a hump yard at night and the dominant sound is not
locomotives. It is a long, rising shriek, repeated every forty seconds or so,
that stops abruptly and is followed by a distant bang. The shriek is a
retarder — a pair of steel jaws clamping the rims of a wagon's wheels as it
rolls past — and the bang is that wagon arriving against the ones already
standing in its assigned track, a quarter of a mile away in the dark. Between
the shriek and the bang, nothing is driving the wagon at all. It is falling
down a hill.

That is the whole idea. A hump yard is a machine for taking a freight train
apart and putting a different one together, and the energy it uses to move
individual wagons around is mostly gravitational. Somebody in the nineteenth
century worked out that if you build an artificial hill, shove a train slowly
over the top, and uncouple it as it goes, the wagons will separate themselves
and run away downhill, and all you have to do is point each one at the right
track and slow it down enough that it does not destroy whatever it hits.

## Why Freight Needs Sorting at All

A passenger train is a fixed thing. It leaves as a unit, arrives as a unit,
and turns around. A conventional freight train is nothing of the kind. It is
an assembly of wagons that boarded at different sidings, belong to different
customers, and are going to different places, and at some point that
assembly has to be dismantled and recombined with wagons from other trains
heading the same way. This is what the industry calls classification, and
until very recently it was the defining activity of a freight railway.

Small yards do it flat. A locomotive stands at one end of a fan of tracks and
shunts wagons about, either by pushing a cut into a track and stopping, or by
kicking — accelerating, uncoupling on the move, braking hard and letting the
wagon roll on by itself. Flat switching is flexible and works anywhere, and
it is slow. Every wagon or cut requires the locomotive to reverse out and
come back, so the throughput is set by how fast a shunting engine can change
direction, which is not fast.

The hump removes the locomotive from the inner loop. The train is drawn into
a receiving yard, the air is released from the brake pipe so the wagons roll
freely, an inspector walks the length checking for defects, and the couplings
are prepared according to a cut list generated from the train's manifest. A
hump engine then pushes the whole train, slowly and steadily, up the incline
towards the crest. At the top, each cut is uncoupled as it tips over the
summit. Gravity does the rest, and the engine never stops pushing.

## The Bowl and the Brakes

Past the crest the track fans out through a cascade of points into the
classification bowl — a wide, shallow set of parallel sidings, commonly
thirty or forty of them in a large yard, each assigned to a destination or a
block of destinations. Each cut has to be routed through a sequence of points
that are thrown ahead of it as it approaches, one set after another, with a
second or two of margin between cuts. Early yards did this with a man in a
tower and a bank of levers, and the timing was a craft. Automation arrived in
the middle of the twentieth century and the routing has been machine-driven
ever since.

Speed control is the harder half of the problem. A wagon must arrive against
the standing cars gently enough not to damage the lading — a few miles per
hour is the usual target, which is about walking pace — but it must also
reach them, and the far end of a nearly empty bowl track may be three
thousand feet from the crest. Getting that right means predicting how
freely each individual wagon will roll, and wagons differ enormously. A loaded
hopper carries momentum that an empty flat does not. Cold bearings on a
frosty night drag; a warm wagon that has been running all day does not. A
headwind on the bowl can strip a surprising amount of speed off a light
empty. Curve resistance through the points depends on which track the cut is
headed for.

So the retarders are not simply brakes, they are the output of a continuous
calculation. A master retarder on the hump lead takes the first bite, sizing
each cut's speed to the distance it must cover; group retarders further down
trim it per track. Radar measures the actual speed leaving each retarder,
wheel sensors time the cut between fixed points, and the control system
compares what the wagon did against what it predicted and corrects. Some
yards add mechanical retarders spread along the bowl tracks themselves,
small hydraulic units set into the rail that absorb energy from anything
rolling faster than a set speed and leave slower wagons alone.

Not everything can go over a hump, and the ease of the whole operation turns
on what kind of coupler the wagons carry. North American stock uses knuckle
couplers that lock automatically on impact and are released by lifting a pin,
so a worker at the crest — or, in modern yards, a mechanical pin puller —
can separate a cut with a single motion while the train is still moving.
European wagons inherited buffers and a screw coupling that has to be lifted
off its hook by hand, which is slower, more dangerous, and a large part of
why European yards never mechanised the crest to the same degree and why the
continent has spent decades arguing about a common automatic coupler. Beyond
that, individual vehicles are often barred from the hump and placarded to say
so: tank cars carrying certain commodities, loads that span more than one
wagon, vehicles with long cushioned underframes that behave unpredictably in
a retarder, and anything with a person riding on it. Those have to be taken
round the hump and set into their tracks by a switching locomotive, which is
precisely the slow flat move the hump exists to avoid.

At the far end, a trim crew — a locomotive working the pull-down end of the
bowl — couples the accumulated cuts into outbound trains, draws them into the
departure yard, connects the air, and carries out a brake test before the road
engine comes on. Between the humping and the departure, a wagon might sit for
a few hours or the better part of a day, waiting for enough traffic to
justify a train.

## The Blocking Plan Is the Real Machine

The hump is the visible part, but the intelligence of a freight network lives
in something less photogenic: the blocking plan. Each yard has a finite number
of bowl tracks, and each track can hold one group of wagons destined for one
place. Since a network serves thousands of destinations and no yard has
thousands of tracks, wagons are sorted into blocks that are themselves bound
for other yards, where they will be sorted again.

Every re-sort costs time. A wagon that is classified three times on its
journey spends far more of its life sitting in bowls than moving, and freight
railways have always known that their weakness against a truck is not speed
in transit but dwell in yards. The blocking plan is the network-wide decision
about which destinations to group where, and adjusting it is a genuinely hard
optimisation: pre-blocking a long way out saves a sort later but wastes
capacity if the volume is not there, while sorting late keeps trains full and
adds a day.

The most profitable answer, everywhere it can be arranged, is to skip the
yards entirely. A unit train — a full trainload of one commodity from one
origin to one destination, coal, grain, ore, crude oil — never gets
classified at all. Intermodal does something similar with containers, which
are sorted by gantry crane at terminals rather than by gravity in a bowl, and
which travel in trains that run point to point on a schedule.

## What Happened to the Humps

Both of those trends have been eating the hump yard's lunch for decades. In
North America the retrenchment became explicit in the 2010s when the operating
philosophy that came to be called precision scheduled railroading pushed hard
towards fewer, longer, more regular trains and away from carload traffic that
needed sorting. Humps were switched off in numbers; some yards were downgraded
to flat switching, others closed outright, and the tall retarder towers came
down. In Europe, the single-wagonload networks that once made it possible to
send three wagons of anything to anywhere shrank for the same reasons, and
several national operators came close to abandoning them.

What survives is worth looking at properly, because a working hump yard is one
of the last large industrial processes where gravity does the heavy lifting
and the machinery exists mainly to regulate it. The instrumentation around the
edges has improved beyond recognition — hot bearing detectors and dragging
equipment detectors placed on the approach lines, acoustic bearing monitors,
automated optical inspection portals photographing every wagon as it enters —
but the core of it would be legible to a yardmaster from a century ago. Push
the train over the hill. Pull the pins. Aim each wagon and slow it down.
