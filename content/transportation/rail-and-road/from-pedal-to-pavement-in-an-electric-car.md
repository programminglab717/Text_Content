# From Pedal to Pavement in an Electric Car

Press the accelerator of an electric car and the response arrives with no preliminaries. There is no rising engine note, no gear selection, no moment while a turbine spools. What you feel instead is a sensor under your foot reporting a position, a computer converting that into a torque request, and a set of transistors switching hundreds of amps of direct current into three alternating phases, all of it settling out in a few thousandths of a second. The drivetrain that does this contains fewer moving parts than a manual gearbox, and understanding it is mostly a matter of following the energy from the pack to the tire contact patch.

## The Pack Is a Structure, Not a Box

A traction battery starts with cells — cylinders the size of a small flashlight, flat pouches, or rigid prismatic cans — and the individual cell is a low-voltage device, delivering something in the region of three and a half to four volts depending on chemistry and charge state. Hundreds are wired in series to reach a pack voltage of roughly three hundred and fifty to four hundred volts, or twice that in the newer high-voltage architectures, and groups are paralleled to reach the capacity. The pack is then a structural member in its own right, a flat slab bolted into the floor of the car, sealed against water, and increasingly doing double duty as part of the body's stiffness and side-impact protection.

Governing it is the battery management system, which is less dramatic than it sounds and more important than almost anything else in the car. It monitors the voltage of every series group and the temperature at many points, estimates state of charge from a combination of current integration and voltage behaviour, and enforces limits on how much power may be drawn or accepted at any moment. It also balances: because cells age at slightly different rates, the pack's usable capacity is set by its weakest member, and balancing circuits bleed or shuffle small amounts of charge to keep them in step. Every serious pack also keeps a reserve at each end of the range that the driver never sees, since a lithium-ion cell driven to a true full charge or a true empty degrades far faster than one kept in the middle.

Connecting all this to the rest of the car are contactors — heavy relays that physically isolate the pack whenever the vehicle is off or a crash sensor fires. They close in a sequence, through a precharge resistor first, because slamming a four-hundred-volt supply into the inverter's empty capacitors would produce an inrush current capable of welding the contacts shut.

## The Inverter Does the Real Work

The motor runs on alternating current; the battery supplies direct current. Between them sits the inverter, three pairs of high-power semiconductor switches that chop the DC supply on and off tens of thousands of times a second, varying the proportion of on-time in a pattern that makes each phase output approximate a sine wave. Change the frequency of that synthesised wave and the motor's speed changes; change the amplitude and phase relative to the rotor and the torque changes.

Doing this well requires knowing exactly where the rotor is, at all times, which is why there is a resolver or encoder on the motor shaft. The control scheme — field-oriented control, in the literature — mathematically transforms the three measured phase currents into two components in a reference frame that rotates with the rotor: one aligned with the magnetic field, one at right angles to it. The component at right angles produces torque, and the controller simply regulates it to whatever the pedal asked for. It is a clean abstraction, and it is what gives an electric drive its characteristic precision. The audible whine in a quiet cabin is the switching frequency and its harmonics shaking the motor laminations.

Most inverters historically used silicon IGBTs. The shift to silicon carbide devices matters for a mundane reason: they switch faster and drop less voltage when conducting, so a few percent less of the pack's energy turns into heat in the power electronics, which shows up directly as range.

## Two Kinds of Motor, and Almost No Gearbox

Two motor types dominate. The permanent-magnet synchronous machine has rare-earth magnets bonded into or buried within the rotor; it is compact, efficient across a wide range, and delivers excellent torque per kilogram. The induction machine has no magnets at all — the rotor is a cage of conductors in which the stator's rotating field induces its own currents — which makes it cheaper and free of supply-chain exposure to rare earths, and gives it one genuinely useful trait: with no excitation applied, it spins freely and produces essentially no drag. Cars with a motor on each axle sometimes use one of each, running the efficient permanent-magnet unit at cruise while the induction motor idles harmlessly, and bringing it in only for hard acceleration or when grip demands it.

Above a certain speed, a permanent-magnet motor's own magnets become a problem: the voltage they generate as the rotor spins approaches the pack voltage, and there is nothing left to push current with. The controller handles this by field weakening, injecting current that partially opposes the magnet flux, trading torque for speed. The practical result is a machine that produces flat maximum torque from a standstill up to some corner speed, then roughly constant power beyond it.

That torque curve is why electric cars need almost no transmission. A combustion engine makes nothing below idle and must be kept within a narrow useful band, so it needs a clutch and half a dozen ratios. An electric motor covers the whole road-speed range on one fixed reduction — typically somewhere around a tenth of motor speed at the wheels — feeding a conventional differential. A handful of performance cars fit a two-speed unit to get both fierce launch torque and high top speed, but they are the exception, and the complexity rarely pays for itself.

## Braking That Puts Energy Back

Run the same machine as a generator and the flow reverses: the wheels turn the rotor, the inverter's switches steer the resulting current back into the pack, and the car decelerates. Recovering even a moderate share of the kinetic energy of city driving is a substantial part of why electric cars do better in traffic than on the motorway — the exact inverse of a combustion car.

The subtlety is blending. Regeneration is limited by several things at once: the motor's torque, the inverter's current rating, and above all what the battery will accept, which collapses when the pack is cold or already near full. A driver pressing the brake pedal expects the same deceleration every time regardless, so a modern brake system decouples pedal feel from the hydraulics, reads the driver's demand, takes as much of it as possible from the motor, and quietly makes up the rest with friction. Done well it is undetectable. A side effect is that brake pads and discs on an electric car see so little use that corrosion, not wear, becomes the reason to replace them.

## Heat Is the Constraint on Charging

Charging from an ordinary outlet or a wallbox sends alternating current to an onboard charger, which rectifies it and feeds the pack, typically at a rate measured in single-digit or low double-digit kilowatts. DC fast charging bypasses that entirely: the station itself does the conversion and hands the car direct current at pack voltage, with the car's management system dictating how much it will take from moment to moment.

What it will take depends almost entirely on temperature and state of charge. A cold pack cannot absorb high currents without risking lithium plating on the anode — metallic lithium depositing where it should have intercalated, permanently reducing capacity — so the car limits the rate until its heaters and coolant loops bring the cells into range. This is why cars with route planning preheat the battery on the way to a charger. As the pack fills, acceptance tapers steeply, which is why the last portion of a charge takes disproportionately long and why long-distance driving on fast chargers is usually quickest in repeated short sessions from low to moderate charge rather than one long fill.

Raising pack voltage from four hundred to eight hundred is largely a response to the same constraint. Power is voltage times current, and it is current that heats cables, connectors and busbars, so doubling the voltage allows the same charging power through half the current and much less waste heat, at the price of more expensive semiconductors and a car that must be able to talk sensibly to chargers built for the lower standard.

Everything downstream of the cells is, by the standards of a combustion drivetrain, remarkably efficient: the large majority of what leaves the pack arrives at the wheels. Which means that once the drivetrain is settled, the remaining engineering argument moves elsewhere — to aerodynamic drag, which dominates at highway speed, to mass, which the pack itself contributes generously to, and to the rolling resistance of tires that now have to carry several hundred extra kilograms without wearing out in a season.
