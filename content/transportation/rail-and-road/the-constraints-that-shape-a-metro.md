# The Constraints That Shape a Metro

The first underground railway in the world opened in London in January 1863,
running between Paddington and Farringdon, and it was hauled by steam
locomotives. This sounds like a category error, and to the passengers it
largely was. The engines were fitted with condensing apparatus meant to divert
exhaust into water tanks rather than into the tunnel, the arrangement worked
imperfectly, and contemporary accounts of the atmosphere in the tunnels are
uniformly grim. The railway was nonetheless an immediate success, because the
streets above it were worse.

Almost everything distinctive about metro engineering comes from that
situation — a railway forced underground by the cost of surface land, and then
forced to solve a series of problems that only exist underground. The trains
are the easy part. The tunnel, the station, the air and the timetable are
where the difficulty lives.

## Two Ways to Get Under a City

The Metropolitan was built by cut and cover: dig a deep trench along the line
of an existing street, build brick retaining walls and a roof, backfill, and
put the road back. It is conceptually simple and, for shallow work, cheap. Its
drawbacks are the ones you would guess. The street above is destroyed for the
duration, every buried pipe and sewer has to be found and moved, and the
tunnel has to follow the street, because digging under buildings from above
means demolishing them. This is why so many older metro lines trace the road
plan of their cities and turn awkward corners that no railway engineer would
have chosen.

The alternative arrived in 1890, when the City and South London Railway opened
the first deep-level electric underground line, bored through London clay
behind a tunnelling shield and lined with segmental cast iron. Going deep
freed the alignment from the street entirely. It also forced electrification,
because nobody was going to run a steam locomotive through a tube barely
larger than the train, and the C&SLR's cramped little carriages with their
tiny high windows earned the nickname padded cells.

That bore diameter turned out to be one of the most consequential decisions in
the system's history. London's deep tubes were driven small to save money, and
the loading gauge they fixed is still the gauge of the trains running in them
today — which is why tube stock has a curved roof that intrudes on the seating
at the edges, why the trains are hot, and why fitting anything new inside them,
air conditioning in particular, has been an intractable problem for decades.
Later systems bored bigger. The choice made in the 1880s is still being paid
for.

Modern tunnelling has industrialised the deep option. A tunnel boring machine
supports the face with pressurised soil or bentonite slurry, cuts forward,
and erects precast concrete rings behind itself, grouting the annulus as it
goes. The machine can drive under buildings, provided the settlement it
causes is kept within tolerance; sensitive structures are monitored
continuously and, where necessary, protected by compensation grouting —
injecting grout into the ground between the tunnel and the foundation to jack
the building back up as fast as the tunnelling lets it down.

What has not industrialised is stations. A running tunnel is a repetitive
process with a machine designed for it. A station is a large irregular cavern
with platforms, cross-passages, escalator inclines, ventilation shafts,
plant rooms, and a ticket hall that has to surface somewhere in a dense city
where land is expensive and the owners are not necessarily willing. On a new
deep line the stations, not the tunnels, dominate the cost and the programme.

## Heat, Air and Current

A metro tunnel is a long thermal accumulator. Nearly all the energy a train
uses ends up as heat in the tunnel — traction losses, friction, brake heat,
lighting, and passengers, who radiate more than people expect in a crowd.
When the London tubes were new, the surrounding clay was cold and absorbed
that heat happily; the system was advertised as a cool place to travel in
summer. A century of operation has warmed the ground around the tunnels to the
point that it no longer takes much, and the temperature in the tunnels
themselves has drifted upward accordingly. Regenerative braking helps a great
deal, since a train that returns energy to the supply rather than dumping it
into resistors is not heating the tunnel with it. Ventilation helps too, but
shafts need land at the surface, and in a built-up city every shaft is a
planning battle.

The same shafts carry a more urgent duty. Underground fire is the scenario
that governs metro design, and it has done ever since a sequence of serious
fires in the twentieth century forced the issue. Smoke control, non-combustible
finishes, protected escape routes, escalator machinery access and the
discipline about what may be stored in a station are all downstream of those
events.

Power delivery underground almost always means a conductor rail rather than
overhead wire, simply because a third rail needs no headroom and headroom is
what a bored tunnel does not have. The voltage is correspondingly modest — a
few hundred volts direct current — which limits speed and means substations
every mile or two. London does something unusual and uses four rails, with a
separate negative rail in the middle of the track instead of using the running
rails for the return current. The reason was corrosion: stray return current
leaking into the ground was eating the cast-iron water and gas mains that the
Victorian city was plumbed with, and insulating the return circuit from the
earth was the fix.

## Capacity Is Decided on the Platform

The theoretical capacity of a line is easy to state — the number of people a
train holds multiplied by the number of trains per hour. In practice the
second term is set by whichever constraint bites first, and it is usually not
the signalling.

Modern communications-based train control can hold trains ninety seconds
apart, and drives them automatically to do it, which is why many recent lines
run without drivers at all and why the retrofit of such systems onto old lines
has been the main way of squeezing more capacity out of existing tunnels. But
a ninety-second headway only survives if every train stops for a predictable
and short time at every station. Dwell time is the real governor. It is set by
how long it takes for the doors to open, for a crowded platform to exchange
with a crowded train, and for the doors to close against people who want them
not to — and it degrades catastrophically under load, because the busier the
station, the longer the exchange, and the longer the exchange, the more people
accumulate on the platform behind it.

Almost every visible feature of metro rolling stock is an answer to dwell
time. Wide double doors, lots of them, at close spacing. Longitudinal seating
along the sides to leave a clear standing aisle. Open gangways between cars so
that passengers redistribute themselves while moving. Platform edge doors,
which keep people off the track, allow the platform to be air-conditioned
separately from the tunnel, and enforce orderly boarding positions. Stepping
distances kept small, which is harder than it sounds on a curved platform in
a bored tunnel.

Then there is the terminus, which is where many lines actually run out of
capacity. A train arriving at the end of the line must be emptied, the driver
must change ends or the automatic system must reverse the train, and it must
cross to the opposite track before the next one arrives. A single crossover
beyond the platforms can throttle an entire line. The good solutions are
expensive: two terminal platforms with independent approaches, sidings past
the station so trains can clear the platform immediately, or a turning loop
that avoids reversing altogether.

## The Shape of the Network

Beyond the engineering, metro systems are shaped by a decision about topology.
The simplest pattern is radial — lines converging on the centre from the
suburbs — which is efficient for commuting and terrible for travel between
outer districts, and which concentrates every interchange in the most
expensive square mile. Orbital lines fix this and are historically hard to
justify, because the demand for any single orbital journey is small even when
the total is large.

The other question is whether lines terminate in the centre or run through it.
Terminating wastes the most valuable platform capacity on reversing trains.
Through-running, as the Paris RER, the German S-Bahn pattern and London's
Elizabeth line all do, turns two suburban railways into one line and doubles
the usefulness of both — at the price of a tunnel across the centre and the
operational fragility that comes with a delay at one end propagating to the
other. That trade-off, between the efficiency of joining things up and the
resilience of keeping them separate, is argued over in every city that has
ever considered digging.
