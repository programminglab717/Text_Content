# Ship Stability and the Physics of Staying Upright

The warship Vasa sailed less than a nautical mile. She left her moorings in Stockholm in the summer of 1628, caught a gust off the bluffs, heeled far enough to put her open lower gunports under water, and went down in full view of the crowd that had come to watch her leave. She was recovered from the harbour mud more than three centuries later, remarkably intact, and her hull has since been measured in every dimension anyone could think of. The verdict is unglamorous. She was too narrow for her height, carried two gun decks where the structure and the ballast suited one, and had nowhere near enough stone in her bottom to hold the whole assembly down. A ship that floats perfectly well at rest can still be incapable of standing back up once something pushes it over, and the difference between the two conditions is the entire subject of stability.

## Displacement, and the Buoyancy You Are Not Using

Floating is the easy part. A hull settles until the weight of water it has pushed aside equals its own weight, which is why naval architects speak of a ship's displacement rather than its weight — the two are the same number, and the water measures it for you. Seawater runs about two and a half percent denser than fresh, so the same ship floats a few centimetres deeper leaving a river port than it does at sea, and the load line marks painted on the side of every merchant hull account for exactly that, with separate lines for fresh water, tropical water, summer and winter salt water, and the North Atlantic in winter.

What those marks really regulate is freeboard: the height of watertight hull left above the surface. That volume is reserve buoyancy, and it is not idle. It is what lets a hull rise to an oncoming sea instead of driving through it, and what keeps a ship afloat when a compartment fills. A vessel loaded to its marks with a hold flooded is relying on the unflooded volume elsewhere to take up the slack. Freeboard also sets the height of the deck edge, and the angle at which the deck edge goes under is one of the practical limits on how far a hull can heel before it loses the ability to recover.

Stability also has a fore-and-aft dimension, though it rarely causes trouble. A hull is so much longer than it is wide that its longitudinal metacentric height is enormous, typically on the order of the ship's own length, and no ordinary loading will tip a vessel end over end. What shifting weight lengthwise does instead is change trim, the difference between forward and after draught, and the sensitivity is expressed as the moment required to change trim by one centimetre. Trim matters for propeller immersion, for the view from the bridge, and for whether the ship touches bottom in a shallow berth, but the restoring forces behind it are never in doubt.

## The Metacentre

Two forces act on a floating hull, and only two matter for stability. Gravity pulls the whole mass down through the centre of gravity, G, which is the weighted average position of everything aboard — steel, fuel, cargo, crew, the water in the swimming pool. Buoyancy pushes up through the centre of buoyancy, B, the geometric centre of the underwater volume. Sitting upright and undisturbed, the two are vertically aligned and nothing happens.

Heel the hull, and the underwater shape changes: more of the low side is immersed, less of the high side. B, being the centroid of that shape, shifts toward the low side. G does not move at all, provided nothing aboard slides. The two forces are now offset, and they form a couple that tries to rotate the hull back upright. The horizontal distance between them is the righting arm, GZ, and multiplied by the displacement it gives the righting moment in tonne-metres.

For small angles, the upward line through the shifted B crosses the ship's centreline at a point called the metacentre, M, and the height of that point above G — the metacentric height, GM — is the single number most often used as shorthand for stability. Positive GM means M is above G and the couple acts to restore. Negative GM means it acts to capsize. The metacentre's height above the keel depends mostly on the waterplane: broad beam gives a large second moment of area, which puts M high, which is why a raft is hard to tip and a log rolls. That relationship is also why beam is the cheapest stability a designer can buy, and why the Vasa's narrow hull with its tall superstructure was doomed by arithmetic before she was ever rigged.

## The Curve of Statical Stability

GM only describes behaviour in the first few degrees. Push further and the geometry stops being linear: the deck edge immerses, the bilge emerges, and the righting arm stops growing. Plotting GZ against heel angle for a given loading produces the curve of statical stability, and it says far more than a single number can.

A good curve rises steeply at first, peaks somewhere between thirty and fifty degrees, then falls, crossing zero at the angle of vanishing stability. Past that angle the couple reverses sign and the hull keeps going over. The range of positive stability is the span of angles where the ship will still return, and the area under the curve represents energy — the work required to heel the hull to a given angle. That area is what matters in a seaway, because a wave or a gust delivers a dose of energy, not a static push. A hull with a modest peak but a long range can absorb a knock that would roll over a hull with a sharper peak and an earlier zero crossing. International intact stability rules are written largely in terms of these areas, along with a minimum GM and a minimum peak value.

Sailing yachts are the extreme case in both directions. A deep ballast keel keeps G very low and can push the range past 120 degrees, so a knocked-down boat rights itself from her masthead in the water. A wide, shallow multihull has enormous initial stiffness and almost no range at all: immensely stable right side up, and equally stable upside down.

## Stiff, Tender, and the Free Surface

A large GM produces a violent ship. Roll period goes roughly with beam and inversely with the square root of GM, so a stiff vessel snaps back upright with short, hard accelerations that break lashings, shift cargo, and exhaust the crew. A tender ship with small GM rolls slowly and comfortably and has correspondingly less margin. Loading a container vessel is partly an exercise in landing between these, by choosing which boxes go high and which go low and how much ballast to carry, and the same calculation governs whether a stack of containers on deck will survive the passage.

Stability is not a fixed property of a hull, either, because the waterplane that sets the metacentre changes as waves pass. A ship running in a head or following sea with a wave crest amidships is momentarily supported by a narrow slice of hull, with the flared bow and the broad stern overhang lifted clear; in a trough she has the full beam of both ends in the water. Righting ability therefore oscillates at the wave encounter rate, and if that rate happens to be close to twice the ship's natural roll frequency, each half-roll is fed a little energy at exactly the right moment. This is parametric rolling, and it has thrown containers off large box ships that were running in seas nobody on the bridge considered dangerous, sometimes building from nothing to thirty degrees of roll in a handful of cycles. The remedy is to change course or speed and break the timing.

The most efficient way to ruin a stability condition is free surface. Liquid in a partly filled tank runs to the low side as the ship heels, moving weight in exactly the wrong direction. The effect is mathematically identical to raising G, and it depends on the width of the liquid surface rather than the amount of liquid, so a shallow film across a wide space is worse than a deep column in a narrow one. Tanks are therefore subdivided lengthwise, and good practice keeps them either pressed full or empty rather than slack. The same physics has drowned ferries. Water on an undivided vehicle deck, whether from a bow door left open or from firefighting, spreads into a broad sheet a few centimetres deep, and that sheet can take away a hull's entire righting ability in minutes.

When G rises above M the ship does not capsize immediately. It heels to an angle of loll, where the changing hull shape restores a small positive arm, and sits there, flopping to the other side occasionally. It looks like a list, but the cure is the opposite: ballasting the high side to level it will drive the hull straight over. G has to be brought down first, usually by filling low tanks on the side she is already lying on.

## Once the Hull Is Opened

Everything above assumes a watertight hull. Damage stability asks what happens when it isn't. Flooding a compartment adds weight low down, which can help, while simultaneously destroying the waterplane in that compartment, which almost never does. The flooded space also brings its own free surface. A ship is built to survive a specified extent of damage — one compartment, or two — by subdividing the hull with transverse watertight bulkheads carried high enough that the deck they reach stays above water in the damaged condition. The Titanic's bulkheads famously did not, and once enough forward compartments filled, the bow settled far enough for water to spill over the tops of them into the next space in sequence.

Asymmetric flooding is the other hazard, because a list can put the deck edge under before the ship is remotely in danger of sinking. Warships and many ferries carry cross-flooding ducts precisely to let water find its way to the undamaged side and restore an even keel, accepting a deeper draught in exchange for staying upright.

Modern ships carry loading computers that recompute GM, the GZ curve, trim, and hull girder bending moments as cargo and fuel plans change, and the lightship figures they depend on come from an inclining experiment performed at delivery: known weights shifted across the deck, the resulting angle read off a long pendulum, and G located by simple trigonometry. It is an old, cheap, entirely reliable test, and it is still the foundation under all the software.
