# Shipbuilding from Keel to Launch

Steel arrives at a shipyard looking like nothing in particular: stacks of mill plate on a lay-down area, rust-bloomed, stencilled with heat numbers and grade marks, waiting in the weather. Two or three years later some of that same steel will be a hull weighing tens of thousands of tonnes, sliding into water for the first time. The distance between those two states is not mainly a matter of welding. It is a matter of geometry, sequence, and a very long chain of decisions that have to be made correctly before anyone cuts anything.

## Drawing a Shape That Floats

A ship's hull form is a compromise argued out on paper long before the yard is involved. The owner wants a certain deadweight at a certain speed on a certain route, and every one of those numbers pushes the shape in a different direction. Cargo capacity wants a full, boxy hull. Speed wants a fine, slender one. Shallow ports want draft limited; canal locks want beam limited; stability wants weight low and beam generous. The naval architect resolves these into a set of lines — the curves of the hull expressed as horizontal waterlines, vertical sections, and longitudinal buttocks, each view a slice through the same three-dimensional surface, and each one required to agree with the other two at every point where they cross.

From those lines come the numbers that govern everything downstream. Displacement is simply the weight of water the hull pushes aside, which by Archimedes' logic equals the weight of the ship and everything in it. Block coefficient describes how close the underwater form comes to filling the rectangular box that would just contain it: a tanker might be well over eight-tenths of that box, a fast container ship considerably less. The bulbous bow bolted onto the stem of most large modern vessels exists to generate its own bow wave slightly ahead of the hull's, so that the two partially cancel and the ship drags less water along behind it. It works only within a narrow band of speed and draft, which is why a ship running light and slow can look like it is making more fuss at the bow than one fully loaded.

Stability is its own discipline. What matters is not just where the centre of gravity sits but how the centre of buoyancy shifts when the hull heels, because the horizontal separation between those two points is what generates a righting moment. Too little and the ship is dangerously tender. Too much and it snaps upright so violently in a seaway that cargo lashings fail and crew cannot work.

## Lofting and Cutting

Drawings are scaled; steel is not. Traditionally the gap between them was closed in the mould loft, an enormous clear-floored building where the ship's lines were redrawn at full size in chalk and paint, faired by hand using long flexible battens held down by lead weights called ducks. If a curve refused to run sweetly through all its plotted points, the loftsmen knew the design had an error in it, and they found it there rather than in steel. From the faired full-size lines they made wooden templates for every frame, bracket, and plate edge in the ship.

That floor has mostly gone. Fairing is now done numerically, and the geometry passes directly from the hull surface model to the cutting machines, which is why modern plate parts arrive already marked with the positions of every stiffener that will land on them. Plates are cut by plasma or oxy-fuel torch on a gantry, often nested so that the offcut from one part becomes another. Flat plate is then given its shape: rolled between three rollers for simple curvature, or, where the surface is doubly curved as it is at the bow and stern, worked with a line-heating technique in which a torch draws heat lines across the steel and the differential expansion and cooling pulls the plate into a curve. A skilled plater doing this is reading the metal by eye and by the sound of it.

## The Block

Almost nobody builds a ship from the keel upward any more, plate by plate, in the open air. The modern method assembles it from blocks — three-dimensional modules of hull, each perhaps a slice of one deck across part of the beam, built upside down or on its side in a covered shop where welding can be done downhand, which is faster and produces better welds than welding overhead in the rain.

Once a block is structurally complete it is outfitted while it is still accessible: pipe runs, cable trays, ventilation trunks, ladders, and foundations all installed while the thing can be turned to suit the worker instead of the worker contorting to suit the ship. This is where the real savings live. Outfitting, not steelwork, accounts for the bulk of labour hours on most merchant vessels, and every hour of it done in a shop is worth several hours of it done later in a dark, half-finished compartment reached through a temporary hatch.

The switch from riveting to welding, which happened decisively during the Second World War, made all of this possible. Riveted construction required overlapping plates and drilled holes and gangs working in pairs on opposite sides of a joint. Welding allowed butted plates, lighter structure, and — crucially — subassemblies fabricated separately and joined later. The American emergency programmes that turned out Liberty ships demonstrated the method at extraordinary speed, and also demonstrated its hazard: a welded hull is one continuous piece of metal, so a crack that starts in a poorly detailed corner has nothing to stop it, and several of those ships fractured in cold water. The lessons about steel toughness at low temperature and about avoiding sharp geometric notches were learned expensively and are now designed in from the start.

## Keel Laying and Erection

The keel laying is partly ceremonial, with a coin traditionally placed under the keel block for luck, and partly a legal event. Which regulations a ship must satisfy usually depends on the date her keel was laid or she reached an equivalent stage of construction, so the date has consequences that outlive the ceremony. A yard racing to beat the entry into force of a new rule is racing toward that moment specifically.

Erection is the process of lifting blocks into the building dock or onto the slipway and joining them. The tolerances are not tight in absolute terms but they accumulate ruthlessly, so blocks are set out from reference lines and checked continuously by survey instruments. The awkward part is that welding shrinks steel. A long seam pulls its plates toward each other as it cools, and a hull with hundreds of metres of seams will end up measurably shorter than the sum of its parts unless extra material — green, in the trade — is deliberately left on the mating edges of each block and trimmed to fit on the day. Distortion also shows on the skin. The faint dishing visible between frames on a welded hull, most obvious in raking light, is the ordinary residue of that shrinkage.

The main engine usually goes in through an opening left in the deck for the purpose, landed on chocks and then aligned to the shaft line. Alignment is fussy work: the propeller shaft, its stern tube bearings, the intermediate bearings, and the engine crankshaft all have to sit on a common line that will still be a common line once the hull is afloat and flexing, which is not the same shape it holds when resting on blocks. Some yards deliberately set a slight offset to account for the change.

## Getting It Into the Water

Most large ships today are built in a dry dock and simply floated out: the dock is flooded, the ship lifts off her blocks, and tugs walk her to a fitting-out berth. It is undramatic, which is precisely the point, because the alternative is one of the most highly loaded few seconds in a hull's life.

On a traditional inclined slipway, the hull sits on ground ways with sliding ways built beneath it, the two separated by tallow and soap. When the triggers release, the ship accelerates down the declivity stern-first. As the stern enters the water it gains buoyancy and begins to lift while the bow is still resting on the ways, and for a moment the entire weight of the ship bears down through a small area at the forward end — the fore poppet — with the hull effectively cantilevered. Launch calculations exist mostly to guarantee that the ship will pivot before she reaches the end of the ways and that she will not drop her stern onto the riverbed. Drag chains ranged along the bank take the way off her once she is afloat, because a hull moving stern-first into a narrow river has no steerage and no brakes.

Where the water is too narrow for an end-on launch, smaller vessels are launched sideways, entering with a spectacular roll and a wave that soaks everyone on the opposite bank. Anyone who has watched one understands why Brunel's attempt to launch the enormous Great Eastern sideways became a byword for the difficulty of the method.

Launching is not completion. The ship goes to a quay for months more of outfitting, commissioning, and testing, and then to sea for trials: progressive speed runs, a crash stop from full ahead to full astern with the distance and swing recorded, turning circles to port and starboard, anchor trials, endurance runs on the main engine. Somewhere in there comes the inclining experiment, in which known weights are shifted across the deck of an essentially complete ship and the resulting small angles of heel are measured against a long pendulum. From those angles the ship's actual centre of gravity is calculated rather than estimated, and that number goes into the stability book the master will use for the rest of the vessel's working life to decide how she may be loaded.
