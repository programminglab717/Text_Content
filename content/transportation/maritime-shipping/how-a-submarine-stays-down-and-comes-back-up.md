# How a Submarine Stays Down and Comes Back Up

Sinking a boat is easy. Anyone can do it once. The whole engineering problem of the submarine is the second half — going down deliberately, holding a chosen depth for weeks while the water outside tries to crush the hull and the air inside slowly turns poisonous, and then arriving back at the surface at a time and place of your choosing.

Start with the weight. A submarine on the surface floats for exactly the same reason a canoe does: it displaces more water than it weighs. The difference is that a submarine carries a set of large tanks, the main ballast tanks, wrapped around the outside of its strong hull, and those tanks are the entirety of its reserve buoyancy. They are open to the sea at the bottom, through flood ports that are never closed, and sealed at the top by vent valves. On the surface the tanks hold air and the vents are shut. To dive, the vents open, the air blows out of the top, water rises in from the bottom, and the boat's reserve buoyancy simply leaves. It is less like filling something than like letting go.

Main ballast tanks are binary — full or empty, nothing in between. Fine control belongs to a separate set of variable ballast or trim tanks inside the pressure hull, which can take on or pump out relatively small quantities of water. This matters more than it sounds, because a submerged submarine is trying to hold a balance that keeps changing underneath it. Burn fuel, launch a weapon, run the evaporators, take on stores, and the boat's weight changes. Move from cold, salty water into warmer or fresher water, and the density of the sea changes instead, which has the same effect. The crew compensates continuously, shifting water fore and aft to hold the boat level and pumping it overboard or flooding it inboard to hold neutral buoyancy.

There is a nastier wrinkle. The pressure hull is not perfectly rigid; it compresses slightly as depth increases. A boat that is exactly neutral at one depth becomes marginally denser and heavier as it goes deeper, and marginally lighter as it rises — an unstable arrangement that will run away in either direction if nobody intervenes. In practice most submarines do not try to hover. They fly. Moving through the water with way on, they use hydroplanes — control surfaces on the bow or sail and at the stern — exactly as an aircraft uses elevators, and depth becomes a matter of angle and speed. Hovering motionless at depth is a specialist evolution requiring careful trimming, and it is one of the harder things a control room team does.

## The Shape That Holds the Sea Out

Pressure rises by roughly one atmosphere for every ten metres of depth, so a few hundred metres down, every square metre of hull carries a load measured in hundreds of tonnes. The shape that handles uniform external pressure best is a cylinder with domed ends, and that is what the pressure hull is: a long tube of high-strength steel, circular in section, reinforced by ring frames spaced along its length like the ribs of a barrel. Rings resist the failure mode that actually kills pressure vessels, which is not the material tearing but the wall buckling inward in lobes.

Everything else — the casing you walk on, the free-flooding superstructure, the sail, the ballast tanks — sits outside that tube and floods to sea pressure. It carries no load. The design effort concentrates on the pressure hull itself and on the places where something must pass through it: shafts, torpedo tubes, hatches, masts, and the electrical and hydraulic penetrations, every one of which is a deliberate hole in an otherwise perfect shape and every one of which is engineered and tested accordingly.

Navies publish a test depth, the depth to which the boat is certified to operate, and keep quiet about the collapse depth, which is a calculated estimate with a safety margin folded in. Crews in old submarines used to listen to the hull talk as they went deep — sharp pops and groans as frames took up load and paint flaked off.

## Power, and the Problem of Air

A conventional submarine has two power systems because it lives in two worlds. On the surface or at snorkel depth, diesel engines drive generators, which propel the boat and recharge an enormous battery. Fully submerged, the diesels are useless — they need air and must exhaust somewhere — so the battery alone drives the motors. That battery is the whole limit of a conventional boat's underwater life. Crawl at a few knots and it may last days. Sprint, and it may be gone in an hour. Then the boat must return to periscope depth and raise the snorkel mast, a German wartime invention that lets the diesels breathe through a head valve just above the waves, which is also the moment the submarine is most detectable.

Air-independent propulsion narrows the gap. Some designs carry closed-cycle Stirling engines burning diesel fuel with stored liquid oxygen; others use hydrogen fuel cells, which are nearly silent because almost nothing rotates. These systems give a modest amount of power for a long time — enough for a quiet patrol at low speed for a week or two, not enough for a high-speed transit.

Nuclear propulsion removes the limit entirely. A pressurised water reactor produces steam, steam drives turbines, and the turbines drive the shaft and the ship's generators. Nothing is consumed that has to be replaced for years. The endurance of a nuclear submarine is set by food and by the tolerance of the people inside it, which is why patrol lengths are measured against crew fatigue rather than fuel.

Whatever drives the propeller, the atmosphere has to be manufactured. Oxygen comes from electrolysing distilled seawater, splitting it into oxygen for the boat and hydrogen pumped overboard; chemical oxygen candles serve as backup. Carbon dioxide is stripped out continuously in scrubbers that pass air through an amine solution which absorbs the gas when cool and releases it when heated, so it can be pumped over the side. Carbon monoxide and stray hydrogen go through catalytic burners. The whole mix is monitored constantly, and a submarine's air is an engineered product, slightly stale, carrying a persistent smell of amine, oil, and cooking that every submariner recognises.

## Hearing, and Not Being Heard

Seawater is opaque to radio and light and almost transparent to sound, so a submarine perceives the world acoustically. The primary sensor is passive sonar: large hydrophone arrays in the bow, along the flanks, and streamed astern on a towed cable far enough back to escape the boat's own noise. Passive sonar gives bearing and, with patience and manoeuvring, a solution for range, course and speed. Active sonar — a transmitted ping — gives range immediately and announces your presence to everyone listening, which is why it is used sparingly.

Sound in the ocean does not travel in straight lines. Its speed varies with temperature, pressure and salinity, so rays bend toward slower water. A sharp temperature gradient, the thermocline, forms a layer that reflects and refracts sound, and a submarine sitting beneath it is substantially harder to hear from above. In deep water, rays that bend downward may be turned back up by increasing pressure and resurface tens of miles away, producing convergence zones where a distant contact is audible while nearer water holds a shadow zone that is effectively deaf. Knowing the sound profile of the water you are in is a tactical asset, which is why boats routinely measure it.

The counterpart to listening is silence. Machinery is mounted on rafts and resilient mounts so that vibration never reaches the hull directly. Hulls are clad in rubbery anechoic tiles that absorb incoming pings and damp outgoing noise. Propellers are made with many skewed blades, or replaced by ducted pump-jets, to keep blade tips from cavitating — because the collapse of vapour bubbles is loud, and cavitation inception speed is one of the most jealously guarded numbers about any submarine. Nuclear boats are designed so their reactor coolant circulates by natural convection at low power, allowing the main coolant pumps, a notorious noise source, to be shut down entirely while creeping.

## Knowing Where You Are Without Looking

Satellite positioning does not penetrate seawater, so a submerged submarine navigates by inertia. Rings of gyroscopes and accelerometers track every acceleration and rotation from a known starting point and integrate them into a position. This drifts, slowly and predictably, and is corrected whenever the boat can come shallow enough to expose a mast and take a satellite fix, or by comparing measured seabed depth against a bathymetric chart.

Communication runs the same one-way street. Very low frequency radio penetrates some tens of metres of water, enough to reach a boat at patrol depth, but the bandwidth is so narrow that messages are terse and slow; extremely low frequency, slower still, is essentially a bell to tell a deterrent patrol to come shallow and listen properly. Boats trail floating wire antennas or release buoys to receive without surfacing. Transmitting is another matter, since a radio transmission can be located, and a ballistic missile submarine on deterrent patrol may go its entire time at sea without saying a word to anyone.
