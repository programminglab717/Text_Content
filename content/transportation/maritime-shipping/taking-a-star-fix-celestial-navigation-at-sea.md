# Taking a Star Fix: Celestial Navigation at Sea

The window for a star sight is short. You need the stars out and the horizon still visible, and those two conditions overlap for perhaps twenty or thirty minutes at each end of the night, during the band of civil twilight when the sky is dark enough for the bright stars to show and light enough that the line where sea meets sky is a clean edge rather than a guess. So the navigator comes out on the bridge wing with a sextant and a stopwatch and a list prepared half an hour earlier, finds a planned star at a predicted altitude and bearing, brings it down to the horizon, rocks the instrument gently until the reflected image kisses the sea at the bottom of its swing, and says "mark" to whoever is holding the watch.

Four or five of those in ten minutes, worked through a set of tables, will put the ship inside a couple of miles of where it actually is. The arithmetic is tedious and entirely mechanical. The interesting part is what the measurement means.

## What the Sextant Actually Measures

A sextant measures one thing: the angle between a celestial body and the visible horizon. It does this by double reflection. Light from the star strikes an index mirror mounted on a pivoting arm, bounces to a half-silvered horizon mirror, and arrives at the eye superimposed on the direct view of the horizon through the clear half of that same mirror. Swinging the arm moves the star's image up or down against the real horizon. Because the light reflects twice, the arm moves through half the measured angle, which is why an instrument with an arc of sixty degrees — a sixth of a circle, hence the name — reads up to a hundred and twenty.

The raw reading is not yet an altitude. Several corrections stand between them, and all of them matter, because an error of one arcminute is an error of one nautical mile in the final position.

First comes index error: no mirror is ever quite perfectly aligned, and the navigator checks this before every round of sights by setting the instrument to zero on the horizon and noting how far off it reads. Then dip. Standing above the water, you see slightly beyond the geometric horizontal, so the visible horizon lies a little below true level, and the angle must be reduced by an amount that depends on height of eye — small from a yacht's cockpit, several arcminutes from the bridge of a large ship. Then refraction, because the atmosphere bends starlight downward and makes every body appear higher than it is, negligibly near the zenith and by more than half a degree at the horizon, which is why low sights are treated with suspicion. For the sun and moon you also apply semidiameter, since you measured the lower edge rather than the centre, and the sun's disc is about sixteen arcminutes across. The moon needs a parallax correction as well, being close enough that an observer on the surface of the earth sees it from a noticeably different angle than an observer at the centre would.

Apply all of that and you have the observed altitude, usually written Ho. Along with it you have a time, read to the second, and the time matters as much as the angle.

## Longitude Is a Clock Problem

The earth turns fifteen degrees of longitude in an hour, a degree in four minutes, an arcminute in four seconds. That equivalence is the whole of the longitude problem: if you know precisely what time it is at a reference meridian and precisely what time it is where you stand, the difference is your longitude, and an error of four seconds costs you a mile.

Latitude had never been especially hard — measure the sun at noon or Polaris at night and you have it. Longitude defeated navigators for centuries, and the wrecks that resulted were not hypothetical. Two solutions eventually arrived at nearly the same moment. One was the marine chronometer, a clock that would keep reference time through months of damp, heat, and a pitching deck, which John Harrison pursued across decades of work culminating in the 1760s. The other was the method of lunar distances, in which the moon's rapid motion against the background stars becomes a clock face readable from anywhere, provided you have tables predicting where it will be. Those tables meant a permanent institutional commitment to computing and publishing celestial positions in advance, which is what the Nautical Almanac has done, without interruption, since the late 1760s.

Chronometers won on practicality, and lunars faded once accurate clocks became affordable. But the almanac stayed, and it is still the second half of every sight taken today. Open it to the right date and it gives, for each hour, the Greenwich hour angle and declination of the sun, moon, the navigational planets, and the point in the sky called Aries against which the fixed stars are catalogued by their own sidereal hour angle. Interpolation pages supply the increments for minutes and seconds.

## The Circle You Cannot Draw

Here is the geometry that makes the whole system work. At any instant, every celestial body sits directly above exactly one point on the earth's surface — its geographical position, which the almanac gives you. If you measure that body at ninety degrees, straight overhead, you are standing on that point. If you measure it at eighty degrees, you are ten degrees away from it, in some direction. Ten degrees of arc on the earth is six hundred nautical miles, since a degree is sixty miles and an arcminute is one.

So a single sight does not give a position. It gives a circle: the set of all points on earth from which that body would appear at exactly that altitude at exactly that second, centred on the body's geographical position, with a radius equal to the zenith distance. Two sights of two bodies give two circles, which intersect in two points, and those are usually so far apart that only one can plausibly be your ship.

The trouble is that the circle is hundreds of miles across and cannot be drawn on a working chart. The workaround, developed in the late nineteenth century by the French naval officer Marcq Saint-Hilaire and building on an accidental discovery by the American captain Thomas Sumner some decades earlier, is to give up on the circle and use a short piece of it. Pick an assumed position near where you think you are — for table work, a convenient whole degree of latitude and a longitude chosen to make the arithmetic clean. Compute, from the almanac, what altitude the body would have had from that assumed position, and on what bearing it would have been seen. Compare that computed altitude with what you actually measured. If your measured altitude is greater, you are closer to the body's geographical position than your assumed position is, by exactly one mile for every arcminute of difference. If it is less, you are farther.

That difference is the intercept. Plot the assumed position, draw the body's bearing from it, step off the intercept along that bearing toward or away from the body, and at that point draw a line at right angles. Over the few miles you care about, the enormous circle is indistinguishable from a straight line, and that line is a line of position: you are somewhere on it. Take another body on a different bearing and you get a second line. Where they cross is your fix. Three or four give you a small triangle or a cocked hat, and the size of that figure is your own report card on the round of sights.

Sight reduction — turning almanac data and an assumed position into a computed altitude and bearing — is spherical trigonometry that can be done with a calculator in a minute or dug out of precomputed volumes designed so that the navigator never multiplies anything. The tables for selected stars will even hand you altitude and azimuth for seven good stars at your latitude directly, which is how the twilight list gets prepared before going on deck.

## Noon and Polaris, the Cheap Answers

Two shortcuts deserve mention because generations sailed on them. At local apparent noon the sun crosses your meridian and reaches its highest altitude of the day. At that instant no clock is needed at all: subtract the altitude from ninety and combine with the sun's declination, and you have latitude. That is why the old sailing directions told masters to run north or south until they struck the latitude of their destination and then hold it, sailing down the parallel until land appeared, a slow method but a reliable one.

Polaris offers the same gift at night. It sits close enough to the celestial pole that its altitude is very nearly your latitude, and the almanac supplies three small corrections to remove the wobble of its circle around the pole.

Most people who learn this today learn it as a backup. Satellite positioning is better in every ordinary respect — faster, continuous, immune to overcast. But it arrives as a signal from elsewhere, and signals can be jammed, spoofed, or simply lost, which is a large part of why the United States Naval Academy put celestial navigation back into the curriculum in the mid-2010s after having quietly dropped it. An angle measured between a star and the horizon comes from nowhere but the sky, and nobody can turn it off.
