# Lighthouses and the Engineering of Light

Fourteen miles off Plymouth there is a reef that spends most of its life underwater. At low tide on a calm day a few black teeth of gneiss show above the surface, streaming; at high tide in a gale there is nothing to see but broken water where the Atlantic swell, having crossed three thousand miles of open ocean without meeting anything, finds the bottom coming up beneath it. The Eddystone rocks lie directly across the approach to one of England's great naval ports, and for most of recorded history the only sensible response was to stay well clear and accept that some ships would not.

The attempt to put a light on that reef, and the century and a half of failures and rebuildings it took, is essentially the story of how lighthouses came to be built at all.

## Four Towers on One Rock

Henry Winstanley, a merchant and showman who had lost ships on the reef, began the first tower in the closing years of the seventeenth century. It was an extraordinary object, ornamented with scrollwork and galleries and wrought-iron flourishes, more folly than fortification, and he strengthened it after its first winter when he found the sea coming higher than he had imagined possible. He is said to have wished to be inside it during the greatest storm that ever blew. He got his wish. In the enormous storm of 1703 the tower disappeared completely, with Winstanley and his workmen in it, leaving a few twisted iron stubs in the rock.

A silk merchant named Rudyerd built the second, and built it better by building it dumber: a conical wooden tower, heavily ballasted with granite in its base, smooth-sided so that waves would slide rather than catch. It stood for nearly half a century, which was a triumph. Then, in the 1750s, the lantern caught fire. The keepers retreated downward ahead of the flames, and one elderly man, looking up as the molten lead of the roof ran down, swallowed some of it. He died days later, and the surgeon who opened him afterwards found a lump of lead in his stomach, a detail so implausible that it was disbelieved for years.

The third tower was John Smeaton's, finished in 1759, and it is the one that changed engineering. Smeaton reasoned from the shape of an oak trunk — broad and flared at the base, tapering with a concave curve, the profile of something that has evolved to resist being pushed over. He cut the rock into level steps and dovetailed his granite blocks into those steps and into each other, so that the tower was not a stack of stones held down by weight but a single interlocked mass. To bind it he developed a lime mortar that would set under water, working out that the hydraulic property came from clay content in the limestone. That discovery ran directly into the development of modern cement.

Smeaton's tower stood for over a century and was eventually replaced not because the tower failed but because the rock beneath it was being undercut by the sea. The upper portion was dismantled and re-erected on Plymouth Hoe, where it remains a monument and a public curiosity, while James Douglass built the present tower on sounder rock nearby. Smeaton's stump is still out there beside it, too solid to be worth removing.

## The Stevensons and the Impossible Sites

What Smeaton proved at Eddystone the Scots took to its logical extreme. The Bell Rock, off the Angus coast, was worse than Eddystone in one specific way: it dried only briefly around low water, and only on some tides, so the working day might be two hours long and then not exist again for a fortnight. Robert Stevenson built there in the first decade of the nineteenth century, housing his men first on a ship moored nearby and then on a temporary beacon of wooden legs erected on the reef itself, from which they could work the moment the water dropped. The tower was completed and lit in 1811 and has never been rebuilt.

Stevenson founded a dynasty. His sons and grandsons built lights around the Scottish coast for a century — Skerryvore, on a reef in the Atlantic west of Tiree, is usually judged the most beautiful masonry tower ever built, and Dubh Artach the grimmest. The family's most famous member declined the trade entirely and wrote books instead, but Robert Louis Stevenson had been trained for it, and the sea in his fiction has the specific quality of someone who has stood on a wet reef waiting for a boat.

## Making a Light Worth Seeing

A tower is only a support. The hard problem is optical, and until the nineteenth century the answer to it was feeble. Early lights were open coal fires on a platform, which produced impressive quantities of smoke and consumed tons of fuel, or banks of candles behind glass. The first real improvement was the Argand lamp, a burner with a hollow cylindrical wick and a glass chimney that draws air through the centre of the flame as well as around it, giving a bright, steady, comparatively smokeless light. Set an Argand lamp at the focus of a polished parabolic reflector and you have a beam. Banks of these reflectors, all aimed slightly differently, gave the first lights that could be seen at a useful distance.

The transformation came from Augustin-Jean Fresnel, working in France in the 1820s. Fresnel understood that a lens does its refracting at its surface, and that the bulk of glass behind the surface is dead weight that absorbs light and makes the lens impossibly heavy at large sizes. So he collapsed the lens into concentric annular rings, each one a section of the original curve carried on a flat backing. Around the central rings he added prisms that catch light going out at steep angles above and below the flame and bend it by internal reflection back into the horizontal. A large Fresnel assembly is a barrel of glass taller than a person, and it takes nearly all the light a single flame throws in every direction and lays it flat into a beam that skims the sea. Lenses were graded into orders by focal length, the first order being the largest, reserved for coastal landfall lights where range mattered most.

Because a beam has to sweep, the whole optic was made to rotate — often floating on a bath of mercury, which is nearly frictionless and lets a multi-tonne assembly be turned by a small falling weight on a chain, exactly like a grandfather clock. Winding that weight up again, every few hours, all night, was a defining part of a keeper's job.

Rotation also solved the identification problem. Two lights on the same coast are worse than one if a navigator cannot tell them apart, so every major light was given a characteristic: a unique rhythm of flashes and eclipses, produced by the number and spacing of the lens panels and the speed of rotation. A mariner with a watch and a chart could time the interval, look up the pattern, and know precisely which headland he was looking at. Lights that show a steady beam with brief eclipses, lights that are dark with brief flashes, lights that group their flashes in twos or threes — all of it is a code read with a stopwatch, and it is still in use.

Fuel evolved underneath the optics: sperm whale oil first, then colza and other vegetable oils, then mineral oil, and eventually vaporised paraffin burned under an incandescent mantle, which gave an enormous jump in brightness for the same fuel. Electricity arrived late at remote stations, because a light that fails is worse than no light and a generator two hours from the nearest human being is a liability.

## The Keepers, and Their Absence

Offshore rock stations were manned by two men for a long time, until the Smalls light off the Welsh coast produced the lesson that changed the practice. One of the two keepers there died. The survivor, fearing that if he committed the body to the sea he would be accused of murder, built a makeshift coffin, lashed it outside on the gallery, and went on tending the light with his dead colleague's arm working loose in the wind until relief finally came weeks later. He was reportedly unrecognisable to his friends afterwards. Three keepers became the rule.

The trade produced its own folklore: the vanishing of all three men from the Flannan Isles station in 1900, discovered by a relief crew who found the lamp trimmed, a meal half-eaten by some tellings, and nobody aboard the island at all. The likeliest explanation involves a freak sea catching men working at the west landing, but the absence of any witness is exactly what makes the story survive.

Automation ended all of it within living memory. Sensors switch the lamp at dusk, solar panels charge batteries, small sealed acrylic optics with light-emitting diodes do work that once needed a tonne of ground glass and a barrel of paraffin, and a technician visits by helicopter. Satellite navigation has meanwhile made the whole network technically redundant for position-finding, and the number of lights maintained has quietly fallen.

They are kept anyway, most of them, and the reason is unglamorous. A receiver can fail, a constellation can be jammed or spoofed, an electrical fault can darken a bridge. A light on a headland cannot be hacked, requires no equipment to receive, and tells you the one thing that matters at two in the morning in poor visibility: not where you are in coordinates, but where the rock is.
