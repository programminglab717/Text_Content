# A Field Guide to Commercial Fishing Gear

You can read a fishing harbour the way a birder reads a hedgerow. The boat with the towering A-frame gantry at the stern and two scarred steel slabs hanging in the quarters is a trawler, and those slabs are its doors. The one with a sagging stack of shallow rectangular nets on deck and a tall cylindrical block on the boom works a purse seine. The small open boat with a line of plastic floats coiled in a bin and a hydraulic hauler clamped to the gunwale sets gillnets, and the one stacked four deep with wire cages and a forest of buoy sticks is going after crab. Every method has a shape, and the shape is dictated by the behaviour of the animal being caught.

Broadly, fishing gear does one of four things. It is towed through the water, it surrounds a school, it waits in place to entangle, or it offers something the animal takes voluntarily. Almost everything on any quay in the world is a variation on those four.

## Gear That Is Towed

Trawling accounts for a great deal of the world's whitefish and shrimp, and the essential problem it solves is holding a net's mouth open while dragging it through water that wants to close it. The otter trawl does this with a pair of heavy doors — the otter boards — shackled ahead of the net on the towing warps and angled so that the water flowing past pushes them apart, like a pair of kites flown sideways. Behind them the net widens into wings, then a belly, then narrows to the cod end where the catch collects. Floats along the headline lift the top; a weighted footrope holds the bottom down.

What is fitted to that footrope tells you the seabed. On clean sand it may be little more than a chain. On rough ground, trawlers run rockhopper or bobbin gear — rubber discs the size of tyres that let the net bounce over boulders rather than snag and tear. This is also where bottom trawling earns its reputation, since gear built to roll over structure inevitably flattens some of it, and repeated towing over the same ground converts a complex seabed into a smoother, poorer one.

Not all trawls touch bottom. Pelagic or midwater trawls target schooling fish in open water, using much larger, lighter nets with enormous mouths of fine mesh at the front that herds fish inward rather than filtering them. Pair trawling replaces the doors entirely: two boats tow one net between them, each holding a warp, and the spread comes from the distance between the vessels. The beam trawl, a North Sea staple for sole and plaice, holds its mouth open with a rigid steel beam on shoes and drags tickler chains ahead of the net to startle flatfish out of the sand. Danish and Scottish seining occupies a gentler middle ground, laying out long ropes on the seabed in a great loop and then hauling them in so the ropes sweep fish toward a small net, which disturbs less ground and burns far less fuel than dragging doors.

## Gear That Surrounds

Purse seining is the method for fish that school tightly near the surface: tuna, sardine, anchovy, herring, mackerel. A skiff takes one end of the net and holds it while the seiner steams a circle around the school, paying out a wall of netting perhaps a kilometre long and deep enough to hang below the fish. The critical component is the purse line, a cable running through a row of heavy rings along the bottom edge. Heave that line in and the bottom of the net closes like an old-fashioned drawstring purse, and the school is trapped in a bag of net alongside the vessel. The net is then hauled through a power block on the boom, the bag shrinks, and the fish are brailed aboard with a dip net or pumped.

Purse seining is extraordinarily efficient, which is precisely the source of its controversies. Tuna often swim under floating objects, so fleets deploy drifting fish aggregating devices — rafts with dangling structure and satellite buoys — and return to set on whatever has gathered. It works, and it catches juveniles and non-target species along with the tuna. In the eastern Pacific, yellowfin associate with dolphin schools, and setting on the dolphins to catch the tuna beneath them killed dolphins in very large numbers before a combination of regulation, observer coverage and gear changes — the backdown manoeuvre and finer safety-panel mesh that lets dolphins escape over the corkline — cut the toll dramatically.

## Gear That Waits

A gillnet is a wall of fine, nearly invisible mesh hung between a float line and a lead line. Fish swimming into it pass their heads through a mesh, cannot back out because their gill covers catch, and are held. The whole method turns on mesh size: too small and the fish bounce off, too large and they swim through, so a given mesh catches a startlingly narrow range of body sizes. That makes gillnets among the most size-selective gears in existence and among the least species-selective, since anything of roughly the right girth is caught, including seabirds and small cetaceans. Trammel nets, three layers hung together with a loose fine sheet between two coarse ones, catch by pocketing rather than gilling and are less size-selective still.

Nets can be anchored as set nets or allowed to drift. Enormous high-seas driftnets, tens of kilometres long, were widely used in the Pacific in the late twentieth century until an international moratorium ended the practice, and the phrase "walls of death" from that period still clings to the gear. The more persistent problem is lost gear. Synthetic netting does not rot; a gillnet torn free of its buoys can go on catching and killing for years, a phenomenon known as ghost fishing, which is why some fisheries now require biodegradable panels or escape mechanisms that fail open when the gear is abandoned.

## Gear That Is Taken Voluntarily

Longlining hangs baited hooks from a mainline on short branch lines called snoods or gangions. Pelagic longlines float near the surface on buoys for tuna and swordfish; demersal longlines are anchored on the bottom for cod, halibut, hake and toothfish. A single set can carry thousands of hooks over many miles, shot from a spool at the stern and hauled hours later over a roller at the rail. Longlining catches fish in excellent condition and touches no seabed, which is why it supplies much of the premium sashimi market. Its characteristic damage is to seabirds: albatross and petrels dive on baits as they sink behind the boat and drown on the hooks. The fixes are mundane and effective — streamer lines that flap above the sinking baits, weighted branch lines so hooks sink faster, setting at night, thawing bait so it does not float.

Trolling drags lures on multiple lines from outriggers at slow speed, catching salmon and albacore one at a time. Pole-and-line fishing, still practised on tuna in parts of the Indian and Pacific Oceans, chums live baitfish over the side and sprays water to simulate a feeding frenzy, then lifts fish aboard on barbless jigs at a pace that has to be watched to be believed. Squid jigging works at night under banks of lights bright enough to be visible from orbit, with machines cycling rows of unbaited barbed jigs up and down.

Pots and traps ask the animal to enter a baited chamber through a funnel it cannot find again. They are the standard gear for crab, lobster, whelk and some prawns and finfish, set in strings along a groundline and hauled by a block. Traps are gentle on the bottom and highly selective, because undersize animals leave through escape rings and rot cords are fitted so a lost trap eventually opens itself. Their unresolved problem is vertical rope: the lines connecting pots to surface buoys entangle large whales, which has driven serious work on ropeless systems that release a buoy on acoustic command.

Dredging sits at the opposite end. A scallop or oyster dredge is a heavy steel frame with a toothed bar and a chain-mesh bag, dragged to rake shellfish out of the substrate. It is effective, it is hard on everything it passes over, and it is regulated accordingly in most places where it is still permitted.

## What the Gear Decides

Choosing a method is choosing a bycatch profile, a fuel bill, a crew size, and a footprint on the seabed, and those choices are what fisheries management mostly regulates. Quotas control how much comes out; gear rules control what else comes out with it. Square-mesh escape panels in trawl cod ends stay open under tow so juveniles can swim free where diamond mesh closes under load. Turtle excluder devices are grids that deflect anything large out through a flap while shrimp pass into the bag. Hook shape, mesh size, headline height, soak time, the hour of day a line is set — each is a small dial, and turning one changes what a boat brings home.
