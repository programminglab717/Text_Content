# The Panama and Suez Canals

Fourteen merchant ships spent eight years anchored in the Great Bitter Lake. They had entered the Suez Canal in June 1967, in convoy, when the Six-Day War closed both ends around them, and there they stayed — Swedish, British, Polish, West German, Czechoslovak, French, American, Bulgarian — while the desert quietly buried their decks in dust and turned their hulls the colour of sand. The crews, rotated home and replaced over the years by skeleton watches, organised themselves into an association, ran their own postal service and held their own small Olympics. When the canal finally reopened in 1975, two of the ships sailed out under their own power. The rest were towed.

Nothing illustrates better what a canal actually is. Not a shortcut, exactly, but a narrow place through which an enormous fraction of world trade agrees to pass, and which can therefore be closed — by war, by politics, by drought, or by one badly handled ship touching the bank at the wrong angle.

## A Ditch Through a Desert

The Isthmus of Suez had tempted engineers since antiquity; ancient Egypt dug canals linking the Nile to the Red Sea, and they silted up. What made the nineteenth-century attempt different was the discovery that the Mediterranean and the Red Sea stand at essentially the same level. That single fact meant no locks were needed. A canal there could be a simple cut: a trench a hundred miles long through sand and salt flat, joining two seas that would then flow gently back and forth through it with the tides.

Ferdinand de Lesseps, a French diplomat rather than an engineer, secured the concession from the Egyptian viceroy and organised the company that built it between 1859 and 1869. The early digging was done largely by Egyptian corvée labour — conscripted, unpaid and worked in conditions in which deaths were never reliably counted — before international pressure and practical difficulty pushed the company toward mechanical dredgers. The canal that opened in late 1869 ran roughly 190 kilometres from Port Said to Suez, passing through Lake Timsah and the Bitter Lakes, which did much of the work of the route for free.

Its simplicity is its enduring advantage. With no lock chambers to fit into, there is no hard limit on a ship's length or, within reason, its beam. What constrains the canal is depth and the height of the bridge that crosses it, so the biggest container ships and tankers afloat can pass while the very deepest-laden crude carriers must lighten first. It also means that traffic is managed in convoys, since the channel has for most of its life been too narrow for two large ships to pass. Egypt's 2015 works added a second parallel channel along part of the route, allowing simultaneous transits in that stretch and shortening the waiting.

## The Canal as Politics

Suez has been contested continuously since it opened. Britain, which had opposed the project, bought the Egyptian government's shareholding in 1875 when the viceroy's debts came due, occupied Egypt in 1882, and treated the canal thereafter as an imperial artery. An 1888 convention declared it open to all vessels of all flags in peace and war, a principle honoured unevenly ever since. When Egypt nationalised the canal company in 1956, Britain and France, in concert with Israel, invaded — and were forced into humiliating withdrawal by American and Soviet pressure, in one of the clearest demonstrations of how the world had changed since 1869.

The eight-year closure that trapped the Bitter Lakes fleet forced a generation of tankers around the Cape of Good Hope and, not incidentally, encouraged the construction of crude carriers so large that the canal could not have taken them anyway. And in March 2021, a single very large container ship wedged herself diagonally across the channel in a sandstorm and stopped world traffic for six days, producing a queue of hundreds of ships and a great deal of nervous arithmetic in insurance offices. More recently, attacks on merchant shipping in the southern Red Sea pushed a large share of Asia–Europe traffic back around Africa voluntarily — a reminder that a canal can be closed by the water leading to it as effectively as by the canal itself.

## The French Failure at Panama

Flush with the prestige of Suez, de Lesseps turned to the Isthmus of Panama in 1881 and made the mistake of assuming the same answer would work twice. He proposed a sea-level cut again. Panama, however, is not a desert. It is a mountainous, saturated tropical isthmus with a continental divide running through it and a river, the Chagres, that could rise metres in a day and had to be crossed or tamed. Excavating the divide at Culebra meant cutting a trench through unstable volcanic clays that slid back into the cut whenever it rained, which was most of the time.

Disease did the rest. Yellow fever and malaria killed workers faster than they could be recruited, and the prevailing medical theory blamed foul air, so hospital bed legs stood in pans of water to keep ants off the patients, breeding mosquitoes indoors. The company exhausted its capital and collapsed in 1889, ruining tens of thousands of small French investors and setting off a political scandal at home. Estimates of the death toll over the French decade run into the tens of thousands.

## A Staircase and a Lake

The American effort, begun after Panama separated from Colombia in 1903 with pointed United States naval encouragement, succeeded by changing three things. First, sanitation: William Gorgas, acting on the newly confirmed understanding that mosquitoes transmitted yellow fever and malaria, fumigated, drained, oiled standing water and screened housing across the whole work zone, and effectively removed the disease that had defeated the French. Second, logistics: John Stevens, a railway man, concluded that the central problem was not digging but hauling spoil away, and rebuilt the Panama Railroad into a machine for moving dirt on a continuous schedule. Third, and decisively, the design itself changed. Stevens argued, and Washington eventually accepted, that a sea-level canal was folly.

What was built instead is a water staircase. The Chagres was dammed at Gatun, drowning a large stretch of jungle to create an artificial lake about twenty-six metres above the sea. Ships entering from the Atlantic are lifted to that level in three steps, sail some fifty kilometres across the lake and through the Culebra Cut, and are lowered again in three more steps on the Pacific side. Taming the river became the solution rather than an obstacle: the lake is both the summit reach and the reservoir that feeds every lockage. George Goethals carried the project through to its opening in August 1914, weeks after Europe went to war and largely unnoticed as a result.

The locks were a piece of industrial art in their own right — chambers about 110 feet wide with mitre gates, filled and emptied entirely by gravity through culverts in the walls, with no pumps involved, while electric locomotives running on rack rails along the lock walls held the ships centred. Those dimensions defined the Panamax ship, and for most of a century naval architects designed hulls to fit them almost exactly, accepting an odd beam-to-length ratio because a metre too wide meant the long way around South America.

Transiting either one is a curiously formal business. Pilotage is compulsory in both, but Panama goes further than almost anywhere else in the world: the canal pilot takes actual control of the ship's navigation rather than advising the master, which is the price of manoeuvring a vessel with a metre or less of clearance on each side into a concrete box. A Panama transit takes most of a working day, a Suez transit rather longer, and in both cases a ship spends a good part of it waiting for a slot in a convoy or a lock queue. Tolls are assessed on capacity and vessel type and run to six figures for a large ship, which sounds extortionate until it is set against the fuel and the fortnight saved. The most charming illustration of the pricing principle remains Richard Halliburton, who swam the length of the Panama Canal in 1928, locked through chamber by chamber like any other vessel, and was assessed a toll of a few cents on his own displacement.

## Two Sets of Constraints

The 2016 expansion added a third, larger lane with rolling gates that slide sideways into recesses instead of swinging, tugs in place of the locomotives, and side basins that catch part of each chamber's water and return it to the next lockage. That last feature addresses the canal's peculiar vulnerability. Every ship that transits Panama is floated up a staircase on fresh water that then runs down to the sea, and there is only as much of it as the watershed provides. The same lake supplies drinking water to Panama City. In dry years the canal authority has had to cut the number of daily transit slots and impose draught limits, auctioning the remaining slots to shippers desperate enough to bid — a canal rationed not by war or politics but by rainfall.

The two waterways therefore fail in completely different ways, and constrain ships along different axes. Suez limits draught and the air height of a vessel passing beneath its bridge, and is vulnerable to blockage, to the security of the sea approaches, and to the politics of a single nation's sovereignty. Panama limits length, beam and draught by the hard geometry of a concrete chamber, and is vulnerable to drought. Suez is the shorter path from Asia to Europe; Panama the shorter path from Asia to the eastern United States, and the alternative to a voyage around Cape Horn that sailing ships once measured in weeks of misery.

Panama's sovereignty was settled later than Suez's and more peaceably. Treaties signed in 1977 provided for the gradual handover of the canal and its zone, completed at the end of 1999, and the waterway has been operated since by a Panamanian authority that has run it, by general agreement, at least as well as its predecessors did. Both canals remain what they were built to be: privately unremarkable stretches of water, a few hours' transit apiece, on which a startling share of everything anyone owns has at some point floated.
