# Marine Diesel Engines

The main engine of a large container ship is not something you look at so much as something you walk around inside. It stands three or four storeys from the tank top to the top of the cylinder heads, with gratings and ladders at every level, and the cylinder covers arranged in a row along the top like manhole lids. The bore of each cylinder is wide enough for a person to climb into during an overhaul, and the crankshaft below is a forging so large it is assembled from sections. At full power it turns at something close to the speed of a bicycle wheel on a leisurely ride — under a hundred revolutions a minute — and you can stand beside it and count the firing strokes.

It is also, by a comfortable margin, the most thermally efficient heat engine in widespread commercial use. The best of these engines convert more than half the chemical energy in their fuel into work at the shaft, a figure no car engine, gas turbine, or conventional power station approaches. Understanding why involves almost every design decision taken in the engine room, most of which look strange from the perspective of anything that drives on a road.

## Two Families

Marine propulsion splits into two broad types, and the split is about speed. Low-speed two-stroke engines run at 60 to about 120 revolutions a minute and are bolted directly to the propeller shaft with no gearbox and no clutch anywhere in between. Medium-speed four-strokes run several hundred to a thousand revolutions a minute, are far smaller for a given power, and drive through a reduction gear or, increasingly, through a generator into an electric motor. Ferries, cruise ships, dredgers, tugs and offshore vessels mostly use the second kind, often several of them, which lets the ship shut down engines it does not need. Deep-sea cargo ships almost universally use the first.

The reason comes down to the propeller. A large, slowly turning propeller is more efficient than a small, fast one, because it accelerates a greater mass of water by a smaller increment, and a ship that crosses an ocean at steady power cares about that difference more than about anything else. So the engine is built to turn at propeller speed rather than being geared down to it, and everything about its architecture follows from the decision to accept enormous size in exchange for direct drive.

## The Crosshead

The two-stroke cycle means there is no idle stroke: every downward travel of the piston is a power stroke. Air is blown in through a ring of ports cut around the bottom of the cylinder liner, which the piston uncovers at the end of its travel, and the exhaust leaves through a single large valve in the centre of the cylinder head. The gas therefore moves in one direction only, bottom to top, sweeping the burnt charge out ahead of the fresh air rather than mixing with it — uniflow scavenging, and it works well enough that these engines breathe cleanly at a stroke length that would stall any other arrangement.

The piston does not sit on a connecting rod. It is carried on a rigid piston rod that runs down to a crosshead — a bearing block sliding in vertical guides — and only below that does a conventional connecting rod angle away to the crank. This costs height and weight, and buys two things that matter enormously. First, the side thrust generated by the angled connecting rod is taken by the guides rather than by the piston skirt, so the piston can be a light cap that does nothing but seal, and the liner wears evenly instead of ovalling. Second, the piston rod passes through a gland in a diaphragm that separates the cylinder space above from the crankcase below.

That separation is what makes the lubrication system possible. Cylinder walls are fed a measured dose of expensive, strongly alkaline oil injected through quills in the liner, timed to the passing piston rings, and that oil is consumed. The alkalinity exists to neutralise sulphuric acid, which forms whenever sulphur in the fuel meets water vapour on a cool liner wall; too little and the liner corrodes, too much and the combustion space fills with hard deposits. Below the diaphragm, a completely separate charge of ordinary system oil circulates through bearings and the crosshead and is never contaminated by combustion products. A four-stroke engine, where one oil does both jobs, has to compromise on both.

## Burning the Bottom of the Barrel

Deep-sea ships have traditionally run on residual fuel — the thick, dark remainder left after the valuable fractions have been distilled out of crude. At room temperature it is closer to roofing tar than to diesel. Bunker tanks carry steam heating coils to keep it liquid at all, fuel lines are traced with steam or electric heating along their whole length, and before injection the oil is heated well past the boiling point of water to bring its viscosity down to something an injector can atomise.

It arrives dirty, too, with water and sludge and a particular hazard called catalytic fines: hard aluminium-silicon particles left over from refinery cracking units, which behave in a cylinder like grinding paste. Every ship therefore runs a fuel treatment plant — settling tank, then high-speed centrifugal purifiers, then a service tank — whose whole purpose is to hand the engine something cleaner than what came aboard. Engineers who skip a purifier service find out about it later in the form of scored liners.

That fuel economy came at an atmospheric price, and the rules eventually caught up. A global cap dropped the permitted sulphur content of marine fuel to half a percent in 2020, and a tenth of a percent in coastal emission control areas, so operators now choose between low-sulphur blends, distillate, or fitting exhaust gas scrubbers and continuing to burn the cheap stuff. Separate limits on nitrogen oxides have pushed newer ships toward exhaust gas recirculation or catalytic reduction with urea injected into the uptake. A growing minority of new vessels are dual-fuel, burning liquefied natural gas or methanol with a small pilot injection of liquid fuel to start combustion.

The main engine is not alone down there, either. A cargo ship carries two or three medium-speed auxiliary engines driving alternators to supply the ship's electrical load, plus an emergency generator sitting high in the accommodation block with its own fuel tank and starting battery, on the assumption that a flooded or burning engine room is exactly when power matters most. Many ships also take electrical power off the propeller shaft through a shaft generator, which is cheaper per kilowatt-hour than running a separate diesel but ties the frequency of the ship's supply to the speed of the shaft unless a converter sits in between. Deciding which combination of machines to run at any given moment is a daily optimisation problem the engineers actually solve, by habit rather than by software.

A shaft turning at engine speed also has to survive torsional vibration. The crankshaft, the intermediate shafting and the propeller form a torsional spring-mass system with natural frequencies of its own, and at certain revolutions the firing impulses line up with one of them. The result is a barred speed range, painted on the bridge telegraph and enforced by the control system: a band of revolutions the ship is permitted to accelerate through but never to sit in.

## Where the Efficiency Comes From

No large marine engine makes its rated power without a turbocharger, and the turbocharger is not an accessory bolted on for extra performance; the engine is designed around it. Exhaust gas spins a turbine, the turbine drives a compressor, and the compressor delivers scavenge air at several times atmospheric pressure into a receiver that runs the length of the engine. At low load there is not enough exhaust energy to keep it spinning usefully, so electric auxiliary blowers cut in automatically to supply the difference.

The rest of the efficiency is a matter of scale and patience. A very long stroke allows the combustion gases to expand almost fully before the exhaust opens, extracting work that a shorter engine throws away as heat and noise. Low rotational speed means low piston friction and long, unhurried combustion. Sheer size means a small surface area relative to the volume of gas inside, so proportionally less heat leaks into the cooling water. What heat does escape is not entirely lost either: an exhaust gas economizer in the funnel uptake raises steam for fuel heating, tank heating and the accommodation, and some ships add a power turbine or a shaft generator to recover more still.

## Stopping, Starting and Going Astern

With no gearbox and no clutch, a ship with a fixed-pitch propeller can only go astern by running its main engine backwards. The engine is stopped, the fuel and exhaust valve timing is shifted to the reversed sequence — historically by sliding the camshaft, now by rewriting the timing in software — and compressed air at about thirty bar is admitted through starting air valves in the cylinder heads to turn the crankshaft the other way until combustion takes over. The whole manoeuvre takes seconds, and in a busy harbour an engine may be reversed a dozen times in an hour, which is why air receivers are sized for a long series of consecutive starts.

Electronic control has quietly reorganised the rest. Replacing the camshaft with a high-pressure servo oil system and a computer means injection timing, injection pressure profile and exhaust valve timing become independent variables that can be optimised separately at every load. That flexibility is what made slow steaming practical: an engine designed for twenty-five knots and then operated for years at sixty percent power would once have run dirty and inefficiently, and now simply gets a different injection strategy.

What remains is the maintenance, and it is relentless. Liner wear is measured with a micrometer bridge. Indicator diagrams are taken to check peak pressures cylinder by cylinder. Pistons come out on a schedule counted in thousands of running hours. Oil mist detectors watch the crankcase continuously, because a bearing running hot will vaporise lubricating oil into a fog that can explode, and scavenge spaces are inspected for the smouldering oil and carbon deposits that start scavenge fires. Most ships are certified for unattended machinery space operation, which means the engine room runs empty overnight with an alarm system watching a few hundred parameters and one engineer on call — a quiet arrangement that depends entirely on everything having been maintained properly during the day.
