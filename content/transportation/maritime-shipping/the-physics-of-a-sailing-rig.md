# The Physics of a Sailing Rig

A boat beating to windward is not being pushed. Put your hand on the mainsheet of a dinghy going to weather and the load you feel is a pull, angled out over the lee rail, most of it trying to shove the boat sideways and only a modest fraction of it doing anything useful. The wind is coming from somewhere forward of the beam, and the boat is climbing toward it. Nothing about that is intuitive, and for most of human history nobody could do it: for thousands of years a sail was a bag you hung up to catch a following breeze, and a ship that needed to go the other way waited, or got out the oars.

The modern rig is a machine for converting a sideways problem into forward motion, and understanding it means giving up the idea of the sail as a wind-catcher entirely.

## The Wind the Boat Actually Sees

The first thing to abandon is the true wind. A moving boat generates a headwind of its own equal to its speed, and the sails feel the vector sum of the two — the apparent wind. Sailing upwind, that sum is stronger than the true wind and shifted forward of it, which is why a close-hauled boat appears to be sailing almost into the breeze when the rig is in fact set at a perfectly reasonable angle to the air actually flowing over it. Sailing downwind the two subtract, and a boat running dead before a fifteen-knot breeze at eight knots feels seven knots of wind on deck, warm and quiet, with the masthead fly barely stirring.

This is not bookkeeping. It is the central feedback loop in sailing. Go faster and the apparent wind builds and swings forward, which changes the angle every sail must be trimmed to, which changes the force, which changes the speed. Fast boats live inside that loop; slow ones barely notice it.

## Two Foils in Two Fluids

A sail on a close-hauled boat is a thin cambered wing standing on end. Air arriving at the luff divides and is turned by the sail's curvature, leaving the leech in a different direction than it arrived, and the deflection is accompanied by a pressure difference across the cloth — low on the outside, high on the inside. The resulting force is roughly perpendicular to the apparent wind, tipped a little aft by drag.

Now resolve that force in the boat's frame. With the apparent wind twenty-five or thirty degrees off the bow, a force perpendicular to it points mostly out to the side, with only a small component pointing where the boat is trying to go. Upwind, the sideways share is commonly four or five times the forward share. The rig is producing a great deal of force and offering the hull a thin slice of it.

That slice is all you get, and the only way to keep it is to defeat the rest. This is the job of the keel, centreboard, daggerboard or leeboard, and it is the other half of the invention. The fin under the hull is a second foil working in water, and to generate lift it needs an angle of attack, which it gets because the boat is being pushed bodily sideways. The hull crabs a few degrees off its heading — leeway — and the keel, meeting the water at that small angle, produces a side force that opposes the rig's. Because water is some eight hundred times denser than air, a fin of modest area can balance a sail plan many times its size. The boat then travels along the resultant: forward, with a small persistent slip to leeward that the helmsman corrects for without thinking.

Sailing to windward is therefore a negotiation between two wings in two different fluids, each producing side force in opposite directions, with the boat squeezed forward between them. Remove either one and the whole thing collapses. A keelboat with its rudder and keel removed would make almost pure leeway; a boat with no sails and a perfect keel would sit still.

## What the Side Force Costs

The two opposing side forces are not applied at the same height. The rig's acts several metres above the water; the keel's acts a metre or two below it. They form a couple, and the boat heels. Resisting that couple is what ballast, beam, hiking straps, trapezes, water ballast, canting keels and the outrigged hulls of multihulls are all for, and the amount of sail a boat can usefully carry is set almost entirely by how much heeling moment its righting moment can answer.

Heel is not free even when it is survivable. A heeled rig tilts its force partly downward, so less of it drives the boat; the hull presents an asymmetric shape to the water, and the fuller immersed bow on the lee side generates a turning moment that pushes the boat up into the wind. That is weather helm. A few degrees of it are wanted — it loads the rudder enough to give the helmsman feel, the rudder blade itself contributes lift to windward, and a boat that rounds up when the tiller is dropped is a safer boat than one that bears away. Too much of it is pure drag, with the rudder held at a permanent angle, and the cure is nearly always to reduce heel rather than to fight the helm: ease the traveller, flatten the main, reef.

## Trim, Twist and the Slot

Wind near the surface is slowed by friction with the water, so the true wind at the masthead is stronger than at the boom. Combine that gradient with the boat's own speed and the apparent wind aloft is not only stronger but comes from further aft. A sail set at a single flat angle would therefore be stalled at the bottom or luffing at the top. Real sails are built and trimmed with twist, the upper sections sheeted progressively more open, and most of the controls on a modern boat exist to adjust the balance between overall sheeting angle and twist: mainsheet and vang pull the boom down, the traveller moves it across without changing leech tension, the jib lead fore and aft changes how much the sheet pulls down versus back. Telltales streaming on both sides of the luff tell the helmsman the flow is attached; a windward telltale lifting means the angle of attack has grown too large.

The gap between jib and mainsail is the other classic subject, and the folklore about it is wrong. The old story has air accelerating through the slot like a venturi and energizing the flow on the back of the main. In fact the flow in the gap is slowed relative to the free stream. The two sails act as a single multi-element foil: the jib sits in the upwash ahead of the main and works at a higher effective angle than it would alone, while the main, sitting in the jib's downwash, has its leading-edge suction peak reduced, which lets it be sheeted harder before the flow separates. Each makes the other better at its job, which is why a sloop's two small sails can outperform a single sail of the same total area.

## Beating, Reaching and Outrunning the Wind

No sailing craft goes directly upwind, so progress to windward is made by tacking, and the figure of merit is velocity made good — the component of boat speed in the direction you actually want. Sailing closer to the wind means less speed; sailing further off means more speed in a less useful direction. Somewhere between is an optimum, and it differs for every boat and every wind strength. A cruising keelboat may do best at around forty-five degrees off the true wind; a racing machine at thirty-five or better. Plotted as speed against wind angle for a range of wind strengths, this becomes the polar diagram, which is the honest description of what a given hull and rig can do.

Downwind the physics inverts. A sail used purely as a drag device can never push a boat faster than the wind, because once the boat matches the wind the apparent wind is zero and the force disappears. But a boat that refuses to run dead downwind and instead reaches across at forty or fifty degrees off keeps its apparent wind forward, keeps its sails working as foils rather than parachutes, and can sail a longer distance at enough extra speed to arrive sooner. Fast multihulls and foiling boats do this so effectively that they gybe downwind the way slower boats tack upwind. The limiting case is the ice boat, which has almost no resistance to overcome, sails at several times the true wind speed with its sail sheeted in nearly flat, and feels a screaming apparent wind on the bow that is very nearly its own creation.

Tall rigs win for the same reason long-winged gliders do: for a given sail area, greater height means less induced drag, and the upper part of the sail reaches stronger air. What stops designers from building rigs ever taller is the heeling moment, which grows with the height of the sail's centre of effort, and the structure needed to hold the thing up.

This is also, in retrospect, the whole story of the square rigger. A sail set on a yard across the ship, braced round as far as the standing rigging allows, cannot be trimmed anywhere near close enough to the centreline to work well on the wind; those ships were lucky to make good sixty or seventy degrees off the true wind, and they made their livings on routes chosen to keep the wind behind them. The trade wind circuits of the sailing merchant fleets were not tradition. They were the route that the geometry of the rig permitted.
