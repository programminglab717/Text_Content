# How Icebreakers Open a Route Through Sea Ice

The name suggests a blade, and that is the wrong picture. An icebreaker does not slice a floe the way a knife opens a sheet of plastic. It rides up onto the ice, balances there with several thousand tonnes of displacement bearing down on a small patch of frozen seawater, and waits for the sheet to fail in bending underneath it. The sound is not a crack so much as a long grinding groan that travels the length of the hull and into every bunk frame, so that anyone trying to sleep off watch learns the rhythm of it: lift, hesitate, collapse, surge, lift again.

Everything distinctive about these ships follows from that one mechanical fact. Sea ice is strong in compression and weak in bending. Shoving horizontally at a metre-thick sheet accomplishes very little, and a hull built to do that would need power out of all proportion to its size. Loading the sheet from above asks it to do the thing it is worst at. So the bow of an icebreaker is blunt and sloped rather than sharp — a ramp presented to the ice edge at a shallow angle, so that forward motion is converted into upward motion and the ship's own weight becomes the cutting tool.

## The Shape Below the Waterline

Look at an icebreaker in drydock and the first thing that registers is how little of it resembles a cargo ship. There is almost no flat bottom and almost no straight side. The bilges are rounded, the sides flare outward from the waterline, and the parallel middle body — the long unchanging box amidships that gives a tanker its cargo volume — has been shortened or eliminated. A conventional hull with vertical sides is a gift to converging ice: the floes close in, grip the flat plating, and hold the ship immobile. A rounded, outward-flaring hull responds to the same squeeze by being lifted, and ice that is trying to lift a ship is not trying to crush it.

Around the waterline runs the ice belt, a band of much thicker plating supported by closely spaced frames, extending well above and below the load line because the ship will roll and because the waterline itself changes with cargo. The plating there is not merely thicker steel but steel chosen for toughness at low temperature, since ordinary shipbuilding grades become brittle in the cold and a brittle hull is precisely what you do not want when it is being hammered.

Friction matters as much as strength. A sheet that has been broken still has to be pushed aside and down under the advancing hull, and broken ice sliding along steel is a large fraction of the total resistance. Hulls are painted with hard, slick ice coatings that are reapplied often because the ice sands them off. Many ships carry air-bubbling systems that blow low-pressure air out of ports below the waterline to create a lubricating curtain of bubbles, or water-wash systems that flood the ice edge ahead of the bow. Nearly all of them have heeling tanks — a pair of tanks port and starboard connected by pumps that can throw hundreds of tonnes of water from one side to the other in under a minute, rolling the ship deliberately to break the grip of ice on the sides.

Propulsion is chosen for a duty cycle that would destroy an ordinary drivetrain. Propellers stall, foul on ice blocks, and sometimes lose a blade, so blades are frequently bolted on individually in stainless steel to be replaced at sea or at a pier rather than cast integral with the hub. The machinery behind them is almost always electric: diesel engines or a nuclear reactor generate electricity, and electric motors turn the shafts. An electric drive delivers full torque from a standstill, tolerates a propeller being stopped dead by an ice block without stalling the prime mover, and reverses in seconds. Russia has operated nuclear-powered icebreakers since the late 1950s, beginning with the Lenin, the first nuclear-powered surface ship of any kind, and a nuclear plant suits the job unusually well: enormous sustained power with no need to refuel at ports that may be a thousand miles of frozen coastline away.

## Working a Passage

In ice a ship can handle, the technique is called continuous breaking. The vessel simply keeps moving, the bow riding up and the sheet failing ahead of it in a steady procession of plates that tip up, roll under, and grind down the sides. Speed stays low, perhaps a few knots, and the channel left behind fills with broken pieces known as brash.

When the ice thickens or a pressure ridge blocks the way, the ship shifts to ramming: back off several ship-lengths, run up at the obstruction, drive the bow as far onto it as it will go, and if that fails, back off and do it again into the same notch. A bad ridge can take dozens of charges. This is where the heeling tanks earn their keep, because the surest way to end a ramming sequence badly is to get the bow stuck on top of the ridge with the ship immovable and the ice closing in behind — to be beset, in the old word.

Some modern ships solve the problem by turning around. Double-acting designs, developed largely in Finland, run bow-first in open water like any efficient merchant vessel, and then, when the ice gets serious, turn and steam astern. The stern is shaped for breaking, and the azimuthing pods that drive the ship can be rotated to direct their propeller wash directly at the ice, flushing broken pieces away and milling through rubble that a conventional bow would never manage. The same trick lets ice-capable tankers and gas carriers serve Arctic terminals for much of the year without an escort at all.

Escort work is its own craft. An icebreaker leading a convoy has to judge a channel width that its charges can use, keep a speed that does not let the channel refreeze or close before the last ship is through, and manage the gap behind it, because a merchant ship that falls too far back will find brash congealed into something nearly as hard as the original sheet. In the worst conditions the escort takes a ship in close tow, winching the merchant's bow into a notch cut into the icebreaker's stern so the two move as one articulated vessel.

## Reading the Ice

None of this is much use without knowing what is ahead, and ice is not uniform. First-year ice — a single winter's growth, still salty and relatively soft — is what most ice-capable ships are designed for. Multiyear ice has survived a summer, drained most of its brine, and become dramatically harder and more elastic; a floe of it is a different material, not just a thicker one. Older navigators learned to read the colour, the blue of well-drained old ice against the grey of a first-year sheet.

Thickness measured on level ice also misleads, because level ice is rare. Wind and current drive floes together, and where they meet they build pressure ridges: a rubble wall above the surface and, far more significantly, a keel extending several times as deep below it. Ridges are what stop ships. So do the compressive fields where ice is being driven against a coast, in which a lead that was open water an hour ago closes on the ship like a vice.

Modern ice navigation runs on satellite radar imagery, which sees through cloud and polar darkness, combined with charts issued by national ice services that code age and concentration into a standardised egg-shaped symbol. But the interpretation still falls to an ice pilot or a master with years in the trade, standing high in a forward-facing bridge that runs the full width of the ship, choosing between a lead that may pinch shut and a field that will cost fuel but not risk.

## The Shortcut That Has Not Taken Over

Arctic sea ice has thinned and retreated enough over recent decades that the Northern Sea Route along the Russian coast is navigable for a lengthening season, and the distance saving between northern Europe and East Asia compared with the Suez route is genuinely large. The traffic has grown, especially for cargoes that originate in the Arctic itself: liquefied natural gas from the Yamal peninsula, nickel, oil.

What has not happened is a general migration of container shipping north, and the reasons are mostly not about ice at all. Container lines sell schedule reliability, which is the one thing a route with variable ice cannot offer. An ice-strengthened hull costs more to build and burns more fuel in open water because of its blunter, heavier form. Escort and insurance are expensive. Search and rescue assets, salvage tugs, spill response equipment, and repair yards are thin to nonexistent over thousands of miles. Crews need specific training, which the international Polar Code now formalises along with survival equipment, stability calculations against topside icing, and limits on how far a ship may go from safety. Above roughly seventy-five degrees north the geostationary communications satellites most merchant ships rely on sink below the horizon, so polar-orbiting systems become necessary. The heaviest, dirtiest bunker fuels are being pushed out of Arctic waters, partly because soot landing on snow darkens it and accelerates the melt, and partly because a spill of heavy fuel oil under ice is a problem for which no one has a convincing answer.

So the shortcut stays what it has always been: a specialist route, worked by specialist ships, in seasons chosen carefully, with an icebreaker somewhere within call.
