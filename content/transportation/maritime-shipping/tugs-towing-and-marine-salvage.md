# Tugs, Towing and Marine Salvage

A container ship coming up a river to her berth is three hundred metres of steel with an engine designed to push her efficiently in a straight line across an ocean and a rudder that does almost nothing below about four knots. She has no brakes. Her propeller, turned astern, will pull her stern sideways as much as it slows her. Alongside this object, looking absurdly small against it, a tug of perhaps thirty metres puts her bow against the hull plating and begins to push. Within a minute the big ship is swinging, and she is swinging at a rate the tug's master chose.

Nothing about that is obvious from watching it. A modern harbour tug is one of the most concentrated pieces of marine engineering afloat — most of her volume is machinery, her hull is designed around stability rather than speed, and her crew are doing something considerably more dangerous than it looks.

## What Makes a Tug a Tug

The number that describes a tug is bollard pull, not horsepower, and it is measured rather than calculated: the tug is tied by her towline to a heavy shore bollard fitted with a load cell, told to go to full power, and the steady pull she achieves is recorded in tonnes. What matters at zero speed is thrust, and thrust is produced by accelerating a mass of water astern. A large, slow-turning propeller shifts a lot of water gently and is far more efficient at this than a small fast one, which is why tug propellers look oversized and are usually shrouded in a Kort nozzle — a ducted ring that both protects the blades and adds thrust by recovering energy from the accelerated flow.

Manoeuvrability is the other half. A conventional single-screw tug can only push in the direction she is pointing, and turning her takes time nobody has in a confined basin. Two solutions became standard. The Voith Schneider unit hangs a set of vertical blades beneath the hull on a rotating disc, varying each blade's angle through its circuit so that thrust can be directed anywhere in the horizontal plane, at full magnitude, almost instantly. The azimuth stern drive uses a pair of steerable pods that rotate through a full circle. Either arrangement lets a tug hold station against wind and current, swing her thrust from ahead to abeam without turning her hull, and get out of trouble fast.

Tugs come in recognisable families. Harbour ship-handling tugs are short, deep, and enormously powerful for their size. Escort tugs are similar but built to work at speed alongside a laden tanker in a channel, ready to steer or stop her if she loses steering. Ocean-going tugs are larger, with fuel for weeks at sea, a big towing winch, and accommodation for a delivery crew. Anchor-handling vessels working offshore oil fields are tugs with an open stern and the deck gear to drag multi-tonne anchors around. And on the American inland rivers the towboat is not really a tug at all but a pusher: a squared-off bow fitted with vertical towknees, lashed rigidly by wire and ratchet into a raft of barges that may be a quarter of a mile long and is steered as one very long, very unforgiving vessel.

## The Way Tugs Get Killed

The characteristic fatal accident in towage is girting, sometimes called girding, and understanding it explains most of how tugs are designed and handled. A tug is made fast to a ship by a line from a point near her own centre. If the ship moves ahead while the tug is off to one side, the towline comes abeam and starts dragging the tug sideways through the water. A hull dragged sideways meets enormous resistance, so it heels. Once heeled, the towline's pull is applied above the effective pivot point and the heeling gets worse, and a tug can be rolled flat and downflooded in seconds by a ship whose bridge team has not even noticed.

Everything about a tug is arranged against this. Low freeboard aft and a high righting moment. Towing points positioned to reduce the capsizing lever — one of the main arguments for tractor-type tugs, where the propulsion units are forward and the tow is taken from aft. Gog ropes and rendering hooks to control the line's angle. And above all a quick-release mechanism on the towing hook or winch that can be fired from the wheelhouse by the person watching the angle, because the only reliable answer to girting is to stop being attached.

The same physics, used deliberately, is the basis of indirect towing. An escort tug astern of a moving ship can sheer out to one side and hold herself at an angle to the water flowing past, so that her hull and skeg act as a hydrofoil. The lift generated by that flow is transmitted up the towline as a steering or braking force on the ship, and at a decent speed it can be several times the tug's own bollard pull. The tug is borrowing energy from the ship's own motion to control her. It requires precise handling, because the difference between an indirect tow and a girting is largely a matter of whether the tug's master meant it.

## Towage Is a Contract; Salvage Is Not

The legal distinction here is sharp and old. Towage is an ordinary commercial service: an agreed job at an agreed price, performed under a contract signed in advance, and the tug gets paid whether the job goes well or badly. Salvage is something else. It is a voluntary service rendered to a vessel or cargo in genuine peril, and the salvor's reward is contingent on success. No cure, no pay. Spend a fortnight and a million in fuel trying to save a burning ship, lose her, and you collect nothing.

In exchange for that risk, a successful salvage award is not calculated as a fee for work done but as a reward, assessed afterwards with the value of the property saved, the degree of danger, the skill shown, and the salvor's own exposure all weighed in. It is one of the few places in commercial law where a court or arbitrator deliberately awards more than costs plus a margin, on the explicit policy ground that society wants competent people willing to go out in bad weather on speculation.

The standard instrument is Lloyd's Open Form, a famously short document that a ship's master can sign over the radio in the middle of a casualty without lawyers present. It settles nothing about money; it simply binds both sides to the no-cure-no-pay principle and refers the amount to arbitration in London later. That is exactly what a master in trouble needs — an agreement he can reach in two minutes while his engine room floods.

The pure form had one perverse consequence. A rusting, low-value ship carrying heavy fuel oil toward a coastline is a disaster for everyone and worth almost nothing if saved, so a salvor working on no-cure-no-pay had every financial reason to stay home. The 1989 salvage convention addressed this by creating a right to special compensation where a salvor works on a vessel threatening environmental damage, and the industry built the practical version of it into an add-on clause that lets a salvor invoke an agreed tariff of day rates for personnel and equipment, paid by the shipowner's liability insurer, with the salvage award assessed separately. It converts an all-or-nothing gamble into something a salvage company can actually price.

## Getting a Ship Back

The work itself is less exotic than the contracts. Most casualties are groundings, and most groundings are resolved by making the ship lighter and pulling in the right direction at the right state of tide. Lightering — transferring cargo or bunkers to a barge — is usually the first move, because every tonne removed reduces the ground reaction holding the hull in place. Ballast is shifted, sometimes counterintuitively, to rock a hull free or to keep it from working itself further up the bank.

Where tugs alone cannot supply enough pull, salvors lay beach gear: heavy anchors run out astern by boat, connected through multi-part purchases to winches on the casualty's own deck, so that the stranded ship hauls on herself. It is slow, powerful, and almost entirely mechanical.

Flooded hulls are patched and dewatered, or in some cases raised on compressed air injected into sealed compartments. A ship lying on her side may be parbuckled — rotated upright around a longitudinal axis by strand jacks pulling on cables passed beneath and over the hull against a prepared seabed platform, which is how the Costa Concordia was brought upright off Giglio before being refloated on sponsons. And where nothing can be saved, salvage becomes wreck removal, a different trade with different law and different economics: cut the hull into sections underwater with abrasive cutting wire, lift them onto a barge, and deliver the coastline back clear.

The commercial shape of all this is strange. Salvage companies maintain expensive tugs, pumps, and rapid-response teams that earn nothing at all until something goes badly wrong somewhere, then bid against each other to be first on scene in a storm. Several governments, unwilling to rely on that market alone near dangerous coasts, keep emergency towing vessels on permanent station at chokepoints — a public tug waiting, year after year, for a casualty nobody wants.
