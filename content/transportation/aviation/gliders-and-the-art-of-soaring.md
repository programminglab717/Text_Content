# Gliders and the Art of Soaring

The tow release makes a small metallic bang, the rope snaps away forward, and the noise stops. What is left is the hiss of air over the canopy and, in your peripheral vision, an altimeter that has begun, very slowly, to unwind. Two thousand feet above a patchwork of fields. From here the aircraft has no way of gaining height except by finding air that is already going up faster than the glider is sinking through it, and the next four or five hours depend entirely on whether you can keep finding it.

That constraint is what makes soaring a craft rather than a form of transport. A powered pilot navigates; a glider pilot negotiates. The sky is not uniform, and learning to see its structure — where the air is rising, where it is sinking, and how long either condition will last — is a skill that takes years and never stops improving.

## Three Ways Air Goes Up

The most common and least reliable source is the thermal. Sunlight heats the ground unevenly; a ploughed field, a quarry, a town, a south-facing slope all warm faster than the woodland or wet pasture beside them. Air over the warm patch becomes buoyant and eventually detaches as a bubble or a column, rising until it reaches a level where it is no longer warmer than its surroundings. If it carries enough moisture, condensation marks the top with a cumulus cloud, and that cloud becomes a signpost visible from twenty miles away. If the air is dry, the thermal is invisible and you hunt for it by reading the ground and by feel.

Ridge lift is simpler and far more dependable. Wind meeting a hill has nowhere to go but up, and the band of rising air along the windward face can be worked all day as long as the wind holds. British and Appalachian pilots have flown enormous distances by hopping along ridge systems, beating back and forth in a band a few hundred feet deep, close enough to the slope to see individual sheep.

Mountain wave is the strange one. When a stable airmass is pushed over a range by a wind that strengthens and holds direction with height, it does not simply flow over and settle. It oscillates downwind, like water over a submerged rock, in a standing train of smooth rising and sinking bands that can extend far into the stratosphere. The rising portions are glassy and powerful — climbs of many metres per second in air so smooth the instruments seem broken. Lens-shaped lenticular clouds mark the crests and sit motionless in a gale, because the cloud is not travelling with the air; it forms at the upwind edge of the crest and evaporates at the downwind edge as the same air rises, cools, condenses, descends and dries. Beneath the first crest lies the rotor, a horizontal roll of violently turbulent air that has broken bones and taken wings off aircraft, and getting through it is the price of admission.

There are others: sea breeze fronts where cool marine air undercuts warm inland air along a line that can run for a hundred miles, convergence zones over peninsulas, the leading edge of a thunderstorm's outflow, which is dramatic and best left alone.

## Working a Thermal

Thermals are not tidy columns. They lean with the wind, they pulse, they are strongest in the middle and often ringed by sink, and they drift downwind while you are inside them, so the climb is a moving target.

The primary instrument is the variometer, which measures rate of climb and descent. A raw vario is nearly useless for this purpose because it responds to the glider's own manoeuvring — pull the stick back and you zoom upward, and the instrument dutifully reports a climb that is simply your kinetic energy converting into height. Total energy compensation fixes it. A small venturi probe on the fin senses airspeed change and cancels the stick-induced component, so the instrument reports only movement of the air mass. Pilots also run a netto or relative display, which further subtracts the glider's own sink rate and shows what the air is doing in absolute terms, which is what you need to decide whether a patch is worth turning in.

Entry technique has been reduced to something teachable. You feel the wing lift on one side, count a beat, and turn toward it. Once established in a circle, you note where in the turn the vario reads strongest and shift the circle in that direction — roll a little shallower for a few seconds as you pass through the weak side, which displaces the whole circle toward the strong side. Good pilots do this continuously and almost unconsciously, tightening in the core and widening where the lift is broad. The difference between a pilot who averages two metres a second in a thermal and one who averages one is not courage or aircraft quality; it is centering.

Where to look is its own study. Cumulus clouds are the obvious answer, but they have life cycles, and a beautiful crisp cloud with a dark flat base and hard edges is a working thermal, while the same cloud fifteen minutes later with ragged edges and a wispy base is a corpse still hanging in the sky. Under a blue sky, you read the ground instead — the dark field, the industrial estate, the lee edge of a lake where the breeze converges. Birds are the best instrument ever devised. A buzzard circling without flapping is doing exactly what you are trying to do and has more practice.

## Speed to Fly, and Why Going Slowly Is Usually Wrong

Every glider has a polar curve: sink rate plotted against airspeed. Fly too slowly and induced drag and the approach of the stall drive the sink rate up; fly too fast and parasitic drag does the same. Between the extremes sit two useful points. Minimum sink speed gives the slowest descent and is what you fly while circling in lift. Best glide speed, slightly faster, gives the shallowest angle through still air and is what you fly when you simply need to reach something. A modern racing glider will give better than forty feet forward for every foot of height lost, and the big open-class machines with long, slender wings do considerably better than that, with sink rates under a metre per second.

The elegant result, worked out most famously by the engineer Paul MacCready, concerns how fast to fly between thermals. The intuition says fly at best glide to lose as little height as possible. The intuition is wrong, because in cross-country flying the currency is not height but time, and height is cheap if you are confident of getting more of it. If you expect a strong climb at the far end of the glide, you should fly the intervening distance fast, arrive lower, and take the height back quickly. If you expect weak lift, slow down and hoard. The optimum speed for any given expected rate of climb falls out of the polar curve geometrically, and it also tells you to speed up in sinking air and ease off in rising air, which is why a well-flown glider's airspeed varies constantly.

The same logic explains water ballast. Fill the wing tanks and the glider is heavier; it flies the same shape of polar curve but shifted to higher speeds. The best glide ratio is unchanged, it simply occurs faster. In strong conditions this is a large advantage, because the cruise between thermals is quicker while the climbs are only slightly worse. In weak conditions it is a liability, and the pilot dumps the water in a white plume behind the wings — always before landing, because a heavy glider lands fast and an unevenly draining pair of tanks is a handling problem you do not want near the ground.

## Crossing Country

A cross-country flight is a chain of glides and climbs, and the art consists of never quite being forced to accept a bad link. The expert's habit is to keep options ahead: two clouds in view rather than one, a ridge within reach, a known good landing field if everything fails.

Certain conditions allow something better than the climb-and-glide rhythm. When wind and instability line up, thermals organise into cloud streets — long parallel rows that can run for tens of miles — and a glider can fly beneath a street almost without circling, pulling up gently in each patch of lift and pushing over through the gaps. This is dolphin flying, and on the right day it produces average speeds that embarrass light aircraft.

The flight ends with the final glide, the one piece of pure arithmetic in the sport. From the last thermal you need enough height to reach home, allowing for the wind, the sink on the way, and a margin for being wrong. Take too little and you land in a field with the airfield in sight; take too much and you have wasted minutes climbing for height you will throw away in the circuit. Glide computers do the sums now, but the judgment about how much margin the day deserves is still the pilot's.

## Landing in a Field Is Part of the Plan

Every cross-country glider pilot lands out eventually. Treating it as a failure is how people get hurt; treating it as a normal manoeuvre with a decision height is how people do not.

The discipline is to stop soaring at a pre-chosen altitude — not when the last thermal fails, but comfortably above it — and commit the remaining height to inspecting and flying a circuit to a chosen field. Selection favours size, surface, slope and wind, roughly in that order, with a strong preference for landing uphill and into wind if the two can be reconciled. Crops are read like a chart: cut grass and stubble are good, standing wheat will stop the glider hard, and root crops are furrowed in a way that can rip a wing. The real killer is wires, which are invisible from the air at any useful distance. You do not look for the wires. You look for the poles, and then you assume the wires are there.

Gliding grew up, in its modern form, on the bare hills of the Rhön in Germany in the years after the First World War, when restrictions on powered aviation pushed a generation of engineers and enthusiasts toward flight without engines and they discovered, almost by accident, that a slope in a steady wind would hold a sailplane up indefinitely. Everything since has been the extension of that discovery into weather systems those pioneers could not have reached. Pressurised research sailplanes riding Andean wave amplified by the polar vortex have climbed above seventy thousand feet, into air thin enough that the wings need most of a runway's width. Distance flights along the Andes have run past two thousand kilometres in a day. The machine is still a glider. It still has no engine, and it still goes up only because the pilot has correctly guessed where the air is going.
