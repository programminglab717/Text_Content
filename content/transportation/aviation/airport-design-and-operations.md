# Airport Design and Operations

Every airport begins with a question about wind. Before anyone draws a terminal or argues about parking revenue, a planner collects years of local weather observations and builds a wind rose — a circular plot showing how often the wind blows from each direction and how hard. Aircraft want to take off and land into the wind, because headwind is free airspeed and crosswind is a nuisance that becomes a hazard past a certain strength. The runway gets aligned with the direction that will let the largest possible share of the year's wind be handled head-on. If no single alignment covers enough of it, the airport gets a second runway at an angle, a crosswind runway, which will sit idle most of the time and earn its keep on the days it doesn't.

That is why runway numbers are what they are. A runway is named for its magnetic heading rounded to the nearest ten degrees and divided by ten, so a strip pointing roughly northwest and southeast is Runway 31 from one end and Runway 13 from the other, the two numbers always differing by eighteen. Parallel runways take an L, C or R suffix. And because the Earth's magnetic field wanders, airports occasionally have to repaint their numbers and reprint their charts when the drift accumulates past the rounding — an expensive, faintly absurd chore that arrives every few decades.

## The Airside Geometry

Runway length is not a matter of taste. It comes out of the performance of the aircraft the airport intends to serve, adjusted for field elevation, summer temperature, and the runway's own slope, because thin air and hot air both lengthen a takeoff roll considerably. Airports in the mountains and airports in the tropics need runways that would look extravagant at sea level in a cold climate. Published for each runway is a set of declared distances that pilots use in their calculations — how much pavement is available for the takeoff roll, how much for stopping after a rejected takeoff, how much for landing — and these are often different numbers for the same strip of concrete, because displaced thresholds and obstacle clearance requirements carve them up differently.

Beyond the paved ends lie safety areas, graded and cleared so that an aircraft overrunning or landing short has somewhere survivable to go. Where geography makes a long safety area impossible — a runway that ends at a seawall or a highway — some airports install beds of engineered crushable concrete blocks that collapse progressively under an aircraft's wheels and bring it to a stop in a few hundred feet without the passengers being seriously hurt.

The taxiway network is the part travelers see most and think about least. A full-length parallel taxiway is the single biggest capacity improvement a one-runway airport can make, because without it every arrival must back-taxi down the runway and block it. High-speed exits, angled forward at around thirty degrees and placed where the design aircraft will naturally have slowed enough to use them, let an arrival clear the runway seconds sooner, and seconds compound into landings per hour. Taxiway curves are drawn with generous fillets because an airliner's main gear tracks well inside the path of its nose gear, and a pilot steering the nose wheel neatly along the centerline can still drag a main gear into the dirt on a tight turn.

Lighting encodes all of this at night and in fog. Runway edge lights are white; the last stretch before the far end turns amber as a warning. Centerline lights alternate red and white as the remaining distance shrinks, then go solid red. Taxiway edges are blue and taxiway centerlines are green. Approach lighting stretches out beyond the threshold in a long bar-and-rail pattern that gives a descending crew the horizon and alignment cues they cannot get from the runway alone, and a small array of lights beside the touchdown zone shows red or white depending on whether the aircraft is above or below the correct glide path. Places where the taxiway layout has historically confused people get marked as hot spots on the chart, and increasingly get illuminated stop bars that a controller switches off to grant permission to cross.

The pavement itself is engineered to a standard most highway work never approaches. Airfield concrete and asphalt are designed around the load a single main-gear wheel imposes and how many times a year it will be imposed, which is why a runway built for regional jets cannot simply accept a loaded wide-body for a diversion without someone checking the numbers first. Each pavement is published with a strength rating, each aircraft with a corresponding load rating, and the two are compared before an unusual movement is approved. Runway surfaces are also cut with shallow transverse grooves a few centimetres apart, which give water somewhere to escape during heavy rain and keep a landing aircraft's tires in contact with the pavement instead of riding up on a film of water. Airports measure surface friction periodically with instrumented trailers, and in winter they report contamination — depth and type of snow, slush or ice — in a standardized form that crews feed directly into their landing performance calculations. A runway that is legally open can still be unusable for a particular aircraft at a particular weight, and that judgment belongs to the crew rather than the airport.

## Terminals as Machines for Queueing

Terminal design is a compromise between two incompatible desires: passengers want to walk as little as possible, and airlines want as many gates as possible adjacent to as few duplicated facilities as possible. The simplest form is linear — a long building with aircraft parked along one face — which minimizes walking and works beautifully until you need more gates than the frontage allows. Piers or fingers extend into the apron to multiply frontage at the cost of long walks down corridors. Satellites place a cluster of gates on a remote island served by an underground train, which decouples gate count from terminal size entirely and is why every very large hub eventually builds one.

The deeper split is between airports that mostly originate and terminate passengers and airports that mostly connect them. An origin-and-destination airport optimizes for curbside access, check-in throughput, and security screening capacity, because almost everyone arrives from outside. A connecting hub optimizes for the walk between gates and the transfer of checked baggage, because most of its passengers never touch the curb. Hub airlines schedule in banks — a wave of arrivals, a frantic ninety minutes of connections, a wave of departures — which means the terminal must be sized for a peak that occurs a few times a day and sits half empty in between. The minimum connecting time published for a hub is essentially a claim about the worst-case walk plus the baggage system's throughput, and it drives what itineraries can even be sold.

Baggage handling is the least visible and most failure-prone system in the building. A checked bag goes from a counter belt into a sorting network of conveyors and tilting trays, gets its tag read, gets screened by equipment that is now built into the line rather than bolted on beside it, and is routed to a chute for its flight. Transfer bags are the hard case: they arrive on an aircraft with limited time, must be identified, re-sorted and delivered across the field to a different gate, and every minute of that journey has to fit inside the connection time the airline advertised. Denver's airport famously spent years and an enormous sum trying to automate the whole process with a network of independent carts before abandoning most of it, an object lesson in what happens when a baggage system is designed in parallel with the building rather than after it.

## The Turnaround

An aircraft on the ground earns nothing, so the choreography at the gate is rehearsed and unforgiving. As the jet bridge swings into place, a fuel truck or an underground hydrant connection is already approaching, along with a tug, a belt loader or container lifter, a cabin service crew, a catering truck with a scissor lift, a lavatory service cart and a potable water cart. On a short-haul turnaround the whole sequence — everyone off, cabin cleaned, galleys swapped, tanks filled, holds emptied and refilled, everyone on, doors closed — is expected to take well under an hour, and the low-cost carriers that built their economics on faster turns achieve it partly by operating a single aircraft type, avoiding hubs, and not carrying many connecting bags.

Weather adds whole systems. Deicing in winter means spraying heated glycol mixtures to strip contamination off the wings, then often a thicker anti-icing fluid that clings and protects for a limited holdover time that shrinks as precipitation intensifies. Airports that can afford it build remote deicing pads near the runway so the clock starts as late as possible, rather than treating aircraft at the gate and then watching them queue for twenty minutes. Snow clearance runs to an entire fleet of specialized plows and brooms working the runway in an echelon formation, because closing a runway to clear it costs more in delay than the equipment costs to buy.

Then there are the hazards that come with putting a large flat grassland in the middle of a region. Birds are a genuine threat to turbine engines, and airports manage them actively: keeping grass at a height that discourages the species that like open ground, draining standing water, removing berry-bearing landscaping, and dispatching staff with pyrotechnics or trained falcons when a flock settles where it shouldn't. Fire cover is regulated by aircraft size, with crews required to reach any point on the movement area within a few minutes and specialized vehicles carrying foam capable of covering a fuel spill fast enough for an evacuation to finish.

What limits a big airport in the end is rarely the terminal. It is the number of aircraft movements the runways can absorb in an hour, a number that varies with weather, with the mix of aircraft, and with whether departures or arrivals are being favored at the moment. At the most congested airports, that capacity is rationed as slots — permissions to use the runway in a given time window — which are allocated through a coordination process, defended fiercely by incumbent airlines, and traded for sums that make it clear the real asset was never the building.
