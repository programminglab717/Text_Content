# How Helicopters Fly

Watch a helicopter hover and the illusion is that it is being held up by a fan. It is not. The rotor is a pair of long, skinny wings that have given up on the idea of the whole aircraft moving forward and instead move themselves, sweeping around a mast fast enough to generate flying speed over their own surfaces while the machine below them sits still in the air. Everything strange about helicopters follows from that substitution. A fixed-wing airplane has one airspeed, shared by both wings. A helicopter has a different airspeed at every point on the disc, changing continuously as each blade goes around, and the entire design exists to manage the consequences.

## Two Controls and a Swashplate

A pilot in a helicopter has three sets of controls and uses all of them simultaneously, which is why hovering is famously difficult to learn and why students spend their first hours drifting all over an airfield while the instructor talks them down.

The left hand holds the collective, a lever that looks like a parking brake and rises from the floor. Pulling it up increases the pitch angle of every blade at once, by the same amount, at every point in its rotation. More pitch means more lift, and also more drag, so the collective is mechanically or electronically tied to the engine's fuel control: raise the lever and power comes in automatically to hold rotor speed constant. Rotor RPM is the one number a helicopter pilot cannot afford to let sag, because the blades are storing energy as well as making lift, and a drooping rotor loses both at once.

The right hand holds the cyclic, a stick between the knees that tilts the whole rotor disc. It does this without any part of the rotor hub physically tipping over: instead the cyclic changes each blade's pitch once per revolution, adding pitch on one side of the circle and subtracting it on the other, so the blades climb slightly as they pass through one region and descend through the opposite one. The blade tips then trace a tilted plane rather than a level one, and the rotor's thrust vector tilts with it. Push the stick forward and the disc tilts forward; part of the rotor's thrust now pulls the machine ahead, and the rest holds it up.

The mechanism that converts a stationary stick into a pitch command that rotates with the blades is the swashplate, and it is the cleverest piece of hardware on the aircraft. It consists of two plates stacked around the mast on a ball joint: a lower one that can slide up and down and tilt but does not turn, connected to the pilot's controls, and an upper one riding on a bearing that turns with the rotor and connects by pitch links to each blade's grip. Slide the whole assembly up, and every blade gains pitch equally — that is the collective. Tilt it, and each blade gets a high pitch command at one azimuth and a low one half a turn later — that is the cyclic. One assembly, two functions, mechanically superimposed.

There is one wrinkle that confuses everyone the first time they meet it. The blade does not reach its maximum displacement where it receives its maximum pitch. It reaches it roughly ninety degrees of rotation later, because a blade free to flap is being driven near its own natural frequency and responds a quarter cycle behind the input. Designers compensate by rigging the linkages ahead by that amount, so that a stick pushed forward produces a disc that tilts forward rather than sideways. The pilot never sees it. The rigging hides the phase lag entirely.

## The Torque Problem

Spin a heavy rotor with an engine bolted to an airframe and the airframe tries to spin the other way. This is not a subtlety; a single-rotor helicopter with no compensation would simply turn itself into a carousel. The classic answer is the tail rotor, a small propeller mounted sideways at the end of a long boom, pushing air laterally to produce a countering moment. It is controlled by the pedals, which change its pitch rather than its speed, letting the pilot hold a heading or deliberately pivot the nose while hovering in one spot.

The tail rotor is expensive in every sense. It consumes a meaningful fraction of engine power that does nothing to hold the aircraft up. It is fragile, it is at head height for people walking behind a landed machine, and its failure is an emergency. It also produces a sideways force that the whole helicopter drifts toward unless the designer cants the mast slightly or the pilot holds a little opposing cyclic, which is why many helicopters hover with one skid low.

Other configurations dodge the problem by having no net torque to cancel. Tandem-rotor machines put two large rotors fore and aft turning opposite directions, and use differential control between them for pitch and yaw. Coaxial designs stack two rotors on one mast, turning against each other. Intermeshing rotors sit on two masts angled apart, their blades passing through each other's discs in careful synchronization. A few aircraft blow compressed air out slots along the tail boom and a steerable nozzle at the end instead of carrying a tail rotor at all. All of these trade mechanical complexity for the elimination of a vulnerable, power-hungry appendage.

## Why a Helicopter Cannot Go Very Fast

In forward flight, the blade sweeping forward into the oncoming air sees its own rotational speed plus the aircraft's airspeed. Half a turn later, the same blade retreating downwind sees rotational speed minus airspeed. If nothing corrected for this, the advancing side would make far more lift than the retreating side and the machine would roll over on its back within a second of moving off. Juan de la Cierva discovered exactly this while developing autogyros in the 1920s, and his solution — hinging each blade at the root so it is free to flap up and down — made practical rotary-wing flight possible for everyone who came after.

A flapping blade solves the problem automatically. As the advancing blade makes excess lift it rises, and rising into the airflow reduces its own angle of attack, which sheds the excess. The retreating blade droops, which increases its angle of attack and recovers some of its deficit. The rotor equalizes itself continuously, a thousand times a minute, without anyone commanding it. Modern rotors often achieve the same freedom without physical hinges, using composite flexures that bend by design.

But the equalization has a limit, and it sets the top speed of every conventional helicopter. The retreating blade, seeing less and less airflow as the aircraft goes faster, must take on more and more angle of attack to keep up, until the inboard portion of it stalls. Retreating blade stall announces itself as vibration, a pitch-up tendency and a roll, and it is the reason helicopters cruise at speeds an airliner would consider taxiing. At the same time the advancing tip is approaching the speed of sound, where compressibility drives up drag and noise. The rotor is squeezed from both ends, and no amount of engine power widens the gap.

## Hovering, and the Ways It Goes Wrong

Hovering close to the ground is easier than hovering high up, because the ground interrupts the rotor's downwash and reduces the induced flow through the disc. A helicopter can often hover in ground effect at a weight that would not let it hover a hundred feet higher, which matters enormously to pilots operating in mountains or on hot days. Drifting forward out of a hover produces a similar bonus: once the aircraft is moving at roughly the speed of a brisk cycle, the rotor begins working in undisturbed air rather than recirculating its own wake, and lift improves noticeably for the same power. Pilots feel this as a shudder and then a small surge of climb, and it is why a heavily loaded helicopter may take off by sliding forward rather than rising straight up.

Descending vertically is the dangerous version of the same physics. Come down slowly into the column of air the rotor has just pushed downward, and the blades can end up churning a doughnut of recirculating flow that carries no fresh air through the disc. Lift falls, the pilot instinctively raises collective, and the additional pitch feeds the vortex instead of the climb. The machine sinks faster while burning more power. The escape is lateral, not vertical: fly out of the disturbed column sideways or forward into clean air.

The most reassuring fact about helicopters is what happens when the engine quits. The drive is connected through a freewheeling unit, so a stopped engine does not stop the rotor. The pilot immediately lowers the collective, and air begins flowing upward through the disc rather than down, driving the blades like a windmill and keeping them spinning on nothing but the aircraft's descent. The machine glides, steeply but controllably, and near the ground the pilot flares to slow the descent and then trades the rotor's stored rotational energy for one last cushion of lift. Done properly, an autorotation ends with the aircraft on its skids and the rotor almost stopped. It requires altitude and airspeed to set up, which is why every helicopter manual contains a shaded region of low height and low speed that pilots are trained to pass through quickly and never linger inside.
