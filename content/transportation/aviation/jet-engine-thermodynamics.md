# Jet Engine Thermodynamics

Mechanics have a crude but accurate mnemonic for what happens inside a jet engine: suck, squeeze, bang, blow. Air is pulled in, squeezed to high pressure, mixed with fuel and set alight, and the resulting hot gas blows out the back fast enough to shove a two-hundred-ton airliner through the sky. It sounds almost too simple for a machine that took decades of metallurgy and fluid dynamics to perfect, but the four-word version is not wrong. It is the thermodynamic cycle underneath — named the Brayton cycle, after the American engineer who patented an early version of it for a piston engine — running continuously rather than in the intermittent strokes of a car engine, and continuous operation turns out to matter enormously for how smoothly and efficiently the whole thing works.

## Taking In Air

The intake looks like the passive part of the engine, a plain duct, but at cruising speed it is doing real aerodynamic work. Air arriving at several hundred knots must be slowed and diffused before it reaches the compressor, because compressor blades are designed to handle a fairly narrow range of inlet speeds efficiently. The duct's shape, gradually widening from front to back, converts some of that incoming velocity into pressure before the air ever reaches moving machinery, a free first stage of compression paid for entirely by the aircraft's forward speed.

On most modern airliners the air splits here into two paths, and this split defines what kind of engine it is. A large fan at the very front, itself driven by the engine's turbine, pushes a portion of the incoming air around the outside of the engine's hot core entirely, through a bypass duct, to rejoin the exhaust stream near the back. Only the remaining air enters the core to be compressed, burned, and expanded through the turbine. The ratio between the bypassed air and the core air is the bypass ratio, and it has crept steadily upward over the decades because moving a large mass of air modestly faster produces the same thrust as moving a small mass of air a great deal faster, while burning far less fuel and making far less noise. The high-bypass turbofans on a modern wide-body airliner might move several times as much air around the core as through it.

## Squeezing the Air

Air entering the engine's core passes through a compressor built from alternating rows of spinning and stationary blades, arranged in stages, each stage raising the air's pressure a little further than the last. An axial compressor — so called because air flows straight through along the engine's axis rather than being flung outward — can have a dozen or more stages on a large engine, and by the time air exits the last one it has been compressed to several times atmospheric pressure and has heated substantially simply from that compression, well before any fuel has been added.

This compression is not free even in principle. Squeezing a gas takes work, and that work comes from the turbine at the back of the engine, connected to the compressor by a shaft running straight through the core. On many engines this shaft is actually two or three concentric shafts turning at different speeds, a low-pressure spool linking the fan and the rearmost turbine stages, a high-pressure spool linking the core compressor and the turbine stages immediately behind the combustor. Splitting the machinery this way lets each section spin at whatever speed suits its own aerodynamics best, rather than forcing a single compromise speed on the whole engine.

## The Bang

The combustor sits at the engine's narrow waist, and it has one of the strangest jobs in the whole machine: burning fuel continuously in a stream of air moving at enormous speed, without blowing the flame out the back like a candle in a hurricane. Engineers solve this by shaping the airflow inside the combustor deliberately, diverting some of the compressed air to swirl and recirculate near the fuel nozzles, creating a stable pocket of slower-moving, already-burning gas that keeps reigniting the fresh mixture flowing past it. Only a portion of the compressed air actually goes through this fuel-air combustion; the rest is held back and introduced further downstream specifically to cool the scorching combustion products before they reach the turbine.

That cooling matters because combustion temperatures inside the flame zone comfortably exceed the melting point of the metal alloys used to build the engine. Turbine inlet temperature is one of the single most important numbers in engine design, because raising it increases the cycle's thermodynamic efficiency, yet every increase demands better materials and cleverer cooling to keep the hardware from melting. Modern turbine blades are hollow, threaded internally with serpentine cooling passages that bleed compressor air through tiny holes across the blade's surface, wrapping each blade in a thin protective film of cooler air even as the gas rushing past it would otherwise be hot enough to soften the metal outright. Ceramic thermal barrier coatings add another thin layer of insulation on top of that.

## The Blow

Hot, high-pressure gas leaving the combustor expands through the turbine, and this is where the engine collects back the energy it spent on compression. As the gas pushes past each row of turbine blades, it spins the shaft connecting back to the compressor and fan, and by the time it has passed through enough stages it has given up most of the energy needed to keep the front of the engine turning. What is left, still hot and still under some residual pressure, accelerates out through the exhaust nozzle at high speed. That acceleration is the entire point: Newton's third law says that if the engine throws mass backward, the engine itself is pushed forward, and thrust is simply the rate of that backward momentum, adjusted for however much bypass air was mixed in along the way.

## Efficiency and Its Limits

No heat engine converts all its thermal energy into useful work, and a jet engine is bound by the same ceiling as any other: the theoretical efficiency of a Brayton cycle rises with the pressure ratio across the compressor and the temperature reached in combustion, which is precisely why engineers keep pushing both figures upward generation after generation, chasing incremental gains against ever-harder metallurgical limits. Real engines fall well short of the theoretical maximum, losing energy to friction in the bearings, imperfect combustion, aerodynamic losses in every duct and blade row, and the simple fact that the exhaust gas leaves still carrying heat that could in principle have done more work.

The shift toward higher bypass ratios over the decades reflects a separate kind of efficiency, called propulsive efficiency, distinct from the thermodynamic efficiency of the core. A narrow, high-speed exhaust jet wastes a good deal of its kinetic energy simply accelerating a small amount of air to a very high velocity relative to the aircraft; a wider, slower stream of bypass air delivers the same thrust while leaving less kinetic energy behind in the wake, which is also why high-bypass engines are so much quieter than the narrow, screaming turbojets of the early jet age. Every visible change on the outside of a modern engine nacelle, the wide fan, the short stubby core, the elaborately scalloped chevrons sometimes seen on the exhaust nozzle, traces back to this same pursuit: extracting the most forward push from the least fuel burned and the least noise made, one thermodynamic compromise at a time.
