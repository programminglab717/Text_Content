# Fly-by-Wire and Modern Flight Control

Pull back on the sidestick of an Airbus airliner and nothing mechanical happens at the far end of the airplane. The stick is a spring-loaded transducer bolted to the cockpit floor. It produces an electrical signal proportional to displacement, that signal goes to a set of computers, and the computers decide what the elevators and the stabilizer should do about it. There is no cable run, no pushrod, no bellcrank threading aft through the pressure bulkhead. The link between the pilot's hand and the control surface is an argument conducted in software, several times a second, between what the pilot asked for and what the aircraft's sensors say is currently happening.

This sounds like a radical break, and in one sense it is. In another it is the last step in a process that started the moment airplanes got too big for a person to fly with muscle alone.

## The Long Retreat from Direct Connection

The earliest aircraft were flown by direct mechanical linkage: the pilot's arm moved the surface, and aerodynamic loads on that surface pushed back through the same linkage into the pilot's hand. The feedback was honest and free. As airframes grew, control forces grew with them, and designers reached first for aerodynamic tricks — horn balances, servo tabs, trim tabs geared to do the heavy lifting — and then, when those ran out, for hydraulics.

A fully powered hydraulic control breaks the honest feedback loop. The pilot's input now meters fluid to an actuator, and the actuator overwhelms the air loads without telling the pilot anything about them. So designers put the feel back artificially: springs, bobweights, and q-feel systems that increase stick force in proportion to dynamic pressure, so that the aircraft feels heavier at high speed the way an unpowered one would. By the 1960s a large jet's control system was already a substantial piece of engineering that translated, rather than transmitted, the pilot's intentions.

Once the linkage is only carrying information, the argument for keeping it mechanical weakens. Cables and pushrods are heavy, they need routing around everything else, they stretch and need rigging, and they are vulnerable — several hull losses have come from a single event severing hydraulic lines and control runs that happened to pass through the same part of the airframe. Concorde flew with an analogue electrical signalling system and a mechanical backup. NASA put a digital system into a modified F-8 Crusader in the early 1970s, using flight-computer hardware derived from the Apollo program, largely to prove the concept would not kill anyone. The F-16 made the case commercially: designed with its center of gravity deliberately aft of where aerodynamic stability requires, it could not be flown at all without continuous computer intervention, and in exchange it got a smaller tail, less trim drag, and agility that a stable airframe cannot match. The Airbus A320 brought the full digital version to passenger service in the late 1980s, with sidesticks instead of yokes, and the industry has been working through the implications ever since.

## What the Computer Is Actually Being Asked For

The subtlest thing about fly-by-wire is not the absence of cables. It is that the stick often no longer commands a surface position at all.

In the pitch axis of a modern airliner in its normal operating mode, stick deflection commands a load factor — a demanded g. Neutral stick does not mean neutral elevator; it means "hold one g," and the computers will move the elevator as much as they need to in order to keep the flight path where it is. Fly into a gust and the elevator twitches without the stick moving. Roll into a bank and the system feeds in enough back pressure on its own to hold the nose up through the turn, up to a moderate bank angle. The stabilizer trims itself continuously to keep the elevator near the center of its range, so the trim wheel that used to spin beside the pilot's knee has become, in normal operation, something you never touch.

The roll axis is usually a roll-rate command with attitude hold: stick deflection asks for a rate of roll, neutral stick asks for the current bank to be maintained. Both of these are what control engineers call rate-command/attitude-hold laws, and they change the texture of flying. The aircraft holds what you last asked for instead of drifting, which is restful on a long leg and slightly disconcerting the first time, because the airplane no longer tells you through the controls that it is out of trim. It simply is not out of trim.

## Protections, and Two Views of Authority

If a computer is interpreting the pilot's commands anyway, it can decline to execute some of them. This is envelope protection, and it is where the major manufacturers part company philosophically.

The Airbus approach makes the limits hard. In its normal control law the aircraft will not exceed a certain load factor, will not roll past a set bank angle without deliberate persistent input, and — most consequentially — will not be stalled. Pull the stick fully aft and the aircraft goes to a high angle of attack just short of the stall and stays there, wings level and flying, until you let go. The reasoning is straightforward: line pilots almost never need the far corners of the envelope, upsets and terrain encounters are survived by pulling everything you can get without losing control, and a system that guarantees the maximum available performance on demand removes a category of accident.

Boeing's fly-by-wire aircraft implement comparable protections but leave the pilot able to override them with enough force. The yoke is retained and backdriven, so it moves when the autopilot flies, and the tactile world of the older cockpit is preserved. The reasoning here is equally straightforward: the designers cannot anticipate every situation, sensors can be wrong, and the last authority should rest with the human who can see out the window.

Both arguments have accident histories on their side, which is why the debate has lasted thirty years without resolution. Neither camp disputes the underlying fact that on a modern airliner the computers are always in the loop.

## The Engineering of Not Failing

A mechanical control run fails gradually and visibly. Software fails all at once and identically in every copy of itself, which is the central anxiety of the field. Redundancy alone does not fix it: running four identical computers with identical code means a latent bug simply produces four identical wrong answers, and the voting logic confidently ratifies nonsense.

So the redundancy is deliberately dissimilar. An Airbus splits its flight control duties among several kinds of computer of different design, and the Boeing 777's primary flight computers each contain multiple processing lanes built around different processors. Where practical, the software for different channels has been specified and written by separate teams, sometimes in different languages, to reduce the chance that a single misunderstanding of the requirements propagates everywhere. Channels compare results continuously; a channel that disagrees with its peers is voted out and isolated. The hierarchy is arranged so that if the primary computers are lost, a simpler set of secondary computers can still fly the aircraft with reduced functionality, and beyond that there is usually some last-ditch path — a mechanical link to the stabilizer and rudder on the early A320s, a direct electrical mode on later designs — enough to keep the airplane right side up while the crew works the problem.

The electrical system behind all this is its own exercise in paranoia: multiple independent buses, batteries, a ram air turbine that drops into the slipstream to generate power from nothing but forward speed. An aircraft that cannot fly without electricity has to treat electricity the way a glider treats altitude.

## Degraded Modes Are Where the Difficulty Lives

The protections and the comfortable handling depend on the computers having trustworthy information about airspeed, angle of attack, and attitude. When the sensors become unreliable — pitot probes icing over in a high-altitude storm, for instance — the system cannot safely enforce limits it can no longer compute. So it degrades. It hands back a simpler, less protected control law, announces the change, and the aircraft the pilot is now flying is not quite the one that was there a moment earlier.

This is the genuine hazard of the architecture, and it is not a software problem. It is a training and human-factors problem. Degraded modes arrive rarely, usually at night, usually in turbulence, usually alongside a confusion of other warnings, and the crew must recognize within seconds that the stall protection they have implicitly trusted for ten thousand hours is no longer present and that ordinary stick-and-rudder flying, including a conventional stall recovery, is now required. Loss-of-control accidents following sensor failures have pushed the industry toward upset recovery training, more explicit annunciation of control law status, and sensor designs less prone to correlated failure — because if three pitot probes ice up for the same reason at the same moment, three probes provide no more information than one.

What the technology buys, in the end, is not simply lighter airplanes, though the weight savings are real and go straight into range and payload. It buys structural relief, because the same computers can sense a gust and flick the ailerons and spoilers upward to spill load off the wingtips before the spar feels it. It buys relaxed stability and smaller tails. It buys an airframe whose handling can be tuned in software rather than remanufactured in aluminium, and whose behavior at the edges is consistent across an entire fleet. And it buys a new obligation: to keep human pilots fluent in an airplane that, nearly all the time, no longer requires that fluency.
