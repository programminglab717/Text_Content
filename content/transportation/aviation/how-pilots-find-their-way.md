# How Pilots Find Their Way

The magnetic compass is the only navigation instrument in a light aircraft that needs no electricity, no engine, and no maintenance beyond a periodic check. It is also the one that lies most often. Start a turn out of a northerly heading in the northern hemisphere and the card will briefly swing the wrong way before it catches up. Accelerate on an easterly heading and it will indicate a turn toward the north that is not happening. Hit turbulence and it will slosh around uselessly in its fluid. Every student pilot learns a pair of mnemonics for these errors, and every student pilot quietly concludes that the most reliable instrument on the panel cannot be trusted in the moments you most want a heading.

That contradiction is the starting point for the whole history of air navigation. Finding your way in three dimensions, at speed, often with nothing visible outside, is a problem that has been solved four or five separate times with four or five different technologies, and the remarkable thing is that almost all of them are still installed on a modern flight deck.

## Dead Reckoning and the Wind Triangle

Before radio, before satellites, there was arithmetic. If you know your heading, your speed through the air, and how long you have been flying, you can compute where you should be. The complication is that the air itself is moving. An aircraft holding a compass heading of due north in a westerly wind is traveling north and east simultaneously, and the difference between where the nose points and where the aircraft actually goes is drift. Correcting for it means solving a vector triangle: your airspeed and heading on one side, the wind on another, your resulting track and groundspeed on the third.

Generations of pilots solved that triangle on a circular slide rule called an E6B, a metal or plastic disc with a sliding grid on the back, which also handled fuel burn, time to destination, and the conversion between indicated and true airspeed. It is still taught, partly because examiners require it and partly because working the triangle by hand builds an intuition for wind that a magenta line on a screen does not. Dead reckoning's weakness is that errors accumulate silently. A five-degree heading error and a wind that has backed twenty degrees since the forecast will put you thirty miles from where your arithmetic says you are, and nothing in the cockpit will tell you.

So dead reckoning was always paired with pilotage — looking out the window and matching what you see to a chart. Rivers, rail lines, ridges, and towns with water towers that helpfully had their names painted on them. In the 1920s the U.S. Post Office went further and built a chain of rotating beacons across the country on steel towers, so that the night airmail could be flown by hopping from one light to the next. It worked whenever the weather was clear enough to see the next tower, which is exactly the condition under which navigation is easy anyway.

## Gyros, and Instruments That Do Not Lie

The compass's problems come from the fact that the earth's magnetic field does not run parallel to the ground; it dips into the earth, steeply at high latitudes, and the card's attempt to follow that dip while also being swung around in a turn produces the errors. The fix was the gyroscope. A wheel spun fast enough stays rigid in space, so a gyro-driven heading indicator gives a steady, readable heading with no turning or acceleration error at all. The catch is that it has no idea which way north is. It must be set against the magnetic compass in straight and level flight, and because bearing friction and the earth's own rotation make it drift, it must be reset every fifteen minutes or so. A pilot in cloud develops the habit without thinking: level off, let the compass settle, match the gyro, carry on.

Gyros also gave aviation the artificial horizon, which is what actually made flight in cloud possible. Human balance organs cannot distinguish a sustained turn from level flight, and will confidently report wings level while the aircraft spirals. The attitude indicator shows pitch and bank against a fixed reference no matter what the inner ear believes. In many small aircraft these instruments are driven by suction from an engine-mounted vacuum pump, which is why a failed pump in cloud is a classic accident chain: the horizon and heading indicator slowly droop while the pilot, watching instruments that still look plausible, flies steadily into a spiral.

The pressure instruments complete the set. The altimeter is an aneroid capsule measuring static pressure, translated into height through a standard model of the atmosphere, which means it is only accurate if you feed it the local sea-level pressure setting. Above a transition altitude everyone abandons local settings and winds in a common standard value, so that aircraft on adjacent flight levels are separated correctly even though none of them is at the altitude the instrument claims. The airspeed indicator measures the difference between ram pressure at the pitot tube and static pressure, so it reads progressively lower than true airspeed as air thins with height — an error that turns out to be a feature, since the wing responds to the same dynamic pressure the instrument is measuring.

## Beams, Ranges, and Radials

The first system that let pilots navigate reliably without seeing anything was audible. In the 1930s, low-frequency radio ranges transmitted four overlapping lobes, two sending the Morse letter A and two sending N. Fly in one quadrant and you heard dit-dah in your headset; fly in the adjacent one and you heard dah-dit. Where the lobes overlapped, the two interlocked into a continuous tone, and that steady note was the airway. Pilots flew the beam by ear for hours, listening for the tone to break into a letter and correcting back, and knew they had passed the station by a brief cone of silence directly overhead. It was exhausting, and it built the first real instrument airway network.

The range gave way to two systems that are still in the ground today. The non-directional beacon, paired with an automatic direction finder in the cockpit, simply provides a needle that points at the transmitter — crude, vulnerable to thunderstorms and to strange nighttime propagation, but cheap enough that remote airfields worldwide installed them. The VOR replaced it properly. A VOR station broadcasts two signals, one a fixed reference and one whose phase rotates, and the receiver measures the phase difference to determine which of three hundred and sixty radials from the station the aircraft is sitting on. Add distance measuring equipment, which times the round trip of an interrogation pulse to the station and back, and you have a bearing and a range: a fix from a single ground station. Airways were drawn as straight lines between VORs, which is why route maps from that era zigzag from beacon to beacon rather than following great circles.

## Down the Slope

Getting to the airport is one problem; finding the runway in fog is another. The instrument landing system solves it with two beams. A localizer antenna array beyond the far end of the runway defines the extended centerline laterally. A glideslope antenna beside the touchdown zone defines a descent path, conventionally about three degrees. The cockpit shows two needles, and the pilot's job is to keep them centered. Older installations added marker beacons along the approach that chirped in the headset and lit colored lamps as the aircraft passed over them.

Precision categories describe how far down the approach the system is trusted. The basic case takes an aircraft to a couple of hundred feet above the ground, at which point the crew must see the runway or go around. The most capable installations, paired with autopilots that fly the approach and the rollout, permit landings in visibility that would not let a driver find a parking space. Those approaches demand that the ground area around the antennas be kept clear, because a vehicle parked in the wrong place can bend the beam enough to matter.

## Inertia, Satellites, and the Return of the Backup

Long ocean crossings had no ground stations at all, so for decades airliners carried a navigator who shot the sun and stars through a dome or a periscopic port with a bubble sextant. That crew position disappeared with the inertial navigation system, which measures acceleration in three axes on a gyro-stabilized platform and integrates twice to get position. Aligned while the aircraft sits still at the gate, an inertial platform needs nothing from the outside world for the rest of the flight. It drifts — a mile or two per hour, historically — but it is beyond interference, which is the property that matters now.

Satellite navigation made all of this look quaint. A receiver compares the arrival times of signals from several satellites whose positions and clocks are known precisely, solving simultaneously for its own position and its own clock error, which is why four satellites are needed rather than three. Augmentation systems broadcast corrections that tighten the fix enough to fly approaches with vertical guidance down to minima approaching those of an ILS, at small airports that could never afford ground equipment. Flight management systems carry a navigation database of waypoints, airways, and published procedures, updated on a fixed cycle, and will fly a route the pilot never has to compute.

Which is why the older network has not been torn out. Satellite signals arriving from twenty thousand kilometers away are faint, and jamming and spoofing near conflict zones have become common enough that crews are briefed on regions where the position may simply go wrong, sometimes convincingly. Aviation authorities have kept a skeleton of VOR stations deliberately, enough to get an aircraft to an airport with the satellites unavailable. The needle still twitches over a beacon in the middle of nowhere, doing the one job it has always done.
