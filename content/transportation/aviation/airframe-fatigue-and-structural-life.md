# Airframe Fatigue and Structural Life

Every time an airliner climbs away from a runway, its fuselage swells. Cabin pressure is held at something like the atmosphere of a mountain town while the air outside thins toward a fraction of sea-level density, and the difference — roughly eight pounds on every square inch of a thin aluminium shell — stretches the skin outward, lengthens the tube by a measurable amount, and pushes the window cutouts toward roundness. On descent it all shrinks back. That is one cycle. A short-haul narrow-body flying six legs a day does it a couple of thousand times a year, and in between, the wings bend through every gust, every banked turn, every seam in the taxiway concrete.

Aluminium is indifferent to any single one of those events. Load a wing spar to half the stress it can survive in a single pull and it will not break, not that day. Do it a hundred thousand times and it may. That asymmetry — between what a metal can take once and what it can take repeatedly — is the whole subject of fatigue, and it has shaped aircraft design more thoroughly than almost anything else in the discipline.

## Cracks Begin Where the Stress Concentrates

Fatigue damage is local before it is structural. In a polycrystalline metal, cyclic loading well below the yield strength still causes microscopic slip along favorably oriented crystal planes. Slip in one direction is not perfectly reversed on the return stroke, so each cycle leaves a small step at the free surface. Steps accumulate into intrusions and extrusions, an intrusion becomes a notch, and a notch becomes a crack. Nothing has visibly happened yet. The part looks exactly as it did on the day it was machined.

Where this begins is almost entirely a question of geometry. A smooth, uniformly loaded panel distributes stress evenly, but real structures are full of interruptions — fastener holes, cutouts for doors and windows, fillets where a stiffener changes section, the root of a thread. A simple round hole in a loaded plate roughly triples the local stress at its edge. Sharpen that hole into a corner and the multiplier climbs steeply. Scratch the bore of the hole with a drill bit that was dull, or leave a burr, and you have provided a starting notch for free.

Engineers describe a material's endurance with an S-N curve: stress amplitude plotted against the number of cycles to failure, usually on logarithmic axes, and usually descending to the right. Some steels flatten out into a true fatigue limit, a stress below which the material seems to survive indefinitely. The aluminium alloys that make up most airframes do not. Their curves keep sloping downward, which means there is no stress low enough to be safe forever, only stresses low enough to last longer than the airplane needs to. Real flight loads are not constant-amplitude either; they arrive as a jumble of large and small excursions, and the classical bookkeeping method, Miner's rule, simply sums the fraction of life consumed at each stress level and calls failure imminent when the fractions add to one. It is crude — the order in which loads arrive genuinely matters, because a big load can leave a compressive residual stress at a crack tip that retards subsequent growth — but it is tractable, and it was the arithmetic on which a generation of aircraft was sized.

## The Comet Taught the Industry Its Vocabulary

The de Havilland Comet entered service in the early 1950s as the first jet airliner, flying higher and faster than anything carrying passengers before it, and flying higher meant a larger pressure differential across the cabin wall. Within two years, two of them broke up over the Mediterranean in circumstances that offered almost nothing to work with: no warning from the crews, wreckage scattered on the seabed.

The investigation that followed, run at the Royal Aircraft Establishment at Farnborough, did something that had not really been done before. Investigators recovered what wreckage they could from the sea and, in parallel, took a complete Comet fuselage, sealed it inside a tank of water, and cycled the internal pressure up and down while the structure sat submerged — water rather than air because a pressure vessel full of compressed air that fails does so explosively, while one full of water simply splits and stops. After the equivalent of thousands of additional flights, the test article failed, and the failure had started at the corner of a cutout in the skin, propagating from a region of concentrated stress in exactly the way the recovered wreckage suggested the accidents had.

The lesson was not that engineers had been careless about strength. The Comet's structure was strong; it would have survived many times its working pressure in a single application. The lesson was that static strength and fatigue life are different problems, that cutouts in a pressurized shell need generous radii and reinforcement, and that a full-scale fatigue test is not an optional academic exercise. Every large transport aircraft certified since has had a dedicated test airframe cycled in a rig, usually well ahead of the fleet, so that cracks appear first in a hangar in front of inspectors rather than at altitude.

## Safe-Life, Fail-Safe, and Damage Tolerance

Three design philosophies have competed, and to some extent they now coexist.

Safe-life assumes the part is crack-free and retires it after a demonstrated fraction of its tested life — a helicopter rotor hub, a landing gear component, a wing attachment fitting. The part comes off the aircraft at a set number of hours or cycles whether or not anything is wrong with it. The approach is conservative and wasteful, and it depends absolutely on the scatter factor applied to test results being large enough, because there is no inspection in the scheme to catch a part that is worse than average.

Fail-safe accepts that a crack may appear and builds the structure so that any single member can fail completely without bringing down the aircraft. Multiple load paths, redundant straps, skins divided into bays by tear straps that blunt a running crack and turn it to flap outward instead of unzipping the fuselage. The controlled-decompression design of a modern cabin shell owes its layout to this thinking.

Damage tolerance, which became the regulatory backbone in the 1970s, is the most demanding of the three. It presumes that manufacturing flaws exist from day one, that cracks will grow, and that safety comes from knowing how fast. The design must be arranged so that a crack grows slowly and detectably from a size just below what inspection can reliably find to the size at which residual strength falls to an acceptable limit — and the inspection interval must be short enough to catch it at least twice in that window. This requires fracture mechanics rather than S-N curves: a crack's growth rate per cycle correlates with the range of stress intensity at its tip, and final fracture arrives when that intensity reaches the material's toughness. It also inverts an old intuition. A tougher, more crack-resistant alloy can be better than a stronger one, because it buys inspection time.

## Aloha, and the Problem of Many Small Cracks

In 1988 a Boeing 737 operating short hops between Hawaiian islands lost a large section of its upper fuselage in cruise. The aircraft was old by cycle count rather than hours — island flying accumulates pressurization cycles at an extraordinary rate — and it had spent its life in salt air. The crew landed it. A flight attendant was lost.

The structural finding reframed the field. Damage tolerance had been built around the single dominant crack: find it, track it, replace the part. What had happened here was different. Along a riveted lap joint where a bonded seam had corroded and disbonded, load shifted onto the rivet line, and small fatigue cracks had initiated at many adjacent fastener holes at once. Individually, each was below the threshold of easy detection. Collectively, once they linked up, they formed a single crack far longer than any fail-safe feature was designed to arrest, and the structure came apart in a fraction of a second.

This is widespread fatigue damage, and it is the reason the modern regulatory framework gives an aircraft type a limit of validity: a number of cycles beyond which the type cannot fly at all unless the manufacturer has demonstrated, with test and analysis, that widespread damage will not occur. Aging aircraft programs, mandatory structural inspection supplements, and the fleet-leader teardowns that dismantle a high-time airframe rivet by rivet all descend from the same realization — that a fleet does not fail the way a coupon in a laboratory fails.

## Buying Life at the Drawing Board

A good deal of fatigue performance is bought during manufacture rather than design. Cold-expanding a fastener hole by drawing an oversized mandrel through it leaves a ring of compressive residual stress around the bore, and a crack trying to grow out of that ring must first overcome the compression; the gain in life can be several-fold for almost no weight. Interference-fit fasteners do something similar. Shot peening puts the whole surface into compression by pounding it with small spheres. Chemical milling tapers a skin panel smoothly instead of stepping it. Fillet radii grow, sharp corners disappear, and the detailed drawings acquire notes about surface finish that look fussy until you understand what a machining mark is worth as a crack starter.

Corrosion runs through all of it as an accelerant. Pitting supplies notches, exfoliation along grain boundaries relieves nothing and weakens everything, and the two mechanisms feed each other: a crack opens a path for moisture, and corrosion products wedge it further apart. Much of what a heavy maintenance check actually consists of — stripping paint, pulling insulation blankets, peering into the belly bilge where galley spills collect — is corrosion work with fatigue as the reason for caring.

Carbon-fiber structure changes the arithmetic without eliminating it. Composites are far less susceptible to the classic crack-growth-under-cyclic-load problem, which is part of why a composite fuselage can carry a higher pressure differential and larger windows. But they are sensitive to impact, and the damage that matters most is the kind that leaves almost no mark on the surface while delaminating plies underneath. The inspection problem does not go away. It changes its name, moves from eddy-current probes around rivet holes to ultrasonic mapping of laminate, and remains what it has been since Farnborough filled a tank with water: the business of finding the flaw before the flaw finds you.
