# The Brief Age of the Airship

The spire on top of the Empire State Building was not designed as decoration. It was built as a mooring mast for dirigibles, on the assumption that passengers arriving in New York by air would step out of a gondola a thousand feet above Thirty-Fourth Street and ride an elevator down to the lobby. The idea survived exactly as long as it took someone to consider what the wind does around the top of a tall building in Manhattan. A ship that is lighter than the air it sits in has no weight to anchor it, and the updrafts pouring off the building's face would have swung a two-hundred-meter hull like a weathervane over a crowded street. A couple of tentative approaches were made. Nobody ever unloaded a passenger. The mast became a radio antenna, and the whole episode is a fair miniature of the airship's career: technically astonishing, briefly plausible, and defeated by weather and handling rather than by any failure of imagination.

## The Arithmetic of Buoyancy

An airship flies by Archimedes' principle, not by pushing air downward. It displaces a volume of atmosphere and is lifted by the difference between the weight of that displaced air and the weight of the gas inside the envelope. Hydrogen provides a net lift of a little over a kilogram per cubic meter at sea level; helium, being heavier, gives about ten percent less. That single number governs everything else. If each cubic meter lifts only a kilogram, then a ship that must carry fifty tonnes of structure, engines, fuel, crew, and passengers needs a volume in the tens of thousands of cubic meters, and the great transatlantic Zeppelins ran to roughly two hundred thousand. They were the largest objects that have ever flown, and they were mostly empty space.

Size of that order creates its own problems. A hull that big presents an enormous side area to the wind, which is why airships were so hard to handle near the ground and why ground crews numbered in the hundreds of men on lines, physically walking the ship into its shed. It is also why buoyancy control is so awkward. To descend, a commander valves gas overboard; to rise, he drops ballast. Both are one-way expenditures. Worse, burning fuel makes the ship lighter as the flight goes on, so it grows steadily more buoyant, and the crew must dump lift they may want later. The Germans solved this on the Graf Zeppelin with a fuel gas of about the same density as air, so consuming it changed the ship's weight hardly at all. American naval airships used condensers to recover water from the engine exhaust and turn it back into ballast, which meant flying around with radiators on the hull whose only job was to catch what the engines threw away.

## Zeppelin's Rigid Idea

Three architectures competed. The non-rigid — the blimp — is a bag held in shape by the pressure of the gas inside it, with the car slung beneath. The semi-rigid adds a keel along the bottom. The rigid, which is the one that defined the era, builds a skeleton first: rings and longitudinal girders of duralumin, covered in doped fabric, with separate gas cells suspended inside like balloons in a birdcage. The early cells were made of goldbeater's skin, the outer membrane of cattle intestine, which is nearly gas-tight and required the guts of tens of thousands of animals per ship.

Count Ferdinand von Zeppelin got his first rigid ship airborne over Lake Constance in 1900. It was a poor performer, and so were the several that followed, some of which burned or broke up. Before the First World War his company was nonetheless carrying paying passengers on sightseeing flights over Germany, which made it the first airline in any meaningful sense, at a time when aeroplanes could barely cross a county.

The war turned the Zeppelin into a weapon and then exposed it. Night raids on London and the east coast of England killed civilians and forced Britain to divert guns, searchlights, and fighter squadrons to home defense, which was arguably the raids' greatest effect, since the tonnage dropped was trivial by later standards. Once incendiary ammunition reached the defending pilots, a hydrogen ship at a few thousand feet was a target that burned. The German answer was to climb, producing "height climbers" stripped of everything removable, operating at altitudes where the crews suffered frostbite and altitude sickness in open gondolas and where the winds blew them well off the cities they were aiming at.

## The Ocean Liners of the Air

The 1920s and 1930s were the airship's real moment, and it looked briefly like the future. Britain's R34 crossed the Atlantic westward and returned in 1919. The Graf Zeppelin circled the world in 1929 and then spent years running a scheduled service between Germany and Brazil, crossing the South Atlantic on a timetable when no aeroplane could do it at all. Travel aboard these ships resembled a small liner more than a flight: private cabins, a dining saloon with white linen, a promenade with windows that opened, and a ride so smooth that the often-repeated detail about a pen standing on end in flight is hard to disprove. They were slow — call it the speed of a fast car — but a crossing took two or three days rather than a week, and passengers could sleep through most of it.

The military kept experimenting too. The United States Navy, which had access to the world's only significant helium supply, built the Akron and Macon as flying aircraft carriers. Each carried a handful of small biplane fighters in an internal hangar, launching and recovering them in flight on a trapeze that the pilot hooked onto with a skyhook above his upper wing. The purpose was scouting: an airship could loiter for days over empty ocean, and its aeroplanes extended its eyes hundreds of miles further. It worked. What did not work was surviving weather.

## Structure, Weather, and the End

The list of losses reads as a single repeated lesson. The Navy's Shenandoah broke apart in a line squall over Ohio in 1925. Britain's R38 failed structurally during turning trials. The Akron went into the sea off New Jersey in a storm in 1933 with almost all hands, partly because a ship designed for a service that never expected ditchings carried almost no life jackets. The Macon lost an upper fin in 1935 and settled into the Pacific off the California coast, though this time nearly everyone was pulled out alive. The R101, heavy, underpowered, and pushed into a departure for India in bad weather for political reasons, came down on a hillside in France in 1930 and killed most of those aboard including the Air Minister who had championed it. Its privately built rival, the R100 — designed by a team that included a young engineer named Nevil Shute Norway, who later wrote about it with some bitterness — had already flown to Canada and back competently, and was scrapped anyway.

Then Lakehurst, in May 1937. The Hindenburg was designed for helium and built for hydrogen, because the United States held nearly all the world's helium and would not export it for use by Nazi Germany. Arriving late after a rough crossing, the ship was maneuvering to moor when fire appeared near the stern and consumed her in a little over half a minute. The exact ignition source has never been settled, and the argument over whether the flammable doped skin contributed continues; what is not in dispute is that seven million cubic feet of hydrogen was the fuel. What people who know only the newsreel usually get wrong is the death toll: most of those aboard survived, because the ship burned upward and the hull settled slowly enough that passengers could jump and run.

The fire is remembered as the end, but it mostly supplied a date. The airship was already losing. It was slow, it could not fly through a serious storm the way an aeroplane learned to fly above one, it needed sheds the size of cathedrals and ground crews the size of a battalion, and its economics only worked because nothing else could cross an ocean. By 1939 flying boats were running scheduled Atlantic service, and within a decade landplanes would do it faster, higher, and in daylight.

## What Survived

Blimps never quite went away. The U.S. Navy flew them in large numbers on convoy escort and anti-submarine patrol during the Second World War, where the airship's genuine advantages — endurance, a slow, steady platform, the ability to hover over a contact — were exactly what the job required. Their descendants now carry cameras over stadiums and advertising over highways. Tethered aerostats hold radar and surveillance payloads aloft for weeks at a time, which is the same idea with the propulsion problem removed.

Every decade or so a new venture proposes heavy-lift airships for mining camps, remote construction, or humanitarian supply, often as hybrids that get part of their lift from the hull's shape moving through the air rather than from buoyancy alone. The renderings are always beautiful. The proposals keep running into the same wall the Empire State Building's mast ran into: a vehicle that weighs nothing is at the mercy of the wind the instant it slows down, and the ground is where all the wind lives.
