# The First Years of Powered Flight

The photograph everyone knows was taken by a man from the Kill Devil Hills lifesaving station who had never operated a camera before. Orville Wright set the shutter, framed the shot, and told John Daniels to squeeze the bulb if anything happened. Something did: the machine lifted off its launching rail into a stiff December headwind, Orville lying prone on the lower wing with his hips in the cradle that warped the wingtips, and Wilbur running alongside with one hand steadying the tip. The flight lasted twelve seconds and covered about a hundred and twenty feet — less than the wingspan of a modern airliner. Daniels got the picture anyway.

What makes that morning in 1903 remarkable is not that two bicycle mechanics built an engine and a propeller. Plenty of people had built engines. Hiram Maxim had already launched a steam-powered contraption so large it tore up its own guide rails. Clément Ader had gotten something off the ground in France in the previous decade, briefly and uncontrollably. Samuel Langley, secretary of the Smithsonian Institution and a man with government funding and a professional staff, had twice that same autumn catapulted a tandem-wing machine off a houseboat on the Potomac and twice watched it fold up and drop into the river, the second time only days before the Wrights flew. The problem was never simply getting airborne. The problem was staying there on purpose.

## The Control Problem

The Wrights understood earlier than almost anyone that flight was a problem of balance, not power, and that the solution had to be learned the way a cyclist learns to ride rather than engineered on paper. Otto Lilienthal, the German who had made thousands of glides from an artificial hill outside Berlin, shifted his hanging body weight to steer, and it was not enough; a gust rolled him over in 1896 and he died of a broken spine the next day. His death made the Wrights cautious in a productive way. They wanted a machine that the pilot controlled through surfaces rather than by throwing his weight around.

Their insight, supposedly arrived at while Wilbur idly twisted an empty inner-tube box in the bicycle shop, was that a biplane's wings could be twisted in opposite directions — one tip given more angle, the other less — to roll the machine deliberately. Wing warping was the ancestor of the aileron, and it worked. But the first gliders taught them something nastier: raising the angle on one wingtip raises that tip's drag as well as its lift, so the machine yaws the wrong way, toward the rising wing, and can slide into a spin. The fix was a movable vertical rudder, and then the crucial refinement of linking it to the warping cradle so that roll and yaw were commanded together. That coupling, rather than any particular wing shape, is arguably the real invention.

They also discovered that the published aerodynamic tables everyone was using were wrong. Their 1901 glider produced roughly a third of the lift the numbers predicted, and Wilbur came home from the Outer Banks that season telling his brother that man would not fly in a thousand years. Then they built a wind tunnel out of a wooden box with a fan at one end and spent the winter measuring dozens of miniature wing shapes on balances fashioned from hacksaw blades and bicycle spokes. The data they produced was better than anything in circulation, and the 1902 glider built from it flew hundreds of times, turning under control. The engine came afterward, almost as an afterthought — about twelve horsepower, aluminum crankcase, built in six weeks by their shop machinist Charlie Taylor when no manufacturer would supply anything light enough. The propellers were the harder piece. Nobody had a usable theory of them; the brothers worked out for themselves that a propeller is a rotating wing and designed theirs accordingly, achieving efficiencies that engineers would not surpass for years.

## The Quiet Years and the Loud Ones

Very little happened publicly for the next four years, which is one of the strange features of the story. The Wrights went home to Dayton and flew at Huffman Prairie, a cow pasture outside town, launching from a derrick with a falling weight because the Ohio winds were weaker than the Carolina ones. By 1905 their third powered machine could stay up for more than half an hour and fly figure eights. It was, by any reasonable definition, the first practical airplane. Then they stopped flying entirely and spent two years trying to sell it, refusing demonstrations until a contract was signed, convinced that showing the machine would let competitors copy it.

Europe did not wait. Alberto Santos-Dumont, a wealthy Brazilian living in Paris who had already made himself famous steering airships around the Eiffel Tower, hopped a box-kite-like contraption called the 14-bis a couple of hundred feet across a field near Paris in 1906 and was credited by the French as the first man to fly a powered machine in Europe. Gabriel Voisin was building biplanes for anyone who would buy one. Henri Farman, an Anglo-French bicycle and motor racer, flew a closed circuit of a kilometer in one of them in early 1908 and collected a substantial prize. The French machines had no roll control worth the name; they turned flat, skidding, like boats. But they were flying in public, and the public was watching.

Then in August 1908 Wilbur Wright uncrated a Flyer at a racecourse near Le Mans and flew a tight banked circle in front of a crowd that had half convinced itself the Americans were bluffing. The reaction was closer to shock than applause. One French aviator was reported to have said they had simply been beaten — that they did not exist as flyers by comparison. Within weeks Wilbur was flying for more than an hour and taking passengers aloft. That same autumn Orville was demonstrating an identical machine to the U.S. Army at Fort Myer, Virginia, where a propeller cracked and fouled a rigging wire, and the machine came down hard. Orville was badly hurt. His passenger, Lieutenant Thomas Selfridge, died — the first person killed in a powered airplane.

## Everything at Once

After 1908 the pace becomes almost comic. Louis Blériot, a manufacturer of automobile headlamps who had crashed a long series of his own designs, flew a small monoplane across the English Channel in the summer of 1909, navigating by guesswork through haze, landing hard in a meadow near Dover Castle and collecting a newspaper prize. The strategic implications were obvious to everyone, and the British press said so loudly. A few weeks later the first great aviation meeting was held on the plain at Reims, where Glenn Curtiss took the speed prize and dozens of machines flew in front of enormous crowds, several of them at once.

Curtiss is the other essential American figure and the one who complicates the tidy narrative. A motorcycle builder and record-setting rider from upstate New York, he came to aviation as an engine supplier and joined Alexander Graham Bell's Aerial Experiment Association, whose machines used hinged surfaces at the wingtips rather than warping. The Wrights considered this an infringement of a patent they read broadly, and the resulting litigation consumed years, poisoned the American industry, and arguably left the United States building inferior airplanes when the war came. It ended only when the government, needing aircraft in 1917, pressured the parties into a cross-licensing pool.

Meanwhile the machines themselves improved faster than the law could keep up. The rotary engine — the entire cylinder block spinning with the propeller around a fixed crankshaft, air-cooled by its own rotation, throwing castor oil back into the pilot's face — gave an extraordinary power-to-weight ratio and a vicious gyroscopic bias that made some aircraft turn far better one way than the other. Tractor configurations replaced pushers. Enclosed fuselages replaced open frameworks of struts and wire. Ailerons replaced warping almost everywhere. In Russia, Igor Sikorsky built four-engine aircraft large enough to walk around inside at a time when most pilots sat in the open on a plank.

Learning to fly in those years was a matter of surviving your own education. There were no dual controls on most machines and no instructors sitting behind you; a pupil at one of the flying schools that sprang up at Pau, at Reims, at Brooklands and on Long Island typically began by taxiing a clipped-wing trainer back and forth across the grass until he could hold a straight line, then made short straight hops a few feet up, then circuits, then finally turns. The washout rate was not academic. Pilots were killed regularly enough that the newspapers kept a running tally, usually from stalls entered too low to recover, from structural failure in a dive, or from the spin, a phenomenon nobody could explain and most pilots believed was unrecoverable until a British officer worked out the recovery technique and published it shortly before the war. Nor did anyone yet fly by instruments. A pilot judged his attitude by the horizon, his speed by the sound of wire and slipstream, and his engine by feel, and when cloud swallowed the horizon he was in genuine danger of flying a perfectly sound airplane into the ground while convinced he was level.

The distance records fell in a rush. Roland Garros crossed the Mediterranean. Calbraith Perry Rodgers flew across the United States in 1911 in a Wright machine sponsored by a grape soda company, crashing so often that by the end almost nothing of the original airframe remained, taking weeks to do it with a train of mechanics following below. In January 1914 a flying boat began carrying a single paying passenger at a time across the bay between St. Petersburg and Tampa on a published schedule — the first scheduled airline, which lasted a few months before the winter tourists went home.

By then Italy had already dropped small bombs by hand on Ottoman positions in Libya, and every European general staff had a view on what airplanes were for. Wilbur Wright did not see it; he died of typhoid in 1912, worn out by the patent fights. Eleven years separated the twelve-second hop at Kill Devil Hills from the first reconnaissance flights over the Marne, which is roughly the interval between a child's birth and the sixth grade.
