# How Air Cargo Moves

A rose cut on a farm near Lake Naivasha in the Kenyan highlands on a Tuesday afternoon can be in a Dutch auction hall on Wednesday morning and in a London flat by the weekend. Nothing about that is miraculous once you look at the machinery, but the machinery is invisible to nearly everyone. The rose is graded and bunched in a packhouse that is already cold, boxed, trucked to Nairobi in a refrigerated truck, consolidated by a freight forwarder into an aluminum container shaped to fit the curve of a widebody's lower fuselage, and loaded onto an aircraft that takes off around midnight because that is when the flower schedule and the passenger schedule both make sense. It lands in Amsterdam at dawn. The cold chain is never broken for more than the twenty minutes the container spends on the ramp at either end, and those twenty minutes are the part of the journey that people who ship perishables worry about most.

Air freight is the most expensive way to move a kilogram of anything, by a wide margin. That single fact shapes the entire system. Measured by weight, the share of world trade that flies is small — well under one percent. Measured by value, it is on the order of a third. What flies is what is urgent, perishable, fragile, or so valuable per kilogram that the freight cost disappears into the price: semiconductors, phones, pharmaceuticals, aircraft parts, fashion on a deadline, salmon, cherries, live tropical fish, human organs, and the endless stream of small parcels that electronic commerce has generated.

## Bellies and Freighters

Roughly half of the world's air cargo, in an ordinary year, never travels on a cargo aircraft at all. It rides in the lower holds of passenger flights, in the space left over after the suitcases are loaded. A long-haul widebody flying full of passengers still has tonnes of usable volume beneath the cabin floor, and that belly capacity is close to free for the airline, since the flight is departing regardless. It is also completely dependent on where people want to go. Belly cargo follows the passenger network, which means capacity is thick between major cities and thin everywhere else, and it vanishes entirely when passenger flying stops. The spring of 2020 demonstrated this with brutal clarity: as passenger schedules collapsed, half the world's air freight capacity disappeared in a matter of weeks, rates spiked, and airlines began flying empty passenger jets purely to carry boxes in the hold.

The rest moves on dedicated freighters, which come in two flavors. Some are built as freighters from the start, with a reinforced floor, a main-deck cargo door, and no windows. Many more are passenger aircraft converted after fifteen or twenty years of carrying people: a large side door cut into the fuselage, the cabin stripped, a rigid barrier installed behind the flight deck, and the floor strengthened for point loads. Conversion is how an airframe earns its second career, and it is why the freighter fleet always looks a generation behind the passenger fleet.

The oddest freight goes on aircraft built for it. The 747 freighter's distinguishing feature is a nose that hinges upward, letting the main deck be loaded straight in along its length — the reason the flight deck sits on an upper level in the first place. For cargo too large for any scheduled aircraft, there is a small charter market flying Antonov An-124s: turbine rotors, locomotives, satellites, helicopters, mining equipment, loaded up ramps into a hold designed for military vehicles.

## Containers Shaped Like the Fuselage

Almost nothing is loaded loose onto a widebody. Freight is built up in advance into unit load devices — containers and pallets designed around the internal geometry of specific aircraft. The most common lower-deck container looks like a box with one bottom corner sliced off at an angle, because that angle matches the curve of the fuselage where it meets the cargo floor. Pallets are flat aluminum sheets with a rail around the edge, stacked and then netted down. Both are weighed, recorded, and rolled aboard over ball mats and powered rollers, then locked into fittings in the floor that restrain them against a forward deceleration of many times gravity, because in a survivable crash the cargo must not come through the bulkhead.

Building the load is a planning exercise, not just a stacking one. Every container's weight and position feeds a load sheet that determines the aircraft's center of gravity, which has to stay within limits that change with fuel burn. Dense freight goes in one place, light bulky freight in another, and the load planner works to fill both the weight allowance and the volume, which rarely run out at the same time. Smaller narrowbody aircraft mostly skip all of this and are loaded by hand, box by box, by loaders working bent over in a hold not tall enough to stand in.

## The Night Sort

The express parcel business runs on a different principle from general air freight, and it inverts the normal logic of routing. Instead of flying point to point, the integrators — the companies that control the package from door to door rather than just the flight — pull almost everything through one or two enormous hubs. Aircraft converge on Memphis, on Louisville, on Leipzig, in a compressed window late at night. Containers are broken down, parcels poured onto miles of conveyor, scanned, sorted by destination, rebuilt into outbound containers, and flown back out before dawn, so that a package collected in one city at five in the afternoon can be delivered in another by ten the next morning.

Routing everything through a single building seems wasteful until you count the connections it creates. A hub with a hundred inbound flights links every origin to every destination with one stop, using a hundred flights instead of the thousands that direct service would require. The cost is concentration: the sort has a hard deadline every night, and weather over the hub is a problem for the entire network at once.

Geography produces its own hubs. Anchorage handles an enormous tonnage because the great-circle route between East Asia and North America passes nearby, and a fuel stop lets an aircraft depart Asia heavier with freight and lighter with fuel than a nonstop would allow. Freight transfers between aircraft there without formally entering the country, which makes it a sorting yard for the Pacific.

## Who the Airline's Customer Actually Is

Shippers rarely deal with airlines. Between them sit freight forwarders, who buy space wholesale, consolidate many small shipments into one container, handle customs paperwork, and arrange the trucking at both ends. The governing document is the air waybill, a contract of carriage with a number that begins with a three-digit airline prefix, and it is the thread that customs authorities, handlers, and the airline all pull on.

A good deal of what moves under an air waybill never flies. Road feeder services run trucks between airports on published schedules, carrying containers under air documentation, because a truck from Frankfurt to Paris is faster and cheaper than a flight once you count the ground time at both ends. Passengers would be startled by how much of a "flight" is sometimes a lorry.

Billing follows volume as much as weight. A cubic meter of pillows weighs almost nothing but fills space that could have carried something dense, so carriers charge on whichever is greater: the actual weight, or a volumetric weight calculated by dividing the shipment's volume by a standard density figure of roughly a hundred and sixty-seven kilograms to the cubic meter. Shippers who think in mass alone get an unpleasant surprise when they present a crate of foam.

## The Difficult Freight

Pharmaceuticals moved in temperature-controlled containers are among the most demanding cargo in the system, and the most profitable. Some containers are passive, insulated boxes with phase-change material inside that hold a range for a fixed number of hours; others are active, with their own compressors and battery packs, reporting temperature continuously. Either way the vulnerable moments are the same: sitting on hot asphalt waiting for a tug, or on a ramp in a monsoon.

Then there are the categories that carry their own rulebooks. Dangerous goods are packed, labeled, and documented under international rules that determine what may fly at all, what may fly only on a freighter, and what must be declared in a form the captain sees before departure. Lithium batteries dominate the worry list, because a cell in thermal runaway produces its own oxygen and is essentially unquenchable in a cargo hold, and because mislabeled battery shipments keep turning up in general cargo. Live animals travel under a separate set of standards — racehorses in padded stalls with a groom in attendance, day-old chicks shipped within hours of hatching while still living off the yolk.

What holds the whole system together is time, not distance. A container ship crossing the Pacific takes weeks and costs almost nothing per kilogram. The aircraft takes eleven hours and costs many times as much. Everything that flies has, somewhere behind it, a calculation that those weeks were worth more than the difference.
