# Weaving Starts Before the Shuttle

A weaver planning a scarf will spend the better part of two days before a
single pick of weft goes in. The yarn has to be measured out in a specific
order, wound onto the back of the loom under even tension, threaded one end at
a time through several hundred wire eyes, pulled through a metal comb in
groups, and tied on in bundles that are then adjusted and readjusted until the
whole sheet thrums evenly under a flat palm. Only then does the shuttle come
out, and the shuttle part goes quickly. People who take up weaving expecting
it to be mostly throwing a shuttle are often surprised to find they have taken
up a craft that is mostly counting.

## Two Sets of Threads With Different Lives

Cloth is two systems of thread at right angles. The warp runs lengthwise and
is held under tension on the loom for the entire duration of the project. The
weft runs crosswise and is carried in by hand, in a shuttle, one row at a
time.

These are not interchangeable roles. Every warp end is lifted, dropped, and
dragged through a heddle eye and a reed dent thousands of times before the
cloth comes off, so warp yarn has to be strong and reasonably smooth. The
traditional test is to snap a length between your hands: if it breaks easily
it will break on the loom, at the worst possible moment, and you will be
splicing in a repair thread with a pin and a weight hanging off the back beam.
Weft is under no such obligation. It gets laid in and packed down and then
lives a quiet life. Weft can be a soft single, a slubby novelty yarn, a strip
of torn bedsheet, or a length of grass.

How closely the warp ends sit is called the sett, measured in ends per inch,
and it is the single decision that most determines what the cloth will be
like. The quick estimate is to wrap the yarn snugly around a ruler for an
inch, count the wraps, and take about half that number for a balanced plain
weave. Set too open and the fabric is limp and sleazy. Set too close and the
shed will not open cleanly, the ends abrade against each other, and the whole
warp fights you for a week.

## Keeping Several Hundred Threads in Order

The warp is measured on a warping board, a frame of pegs, by walking the yarn
back and forth from peg to peg until you have the length you need, as many
times as you need ends. At one end of that path the yarn goes over one peg and
under the next in an alternating figure eight. This is the cross, and it is
the thing that makes the whole enterprise possible.

Four hundred identical threads, all the same color, all the same length, are
an unrecoverable tangle the moment they leave the pegs. The cross preserves
their order permanently: each thread is physically separated from its
neighbors at that one point, so you can pick them off one at a time, in
sequence, for as long as it takes. Weavers tie the cross before removing the
warp from the board, add choke ties along its length, and chain the whole
thing off the pegs like a crochet chain so it cannot spring apart. Losing a
cross is the kind of mistake that ends an afternoon.

## Dressing the Loom

Spreading comes first: the chained warp is opened out in a raddle, a bar with
pegs at half-inch intervals, so the ends sit at the width the cloth will be
rather than bunched in a rope. Then it is wound onto the back beam, with
sticks or heavy paper rolled in between the layers so the outer wraps cannot
sink into the inner ones and create slack. Even tension at this stage is worth
more than any amount of care later.

Threading follows. Each end is drawn, in the order the cross gives them,
through the eye of one heddle on one shaft, in the sequence the pattern calls
for. Then sleying: the ends are pulled through the dents of the reed, usually
one or two per dent, with a flat hook. Then they are tied in small bundles onto
the front apron rod, and the tension is evened by feel — you press down across
the warp with a flat hand and retie whichever bundle gives.

Then you weave a few inches of scrap yarn as a header, which spreads the warp
to its true width and, more importantly, shows you your mistakes. A crossed
pair of threads, a skipped heddle, one end sleyed into the wrong dent: all of
it appears immediately in the header as a float or a gap, while it is still
cheap to fix.

## What the Shafts Are Doing

A weaving draft is a small program, written as a diagram in three parts. The
threading says which shaft each warp end is assigned to. The tie-up says which
shafts each treadle lifts. The treadling says which treadles to press, in what
order. Press a treadle, some shafts rise, the warp ends threaded on them rise
with it, and the opening that appears between the raised and lowered ends is
the shed. The shuttle goes through; the reed swings forward and packs the new
row against the cloth; the next treadle changes which threads are up. Cloth is
made one binary choice per thread per row, several hundred choices at a time.

Plain weave, over one and under one, needs only two shafts and has the maximum
possible number of interlacements. That makes it the most stable and the
firmest structure and also the stiffest, and it eats yarn, because every
thread is constantly changing levels.

Twill lets each weft pass over two or more warps and shifts the sequence by
one thread each row, so the floats line up into a diagonal. Fewer
interlacements mean the threads can pack closer together, which is why twills
are denser, heavier, and drape far better than plain weave at the same sett.
Denim is a twill with an indigo-dyed warp and an undyed weft, which is exactly
why worn jeans go pale along the seams and knees: abrasion takes the dyed
surface off the warp floats and lets the pale core and the white weft show.
Reverse the diagonal at regular intervals and you have herringbone.

Satin pushes the logic further, scattering the binding points so that no two
are adjacent and no diagonal forms at all, leaving a surface of long floats
that reflects light evenly. It needs five shafts at minimum, and it is why
satin feels the way it does and snags the way it does.

Not all cloth is balanced. In tapestry and rug weaving the warp is set wide
apart and the weft is beaten down hard until it covers the warp completely, so
the visible surface is entirely weft and can be changed color by color across
the row. Narrow bands woven on an inkle loom do the opposite, packing the warp
so densely the weft never shows at all.

## The Hand in the Cloth

How hard you beat determines how many weft picks land per inch, and therefore
what the fabric becomes. The same warp, beaten lightly, gives an airy balanced
cloth; beaten hard, it gives a dense weft-faced one. Consistency matters more
than strength, and a weaver's own state is legible in the finished piece — a
visible band where someone wove tired, or fast, or angry.

There is a persistent beginner's problem called draw-in. The weft has to
travel over and under every warp end, so it needs more length than the bare
width of the warp. Pull it straight and snug and it steals that length from
the edges, dragging the selvedges inward until the outermost ends are under so
much strain that they fray and snap. The fix is to lay the weft into the shed
in a shallow arc, or at an angle, giving it the extra length before beating.
The same effect operates vertically as take-up, which is why cloth measured
under tension on the loom is always longer than the cloth you cut off it.

## It Is Not Cloth Until It Has Been Washed

What comes off the loom is a grid of threads that have never been allowed to
relax. Cut it free and it is still, in a sense, a diagram of fabric rather than
fabric. Wet finishing changes that. Washing lets the yarn bloom and the twist
settle, and the structure closes up and softens into something with a hand.
Wool taken further — agitated in hot water so the scales on the fibers ratchet
irreversibly together — fulls into cloth where the individual threads have
disappeared into a continuous surface, which is what a traditional blanket or
a heavy coating fabric actually is. Weavers who judge their work on the loom
are consistently disappointed and consistently wrong; the piece is not
finished until it has been in the sink.

All of this apparatus is a fairly recent convenience. The warp-weighted loom
of ancient Greece and northern Europe hung its warp from a beam and tensioned
it with clay weights, which is why loom weights turn up in so many
excavations. The backstrap loom used across Mesoamerica and the Andes runs
from a fixed post to a strap around the weaver's hips, so the tension is
supplied by the weaver leaning back, and the entire loom rolls up and goes in
a basket. Figured silk was woven in China on a drawloom, where a second person
sat above the warp pulling cords to select individual ends for each row — a job
eliminated at the start of the nineteenth century by a head that read the same
selections off a chain of punched cards, an idea that went on to have a rather
larger career than textiles.
