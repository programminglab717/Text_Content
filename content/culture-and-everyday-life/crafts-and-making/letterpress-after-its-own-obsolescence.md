# Letterpress After Its Own Obsolescence

The presses that survived did so because they were too heavy to throw away. A
Chandler & Price platen weighs the better part of a ton, and when a job shop
folded in 1968 the cheapest thing to do with it was leave it in the basement for
the next tenant. That is how a significant fraction of the world's working
letterpress equipment ended up in art school annexes and garages: not preserved,
just not worth the crane. Thirty years later people started coming to look for
it, and by the early 2000s a press that had been free for the hauling was worth
several thousand dollars.

It is worth being precise about what was abandoned, because the revival has
altered the story. Letterpress is relief printing: the image stands up from the
surface, ink is rolled onto that surface, paper is pressed against it. That
principle is older than Gutenberg and survives in the humblest rubber stamp.
What Gutenberg contributed, and what mattered, was the system for manufacturing
the image — a punch cut in steel, struck into copper to make a matrix, and the
matrix dropped into an adjustable hand mould that could cast an unlimited supply
of identical letters in a lead alloy, each one a different width but all exactly
the same height. Movable type is a mass-production system for a single component,
and everything downstream depends on the tolerances of that component.

## The Physical Grammar

Type is cast to a fixed height — in the English-speaking world, 0.918 inches
from foot to face — and every printing element in a forme must match it or it
will not touch the paper. The shoulder-to-shoulder width of a letter is its own
measurement, set by the matrix, and it is absolute. You cannot narrow a lowercase
n by two percent. If you want the line tighter, you must remove something, and
the only things available are the pieces of blank metal sitting between the
words.

Those blanks are the real curriculum. Spaces come in fractions of the em, the
square of the type's body size: a three-to-em space, a four-to-em, a
five-to-em, plus hair spaces thin enough to slide in like a shim. A compositor
picks letters out of a case and assembles them backward in a composing stick set
to the measure, and to justify a line — to make it exactly fill that measure —
must adjust the word spaces by hand until the line is tight enough to lift
without collapsing but not so tight that it buckles. Leading is a strip of lead
laid between lines, which is where the word comes from and why a designer still
speaks of leading rather than line spacing. Larger blanks, furniture, fill the
margins, and the whole assembly is locked into a steel frame, the chase, with
tapered wedges called quoins, then planed down with a flat block and a mallet
until every face sits at the same level.

Anyone who has done this for a week reads typography differently afterward.
Spacing stops being a slider and becomes a physical inventory of parts you
either have or do not have, and the rhythm of a well-set page starts to look
like a solved constraint problem rather than a matter of taste.

## Machines, and Then the End of Them

Hand composition was the bottleneck, and it was broken at the end of the
nineteenth century by two rival solutions. The Linotype, from Mergenthaler's
workshop, gave an operator a keyboard that released brass matrices down a chute
into a line, justified the line with expanding wedges, cast the whole thing as a
single slug of type metal, and then sorted the matrices back into their
magazine. It was fast and it was perfect for newspapers, where the slugs could
be dumped back into the melting pot every night. The Monotype took a different
route: a keyboard punched a paper tape, and a separate caster read the tape and
produced individual pieces of type, spaced and assembled in lines. Slower,
fussier, but the output was real type that could be corrected letter by letter,
which is why book publishers preferred it.

For about sixty years this was how nearly everything printed was made, and then
in the space of roughly two decades after the Second World War it stopped. Offset
lithography could print photographs properly, run faster, use cheaper paper, and
did not require a composing room full of skilled union labor. Phototypesetting
replaced metal with film and light. Foundries closed, matrices were scrapped,
and vast tonnages of type went back into the pot for the lead. The knowledge
went with it — not dramatically, just through retirement.

## The Thing the Revival Got Backward

Here is where the story turns strange. When letterpress came back, beginning
with fine-press and private-press work and then exploding commercially around
wedding stationery and business cards, it came back with an aesthetic that
working printers would have regarded as evidence of incompetence.

For the whole industrial era the goal was the kiss impression. Ink should be
laid onto the surface of the paper with only enough pressure to transfer it
cleanly, leaving no debossment, no visible bite. Punching the type into the
sheet was called a fault; it distorted the letterforms by squashing ink out
around the edges, showed through as bumps on the reverse side, and — the
argument that actually mattered in a job shop — it wore out expensive type at a
brutal rate. Makeready, the slow art of pasting tissue patches onto the tympan
behind the paper to bring up the light areas and relieve the heavy ones, existed
precisely to achieve even, minimal contact across an uneven forme.

The revival inverted this completely. Printing on thick, soft, damp-feeling
cotton paper, driving the plate in hard enough to leave a deep sculptural
depression you can read with your fingertips, is now the defining look of the
medium and the reason clients pay for it. And the inversion makes sense, once
you see what the printing is for. Nobody commissions letterpress because it is
the cheapest way to get ink onto card. They commission it because the physical
deformation of the sheet is the one quality that a scan, a screen, or an offset
press cannot reproduce. The impression is not the text. It is proof that a
machine touched the paper.

Purists were furious about this for years, and they were right on the history
and wrong about what was happening. The craft had changed jobs. It stopped being
a way to publish and became a way to make an object that is obviously
unpublishable at scale.

## Photopolymer, and What Counts

The other thing that makes the revival possible is a technology the old printers
never had. Photopolymer plates let a designer set type on a screen, output a
film negative, expose a light-sensitive polymer sheet through it, wash away the
unhardened resin, and mount the resulting relief plate type-high on a base. Any
typeface ever digitized becomes available. Illustrations, logos, and full pages
come as a single unit, with no lockup beyond positioning the base in the chase.

This is genuinely letterpress by the only definition that describes the physics:
a relief surface, inked, pressed into paper. It is also an entirely different
practice from composing in metal, and it removes the constraint that made metal
typography what it was. A polymer plate has no widths you must respect, no
spaces you must own, no risk of a line dropping out of the stick onto the floor.
It is design freedom with a letterpress finish.

Most working shops now run both. Metal and wood type for display lines, where
the character of a worn wood letter or a foundry face with real weight is the
point, and polymer for anything with body text or an image. The two sit in the
same chase. The argument about which is authentic has mostly exhausted itself,
partly because the shops that make a living do not have time for it and partly
because the older position depends on a purity that the industry itself never
had; printers adopted every labor-saving device they could afford, in every
generation, and would have adopted this one instantly.

What survives from the metal era, and what is worth the trouble of teaching, is
not the equipment. It is the habit of thinking about a page as an assembly of
physical parts with fixed dimensions. A compositor could not fake a fit. If a
headline did not fit the measure, the only options were to change the words,
change the size, or change the measure, and each of those had a cost that could
be counted in minutes and pieces of metal. That discipline is invisible in
software, where any string can be squeezed to fit anything, and its absence is
legible on a great many contemporary pages.

There is also, undeniably, the pleasure of the machine itself. A treadle platen
takes a rhythm out of the operator's body and gives it back as pressure; an
automatic windmill press clacks its grippers around in a fast, oddly animal
cycle that can hypnotize a room. The ink is tacky and smells of varnish. You
pull one sheet, look at it, change one thing, pull another. Everything is slow
enough to notice, which is a strange thing to charge money for, and which is
exactly what is being bought.
