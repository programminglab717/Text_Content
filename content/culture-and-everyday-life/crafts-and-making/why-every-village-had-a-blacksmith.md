# Why Every Village Had a Blacksmith

The best show in a country smithy was setting a tire. A wagon wheel is a ring
of wooden felloes pegged onto spokes, and after a few years of dry weather
every one of those joints works loose. The smith would make an iron hoop
slightly smaller than the wheel, heat it in a ring of burning scrap until it
glowed and grew, then lift it with tongs, drop it over the rim, and hammer it
home while an assistant poured water. The hoop shrank as it cooled. Steam went
up, the wood scorched and hissed, and every joint in the wheel closed at once
and stayed closed. No glue, no fastener, nothing but a piece of iron that had
been made temporarily too big.

That was the smith's whole business in miniature: metal is plastic when hot
and stubborn when cold, and a person who knows exactly how hot can make it do
almost anything.

## The Fire Is the Tool

A forge fire is not a bonfire with steel in it. Bituminous coal is banked
around the edges in a damp ring and slowly cokes as the heat drives off its
volatiles, so that the smith is always feeding fresh coke inward from a
smoking perimeter into a clean, hot heart. Air arrives from below through the
tuyere, from a bellows or a hand-cranked blower, and the flame has structure:
an outer envelope where there is plenty of oxygen, and a core where the fuel
has already consumed it.

The work goes in the core. Oxygen is the enemy of hot steel. In the oxidizing
envelope the surface turns to scale — the black flakes that fly off under the
first hammer blow and litter the floor around any anvil — and at high heat the
surface also loses carbon, which quietly ruins a tool steel before it is ever
quenched. Push the heat too far and the steel burns, throwing sparks like a
sparkler, and there is nothing to do but cut the ruined end off and start
again.

Judging that line is done by eye, which is why old smithies were kept dark.
In dim light hot steel moves through a legible sequence — a first faint red
you can barely see, then blood red, cherry, orange, a full yellow, and finally
a white too bright to look at directly. In a shop with a big south window the
distinctions collapse and everything just looks bright. A smith working in a
strange building usually finds a corner to stand in.

Mild steel forges happily from orange up. As it falls back through red it
stops flowing and begins to work-harden under the hammer, and hitting it
below that point puts cracks in it. "Strike while the iron is hot" is not a
metaphor about opportunity. It is a shop instruction about a window of maybe
forty seconds.

## The Anvil and the Vocabulary of the Hammer

A good anvil returns most of the energy of a dropped hammer. The classic
construction is a hardened steel face welded onto a wrought-iron body, and if
the weld is sound the hammer bounces back nearly to the height it fell from;
if the face has delaminated, the hammer lands dead. That rebound is not a
nicety. It does half the lifting, all day, for decades.

The rest of the anvil is a set of fixtures. The horn is a cone for bending
curves of continuously varying radius. The softer step between horn and face
is where you cut, so a chisel never meets the hardened plate. The square hardy
hole takes bottom tools that drop in and stay put: a cutoff, a fuller, swages
for rounding stock, forks for bending. The round pritchel hole gives punched
slugs somewhere to fall.

The operations themselves are few and endlessly recombined. Drawing out makes
a bar longer and thinner, and goes far faster over the horn or a fuller than
on the flat face, because a narrow contact concentrates the blow. Upsetting
does the opposite, thickening a bar by hammering on its end. Bending,
twisting, cutting hot. And punching and drifting, which is how a hole belongs
in forged work: the smith drives a punch most of the way through from one
side, flips, punches through from the other, then opens the hole by driving a
tapered drift. The metal is displaced rather than removed, so the grain flows
around the hole instead of being severed, and the eye of a hammer made this
way is thicker and stronger around the opening than the bar it came from.

## Welding Without Electricity

Two pieces of clean steel will bond in the solid state if you get them hot
enough and press them together hard enough. There is no melting involved, no
filler rod. The obstacle is that steel at that temperature is furiously
determined to oxidize, and even a film of scale between the surfaces will keep
them apart forever.

The answer is flux. Borax, or on old wrought iron simply a handful of clean
silica sand, melts into a syrupy glass that dissolves the oxide already there
and then caps the surface so no more can form. The smith brings both pieces up
past yellow to a heat where the surface looks faintly wet and an occasional
spark drifts off, takes them to the anvil fast, and sets the weld with light
taps — a heavy first blow squirts the flux and the joint out sideways. Only
once it has taken does the real hammering start.

Wrought iron is the forgiving material here, because the slag fibers running
through it act as their own flux; medieval work is full of welds that a modern
smith would sweat over. Mild steel is fussier. High-carbon steel has a narrow
window between welding heat and burning.

This is also the technique behind pattern welding. Stack alternating bars of
two steels, weld the stack solid, draw it out, fold it, weld again, and the
layer count doubles each time. Etch the finished surface with acid and the
alloys corrode at different rates, so the folded geometry appears as light and
dark figuring. It is a different thing from the crucible steel of South Asia,
whose patterns came from carbide banding inside a single cast ingot, though
both have been called Damascus for long enough that the words have given up.

## Straw and Blue

Only steel with enough carbon — somewhere above half a percent — will harden.
Heat it past a critical point, which a smith checks by touching it with a
magnet, since steel loses its magnetism just below the temperature needed, and
then quench it in water, brine, or oil. The carbon has no time to move out of
solution, the crystal structure locks into a strained form, and the result is
extremely hard and about as tough as a glass rod.

So it has to be given some of that hardness back. Polish the quenched piece
bright, then heat it gently and watch the oxide film thicken and run through
colors: pale straw, dark straw, bronze, purple, blue. Each color corresponds
to a temperature, and each temperature to a compromise between hardness and
toughness. Straw for a chisel or a knife edge, where you want the metal hard
enough to hold a cutting edge. Blue for a spring, which must bend and return
and never snap. The smith heats the thick part and lets the color creep along
toward the edge, pulling it out of the fire the instant the right shade
arrives at the right place, and a slow hand at that moment costs the whole
morning's work.

## The Stock in Trade

Before the railways, the village smith was the local manufacturer of nearly
everything made of metal. Hinges, latches, hasps, pot hooks, trammels, fire
irons, chain, hooks, harrow teeth, plow points, clevises, wagon hardware,
horseshoes, and the constant repair of all of it. Good steel was expensive
until the second half of the nineteenth century, so edge tools were commonly
made of wrought iron with a small piece of steel forge-welded on at the
cutting edge — laying a steel on an axe or a plane iron was routine work, and
so was welding a new one on when the old edge was ground away.

Then the economics reversed. Factory nails, rolled hinges, drop-forged tools
and machine-made chain arrived by rail at prices no hand smith could touch,
and each one peeled another line off the order book. The tractor finished what
the factory started. Between the wars most village forges either closed or
became garages, which is why so many rural repair shops sit in old stone
buildings with a wide door and a chimney at one end. Farriery survived because
a horse's hoof grows regardless of the industrial revolution.

The craft came back in the last third of the twentieth century, not as a
supply trade but as a deliberate one — artist-blacksmiths organizing
associations in the early 1970s, propane forges that a person can light in a
suburban garage, imported and rebuilt power hammers, and an enormous wave of
amateur knifemakers who all eventually discovered they needed to understand
heat treatment. What they inherited was not a business model. It was a set of
facts about a material that never changed: that steel has a window, that the
window is legible as color, and that all the information you need comes back
up the hammer handle if you are paying attention to it.
