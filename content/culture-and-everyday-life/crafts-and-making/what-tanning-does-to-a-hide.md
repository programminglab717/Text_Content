# What Tanning Does to a Hide

A fresh cowhide off the killing floor is not a material. It is an organ, wet and
heavy and full of fat, and left to itself in a warm room it will be liquefying
within two days. What it contains, though, is one of the most useful fibrous
structures in nature: a dense three-dimensional felt of collagen bundles,
interwoven at varying angles, tough in every direction and flexible in most of
them. The entire history of leatherwork is the history of methods for killing
everything in that hide that rots while leaving the collagen network intact and
supple.

Every tanning process, whatever the chemistry, does the same two things. It
strips out the parts of the skin that are not collagen — hair, epidermis, fat,
the soluble proteins — and then it locks the remaining collagen fibers against
one another with something that bacteria cannot digest and water cannot
undo. Get the first part wrong and the leather stinks and slips. Get the second
part wrong and it turns to glue the first time it gets wet.

## The Dirty End

The preparatory stages happen in what a traditional tannery called the
beamhouse, after the sloping wooden beam a worker leaned the hide over while
scraping it. Hides arrive salted or chilled and are first soaked back to
something like their original water content, dirt and blood and salt washing
out. Then comes liming: days in an alkaline bath, historically slaked lime and
now usually lime with sodium sulfide, which swells the hide, saponifies fats,
dissolves the proteins that anchor the hair follicles, and loosens the epidermis
so the hair can be pushed off in sheets. Fleshing removes the layer of fat and
connective tissue from the underside, by hand with a two-handled curved knife or
by machine between rollers.

A limed hide is rubbery, translucent and alkaline, and it will not take tannage
in that state. Deliming brings the pH back down with weak acids or ammonium
salts, and then bating — an enzyme treatment that eats the last of the
non-collagen proteins and relaxes the swollen fiber structure — leaves the hide
soft and slightly slack. Bating was for centuries done with warm infusions of
dog dung or bird droppings, which worked because they are rich in proteolytic
enzymes, and which is a large part of why tanneries were legally exiled to the
downwind edge of every medieval town. Purified enzymes now do the same job
without the sociology.

## Two Chemistries, Two Different Materials

Vegetable tanning uses tannins, the bitter polyphenols that oaks and chestnuts
and quebracho and mimosa make to discourage things from eating them. Ground bark
or wood is leached into liquor, and hides move slowly through a series of pits
from weak liquor to strong, over weeks or months, so the tannin has time to
penetrate all the way to the center rather than case-hardening the outside. The
tannin molecules are large and they hydrogen-bond to the peptide chains of the
collagen, effectively gluing the fiber network to itself at countless points.
The result is a firm, dense, faintly sweet-smelling leather that holds a
burnished edge, takes a stamped impression and keeps it, can be soaked and
molded over a form and will hold that shape when dry, and darkens steadily with
sunlight and handling into the color everyone thinks of as the color of leather.
It is also slow to make, and it will shrink and harden if it gets genuinely hot
and wet.

Chrome tanning, worked out in the last decades of the nineteenth century,
replaced months with about a day. Hides are pickled in acid and salt and then
tumbled in drums with basic chromium sulfate; the chromium forms coordination
complexes with the carboxyl groups along the collagen chains, crosslinking them
directly. What comes out is pale blue-grey and is universally called wet blue,
which is the form in which most of the world's leather is traded before anyone
has decided what it will become. Chrome leather is softer, stretchier, far more
resistant to heat and water, and it will not shrink in a boiling pot the way
vegetable-tanned leather will. Nearly all shoe uppers, garment leather,
upholstery and handbags are chrome-tanned. What it will not do is tool. Press a
stamp into it and the impression relaxes out again within minutes.

There are older and stranger methods that persist. Tawing with alum and salt
produces a brilliantly white, very strong material that is not, strictly, tanned
at all — wash it long enough and the alum leaches out and the skin reverts.
Brain tanning and other oil tannages work by driving emulsified fats and oils
into a dried, worked skin and then oxidizing them, giving the soft, stretchy,
smoke-cured buckskin of North American and Siberian traditions. Parchment is the
negative case: a limed skin dried under tension and never tanned at all, which
is why it remains sensitive to damp eight hundred years later.

After tannage comes the step that decides how the leather feels. Fatliquoring
drives emulsified oils between the fibers so they can slide past one another
instead of drying into a board; in traditional vegetable tanning the equivalent
was currying, hand-stuffing the leather with tallow and cod oil over days. The
difference between a stiff strap and a glove is not the tannage. It is what
happened afterward.

## The Hide as a Map

At the bench a hide is not uniform stock. Along the backbone and haunches — the
butt, or in a split hide the bend — the fiber is densest and most uniformly
interwoven, and the leather there is firm, thick, and stretches least. That is
where belts, straps, saddle skirting and knife sheaths come from, and it is why
a strap cut across the hide rather than along it will elongate in service. Toward
the belly the fiber structure is looser and more open; belly leather stretches,
takes dye unevenly, and is what an honest maker uses for linings and a careless
one uses for the visible parts. The shoulder is firm but carries the neck
wrinkles that show as ripples under a finish. The grain side, where the hair
was, is smooth and slightly waterproof and takes burnishing; the flesh side is
fibrous and fuzzy and has to be sealed or lined or it will fur up. Leather can
be split to an even thickness through its middle, which gives a grain-side sheet
and a suede-textured split from the flesh layer, and it can be skived — pared
thin with a very shallow-angled blade — wherever two layers have to overlap
without making a lump.

## Stitching That Cannot Unzip

The defining hand technique is the saddle stitch, and it exists because of a
specific failure mode. A sewing machine makes a lockstitch with two threads, one
above and one below, knotted between the layers. Cut that thread at any point
and the seam will run. The saddle stitch uses one thread with a needle at each
end. Holes are marked and opened first — with a diamond-section awl worked
against the grain of the leather, or pricking irons that punch a row of slanted
slits — and then the two needles pass through the same hole from opposite sides,
each thread crossing the other inside the hole. Every stitch is independently
locked. Cut one and the two on either side hold; a saddle-stitched seam wears
through gradually instead of failing all at once, which is the whole reason
harness makers kept doing it by hand long after machines existed.

The rest of the finishing work is mostly about edges, because a cut edge is the
one place where the leather's internal structure is exposed. The edge is
bevelled to knock off the two sharp corners, damped, and then rubbed hard with a
piece of shaped wood or bone — sometimes with gum tragacanth or wax, sometimes
with nothing but water and friction — until the heat and pressure compress the
fibers into a smooth, hard, faintly glossy surface. A well-burnished edge sheds
water and will not fray. An unburnished one starts fuzzing within a season, and
it is the single fastest way to tell how much care went into a piece.

Vegetable-tanned leather also invites the whole vocabulary of tooling, which
depends on a peculiarity of the material: dampen it to the right degree — cased,
in the trade, meaning wetted through and then allowed to return nearly to its
original color — and it becomes briefly plastic. A swivel knife cuts a line into
the surface without going through, stamps struck with a maul compress the fiber
around that line, and as the leather dries it sets permanently in the new shape,
the compressed areas darker than the rest. Nothing is added. The decoration is
just the hide remembering where it was hit.

That memory is the quality that makes leather peculiar among materials. A
wooden handle wears down; a steel blade loses metal; leather records. A belt
takes the curve of a particular waist, a shoe upper creases where one specific
foot flexes, a sheath opens out to the exact width of the blade kept in it. The
hide stopped being an organ in the lime pit, but it never entirely stopped
responding to what happens to it.
