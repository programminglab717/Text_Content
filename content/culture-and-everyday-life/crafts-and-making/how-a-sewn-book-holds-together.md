# How a Sewn Book Holds Together

Open a mass-market paperback to the middle and press it flat on a table. It
resists, then gives with a small crack, and from then on the book falls open at
that page whether you want it to or not. Now do the same to a well-made
hardback, or to almost any book printed before the middle of the nineteenth
century. It lies down obediently, the pages curving into the gutter, the spine
taking the strain across its whole width, and it closes again without a mark.
The difference is not quality of materials, or not only that. It is that one of
those books is a stack of leaves glued at the edge and the other is a hinged
mechanical structure with moving parts.

A bound book is built from folded sheets, not from single pages. A sheet folded
once makes four pages and is called a folio; folded twice, eight pages, a
quarto; three times, sixteen, an octavo. Whatever the count, the result is a
nested gathering with a single fold at the back, and that fold is the only thing
in the entire object that a needle can pass through without weakening it.
Binders call these units sections, gatherings, or signatures — the last name
comes from the letter or number printed at the foot of each one's first page,
put there so the gatherer could check the order before the book was sewn.

## Grain, and Why Binders Care About It

Machine-made paper has a direction. The fibers in the pulp align themselves with
the flow of the papermaking machine, and the dried sheet is stiffer across that
alignment than along it, expands more across it than along it when it takes up
moisture, and folds cleanly only in one of the two directions. In a book, the
grain must run head to tail, parallel to the spine. Get it wrong and every page
fights the fold, the leaves cockle in humid weather while the boards stay put,
and the whole textblock develops a wave that no amount of pressing will remove.
This is the first thing anyone learns at the bench and the first thing skipped
by anyone in a hurry, and it is the reason a cheaply made book can feel subtly
wrong in the hand long before it starts falling apart.

## The Sewing

The gatherings are stacked in order and sewn one to another through their folds.
In the older European tradition the thread passes around cords — lengths of
twisted hemp or linen stretched vertically in a sewing frame — so that each
section is lashed to every cord it crosses, and the cords run at right angles to
the folds like the rungs of a ladder. At the head and tail of each section the
needle makes a kettle stitch, hooking into the loop of the stitch below to link
the new section to the ones already sewn. The name is probably a worn-down
version of the German Kettenstich, chain stitch, and chain is exactly what it
makes: a continuous linked column at each end of the spine that keeps the stack
from sliding.

Sewn this way, the spine is not a glued face but a woven one. Any single
section can only come loose if its thread breaks, and even then the sections on
either side remain attached to the cords. Later binders sawed shallow grooves
across the folds and dropped the cords into them so the spine would be smooth,
which looks tidier and is structurally worse: the saw cuts the fold. Later still
came flat linen tapes in place of round cords, which is what most hand-bound
books use now.

Sewing also adds thickness. Every pass of the thread leaves a strand of linen
inside a fold, and multiplied by two or three hundred sections that adds up to
noticeable swell at the spine. A beginner treats this as a problem. A binder
treats it as the raw material for the next operation.

## Rounding, Backing, and the Shoulder

With the sewing done and a thin coat of glue brushed into the spine to hold the
sections in register, the binder lays the textblock on the bench and taps the
spine with the face of a backing hammer, working from one side and then the
other, until the flat spine has been persuaded into a shallow convex curve and
the fore-edge has taken a matching concave one. That curve is what lets the
pages fan open without the spine collapsing inward, and it is also why a rounded
book keeps its shape on a shelf while a flat-backed one slumps.

Then the textblock goes into a press between wooden boards, with a few
millimeters of the spine standing proud, and the binder hammers the outer
sections over sideways until they lean out over the press boards like the eaves
of a roof. This is backing. It produces a shoulder on each side, a ledge running
the length of the spine, and into that ledge the cover board will sit. The
shoulder does two jobs at once: it gives the board something to butt against so
it cannot creep toward the fore-edge, and it defines the hinge — the narrow
groove the board swings in every time the book is opened. Nearly all the wear a
book suffers in ordinary use happens in that groove.

## Getting the Boards On

In the oldest European structures the cords were not cut off. They were frayed
out, threaded through holes drilled in wooden boards, laced through channels cut
in the board's face, and pegged or hammered flat. The board and the textblock
became one object; a laced-in binding can survive the loss of its entire leather
covering and still work. Limp vellum bindings of the sixteenth and seventeenth
centuries used the same idea without boards at all, lacing the supports through
a stiff parchment wrapper.

The nineteenth century changed the order of operations in a way that made cheap
books possible. In case binding, the cover is built separately — two boards and
a spine strip glued to a piece of cloth — and the finished case is then attached
to the finished textblock only by the endpapers pasted down inside and whatever
tape ends can be tucked between board and pastedown. It is quick, it can be done
by machine, and it is why the covers of most twentieth-century hardbacks
eventually part company with their contents while the sewing is still perfectly
sound.

There is a further choice at the spine. On a tight back, the covering leather is
glued directly to the sewn spine, so the leather has to flex every time the book
opens; the spine of the book and the spine of the cover move as one, which reads
beautifully but eventually cracks the leather across the bands. On a hollow
back, a folded paper tube sits between the two, letting the textblock's spine
curve open while the cover's spine stays flat. Almost every modern hardback has
a hollow, which is why the lettering on the spine of a heavily read book stays
undistorted while the pages inside have loosened.

## The Small Parts and the Glue

Headbands were once functional. Sewn in silk or thread over a core of cord or
rolled leather, stitched down into the sections themselves and sometimes laced
into the boards, they reinforced the most vulnerable point of the spine, the
place a reader hooks a finger to pull the book off a shelf. By the time of
industrial binding they had become a strip of decorative woven tape glued into
the space where the real thing used to be — the vestigial organ of the book.

The adhesive matters more than it looks. Animal glue, made from hide and bone,
is strong and slightly flexible, and it can be softened with moisture and gentle
heat, which means a conservator two centuries from now can take the book apart
without destroying it. Polyvinyl acetate is stronger, stays flexible longer, and
is effectively permanent; once it is in the spine, the book has been committed
to that structure for good. Conservation binders lean toward reversibility on
principle, on the assumption that anything they do will eventually need undoing.

Which brings the argument back to the cracked paperback. Perfect binding — the
method that made cheap softcovers possible — begins by milling the folds off the
gathered sections entirely, reducing the book to a stack of loose single leaves,
and then bonding the roughened cut edges with hot-melt adhesive. Nothing passes
through anything. Every page is held by a film of glue perhaps a third of a
millimeter thick, and the moment that film goes brittle with age or is
over-flexed at one point, pages 212 through 219 leave together. Better
adhesives have improved the odds. They cannot change the geometry.

What the sewn book has instead is redundancy and a hinge. The thread makes each
section's fold load-bearing, the cords or tapes tie the sections to each other
and to the boards, the round distributes the opening force along a curve instead
of concentrating it on a line, and the backed shoulder gives the cover a joint
to rotate in. Every one of those features costs money and time, and every one of
them is a response to the same fact: a book is not a sculpture. It is a device
that must be opened and closed a few thousand times without complaint, and it
has to be built like one.
