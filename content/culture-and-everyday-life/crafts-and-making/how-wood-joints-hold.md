# How Wood Joints Hold

Pull open a drawer in a chest built in the 1870s and look at the corner. The
dovetails are almost always still tight. In many of them the glue has long
since given up — hide glue goes brittle, and a hundred summers in an unheated
attic will reduce it to powder that falls out when you tap the drawer side —
and yet the joint has not moved, because the shape of it was doing most of the
work all along. Meanwhile a flat-pack carcase from twenty years ago,
assembled with a far stronger adhesive, has come apart at every corner.
Explaining that gap is mostly a matter of explaining what wood does when
nobody is looking at it.

## A Material That Never Stops Moving

A board is a bundle of dead plumbing. The cells that carried water up the
living tree run lengthwise, and they leave the wood enormously strong along
the grain and weak across it: you can hang a car from a straight-grained ash
rail and split the same rail with a thumbnail's worth of wedge driven into its
end.

More consequential for joinery is that wood is hygroscopic and stays that way
forever. Dry lumber is not finished absorbing and releasing water; it is
merely in balance with the air around it at the moment. Raise the relative
humidity and the cell walls take on bound water and thicken. Lower it and they
give the water back and shrink. Crucially, almost all of this movement happens
across the grain, not along it. A board a foot wide can gain and lose
something on the order of a quarter inch of width between a humid August and a
January in a house with forced-air heat, while its length changes by an amount
too small to find with a tape measure.

The movement is not even across the grain either. Wood shrinks roughly twice
as much along the growth rings as it does across them, which is why a
flatsawn board cups away from the side that faced the heart of the tree, and
why quartersawn stock — sawn so the rings run edge to edge through the
thickness — stays so much flatter and costs so much more.

Every wood joint is therefore a negotiation between two pieces that want to
change size in different directions at the same time. Any construction that
rigidly fixes a wide piece of cross grain to a piece of long grain along its
whole length is a crack waiting for the first dry spell. The old furniture
that survives is old because the people who made it worked around that fact
rather than trying to overpower it.

## Glue, and Where It Refuses to Work

Modern woodworking glue is startlingly good at one specific thing. A joint
between two long-grain surfaces — two board edges rubbed together and clamped
— is stronger than the wood itself. Break a glued-up tabletop across the
joint and the failure usually wanders into the adjacent wood rather than
following the glue line.

End grain is the opposite case. It is a field of open tubes, so it wicks the
adhesive away from the joint plane and leaves the surface starved. Worse, it
offers cut fiber ends rather than fiber sides, and those ends peel apart with
very little persuasion. An end-grain butt joint, no matter how well clamped,
is close to worthless.

That single limitation generates most of traditional joinery. Nearly every
named joint is a device for converting an end-grain problem into a long-grain
one, or for replacing glue with geometry so that the joint would hold even if
the adhesive disappeared — which, over enough decades, it often does.

## The Mortise and the Tenon

The workhorse is a tongue cut on the end of one piece and a socket chopped in
the face of another. A common proportion puts the tenon at about a third of
the thickness of the stock, which leaves useful wood on both cheeks of the
mortise without making the tenon itself a wafer.

Each surface has a job. The cheeks — the broad faces of the tenon against the
broad faces of the mortise — are long grain meeting long grain, so they are
the glue joint. The shoulders, the little steps where the tenon leaves the
full width of the rail, do something the glue cannot: they resist racking.
When somebody leans back in a chair, the load on that joint is not a pull but
a rotation, and it is the shoulder bearing against the face of the leg that
stops the frame folding into a parallelogram. A tenon with sloppy shoulders
can be perfectly tight in the mortise and still feel loose in use.

Timber framers took the joint a step further and dispensed with glue
altogether. Bore a peg hole through the mortise, assemble the joint dry, mark
through, then take it apart and bore the tenon's hole a little closer to the
shoulder — a sixteenth of an inch is plenty. Drive a tapered oak peg through
the offset and it levers the shoulder hard against the post and keeps levering
for the life of the building. Barns raised with drawbored joints are still
standing with nothing holding them but wooden pins and the friction they
generate.

## Frames, Panels, and Deliberate Looseness

The six-board chest — a box made of wide planks nailed at the corners — is the
cautionary example. Wide cross-grained boards fastened at their edges cannot
shrink, so instead they split, usually right down the middle of the front.

The answer that spread through European furniture in the late medieval period
was frame and panel: a rectangle of narrow rails and stiles joined with
mortise and tenon, grooved on the inside edge, with a wide panel sitting loose
in the groove and glued nowhere at all. In damp weather the panel grows
further into the groove. In dry weather it withdraws. The frame, made of
pieces narrow enough that their own movement is trivial, never learns anything
happened. A door built this way in 1650 still closes.

The same logic runs through every good piece of case furniture. A breadboard
end on a tabletop is a cross piece held by a long tenon and pinned through
elongated holes, so that only the center pin is fixed and the outer pins can
slide as the top breathes. A tabletop is attached to its apron with buttons
that ride in a groove, or with screws through slotted holes, never with glue
across its width. The maker is not preventing movement. The maker is choosing
where it will go.

## Joints That Work by Shape

The dovetail is the clearest case of geometry doing the work. Tapered tails
on one board drop into matching pins on the other, and because the taper is
one-sided the joint locks against being pulled apart in a particular
direction. Drawers are pulled by the front, so the front corner joint lives
under tension — precisely the load dovetails refuse. Cut half-blind so the
tails do not show on the drawer face, they have been the mark of a competent
drawer for three centuries.

The angle of the taper is a compromise. Too shallow and the joint loses its
mechanical lock; too steep and the corners of the tails become short grain
that crumbles when the joint is driven together. Something near one in eight
suits dense hardwoods, and a blunter one in six suits softwoods, but the
numbers matter less than consistency.

Elsewhere the strategy differs. A box joint has no locking geometry at all and
relies instead on sheer area, alternating fingers that multiply the long-grain
glue surface many times over. A bridle joint is a mortise and tenon turned
inside out so that it can be cut entirely with a saw. Japanese carpenters
developed a whole family of scarf joints for splicing beams end to end, keyed
and wedged so that the load pulling the timbers apart is the same load
clamping the interlock shut.

## Chairs Are the Hard Case

A table only has to stand there. A chair receives a large, shifting,
off-center load several hundred times a year, and every joint in it sees
stress that reverses direction each time someone shifts their weight. The
members are slender, so the joints are small, and every applied force arrives
at the end of a lever.

Windsor chairmakers solved part of this with moisture rather than mechanics.
Turn the leg tenons from stock dried as hard as you can get it, then drive
them into a seat that is deliberately left a few points wetter. The seat gives
up moisture and shrinks onto the tenon; the tenon takes moisture up and swells
into the socket. The joint tightens itself over the following weeks. Add an
oval tenon in a round mortise, or a wedge driven across the seat's grain, and
the leg is not coming out.

There is one more reason old furniture outlives new. Hide glue can be undone
with heat and moisture, which means a chair that racks loose in 1910 can be
knocked apart, cleaned, and put back together in 1960 and again in 2015. Modern
waterproof adhesives are not reversible. A loose joint flooded with the wrong
glue is usually a joint that can never be properly repaired again, and the
chair's remaining life is measured from that day.

The real test of a joint has never been a load rating. It is fifty winters. A
joint fails not because somebody sat down too hard but because it was asked to
hold two pieces of moving wood perfectly still, and wood does not agree to
that. The joints that last are the ones that let the material do what it was
always going to do anyway, and simply refuse to be surprised by it.
