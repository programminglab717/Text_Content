# The Structural Logic of Baskets

There is no glue in a basket. No nails, no joinery, no fasteners of any kind in
most of them, and yet a well-made willow hamper will carry forty pounds of wet
laundry for thirty years and a coiled water bottle from the Great Basin will
hold water. What holds a basket together is friction, the stiffness of bent
plant material trying to straighten out, and the geometry of a curved surface
under continuous compression. Every part of it is pressing against every other
part. Cut one weaver and nothing falls apart; the neighbors take up the load.

Baskets are older than pottery and very much older than metal, and in dry caves
they survive from a startling depth of time — charred fragments, impressions in
clay, whole trays preserved in desert rockshelters. They were the first
containers that were not simply a hollowed gourd or a skin bag, and for most of
human history they were the default technology for moving, storing, sifting,
trapping, and measuring nearly everything. What follows is a way of looking at
them: not a history, but a guide to what you are actually seeing when you turn
one over.

## Four Ways to Build

Nearly every basket in the world belongs to one of a handful of structural
families, and the family is usually obvious within a few seconds of handling.

Plaiting interlaces flat, flexible elements that all do the same job — over one,
under one, or over two and under two, at right angles or on the diagonal. There
is no distinction between warp and weft; both directions are structural. Splint
baskets from the eastern woodlands of North America, palm-leaf mats and
carriers across the tropics, birch-bark containers, and most bamboo work are
plaited. Diagonal or twill plaiting, where elements cross at angles other than
ninety degrees, produces baskets that are noticeably springier and hold a curved
shape without needing rigid uprights.

Twining uses paired flexible wefts that twist around each other between each
passive warp, gripping it in the crossing. Because the warp is caught rather
than merely threaded past, twining can be worked tight enough to be watertight
and is strong even with very fine materials. It dominates the Pacific Northwest,
much of California, Aleutian grass work of almost thread-like fineness, and an
enormous range of soft bags and fish traps.

Coiling is different in kind. A continuous foundation — a bundle of grass,
a bunch of pine needles, a single rod, or a rope of straw — is wound in a rising
spiral, and each new turn is stitched to the one below with a flexible element
threaded through with an awl. The basket is sewn, not woven. Coiling gives the
tightest and most watertight vessels ever made from plants, the ones used with
hot stones for boiling, and it allows almost sculptural control of shape because
the maker decides where each coil sits rather than being governed by a grid.

Stake-and-strand work, which is what most people picture, stands rigid rods
upright and weaves flexible strands in and out of them. It is the willow
tradition of Europe, the rattan tradition of Southeast Asia, and the basis of
everything from a garden trug to a balloon gondola. It is fast, it uses cheap
material efficiently, and it produces the strongest basket per unit of labor of
any of the four.

## The Material Decides

It is tempting to say a maker chooses a technique. More often the plant
available chooses it for them, because each technique demands a particular
combination of length, flexibility, and cross-section.

Willow — osier — is grown deliberately, cut back hard every winter so that each
stool throws up a crop of straight, unbranched one-year rods. The rods are
graded by length, dried, and then re-soaked and wrapped in damp sacking to
mellow for a day before use. That detour through drying and rewetting is not
fussiness: green willow shrinks as it dries and the basket loosens, whereas
willow that has already shrunk once and been rewetted stays put. Black ash in
the northeastern woodlands is processed by an entirely different route, pounded
along the log with the back of an axe until the weak spring growth crushes and
whole annual rings peel away as ribbons, which are then split and scraped into
splints. The technique exists because ash grows that way, and the Wabanaki and
Haudenosaunee traditions built on it are now under direct threat from the
emerald ash borer, which kills the trees faster than anyone can stockpile logs.

Sweetgrass, bulrush, and palmetto in the Carolina Lowcountry gave rise to the
coiled baskets brought there through the rice plantations from West Africa and
made continuously ever since. Bear grass, spruce root, and cedar bark in the
Northwest; devil's claw, yucca, and willow in the Southwest; rye straw bound
with bramble in northern Europe, wound into beehives and bread-proving baskets.
Rattan is the great exception among all of these: a climbing palm with stems
tens of meters long, uniform in diameter, that can be split into round core or
flat cane and shipped anywhere, and its cheap global availability quietly
replaced a great many local materials in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

## Building One From the Bottom

Watching a round willow basket go up makes the structural argument better than
any description of it. The maker starts with a slath: six or eight thick rods
split through the middle and threaded at right angles through six or eight
others, making a cross. Two fine weavers twist around that cross to bind it,
then the arms are separated one by one into a radiating sunburst and the weaving
continues outward in a flat disc until the base is the right diameter.

Then the base is trimmed and new, heavier rods — the stakes — are shoved into
the woven base alongside each bottom stick and kinked sharply upward. This is
upsetting, and it is the moment the basket becomes three-dimensional. A few rows
of waling, where three or four weavers travel together in a rope-like stroke,
lock the stakes at that angle and set the flare of the sides. The rest of the
side is randed, a single weaver in and out of each stake, or slewed with two or
three at once for speed, with occasional bands of waling to keep the stakes
evenly spaced and to stiffen the wall.

The border is where a basket is made or ruined. The stakes are not cut off; each
is bent down in turn, taken behind its neighbors and woven into the rim, so that
the very elements that formed the sides become the structure that finishes them.
A good border is dense and even and does not move. A bad one unravels from a
single failure. Handles are a bow of thick rod pushed deep into the weave on
both sides and then wrapped with split willow that is itself locked back through
the wrapping — again, nothing glued, everything held by the tension of material
that would rather be straight.

## Why They Mostly Went Away, and Where They Didn't

Baskets lost the twentieth century to sawn boxes, corrugated cardboard, woven
polypropylene sacks, and plastic crates, and the reason is entirely about labor.
A basket is cheap in material and expensive in hours, and it cannot be usefully
mechanized, because every stroke depends on judging the taper and stiffness of
one particular rod. Where labor was cheap, baskets were the obvious container.
Where it is not, they are a luxury.

But some jobs still have no better answer. Hot-air balloon gondolas are still
woven from wicker, because the structure absorbs the shock of a hard landing by
deforming and springing back, it is extraordinarily light for its strength, and
it does not shatter or transmit a jolt the way an aluminum box would. Bread
bakers still proof dough in coiled cane bannetons because the wood wicks
moisture off the surface of the dough in a way no plastic does. Eel and lobster
traps in many places are still woven, because a funnel entry made of springy
rods is self-closing in a way that a rigid one is not. Fruit pickers in
orchards still use rigid, light, ventilated baskets for the same reasons they
always did.

Hold a good one up to a window and the structure is legible: the base worked
outward from a cross, the stakes rising, the bands of waling marking the points
where the maker stopped and reset the shape, the border binding the whole thing
shut. It is a complete record of the order in which the object was made,
something almost no manufactured container gives you. The basket is its own set
of instructions, if you know how to look.
