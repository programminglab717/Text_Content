# A Fabric Made of One Thread

Take a sweater that never fit, find the end of the yarn at the shoulder seam,
and pull. Given enough patience the whole garment comes back as a pile of
crinkled yarn and nothing else. There are no cut edges, no waste, no offcuts,
because the sweater was never anything but a single continuous thread bent
into a very large number of loops. That is the odd fact at the bottom of
knitting, and nearly everything distinctive about knitted fabric follows from
it.

## What the Loop Buys, and What It Costs

A knitted fabric is rows of loops, each one drawn through the loop below it.
The loops are free to change shape: pulled sideways they go short and wide,
pulled lengthwise they go tall and narrow, and the yarn itself barely stretches
at all while this is happening. The elasticity of a jumper is geometry, not
fiber.

This is why some garments are knitted and others are not. A suit jacket is
woven, because a jacket should hold a shape imposed on it by cutting and
tailoring. A sock is knitted, because a sock has to accommodate a heel going
through it and then close back around an ankle. Every T-shirt in the world is
machine-knit jersey for the same reason. Woven cloth stretches meaningfully
only on the diagonal, and a garment that needs to move with a body either
gets its ease from clever cutting or gets it free from loops.

The cost of the loop is that nothing is anchored. Cut a woven cloth and the
edge frays a little and then stops; the threads are held by their neighbors.
Cut a knit and the freed loops have nothing to hold them, and the column
directly above unravels all the way to the top — a run in a stocking, a ladder
in a sleeve. The same instability is a gift when you want it. A knitter who
finds a mistake forty rows back can pull the work out and knit the identical
yarn again, which is a kind of reversibility no weaver has.

## Two Faces of a Single Stitch

There is really only one knitting stitch. A knit and a purl are the same loop
made in opposite directions: on a knit you draw the new loop toward yourself,
on a purl you push it away. Look at the fabric from the front and a knit
stitch is a smooth V; turn it over and that identical stitch is a horizontal
bump. Every texture pattern in the craft is a decision about where to put V's
and where to put bumps.

Stockinette — knit on every right-side row, purl back — gives V's on one face
and bumps on the other, and it does not lie flat. The two faces have different
natural proportions, and the fabric relieves that imbalance by rolling: the
side edges curl toward the knit face, the cast-on and bound-off edges roll the
other way. This is not a flaw to be corrected so much as a fact to be planned
around, and it is the entire reason edgings exist.

Garter stitch, which alternates the two faces every row, is perfectly
balanced and lies flat. It is also much shorter per row than stockinette — two
rows make one visible ridge — and it stretches vertically as well as
horizontally, which stockinette really does not.

Ribbing is the most useful trick in the vocabulary. Alternate columns of knit
and purl and the purl columns retreat behind the knit columns, concertinaing
the fabric so that it pulls in hard and springs back. That is the whole
mechanism of a cuff, a hem, and a neckband: no elastic, no shaping, just
stitches arranged so the fabric would rather be narrow.

## Gauge Is the Unit of Measurement

Knitting patterns are written in stitches, not inches, and the conversion rate
between them is gauge — stitches and rows per unit of width and height.
Everything depends on it. A sweater body might be two hundred stitches around;
if your gauge runs half a stitch per inch finer than the pattern's, that body
comes out several inches too small, and no amount of blocking will rescue it.

Gauge is not a property of the yarn. It is a property of the yarn, the needle
size, the stitch pattern, and the individual knitter's hand tension, and two
people working from the same ball with the same needles routinely get
different numbers. This is why patterns demand a swatch, and why knitters
skip it, and why the internet is full of sweaters that fit nobody.

Stitch gauge and row gauge are separate measurements, and in stockinette there
are always more rows to an inch than stitches — something close to four rows
for every three stitches is typical. The asymmetry has consequences beyond
arithmetic. Knitted fabric hangs and lengthens under its own weight far more
readily than it widens, so a heavy cardigan will gain an inch of body length
over a winter that it never gives back.

Stitch pattern changes gauge too. Cables cross columns of stitches over each
other and pull the fabric in noticeably, so a cabled sweater needs more
stitches than a plain one for the same chest measurement. Lace does the
reverse. Stranded colorwork, with floats running behind the work, is
measurably tighter and less elastic than the same yarn in plain stockinette.

## Shaping Without Cutting

Sewn clothing is made by removing material: you cut a curve and the fabric
takes that curve. Knitting shapes by changing how many stitches exist.
Increases add loops, decreases take them away, and a garment's silhouette is
produced stitch by stitch as it is made.

Decreases lean. Knitting two stitches together tips the pair to the right;
slipping two and knitting them together tips it to the left. On a raglan line
or a V-neck, a knitter uses the right-leaning version on one side and the
left-leaning one on the other, so the two slopes mirror each other and the
seam line reads as deliberate. Getting that right is most of the difference
between a handknit that looks homemade and one that does not.

Short rows solve a different problem: adding height to part of a fabric
without changing its stitch count. You knit partway across, turn around, and
come back, building a wedge into the piece. That wedge is a sock heel, the
slope of a shoulder, the extra length over a bust, the flare in a shawl. The
turn leaves a small hole unless it is managed, and the several competing
methods for closing it — wrapping the adjacent stitch, or pulling the yarn
tight to make a doubled stitch — are all attacking the same bit of geometry.

Because tubes are as easy to knit as flat pieces, whole garments can be made
without a single seam, worked in the round from the hem up to the underarms
and then joined with the sleeves into a yoke. Seams are not purely
old-fashioned, though. A sewn shoulder seam stabilizes a garment that would
otherwise stretch out sideways under the weight of its own sleeves, and
knitters who go entirely seamless often end up reinforcing that one line
anyway.

## The Yarn Does More Than the Pattern

Woolen-spun yarn is carded rather than combed, so its fibers lie in every
direction and trap a great deal of air — light, warm, fuzzy, and not very
strong. Worsted-spun yarn is combed so the fibers run parallel — smooth,
dense, durable, and crisp. A cable pattern knit in a smooth round
worsted-spun yarn looks carved; the same pattern in a hairy woolen single
blurs into suggestion.

Twist matters too. A single unplied yarn carries all its twist in one
direction and will bias the fabric, so a stockinette panel knit from it leans
visibly to one side. Plying two or more singles in the opposite direction
cancels that out, and the number of plies changes how round the yarn sits and
therefore how sharply the stitches define.

Then there is blocking, which is the property that makes wool the default
fiber of the craft. Wet a finished wool piece, stretch it into the intended
shape, pin it, let it dry, and it stays there. Lace off the needles is a
crumpled grey rag that gives no hint of the pattern in it; pinned out damp, it
opens into the thing on the pattern cover. Superwash wool, treated to strip or
coat the scales so it survives a washing machine, loses most of that memory in
the bargain — it blocks beautifully and then relaxes and grows, which is worth
knowing before committing to a cardigan in it.

## A Surprisingly Late Invention

Weaving is many thousands of years old. Knitting is not. The earliest
undisputed knitted objects, patterned cotton socks from Egypt, date to around
the first millennium of the common era, and most older finds that look knitted
turn out to be nalbinding — a technique worked with a single needle and a
short length of yarn, structurally different, and notably impossible to
unravel.

Hand knitting spread through the Mediterranean and into Europe, and by the
later Middle Ages knitted caps and stockings were a guild trade. At the end of
the sixteenth century William Lee built a stocking frame, a machine with a row
of hooked needles that formed an entire course of loops at once. It is worth
noticing that this predates the mechanization of weaving by two centuries, and
that the framework-knitting cottages of the English Midlands were still
running on his principle when their workers began breaking frames in the early
nineteenth century.

What survived alongside the machines were the regional hand traditions, most
of them coastal and most of them practical: the ganseys of British fishing
ports, knit in the round in tight worsted yarn dense enough to turn wind, with
the patterning placed above the chest where a man's oilskins would not rub it
away; the cabled sweaters of the Aran islands; the stranded bands of Fair
Isle; Shetland lace shawls fine enough to be pulled through a wedding ring.
Fair Isle knitters also solved the problem that colorwork is far easier worked
in a tube by knitting the whole body closed, armholes and all, and then
cutting it open with scissors. The technique is called steeking, and it works
because traditional Shetland wool is sticky enough that the cut ends grip one
another and stay put. Try it in superwash and the sweater comes apart in your
hands.
