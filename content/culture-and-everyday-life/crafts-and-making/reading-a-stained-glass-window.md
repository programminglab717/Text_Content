# Reading a Stained Glass Window

Stand in the north transept of a large medieval cathedral on an overcast
afternoon and the windows look almost black. Wait for the cloud to thin and they
ignite — not evenly, but in patches, one lancet at a time, as the light moves.
The blues come up first and hold longest, a deep saturated color that seems to
sit forward of the plane of the window rather than behind it. Nineteenth-century
visitors were convinced this blue was a lost secret. It is not. It is cobalt,
melted into the glass itself, in a sheet that is thicker and more uneven than
anything a modern factory would tolerate, and it glows the way it does partly
because of the impurities and the bubbles and the varying thickness that scatter
the light on its way through.

A stained glass window is a structure held together by metal, made of
individually colored pieces, with the drawing done in three separate and quite
different ways at once. Learn to separate those three, and a window that
previously looked like a wash of color resolves into something you can read
almost line by line.

## Color Comes From the Furnace

Medieval colored glass is pot metal: the color is in the batch, stirred into the
molten glass in the pot before anything is blown. Metal oxides do the work.
Cobalt gives blue in very small quantities. Copper and iron in various
oxidation states give greens and the bottle-glass yellow-greens. Manganese gives
purple, and in trace amounts it decolorizes, which is why it was also used to
make glass that reads as white. Antimony and lead compounds give yellows.

Red is the exception and the one that betrays how difficult this chemistry was.
Copper ruby is so intensely colored that a sheet of it thick enough to handle
comes out almost opaque, a near-black that passes no useful light. The solution
was flashed glass: a gather of clear glass dipped into ruby, so the finished
sheet carries a skin of red perhaps a tenth of a millimeter thick on a clear
body. That skin can be abraded or, later, eaten away with acid, cutting back to
the clear layer beneath. A single piece of flashed glass can therefore carry two
colors, and glaziers used the trick to put a white inscription on a red ground
or a pale face on a colored robe without adding a lead line.

The sheets themselves came out of the glasshouse by one of two methods. In the
cylinder or muff process, the blower swung a long bubble into a tube, cut off
the ends, slit it lengthwise, and opened it flat in a kiln — producing rectangular
sheets with a slight waviness and a grain of elongated bubbles running one way.
In the crown process he spun an opened bubble into a flat disc by centrifugal
force, which gave brilliant fire-polished surfaces but a thickness that varied
from the rim inward, and a thick knot at the center where the pontil had been.
That knot, the bullseye, was waste that glaziers used for the least important
corners of a window. Its present career as a symbol of picturesque antiquity is
an accident of taste.

## Drawing With Lead

The window is designed at full size as a cartoon, and the cartoon is really a
map of lead lines, because a lead line is the one mark in the whole medium that
cannot be made any other way. Every change of color requires a cut, and every
cut requires lead. So the composition is worked out as a jigsaw first: where the
cloak ends and the background begins, where a halo separates from a shoulder.

Before the diamond point, glass was cut by running a hot iron along the surface
to start a crack and guiding it with a damp tool, then nibbling the piece to its
final outline with a grozing iron — a notched bar that chews away the edge in
small bites. Grozed edges are visibly ragged. Under the lead they don't show,
and on medieval glass they are one of the reliable markers of original material
against later replacement.

The cames that hold the pieces are H-section strips of lead, cast in molds in
the Middle Ages and later drawn through a mill that both stretched them and
pressed a decorative pattern on the face. Each piece of glass slides into the
channel on either side of the H, the joints are soldered where cames meet, and
then the whole panel is stiffened and weatherproofed by working a putty-like
cement under the flanges on both faces. That cement does more than keep out
rain: it turns a loose lattice of soft metal into a rigid panel. Even so, no
panel of any size can hold its own weight against wind, so windows are built in
sections that rest on horizontal saddle bars, tied to them with copper wire, and
the whole assembly sits in an iron armature — the ferramenta — bedded into the
stone. In the great thirteenth-century windows, those iron frames are shaped
into the medallions and quatrefoils of the design, so the ironwork and the
composition are the same decision.

## Drawing With Paint and Stain

The third kind of mark is painted. Glass paint is not a stain or a dye; it is
finely ground glass mixed with iron or copper oxide and a flux, suspended in
water or wine or vinegar with a binder of gum. It is applied to the surface and
then fired in a kiln until the ground glass softens and fuses to the sheet. Once
fired it is part of the window, as durable as the glass under it, and it is
opaque — it works by blocking light, not coloring it.

The painter lays in trace lines first: the contours of a face, the folds of
drapery, the hard black outlines that give the figure its structure at a
distance. Then a matt, a thin even wash over an area, which can be stippled,
softened with a badger-hair blender, or scrubbed back with a stiff brush and a
sharpened stick to pull out highlights. Modeling a cheek in stained glass means
removing paint rather than adding it. Everything on the glass is subtractive; the
only light available is what comes through from outside.

Then, sometime in the early fourteenth century, glaziers learned silver stain,
and the medium changed. A compound of silver painted on the back of a piece and
fired at the right temperature causes silver ions to migrate into the surface
layer of the glass itself, where they reduce to colloidal metal and produce a
transparent color ranging from pale lemon to deep amber depending on the glass
and the firing. Unlike paint, stain adds color. For the first time a single
piece of white glass could carry a figure with yellow hair and a gold crown and
a white face, no leads required. Fourteenth- and fifteenth-century windows
lighten dramatically as a result — more white glass, more grisaille, more
architectural canopy work in yellow — and the change is as much technical as it
is a matter of taste. By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, colored
enamels let painters treat the glass as a transparent panel to be painted like a
picture, which is when the mosaic logic of the medium largely collapses and
windows start to look like oil paintings backlit.

## What Time Does

Very little medieval glass survives in its original arrangement, and the reasons
are instructive. Lead fatigues: it creeps under its own weight and flexes with
every gust, and after a century or two the cames go brittle and the panel begins
to bow. Every window therefore has to be taken out, stripped, and releaded
periodically, and each time that happens someone makes decisions. Pieces broken
in handling are replaced with whatever is at hand. Panels are reassembled by
craftsmen who cannot read the iconography. Fragments from a smashed window are
combined into a patchwork and installed somewhere inconspicuous.

The glass itself weathers too. Medieval glass with a high potash content is
chemically less stable than modern soda-lime glass, and centuries of alternating
wet and dry leach alkali out of the exposed surface, leaving a pitted crust that
can grow thick enough to block the light almost completely. Conservators now
often deal with this by fitting an outer layer of protective glazing and
ventilating the gap, so the historic panel sits in still, dry air on the inside.

Which means that reading an old window includes reading its repairs. A figure
whose head is a different blue from its body, an inscription running into
nonsense, a border that changes pattern halfway up a lancet, a saint holding an
attribute that belongs to somebody else — these are not failures of the original
design. They are the record of every storm, every restoration campaign, every
wartime evacuation into a cellar and back. The window you are looking at was
made in the thirteenth century and edited continuously ever since, by people
whose names are mostly unknown, working in cold weather on scaffolding, trying
to keep the light coming through.
