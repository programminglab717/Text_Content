# How a Glaze Becomes Glass

Every glaze in a pottery studio looks like the same thing in the bucket:
grey-beige sludge with a skin of clear water on top, stirred with a stick and
labeled in marker on masking tape. Dip a bisque pot into it and the pot comes
out looking like chalk, dull and slightly furry, with fingerprints where you
held it. Two days later it comes out of the kiln as a glass-green bowl with a
rim the color of iron rust. Nothing was added in between except heat, and the
distance between those two states is where most of the interesting thinking in
ceramics happens.

## Why There Is a First Firing

Clay is a stack of microscopic plates, and what makes it plastic is water
films between those plates letting them slide past each other and then hold.
Drying removes the water between the plates, which is why a pot shrinks as it
dries and why it can be slaked back into usable clay at any point before the
kiln.

The first firing ends that possibility. Somewhere around five hundred degrees
Celsius the water that is chemically bound inside the mineral structure is
driven off, and the material stops being clay and becomes ceramic. A little
higher, at 573 degrees, the quartz in the body flips abruptly from one crystal
form to another with a small jump in volume, and flips back on the way down.
Potters climb and cool slowly through that point, because a kiln in a hurry
cracks pots there, often in a clean ring called a dunt.

What the first firing leaves is a pot that is hard enough to handle and still
thirsty. That porosity is the whole point: a bisque pot dipped in glaze pulls
the water straight out of the slurry and leaves the powder behind in an even
coat. It is the simplest possible application system, and it explains why the
bisque is fired lower than the glaze rather than the other way around.

## Three Jobs, One Melt

A glaze is a glass that is manufactured in place on the surface of the pot,
and like any glass it needs three things doing at once.

The glass former is silica. On its own, silica needs something like 1700
degrees to melt, far above any pottery kiln, so the second ingredient is a
flux: an oxide that breaks up the silica network and lets it soften at a
reachable temperature. The alkalis — sodium, potassium, lithium — are
aggressive fluxes. The alkaline earths — calcium, magnesium, barium,
strontium — do their work higher up and carry much of the melting in stoneware
glazes. Boron melts things at remarkably low temperatures and is odd in being
both a flux and a glass former itself. Lead was the great low-fire flux for
most of ceramic history, brilliant, forgiving of almost any error, and
poisonous enough that it has been pushed off food surfaces almost everywhere.

The third job belongs to alumina, and it is the one beginners overlook.
Alumina raises the viscosity of the melt. Without enough of it the glaze
behaves exactly as a melted glass should — it runs down the pot and welds it
to the kiln shelf. Alumina also suppresses crystal growth as the glaze cools,
which is what keeps a transparent glaze transparent instead of clouding over.
Too much and the glaze never flows properly and comes out dry and stony, which
is why an underfired-looking surface is not always a temperature problem.

Real materials arrive as packages rather than pure oxides. Feldspar supplies
alumina, silica, and an alkali flux in a single mineral, which is why it is
the backbone of most stoneware glazes. Whiting supplies calcium. Kaolin
supplies alumina and silica and, as a bonus, keeps everything else in
suspension in the bucket so the glaze does not set up like concrete overnight.
A glaze recipe is a list of rocks that between them add up to the right
proportions of three jobs.

## Heat Work Rather Than Temperature

Potters do not really fire to a temperature. They fire to a cone.

A pyrometric cone is a slender pyramid compounded to soften and bend over at a
particular combination of heat and time. Set three of them in the kiln in a
row and watch through the spyhole: the first slumps, then the target cone bends
until its tip touches the shelf, and that is the moment to shut down. The
reason is that a glaze responds to accumulated heat work, not to the highest
number a pyrometer showed. Two kilns that both reach the same peak will
produce different surfaces if one took two hours longer to get there, because
the slower one spent more time in the range where the melt was actually doing
chemistry.

The numbering is unhelpful to newcomers. The low range runs backwards through
06 and 04, and the high range forwards, so cone 10 is hotter than cone 6,
which is hotter than cone 04. In practice potters use the numbers as names for
whole ways of working: cone 6 means an electric kiln and a particular family
of glazes, cone 10 means gas and a different one.

Cooling matters as much as climbing. Many matte glazes are matte not because
they underfired but because microscopic crystals grew in them on the way down,
scattering light. Slow the cooling and a glossy glaze can turn silky;
crystalline glazes take this to its extreme, holding the kiln for hours in a
narrow band while single crystals grow into visible flowers several
centimeters across.

## The Argument Between Glaze and Pot

Glass and clay body both shrink as they cool from red heat, and they almost
never agree on how much.

If the glaze wants to contract more than the body, it ends up stretched over
the pot in tension, and it relieves that tension the only way glass can: it
cracks. The result is crazing, the fine network of lines you see in old
crockery. Sometimes it happens in the kiln, sometimes weeks later, announcing
itself with faint pings from the shelf as the body slowly takes up atmospheric
moisture and swells. Chinese and Korean potters cultivated crazing
deliberately and stained the cracks to make crackle wares. On a mug it is a
defect, because the cracks hold liquid, harbor whatever was in it, and cut the
strength of the pot considerably.

The opposite mismatch puts the glaze in compression, and a moderate amount of
compression is ideal — a well-fitted glaze measurably strengthens a pot, the
way a tensioned skin stiffens a drum. Push it too far and the glaze shivers,
flaking off rims and handle edges in slivers sharp enough to cut. Fixing fit
is a matter of adjusting the recipe's expansion: less sodium and potassium,
more silica, sometimes a swap to lithium, sometimes a change of clay body
instead.

## What the Air Does

An electric kiln has air in it and stays that way. A kiln burning gas, wood,
or oil can be starved deliberately, running richer than the available oxygen
can consume, and the carbon monoxide that results goes looking for oxygen
anywhere it can find it — including inside the metal oxides in the glaze and
the clay. That is reduction firing, and it rewrites the palette.

Iron is the great example because it is everywhere in ceramic materials. In
oxidation a couple of percent of iron gives ambers and honey browns, and a
heavy dose gives the glossy black-brown of the tenmoku family, breaking to
rust where it thins over an edge. In reduction the same iron gives the cool
blue-greens of celadon. The entire celadon tradition, from Longquan to the
Goryeo kilns of Korea, is a small amount of iron and a kiln kept hungry.

Copper is more dramatic and less obedient. Green in oxidation, it can be
pushed in reduction to metallic copper dispersed through the glass at
colloidal size, producing the reds that Chinese potters called ox-blood.
Copper also volatilizes at temperature and will flash pink onto neighbors on
the shelf, which makes it unpopular in shared kilns. Cobalt, by contrast, is
indifferent to atmosphere and ferociously strong: a fraction of a percent will
blue an entire glaze, and the blue-and-white porcelain trade was built on
cobalt shipped from Persia to the kilns at Jingdezhen.

## Ash, Salt, and Useful Accidents

The first high-fired glazes were almost certainly not applied by anyone.
Wood-fired kilns fill with flying ash, and wood ash is mostly calcium and
potassium — fluxes — so at stoneware heat it melts into the silica of the clay
surface and glazes the pot wherever the flame carried it. Potters noticed,
started collecting ash and applying it on purpose, and the ash glaze became one
of the oldest deliberate recipes in the craft. In a long anagama firing the
surface of a pot is a record of exactly where it stood in the flame path, which
is why those pots are stacked with as much care as they are made.

Salt glazing works by a related trick. Common salt thrown into a hot kiln
vaporizes, and the sodium reacts directly with the silica and alumina already
present in the clay surface, growing a thin, hard, orange-peel-textured glass
out of the pot itself rather than depositing one on top. It gave the Rhineland
its stoneware jugs and the nineteenth century its pickling crocks and sewer
pipe, and it is now often done with soda ash instead, which produces less
hydrochloric acid on the way out of the chimney.

None of this is predictable enough to design on paper. Studios run on test
tiles: line blends stepping between two materials in ten stages, triaxials
crossing three, all fired standing upright so you can see how the glaze moves
on a vertical wall and what it does over an edge. A potter with twenty years
in one studio has a wall of these, and still stirs a bucket of grey sludge that
looks exactly like every other bucket of grey sludge.
