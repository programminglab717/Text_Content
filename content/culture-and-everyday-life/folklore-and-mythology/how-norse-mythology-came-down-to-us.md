# How Norse Mythology Came Down to Us

Forty-five leaves of vellum, written in Iceland somewhere around 1270, carry
most of what anyone knows about the gods of the pre-Christian North. The book
has no title page and names no author. It surfaced in 1643 in the hands of
Brynjólfur Sveinsson, bishop of Skálholt, went to Copenhagen in the 1660s as a
gift to the Danish crown, sat in the royal library for three centuries, and came
home to Reykjavík aboard a Danish naval vessel in 1971, met at the dock by
crowds and schoolchildren. It is called the Codex Regius, the King's Book.
Remove it and Odin loses most of his biography.

That fact is worth holding onto before reading any retelling of Norse myth. The
stories were not written down by the people who believed them. Iceland accepted
Christianity by a decision of its assembly around the year 1000, and every
substantial written source for the old gods was composed at least two centuries
later, by Christians, for purposes that had little to do with worship.

## A Handbook for Poets

The most important of those sources was written by a wealthy, litigious
Icelandic chieftain named Snorri Sturluson, around 1220. Snorri was a politician
who was eventually killed in the cellar of his own farm at Reykholt over a
quarrel about Norwegian royal influence in Iceland. He was also the finest prose
stylist of his century. His Edda was not an act of piety or nostalgia. It was a
technical manual.

Skaldic verse, the court poetry of the Norse world, ran on kennings —
compressed riddling substitutions in which gold is "Sif's hair," the sky is
"Ymir's skull," poetry is "Kvasir's blood," and a ship is the horse of the wave.
Every one of those demands that the listener already know a story. Sif's hair is
gold because Loki sheared it off while she slept and dwarfs forged a replacement
out of spun metal. Kvasir's blood is poetry because Kvasir, a being formed from
the mingled spittle of two families of gods, was murdered by dwarfs and brewed
into a mead that confers the gift of verse. Strip the myths out and an entire
poetic tradition becomes noise — and by Snorri's day it was already becoming
noise to young poets raised on saints rather than giants.

So Snorri laid the stories out in order, in plain prose, so the craft could go
on. The mythological section, Gylfaginning, is staged as an interrogation: a
Swedish king walks into Asgard in disguise and questions three enthroned figures
about the beginning and end of the world, and they answer him at length before
the hall vanishes out from under him. It is elegant narrative engineering, and
it is also a hedge. Snorri is reporting what heathens said, not professing
anything; a prologue explains the gods as clever migrants out of Troy whom
simple people mistook for divinities. That euhemerizing frame let him preserve
the material without being accused of teaching it.

The result is our most orderly account of Norse cosmology, and its orderliness
is exactly what should make a reader careful. Snorri gives a tidy sequence from
the void of Ginnungagap, where the ice of the north met the fire of the south,
through the cow Auðumbla licking a man free from salted rime, through the
killing of the giant Ymir and the construction of the world from his corpse,
down to the fire at the end of things. Living religions rarely file themselves
so neatly. Some of that shape is Snorri's.

## The Poems Behind the Prose

The Codex Regius is a different kind of witness. It contains no connecting
narrative, only poems, many of which Snorri clearly knew and quoted. They come
in two rough groups. Some are mythological: Völuspá, in which a seeress
summoned by Odin recites the world's history from its making to its burning and
its rising again; Hávamál, a long accumulation of gnomic advice attributed to
Odin, much of it startlingly practical about guests, drink, and friendship;
Grímnismál and Vafþrúðnismál, both contests of cosmological knowledge conducted
under threat of death. Others are heroic, concerning Sigurd, the dragon Fafnir,
Brynhild, and the Burgundian kings, material that also fed the German
Nibelungenlied.

Nobody knows how old these poems are. Their language is archaic, their metres
are traditional, and some of the stories they tell appear on carved stones
centuries before any manuscript. But oral verse can be composed in an archaic
register at any date, and the scribe of 1270 was copying from something, which
was itself copied from something. Dating individual poems has occupied
generations of philologists without producing consensus.

What the poems give, and Snorri does not, is texture. They are allusive,
sardonic, frequently obscure. Gods insult each other at a feast in Lokasenna
until Thor arrives and ends the argument by threat. Thor dresses as a bride to
recover his stolen hammer in Þrymskviða, and the poem finds this very funny.
Völuspá refuses to explain itself and moves in flashes. A mythology reconstructed
from the poems alone would be a much stranger and more fragmentary thing than
the one most readers meet.

## What the Ground and the Map Say

Against these late texts sit sources that are contemporary with belief, though
far less articulate. Runestones raised in the tenth and eleventh centuries
sometimes invoke Thor to hallow the inscription. Gotland's picture stones show
eight-legged horses and armed women offering drinking horns. Small hammer
pendants turn up in graves, occasionally from the same workshops as crosses,
which suggests craftsmen supplying a market rather than a confession. The
Oseberg ship burial in Norway held a woman of evident status among textiles,
sledges, and slaughtered horses, and whatever it says about the afterlife it
says without words.

Place-names are the quietest and most useful evidence. Odense in Denmark carries
Odin's name and the word for a sanctuary. Scandinavia is scattered with names
compounded from Thor, Freyr, Ullr, and Njörðr, and the distribution is not even.
Some gods are dense in one region and absent in another. Ullr, barely a
character in the literary sources, is all over central Sweden and Norway in
place-names, which implies he mattered enormously to somebody and simply did not
make it into the stories that got written. Conversely, Loki, who is everywhere in
the Eddas, is nearly invisible in cult toponymy, which may mean he was a
narrative figure rather than an object of worship.

Add the outside observers. Tacitus, writing at the end of the first century,
describes Germanic peoples worshipping gods he calls Mercury, Hercules, and
Mars, which are usually read as Odin, Thor, and Tyr. Adam of Bremen, in the
eleventh century, gives a secondhand and hostile account of a temple at Uppsala
with three idols and nine-yearly sacrifices, a passage argued over ever since.
The Arab traveller Ibn Fadlan watched a Rus funeral on the Volga and recorded a
ship cremation and a killed slave girl. None of these men understood what they
were describing. All of them saw something.

## The Seams Show

Because the sources are late and Christian, the seams show, and scholars have
spent two centuries picking at them. Völuspá's ending, in which the world burns,
sinks, and then rises green again, with a returning Baldr and a hall roofed in
gold, sits uncomfortably close to Christian eschatology. Some read it as pure
heathen apocalypse; some as a poem composed at the moment of conversion by
someone who knew both systems and was deliberately building a bridge. The
evidence does not settle it.

Snorri's neat division of the gods into Æsir and Vanir, reconciled after a war
by an exchange of hostages that sent Njörðr, Freyr, and Freyja to Asgard, has
been read as a memory of two merged cults, as a mythic account of the difference
between sovereignty and fertility, and as literary tidying. The nine worlds hung
on the ash Yggdrasil are famous, but no surviving source actually lists nine
consistently, and the familiar enumeration is largely a modern assembly.

What survives, then, is not a scripture and not a system. It is a rich, patchy,
internally inconsistent body of narrative preserved almost by accident, because
a poetic craft needed footnotes and a bishop kept a book. Four days of the
English week still carry the names — Tuesday for Tyr, Wednesday for Woden,
Thursday for Thor, Friday most likely for Frigg. People say them without
noticing, which is roughly how the whole tradition has travelled: embedded in
something else, doing a job, waiting for someone to look at it closely.
