# The Night Parade and the Catalogue

In a set of Japanese handscrolls painted some five centuries ago, a
procession moves down a street after dark. The marchers are not people. A
lute walks on two human legs. A cooking pot opens a mouth along its rim. A
paper lantern splits down its seam and rolls a single eye at the viewer. A
straw sandal, discarded long enough to have taken it personally, trots along
near the back. The scrolls are known as hyakki yagyo, the night parade of a
hundred demons, and they are funny before they are frightening. That is the
first useful thing to know about the creatures Japanese speakers now gather
under the word yokai.

Yokai is a fairly modern umbrella. For most of the centuries in which these
beings were genuinely believed in, people reached for older and vaguer words:
bakemono, things that change; obake, much the same; mononoke, a presence with
a grievance; ayakashi for whatever was wrong out at sea. None of those words
draws its boundary where a European bestiary would. A yokai can be a ghost, a
shapeshifting animal, a god who has slipped down the social ladder, an
epidemic, an odd noise in a bamboo grove, or a broom. The category is less a
taxonomy of monsters than a name for the residue of explanation — whatever
is left over after the ordinary accounting of an event has been done.

## Gods on the Way Down

A great deal of what became yokai started higher up. Japanese religious
practice has long allowed that a single force can bless or harm depending on
how it is handled, and a spirit that stops being venerated does not politely
cease to exist. Kunio Yanagita, who effectively founded the academic study of
Japanese folklore in the early twentieth century, made the argument that has
framed the subject ever since: many a yokai is a kami who lost his shrine.

The tengu shows the mechanism clearly. Now pictured as a red-faced mountain
figure with an absurd nose and a feather fan, the tengu began as a bird-demon
associated with pride and with the corruption of monks, and along the way
absorbed the reputation of the yamabushi, the mountain ascetics whose small
round caps and pom-pommed robes tengu are almost always shown wearing. The
kitsune runs the same double life. A fox can trick a traveller into wading
into a river in the belief that he is walking a road, and a fox is also the
messenger of Inari, among the most widely worshipped deities in the country,
with shrines in tens of thousands of places from mountain passes to the roofs
of department stores. The same animal does both jobs, and the difference
between the jobs is mostly a question of whether someone is keeping up the
offerings.

This is why yokai cluster so reliably at edges. They belong to bridges,
crossroads, mountain passes, the last stretch of road between the final rice
paddy and the first line of trees. They belong to the hour traditionally
called the time of meeting, when a shape at distance could be a man or could
be something else. Flooded irrigation channels, wells, the bend in a river
where the current digs deep, the empty house at the end of the lane: the
geography of yokai is an accurate map of where a person in a pre-industrial
village was most likely to come to harm and least likely to be seen coming to
it.

## A Short Field Guide

The kappa is the best known river dweller and a good illustration of how
specific these beings get. It is roughly child-sized, green, shelled like a
turtle, webbed, and it carries a depression on the crown of its head filled
with water. That dish is its weakness and its rulebook. Spill the water and
the kappa loses its strength; bow to a kappa and it will bow back out of
ingrained politeness, tipping the dish, which is the whole reason the advice
circulated. Kappa wrestle. They like cucumbers, which is why a certain kind
of sushi roll carries the name. They also drown horses and children, and in
the least charming versions they reach into a swimmer to pull out an organ
believed to sit just inside the anus. A warning about deep water, elaborated
for four hundred years, arrives eventually with a taste in cucumbers and a
code of manners.

Others are briefer. The noppera-bo is simply a person you meet on a road who
turns to face you and has no face at all; the entire content of the encounter
is the turning. The yuki-onna appears in snow, beautiful and fatally cold,
sometimes as a woman asking a traveller to hold her child, which grows
heavier until he cannot move. The rokurokubi looks ordinary by day and lets
her neck extend across the room at night. The tsukumogami are the household
objects of the night parade, and the folk rule attached to them is oddly
bureaucratic: an object that reaches a hundred years of service acquires a
spirit, so worn-out tools should be disposed of with some ceremony rather
than simply thrown out. Temples still hold rites for retired sewing needles,
pressed into blocks of tofu so their last task is a soft one.

## Sekien's Bestiary

The step from scattered village belief to something like a catalogue happened
in the eighteenth century, in print, and it changed what yokai are. Toriyama
Sekien was a painter in the ukiyo-e world — Utamaro trained with him —
and in the 1770s he began publishing illustrated compendia of yokai laid out
in the format of an encyclopedia. One creature per page, a picture, a short
explanatory note, sometimes a pun. He produced several volumes, moving from
figures with real currency in oral tradition to increasingly literary and
invented ones. Scholars are fairly sure that a number of the later entries
are Sekien's own creations, made up in the voice of received knowledge, and
that some of those inventions passed into the tradition and are now cited as
ancient.

The books sold. Once yokai existed as a picture set with names and captions,
they could be turned into other picture sets: karuta card games, sugoroku
boards where players advance past one monster to the next, cheap illustrated
fiction, ghost prints for the hot months. Edo townspeople played a parlour
game called hyakumonogatari kaidankai, in which a group lit a hundred candles
or wicks, took turns telling frightening stories, and extinguished one light
after each tale, so the room darkened as the evening went on. Something was
supposed to arrive when the hundredth was blown out. Reports differ on
whether anyone actually went that far.

Domestication of this kind had a cost and a benefit. The benefit is that a
colossal amount of regional material got written down and drawn during a
period when it might otherwise have thinned out. The cost is that the printed
yokai became a kind of costume set, detachable from the particular pond or
pass that had produced it. A kappa in a Tokyo card game is not warning anyone
about a specific irrigation ditch.

## Debunkers, Villages, and Kitaro

The Meiji period brought the opposite impulse. Inoue Enryo, a Buddhist
philosopher with a reformist streak, built a discipline he named yokaigaku
— monsterology — for the express purpose of sorting apparent marvels into
misperception, fraud, and the small unexplained remainder. He collected
diligently in order to dispose of the collection. Yanagita, a generation
later, collected in order to preserve, treating the tales as evidence about
the people telling them. His early book on the legends of Tono, a district in
the northeast, reads less like a bestiary than like testimony: this is what
they say happens at that place, this is who told me, this is what they do
about it.

Then a manga artist made the whole inventory popular again. Shigeru Mizuki
grew up hearing local ghost stories, lost his left arm in the Pacific war,
and spent decades drawing yokai with an eye for the ones nobody else bothered
with. His long-running series about the boy Kitaro, a one-eyed yokai child
who mediates between humans and monsters, put hundreds of obscure creatures
in front of a mass postwar audience, often with a page of straight-faced lore
attached. A large share of what a Japanese ten-year-old can now name comes
through Mizuki, and through him back to Sekien, and through Sekien to a
scatter of villages that no longer exist in the same form.

The line is still live. In the spring of 2020, Japanese social media revived
an obscure nineteenth-century figure called amabie, a beaked, scaled,
long-haired creature said to have emerged from the sea and predicted a good
harvest followed by an epidemic, with the instruction that its picture be
drawn and shown to others as protection. Tens of thousands of people drew it.
Nobody involved appeared to believe that a sketch would stop a virus. They
were doing what the Edo card players and the candle-blowers were doing:
handling a frightening thing by giving it a shape, a name, and a slightly
ridiculous face, and passing it along.
