# How a Cricket Match Is Built

A fielding side spends a surprising amount of a cricket match walking. Six
deliveries go down from one end of the pitch, and then, with nothing dramatic
having happened, thirteen people rearrange themselves. The fielders cross to
mirror-image positions at the far end. One umpire moves from behind the stumps
out to square leg; the other comes in to take his place. A different bowler
paces out a run-up. That is the over, cricket's basic accounting unit, and the
small ceremony at the end of each one is the first clue that the game is made of
short cycles nested inside very long ones.

Strip away the folklore and the structure states itself in a paragraph. Two
teams of eleven. A strip of rolled and mown earth twenty-two yards long with
three wooden stumps planted at each end and two small wooden bails balanced
across their tops. One side bats, trying to score runs; the other bowls and
fields, trying to get the batters out. When ten of the eleven batters have been
dismissed — the eleventh has nobody left to bat with — the innings ends and the
sides swap roles. Whoever has more runs at the finish wins. Everything else is
elaboration, but the elaboration is where the game actually lives.

## The Over, and Why the Ends Change

A bowler delivers the ball with a straight arm, which is what separates bowling
from throwing and is policed more carefully than any other point of technique.
Almost always the ball is bounced once on the pitch, so that what the surface
does to it — the seam gripping and deviating, the ball turning off a dry patch,
the bounce staying unexpectedly low — becomes part of the contest rather than an
accident of it. Six legal deliveries make an over. Balls that pass too wide of
the batter to be hittable, or that involve the bowler overstepping his crease,
do not count towards the six and cost the fielding side a run on top of that.

When the over is finished, the bowling switches to the other end. No bowler may
bowl two overs in succession, so a captain has to keep at least two in rotation,
and in practice manages four or five across a long innings, reading the pitch
and the wind and his bowlers' legs. The end-change is not decoration. It means
each bowler works with a different slope, a different breeze, a different worn
patch where his opposite number has been landing the ball, and the batter who
was facing suddenly finds himself stranded at the non-striker's end. Rhythm in
cricket is constantly being broken and reset, which is part of why the game
resists impatient watching and rewards slow watching.

## Two Batters, One Ball

Only one batter faces any given delivery, but two are always out there,
standing at opposite ends. This is the detail that generates most of the game's
tactical texture. Runs are scored by the pair running past each other and
grounding bat or body behind the far crease; each completed exchange is one run.
Hit the ball hard enough that it reaches the boundary rope along the ground and
four runs are awarded with nobody running at all. Clear the rope in the air and
it is six.

Because an odd number of runs swaps who is on strike, a great deal of batting
has nothing to do with boundaries and everything to do with deciding who faces
the next ball. A settled batter shepherding a nervous partner will refuse an
easy single in the middle of an over and then take one off the very last ball,
because the ends change afterwards and he keeps the strike for the following
over. Commentators call it farming the strike. The fielding captain answers by
setting a tight ring to cut off the single and force the weaker batter to face.
The running itself is live risk: a fielder who gathers the ball and breaks the
stumps before a batter has grounded his bat has run him out, and run-outs are
the only dismissals that arise purely from a failure of judgement between two
people who are shouting at each other.

## The Ways of Getting Out

Six methods account for nearly everything. Bowled, when the ball beats the bat
and knocks a bail off the stumps. Caught, when a fielder takes the ball cleanly
before it touches the ground after it has come off the bat. Leg before wicket —
lbw — when the ball strikes the batter's body, usually the front pad, in a
position where the umpire judges it would otherwise have gone on to hit the
stumps. Run out. Stumped, when a batter steps out of his crease to a slower
ball, misses it, and the wicketkeeper removes the bails before he can scramble
back. Hit wicket, when a batter destroys his own stumps in the act of playing a
shot. A handful of rarer dismissals exist — obstructing the field, hitting the
ball twice, being timed out — and they surface perhaps once a decade at the top
level, always accompanied by an argument about sportsmanship.

Lbw deserves its own note, because it is the rule that keeps the rest of the
game honest. Without it a batter could simply stand in front of his stumps and
let the ball thud into his legs all afternoon. With it, the bowler has a target
that must be defended with the bat, which is why so much batting technique is
organised around keeping the front pad out of the line of the ball rather than
in it. The judgement required — where the ball pitched, where it struck the
batter, whether it was still going on to hit — is genuinely hard, and it is the
reason ball-tracking technology entered cricket through the umpire review system
before it touched any other kind of decision.

## An Innings as a Budget

What makes cricket unusual among team sports is that different formats hand the
batting side different scarce resources, and the strategic character of a match
follows almost entirely from which resource is scarce.

In a Test match, the long traditional form, each side bats twice across five
days and no limit is placed on how many overs an innings may last. Wickets are
the budget; time presses on both sides at once. A team can bat for two days if
it can keep ten of its players alive, but a team that bats too long may not
leave itself the overs to bowl the opposition out twice, and a match that runs
out of days with the fourth innings unfinished is a draw — a result distinct
from a tie, and one of the few in sport that rewards survival as richly as
superiority. The captain therefore holds a strange power: he may declare,
ending his own side's innings voluntarily, trading runs he might have scored
for time in which to bowl. The follow-on gives him another lever, letting him
send a side that trails by two hundred or more after the first innings straight
back in to bat again.

Conditions shift over five days in ways no other sport has to account for. The
pitch is a living surface. It dries out, cracks, and develops rough patches
where bowlers have been landing the ball, so spin grows more dangerous as the
match ages while the even bounce that batters rely on decays. The ball ages
too, losing lacquer and hardness, swinging conventionally when new and
sometimes reverse-swinging once it is scuffed unevenly; in Tests a fresh one
becomes available after eighty overs, and choosing when to take it is a small
drama in itself. Cloud cover can help a swing bowler for an hour. A captain
winning the toss on the first morning is really being asked to guess what the
surface will be doing on the fourth afternoon.

Limited-overs cricket inverts the whole arrangement. In a fifty-over
international each side receives fifty overs and not one more, so deliveries
are the scarce resource and wickets become, within reason, expendable. Twenty20
compresses this to twenty overs and follows the logic to its conclusion: a
batter who gets himself out in the nineteenth over having scored quickly has
done his job. To stop fielding sides from simply ringing the boundary, the
limited-overs codes cap how many fielders may stand outside a thirty-yard
circle — only two during the opening overs, more as the innings progresses —
and cap each bowler's workload, ten overs in a fifty-over match and four in a
Twenty20. Those two restrictions produce nearly all of the short formats'
tactics: the early assault while the field is still up, the middle-overs
squeeze, the arithmetic gamble of the final few overs.

## What the Structure Produces

Nested inside the over is the individual contest, which is really the game's
smallest meaningful unit: one bowler, one batter, and a plan that may take six
balls or six overs to come off. A fast bowler pushing a batter onto his back
foot with a run of short deliveries so that one full ball becomes unplayable.
An off-spinner drifting the ball across the batter, over after over, waiting
for the drive that goes in the air. Fielders are placed not where the ball
usually goes but where the bowler intends to make it go — a cordon of slips
behind the bat for the thin edge, a solitary man far out at deep square leg for
the mistimed pull — which is why a cricket field, read carefully, announces the
bowler's intention before he has begun his run.

That is what makes the walk between overs something other than dead time. In
those forty seconds the captain moves one fielder ten yards squarer, the bowler
at the far end decides to come round the wicket, and the two batters meet in
mid-pitch to agree on what the last twenty minutes have told them. The game is
slow in the way a long argument is slow. The pauses are where the positions get
worked out.
