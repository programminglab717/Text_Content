# How the Olympic Movement Became an Institution

The announcement of a host city takes about ten seconds. A man opens a card,
reads a name, and a municipality somewhere acquires a decade of construction
contracts, borrowing, expropriation and public argument. What gives a private
association registered in Switzerland the standing to hand out an obligation of
that size? The answer has very little to do with running or swimming. It lies in
the odd legal architecture that grew up around a nineteenth-century educational
enthusiasm, and in the fact that the association owns something nobody else can
manufacture: the only sporting occasion the whole world has agreed to watch at
the same time.

Pierre de Coubertin, a French aristocrat with strong views about physical
education, did not invent the idea of reviving the ancient Games. Versions had
been staged in Greece, in Sweden, and most influentially in the Shropshire
market town of Much Wenlock, where a doctor named William Penny Brookes ran an
annual Olympian festival that mixed athletics with tilting at rings and
wheelbarrow races. Coubertin visited, was impressed, and brought back a
conviction that organised sport made young men governable and nations vigorous.
What he added was internationalism and, crucially, an organisation. At a
congress held at the Sorbonne in 1894, ostensibly convened to discuss
amateurism, he secured agreement to revive the Games and established a committee
to run them. The first modern Games opened in Athens two years later.

## The Constitutional Trick

The committee Coubertin built has an unusual design, and most of what the
Olympic movement has been able to do since follows from it. The International
Olympic Committee is self-perpetuating: existing members choose new ones. Its
members are not delegates sent by their countries. They are, in the movement's
own formulation, the IOC's representatives in their countries — the line of
authority runs outward from Lausanne, not inward from capitals. A body
constituted that way cannot be captured by a government, because no government
appoints anyone to it, and it can credibly claim to answer to no one in
particular.

Underneath sit two other tiers. The International Federations govern individual
sports worldwide: they write the rules of the pole vault, certify officials,
sanction world championships, and decide who is eligible. The National Olympic
Committees, one per recognised territory, select and enter teams. Neither tier is
owned by the IOC, and the relationship among the three is a permanent
negotiation rather than a chain of command. The federations control the sport;
the IOC controls the event; each needs the other. When the IOC drops a sport
from the programme, as it has done periodically, it is exercising the only real
leverage it has over a federation, and the effect on that federation's revenue is
severe enough that the threat rarely needs to be carried out.

Recognition of national committees has its own political weight. The IOC decides
what counts as a territory for its purposes, and its answers have not always
matched the answers given by the United Nations. Taiwanese athletes compete
under the name Chinese Taipei and a flag designed for the occasion. Palestine has
had a recognised committee for decades. Teams have marched under improvised
banners when their states were unrecognised, dissolving, or suspended. These
arrangements are usually described as apolitical, which is a strange word for
decisions about who constitutes a nation.

## Amateurism and Its Collapse

For most of the twentieth century the movement's defining rule was that
competitors must not be paid. The principle arrived from Victorian England,
where amateur status had a frank class function: a rule against anyone who
earned money through physical labour conveniently excluded blacksmiths and
watermen from competition with gentlemen. Transplanted into the Olympic charter,
it hardened into doctrine, enforced with particular severity by Avery Brundage,
who presided over the IOC for two decades after the Second World War. Jim
Thorpe, the finest all-round athlete of the 1912 Games, was stripped of his
medals for having been paid a few dollars a week to play minor-league baseball
before he competed.

The rule became untenable for two reasons. State-supported athletes in the
Eastern bloc were nominally soldiers or students and trained full time, which
made the amateur distinction a fiction that penalised only Western competitors.
And television made the Games valuable enough that the absence of the best
athletes in the world's most popular sports became commercially embarrassing.
Through the 1970s and 1980s the eligibility rules were quietly gutted, then
handed over to the individual federations, which mostly opened their events. The
symbolic end point was the 1992 basketball tournament, where a team of American
professionals won by margins that made the result irrelevant and the point
unmistakable.

## Montreal, Los Angeles, and the Money

Two Games in the space of eight years determined how the modern event is
financed. Montreal in 1976 built extravagantly, ran catastrophically over
budget, and left Quebec taxpayers servicing the debt for about three decades. The
lesson was absorbed internationally: cities stopped bidding. By the time Los
Angeles came forward for 1984 it was effectively the only candidate, which gave
its organisers extraordinary leverage. They refused to guarantee the deficit,
used existing venues and university dormitories, and sold a small number of
exclusive sponsorship categories at high prices rather than many cheap ones. The
Games turned a surplus. Within a few years the IOC had built its own worldwide
sponsorship programme on the same logic, and broadcasting rights — sold in
lengthening packages to networks who wanted certainty — became the dominant
revenue line. The committee distributes the great bulk of that income to
federations, national committees and organising committees, which is both a
genuine subsidy to world sport and the mechanism by which the movement's
constituent parts are kept loyal.

Scarcity of bidders reversed into abundance during the 1990s, and abundance bred
corruption. The investigation into how Salt Lake City won the 2002 Winter Games
exposed a market in tuition payments, medical treatment and gifts to members and
their families. Members resigned or were expelled, and the rules were rewritten:
no more visits by voting members to candidate cities, an evaluation commission,
age limits, published reports. The reforms addressed the bribery. They did not
address the underlying dynamic, which is that cities competing against each
other bid up the promised spending, and the winner is often the city that has
most overestimated its capacity.

That dynamic eventually exhausted itself. Through the 2010s, referendums and
councils in a series of European cities killed bids before they were submitted,
usually over cost. The IOC responded with a package of reforms permitting events
outside the host city, encouraging temporary and existing venues, and replacing
the head-to-head contest with a quieter process of targeted dialogue with
preferred candidates. In 2017, facing a field of two, it awarded the 2024 and
2028 Games simultaneously to Paris and Los Angeles rather than risk leaving one
of them disappointed and the next cycle empty.

## Neutrality as a Working Fiction

The charter asserts that sport is politically neutral and the movement
apolitical, and the organisation has restricted demonstrations by athletes on
the field of play on that basis. The record complicates the claim. Berlin in
1936 was awarded before the Nazi regime took power and staged by it as a
showcase, with the IOC accepting cosmetic assurances about Jewish participation.
In 1968 two American sprinters raised gloved fists on the podium in Mexico City
and were sent home. African nations boycotted in 1976, much of the Western bloc
in 1980, much of the Eastern bloc in 1984. After Palestinian gunmen killed
Israeli team members in Munich in 1972, the Games were suspended for a memorial
service and then resumed, a decision defended at the time as refusing to let
terrorism win and criticised ever since as indecent haste.

Doping produced a parallel institution. After a series of scandals culminating in
a police raid on a professional cycling team in the late 1990s, the movement and
national governments jointly created the World Anti-Doping Agency, with a common
code, a prohibited list, and out-of-competition testing. Its independence is
structurally awkward — it is funded half by the governments and half by the
Olympic movement, whose product is damaged by the findings it publishes — and
that awkwardness surfaced when evidence emerged of a state-organised programme
in Russia around the Sochi Games. The eventual compromise, in which athletes
competed without their flag or anthem under a neutral designation, satisfied
almost nobody and has since become a reusable template.

What holds all of this together is not moral authority, which the organisation
has spent freely, and not sporting expertise, which belongs to the federations.
It is a monopoly on a moment. Every four years, for a fortnight, a very large
fraction of humanity attends to the same events. No broadcaster can assemble
that audience independently, no sponsor can buy it elsewhere, and no city can
stage anything comparable without a licence from Lausanne. The licence is the
asset. Everything else in the institution — the co-opted membership, the
charter's high language, the careful vagueness about what neutrality means — is
scaffolding around it.
