# How Chess Became the Game It Is

The queen could not always do what she does now. For the first eight hundred
years of the game's existence she was a man, a counsellor to the king, and she
crept one square diagonally at a time — the weakest piece on the board apart
from the foot soldiers. The bishop was worse. It hopped exactly two squares
diagonally, jumping whatever sat between, which meant each bishop could reach
only eight of the sixty-four squares and could never in its life meet the other
bishop of its own colour. Games under those rules took a very long time and
looked nothing like ours.

Chess as a set of rules has been revised, argued over, and locally customised
more than most people who play it realise. What has persisted is the underlying
idea: an abstract battle on a grid, with pieces of unequal power, complete
information, no luck, and one target whose capture ends everything. That
skeleton has survived four continents and a millennium and a half of
alteration.

## From Four Armies to One Board

The earliest ancestor that scholars are confident about is chaturanga, played in
northern India by around the sixth century. The name refers to the four
divisions of an army — infantry, cavalry, elephants, and chariots — and the
pieces map onto them directly. The chariot is the one that survived unchanged:
it swept along ranks and files then exactly as the rook does now. The horse
moved in its crooked leap. The elephant and the foot soldiers were altered
later; the king stood in the middle with his counsellor beside him.

The game crossed into Persia, where it was called chatrang and then, after the
Arab conquest, shatranj. Persian vocabulary is still embedded in the English
version: shah for the king, and the phrase describing the end of a game — the
king is trapped, defeated, helpless — worn down over centuries into checkmate.
The rook keeps its name from rukh, the chariot.

Under the Abbasid caliphate chess acquired something no game had really had
before: a literature. Players like al-Adli and, a generation later, as-Suli were
famous enough that their reputations outlived their books. They catalogued
opening arrangements — ta'biyat, standard formations both sides shuffled into
over a dozen moves before contact, since the slow pieces made rapid attack
impossible — and they composed mansubat, problems with a specified winning
sequence, some of which are still solvable and elegant. A strong player was
expected to have memorised a stock of them.

Chess entered Europe along the usual three seams: Muslim Spain, Norman Sicily,
and the Byzantine frontier. The pieces were renamed to fit a feudal imagination
that had no use for elephants or viziers. The counsellor became the queen. The
elephant became, depending on the country, a bishop, a jester, a runner, or an
archer. The chariot in some places turned into a tower, which is how a piece
that began as a war cart ended up looking like castle masonry.

## The Mad Queen

Then, in the last quarter of the fifteenth century, somewhere in Spain or
Italy, someone changed the rules and the game exploded. The queen was given the
combined movement of rook and bishop — the most powerful piece ever put on the
board — and the bishop was released to slide as far as it liked along its
diagonal. Contemporaries called the new version chess of the mad queen, or
chess of the lady, and it spread with startling speed, displacing the old game
almost everywhere within a couple of generations.

The consequence was not merely faster play. It restructured the entire game.
Long-range pieces meant that a mistake on move six could be punished on move
seven, which made opening theory urgent rather than ceremonial. The pawn's
double first step and the en passant capture arrived as corrections to keep
pawns from simply running past each other. Castling settled into its present
shape over the following century or so, a compromise among several regional
versions of a king's leap. Printed books followed the new rules almost at once:
Lucena in Spain around the turn of the sixteenth century, Damiano in Italy,
then Ruy López de Segura, a Spanish cleric whose 1561 treatise analysed
openings with a rigour that still reads as modern and whose name is attached to
one of them.

## Coffee Houses and the First Champions

For two centuries the strongest players were mostly court professionals and
gamblers. Then chess found its natural habitat: the café. The Café de la
Régence in Paris and later Simpson's Divan in London were places where you
could buy an hour of a master's time, and the culture that grew there — stakes,
odds, spectators, an oral tradition of famous games — produced the game's first
true theorist. François-André Danican Philidor, better known in his own day as
an opera composer, published an analysis in 1749 arguing that pawns were not
mere filler but the determining structure of a position. The claim that the
pawns are the soul of chess sounds obvious now. It was not obvious then, when
attacking play meant throwing pieces at the enemy king and hoping.

The nineteenth century gave chess its institutions. Jaques of London began
selling a standardised piece design in 1849, endorsed by the English master
Howard Staunton, and its clean silhouettes solved a real problem: before it,
sets varied so much between countries that players could misidentify pieces
mid-game. The 1851 London tournament, the first serious international
gathering, was won by Adolf Anderssen, whose sacrificial masterpieces against
Kieseritzky and Dufresne became the emblem of the Romantic style — material was
something you spent to buy an attack. Then Paul Morphy came from New Orleans,
beat everyone in Europe who would sit down with him, and went home and quit.
His games still get shown to beginners because he demonstrated something the
Romantics had half-missed: the attacks work when you have developed more pieces
than your opponent and opened the position, and not otherwise.

Wilhelm Steinitz made that instinct into a doctrine. He argued that a position
contains accumulable small advantages — a better pawn structure, a weak square,
the bishop pair, control of an open file — and that an attack launched without
them will fail against correct defence. His contemporaries found the resulting
play ugly and cowardly. He beat them anyway, and in 1886 became the first
recognised world champion. Emanuel Lasker held the title for twenty-seven
years, longer than anyone since, playing in a practical, opponent-aware style
that irritated purists. José Raúl Capablanca made the game look effortless and
frightened a generation into believing chess might be drawn out, exhausted,
analysed to death.

## Argument as Method

Every dominant school has produced its own opposition. In the 1920s the
hypermoderns — Nimzowitsch, Réti, Breyer, Tartakower — attacked the classical
rule that you should occupy the centre with pawns, proposing instead that you
should let your opponent build a big centre and then undermine it from a
distance with bishops and flank pawns. Nimzowitsch's *My System* is the most
readable polemic in the game's literature: opinionated, funny, and still the
source of the vocabulary most club players use for prophylaxis, overprotection
and blockade.

After the Second World War the Soviet state adopted chess as a demonstration
sport, funded coaching at scale, and reaped a run of champions that lasted
almost unbroken for half a century. Mikhail Botvinnik codified a preparation
method — analyse your own losses publicly, research opening novelties, train
under deliberately uncomfortable conditions — that turned a talent into a
profession. The one long interruption was Bobby Fischer, who in 1972 in
Reykjavik dismantled Boris Spassky in a match freighted with more political
meaning than either player wanted, and then withdrew from competition almost
entirely.

The deepest change since the mad queen has been the arrival of enormous
databases and strong engines. Openings that were matters of taste became
matters of fact, checkable to a depth no human can see, and preparation shifted
from principles to memorised lines twenty or thirty moves long. Endgames with
few pieces were solved outright and shown to contain wins requiring absurd,
apparently aimless sequences no player would ever find. Some of the response
has been to move away from that ground entirely — faster time controls, where
memory matters less, and randomised starting positions, which throw opening
books away at move one.

What has not changed is the thing that made the game portable across so many
cultures. Both players see everything. Nothing is hidden, nothing is shuffled,
and when you lose, the reason is sitting there on the board where you and your
opponent can both look at it.
