# How a Pidgin Becomes a Creole

On the Arctic coast, for something like a century and a half, Russian grain
traders and Norwegian fishermen did business in a language that belonged to
neither of them. It is usually called Russenorsk. Its vocabulary ran to a few
hundred words, divided fairly evenly between Russian and Norwegian sources, with
a scattering of Dutch, German and English picked up in port. Its grammar was
spare almost to the point of transparency: one general-purpose preposition doing
the work of a dozen, verbs that mostly ended in the same invariant syllable,
pronouns pressed into service as possessives. Its best-remembered phrase
translates word for word as *my on your*, and meant something close to "I speak
your way." Nobody grew up speaking it. When the Russian Revolution shut down the
Pomor trade, the language died inside a generation, leaving word lists and a few
sceptical travellers' notes.

That is the normal fate of a pidgin. It is built for a job, and when the job
ends, so does it.

## What a Pidgin Actually Is

A pidgin is not broken language, and it is not baby talk, though almost everyone
who has encountered one from the outside has described it as both. It is a
negotiated compromise between groups who need to transact and share no common
tongue. The defining property is negative: a pidgin has no native speakers.
Everyone who uses it has some other first language, and uses the pidgin only in
the restricted arena where it applies — a market, a dock, a ship's deck, a
labour line.

The shape of that compromise is rarely symmetrical. One language, typically the
one spoken by whoever holds the money or the whip, supplies most of the words.
Linguists call it the lexifier or superstrate. The other languages in the
contact situation — the substrate — tend to leave their mark on pronunciation
and on the deeper patterns of how a sentence is put together. Grammatical
machinery that has to be learned word by word is the first thing to go.
Inflectional endings vanish. Irregular verbs regularise. Case disappears and
word order takes over the job of marking who did what to whom. A small,
hardworking set of particles replaces tense and aspect morphology.

The historical record is full of these languages and most of them are gone.
Chinook Jargon carried trade across the Pacific Northwest for generations,
drawing its core from Chinookan and Nuu-chah-nulth and its later additions from
French and English. The Mediterranean Lingua Franca, sometimes called Sabir, was
a Romance-based contact tongue used for centuries between European sailors and
North African ports; the term "lingua franca" survives in English as a common
noun while the language itself does not. Chinese Pidgin English served the
Canton trade and then evaporated when the trade reorganised.

## When Children Get Hold of It

Sometimes the contact situation does not end. Instead it hardens into a society.
People from many language backgrounds are brought together permanently — on
sugar plantations, in mining compounds, in port towns — and the pidgin becomes
the only language they all share. They marry across language lines. They raise
children. And those children acquire the pidgin as a first language, which is
the moment everything changes.

What emerges is not what went in. A pidgin is thin; a first language cannot
afford to be. Vocabulary expands, often by compounding what is already there
rather than borrowing. Phonology settles into a consistent system instead of
varying with each speaker's mother tongue. Most strikingly, grammar grows back.
Creoles develop regular ways of marking tense, modality and aspect, usually with
short preverbal particles in a fixed order. They develop relative clauses,
complement clauses, passives or their functional equivalents, articles,
elaborate systems for reporting speech. None of that was reliably present in the
input.

Tok Pisin shows the process clearly because so much of it happened recently
enough to be documented. Its words are mostly English in origin but no longer
behave like English words. *Bilong*, from "belong", is a general possessive and
relational linker: hair is *gras bilong het*, grass belonging to the head. The
suffix *-pela*, from "fellow", attaches to adjectives and to some pronouns —
*bikpela haus*, a big house. The pronoun system makes a distinction English
lacks and the Austronesian substrate languages insist on: *yumi* means "we
including you", *mipela* means "we not including you". A predicate marker *i*
sits before the verb in third-person clauses. Tok Pisin is now one of the
official languages of Papua New Guinea, spoken natively by a growing population,
used in parliament, on the radio, and in print.

Haitian Creole tells the same story with a French lexicon. Many of its nouns
have swallowed the French article whole — *zwazo* from *les oiseaux*, *lari*
from *la rue*, *diri* from *du riz* — which is a reliable sign that the words
were learned by ear rather than read. Its own definite article follows the noun
instead of preceding it: *liv la*, the book. Tense and aspect are carried by
free particles before the verb rather than by endings on it. It is the first
language of essentially the whole Haitian population, while fluency in French
belongs to a minority, and it has been co-official since the constitution
adopted in 1987.

## The Argument About Where the Grammar Came From

If children build the missing grammar, where do they get it? This has been the
central quarrel of creole studies for decades, and it is unresolved because
several answers are partly right.

Derek Bickerton's language bioprogram hypothesis, developed largely from
Hawaiian plantation material, proposed the boldest version: children confronted
with impoverished input fall back on innate defaults, and those defaults are why
creoles that share no history nonetheless share structural features. It is an
elegant claim and it made creoles suddenly interesting to theoretical
linguistics. It also strained against the evidence that the input was rarely as
impoverished as the model required.

The substrate camp points instead to the languages the first generation actually
spoke. Claire Lefebvre's long study of Haitian Creole and Fongbe argues that
speakers relabelled West African grammatical structures with French-derived
sounds. The Atlantic creoles do cluster around features common in the West
African languages of the relevant period — serial verb constructions, determiners
after the noun, predicate clefting for emphasis. The superstrate camp, associated
with Robert Chaudenson, replies that the seventeenth-century regional French
actually spoken by sailors and indentured servants was far less like textbook
French than anyone assumes, and that much of what looks like innovation is
inheritance from a nonstandard source.

Salikoko Mufwene's framing has probably aged best: think of a feature pool.
Every variety present in the contact setting throws its structures into a common
pool, and which features win depends on frequency, salience, and above all on
the demographic history — who was there first, how fast the population changed,
what the ratio of newcomers to established speakers was at each stage. Colonies
that began as smallholdings with close daily contact between groups produced
different outcomes from those that switched quickly to large plantations where
new arrivals vastly outnumbered anyone who already spoke the local variety. The
grammar encodes the census.

## Continuums, Stigma and the Limits of the Label

Where a creole coexists with its lexifier, the two do not stay neatly apart.
Jamaica and Guyana are the classic cases: speakers command a range of varieties
running from a basilect furthest from English through intermediate mesolects to
an acrolect close to standard, and they move along that range according to
audience and occasion. Early descriptions called this decreolisation, as though
the creole were dissolving back into English. It looks more like an ordinary
sociolinguistic repertoire, of the kind every speech community has, made visible
because the endpoints are so far apart.

The stigma has been durable and costly. Creoles have been called broken French,
bad English, patois in the dismissive sense, for as long as outsiders have
written about them, and that judgement has shaped schooling — children taught to
read in a language they do not speak, then blamed for failing. Spelling has been
a proxy battle. Haitian Creole settled on a phonemic orthography that looks
nothing like French, a decision that was fought over precisely because looking
like French was the point for some and the problem for others. Papiamento is
still written two ways, more etymologically in Aruba and more phonemically in
Curaçao.

It is also worth noticing how much the familiar picture depends on the Atlantic.
Sango, the national language of the Central African Republic, draws its lexicon
from Ngbandi, not from any European source. Kituba does similar work across the
Congo basin. Nubi, spoken in Uganda and Kenya, is Arabic-lexified and descends
from the speech of nineteenth-century soldiers. Unserdeutsch, a German-lexified
creole that arose at a mission school in what is now Papua New Guinea, survives
in the memory of a handful of elderly speakers. Nothing structural unites these
languages that does not also appear in languages nobody calls creoles. What
unites them is a kind of history: a population assembled by force or commerce out
of people who could not understand each other, and a generation of children who
had to make a language out of what they were handed.
