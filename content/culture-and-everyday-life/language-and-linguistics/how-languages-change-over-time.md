# How Languages Change Over Time

The English word *knight* carries two silent letters, and both of them were once audible. A speaker in Chaucer's London said something close to *k-nicht*, with the k pronounced and the *gh* rasping in the back of the throat like the *ch* in *loch*. The spelling was a reasonable record of the sound. Then the sounds moved and the spelling did not, and what remains is a fossil: a small piece of fourteenth-century pronunciation trapped in the orthography of a language that stopped pronouncing it centuries ago. English is full of these. So is every written language old enough to have outlived its own phonetics.

Language change is often described as though it were erosion, something that happens to a language while nobody is looking. It is more accurate to say that change is what speaking a language consists of. Every act of speech is a fresh production, assembled by a nervous system under time pressure, and every act of listening is an inference about what the speaker intended. Between production and inference there is slack, and in that slack a language reshapes itself continuously, in ways that are surprisingly patterned.

## The Regularity of Sound Change

The central discovery of nineteenth-century linguistics was that sound change is not random word-by-word drift. It is regular: when a sound changes, it tends to change in every word where it occurs in the same phonetic environment, across the whole speech community, over a few generations.

Grimm's Law is the standard illustration. Compare Latin *pater* with English *father*, Latin *piscis* with *fish*, Latin *cornu* with *horn*, Latin *decem* with *ten*, Latin *genu* with *knee*. The pattern is not coincidence in a handful of words; it is a wholesale reorganization of the consonant system that the Germanic branch underwent and the Italic branch did not. Voiceless stops became fricatives, voiced stops became voiceless, and a third series shifted to fill the gap. Where exceptions appeared, they turned out to be regular too: Karl Verner showed that the apparent misfits were conditioned by where the accent had fallen in the older language, which is why *father* has a soft consonant in the middle where the law seems to predict a hard one.

English underwent a second wholesale reorganization much later, this time of its long vowels. Over roughly the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries, the long vowels raised and the highest ones broke into diphthongs. The vowel of *bite* had been something like the vowel of modern *beet*; the vowel of *house* had been like modern *whose*. Everything shuffled up one position and the two at the top fell off into diphthongs. The printing press, meanwhile, was busy freezing spellings that had been designed for the older system. That mismatch is most of what makes English spelling feel arbitrary to learners; the spellings are not arbitrary, merely obsolete.

Sound changes keep happening. Many speakers of southern British English stopped pronouncing *r* except before a vowel over the course of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, which is why *father* and *farther* now sound identical there and do not in Dublin, Edinburgh, or most of North America. Nobody voted on it.

## Analogy and Reanalysis

Sound change is blind to meaning, which means it constantly wrecks the tidiness of grammatical patterns. Speakers, particularly young ones, repair the wreckage by analogy — extending a productive pattern over an irregular form that has become isolated.

English verbs have been losing their strong past tenses this way for a thousand years. The past of *help* was once *holp*; the past of *climb* was *clomb*. Both surrendered to the *-ed* pattern, which was already the majority rule and therefore the default a child reaches for. The plural of *cow* was *kine*, of *shoe* was *shoon*, of *eye* was *eyen*. A few irregulars survive precisely because they are common enough to be heard constantly and therefore learned as separate items: nobody regularizes *went* or *feet*, because no child gets through a week without hearing them.

Reanalysis is stranger and more entertaining. It happens when listeners cut a stretch of speech at a different seam than the speaker did. *An apron* was once *a napron*, related to *napkin*. *A newt* was once *an ewt*. *A nickname* was an *eke-name*, an "also-name." The article and the noun trade a consonant in the dark, and once a generation has learned the new parsing there is no way back. Larger structures get reanalyzed too, and that is where new grammar comes from.

## Where New Grammar Comes From

Grammatical machinery is not inherited from some prior era of grammar. It is manufactured, repeatedly, out of ordinary content words that get used so often in a particular slot that they stop being independent and become markers.

English *going to* is a live example, visible in mid-transition. It began as a verb of motion with a purpose: *I am going to London to buy cloth*. Used often enough with a following verb, it shed the motion and kept the futurity — *it is going to rain* involves nobody going anywhere. Then it phonetically eroded, as grammaticalized items reliably do, into *gonna*, which cannot be used in the original motion sense at all. Nobody says *I'm gonna London*. A word has become a piece of grammar, and its erosion is the receipt.

The Romance future tense records the completed version of the same process. Latin had no descendant of its synthetic future; speakers instead said something like *cantare habeo*, "I have to sing," with the verb *habere* trailing behind. The trailing verb fused onto the end of the infinitive and became a suffix, which is why French *chanterai* and Spanish *cantaré* end in forms that are worn-down versions of "have." Definite articles in the same languages came from the Latin demonstrative *ille*, "that one," used so routinely that it thinned into a grammatical particle. English *will* was once a verb meaning "want," and *shall* meant "owe."

Negation cycles through the same mill. French negation once used *ne* alone; speakers added reinforcing nouns for emphasis — *pas* ("step"), *point* ("dot"), *mie* ("crumb") — and *pas* won, becoming obligatory. In modern colloquial French, *ne* is routinely dropped and *pas* carries the negation by itself, ready in principle to be reinforced by something new.

## Meanings on the Move

Words drift semantically along well-worn paths: from general to specific, specific to general, concrete to abstract, neutral to loaded. Old English *deor* meant any animal, and narrowed to one. *Mete* meant food of any kind, and survives in *sweetmeat*. *Starve* meant simply to die. *Silly* began as "blessed," passed through "innocent" and "harmless," and arrived at "foolish," a trajectory that a word can take without any single speaker noticing the slippage. *Nice* descends from a Latin word for "ignorant" and spent centuries meaning "precise" or "fastidious" before settling into vague approval.

Borrowing accelerates everything. English absorbed a layer of Norse in the Viking centuries, including items languages rarely borrow — the pronouns *they*, *them*, *their*, plus *sky*, *skin*, *egg*, *knife*, and *window*, literally "wind-eye." After 1066 it absorbed a much thicker layer of Norman French, which is why the animal in the field usually has a Germanic name and the meat on the table a French one: *cow* and *beef*, *pig* and *pork*, *sheep* and *mutton*. The Renaissance added Latin and Greek wholesale, often producing triplets of roughly parallel meaning at different registers: *kingly*, *royal*, *regal*.

## Who Starts It, and How It Spreads

For a long time the mechanism of spread was a blank. Sociolinguistic fieldwork in the twentieth century filled it in by catching changes in progress. William Labov's study of Martha's Vineyard found that islanders were pronouncing certain diphthongs with a centralized onset, and that the strongest users were not the oldest or most isolated but the men who most identified with the island and resented the summer influx. The variant carried social meaning: it marked you as belonging. His later work in New York department stores, where he prompted clerks to say "fourth floor" and recorded how often they pronounced the *r*, showed the same variable tracking store prestige and self-correction.

That is the general shape. A variant arises — from sound change, contact, or ordinary variation — and acquires an association with some group people want to be read as belonging to. It spreads through dense social networks, usually slowly at first, then rapidly once a critical mass makes it unremarkable, then slowly again as the last holdouts convert. Plot the frequency over time and you get an S-curve. Because people's core pronunciation habits settle in adolescence, comparing the speech of eighty-year-olds and twenty-year-olds in the same town gives a usable snapshot of a change unfolding across decades.

None of this is decay, though every generation of commentators has insisted it is. The complaint that the young are ruining the language is older than most of the features the complainers are defending, and the features they defend were themselves innovations that somebody's grandfather deplored. What actually happens is redistribution: a language loses a case ending here and develops a fixed word order there, sheds a tense and grows an article, trades morphological complexity for syntactic complexity or the reverse. The total expressive capacity stays where it was, which is roughly everywhere a speaker needs it to be.
