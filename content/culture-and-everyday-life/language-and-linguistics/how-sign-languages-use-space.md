# How Sign Languages Use Space

Watch two fluent signers discuss someone who is not in the room. At some point
one of them will put that person into the air — a spot off to the left, about
shoulder height, established with a name sign and a quick point — and then leave
them there. For the next ten minutes, verbs will lean toward that spot or away
from it. Eye gaze will flick to it. A pronoun will be a finger aimed at a patch
of empty air that both signers now agree is occupied. The conversation acquires
a floor plan, invisible but binding, and nobody has to keep restating who did
what to whom, because the geometry has already said it.

This is the part of signed language that speech has no equivalent for, and it is
why the old assumption — that signing is a manual cipher for the surrounding
spoken language, a gestural shorthand for people who cannot hear — falls apart
the moment anyone looks closely.

## Not a Code for Speech

The close looking began in earnest with William Stokoe, a professor of English
at Gallaudet University who argued, in work published in 1960, that American
Sign Language was a language in its own right with its own internal structure.
The claim was unpopular, including among hearing colleagues who had spent
careers treating signing as a crutch. What eventually made it undeniable was
that ASL refused to line up with English in any of the places a code would have
to. Its word order is not English word order. It has grammatical machinery
English lacks entirely, and lacks machinery English cannot do without.

The family tree makes the point too. American Sign Language descends in large
part from French Sign Language, carried to Hartford in 1817 by Laurent Clerc, a
deaf French teacher recruited by Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet, and blended there
with the signing children already brought from their own communities — including
Martha's Vineyard, where hereditary deafness was common enough that hearing
islanders signed as well. British Sign Language has an entirely separate
ancestry. The result is that ASL and BSL are mutually unintelligible, despite
serving two countries that share a spoken language, while ASL and French Sign
Language share a recognizable core of vocabulary. Even the manual alphabets
differ: one-handed in the American tradition, two-handed in the British.

Then there is the long interruption. In 1880 an international congress on deaf
education, meeting in Milan, resolved in favor of oral methods — speech and
lipreading — and against signing in the classroom. Deaf teachers were pushed out
of the schools that had employed them across much of Europe and North America,
and children were punished for using their hands. The languages survived anyway,
transmitted in dormitories and on playgrounds from older children to younger
ones, out of sight of the adults. That transmission route is unusual, and it has
consequences: a signed language's continuity has often depended less on parents
than on the simple fact of deaf children being in the same building.

## The Parts of a Sign

Stokoe's structural insight was that a sign is not an indivisible gesture but a
bundle of simultaneous components: a handshape, a location on or near the body,
a movement, plus orientation and a set of non-manual features that later analysts
added. Change one component and you change the word. In ASL, the signs for
mother and father use the same open hand with the thumb touching the face, and
differ only in where: chin for one, forehead for the other. Whole families of
signs cluster at those two heights, with a loose and historically accidental
association between the forehead and male reference.

Movement carries its own load. Across many pairs, the noun and the verb differ
only in how the motion is executed — a doubled, smaller, more restrained version
of the verb's movement yields the related noun, a pattern that was shown in the
1970s to be systematic rather than anecdotal. Because these parts are real,
signers make phonological errors with them. A slip of the hand produces the
wrong handshape on the right movement, exactly as a slip of the tongue produces
the wrong consonant on the right vowel frame. Signers also get stuck in a
tip-of-the-fingers state, able to retrieve a sign's location and movement while
the handshape stays out of reach.

Signs also erode. Over generations they drift toward the center of the signing
space, toward symmetry when two hands are involved, and toward the face when
they need to be visible to someone reading the signer's expression at the same
time. A sign that began as a transparent picture of an action often ends up as
something no outsider would guess. That erosion is not a defect. It is what
happens to any linguistic form that gets used a few million times.

## Grammar on the Face

The most persistent misreading of signing is that the face is doing emotion. It
is doing syntax. In ASL, raised eyebrows with a slight forward head tilt mark a
yes-or-no question; lowered, furrowed brows mark a question with a wh-word.
Raised brows also mark topics and the if-clause of a conditional. What proves
these are grammatical rather than expressive is their scope: the brow position
begins exactly where the marked constituent begins and ends exactly where it
ends. It behaves like intonation in a spoken language, which is to say it is
rule-governed, obligatory, and learned.

The mouth participates too, independently of any spoken word. A pursed mouth
producing a silent "mm" means the action was done normally, easily, without
incident; a protruded tongue between the teeth means it was done carelessly or
sloppily; a tensed, drawn-back mouth marks something close in time or space.
These are adverbs, and they attach to verbs. A signer who keeps a blank face is
not signing neutrally. They are signing ungrammatically, and to a fluent reader
they look roughly the way a monotone robot voice sounds.

## Space as Syntax

Once referents are placed, the space between them becomes a grammatical medium.
A large class of ASL verbs — give, ask, help, send — is directional: the sign
begins at the location of the giver and ends at the location of the receiver, and
reversing the path reverses the roles. No separate pronouns are needed, and no
word order is needed to disambiguate. Another class of verbs, including like and
want, is anchored to the body and cannot move this way, so those sentences
recruit ordinary word order instead. The split between the two classes is
arbitrary in the way grammatical classes usually are, and it has to be learned.

Beyond the loci sit the depicting constructions, traditionally called classifiers.
A particular handshape stands for a class of thing — a vehicle, an upright
person, a flat surface, a long thin object — and then moves through the signing
space tracing an actual path at an actual speed with an actual manner. A car
climbing a hill, drifting into the other lane, correcting, and stopping short of
a wall is several clauses in English and one continuous construction here, with
the hill's slope and the swerve's sharpness encoded in the gradient properties of
the movement itself. The two hands can also divide labor: one holds a referent
in place while the other comments on it, so that two pieces of information sit in
the visual field simultaneously rather than queueing up as speech must.

And signers shift roles. By turning the torso, changing eye gaze, and adjusting
facial posture, a narrator steps into a character and signs as that character —
the equivalent of direct quotation, except extended to actions and attitudes as
well as words. Skilled storytellers move between two or three characters in a
few seconds, and the audience tracks who is speaking by body angle alone.

## Languages That Start From Nothing

Deaf children born to hearing parents who do not sign typically invent something
anyway. These homesign systems have consistent word order and stable forms, but
they stay small; one person cannot build a full language alone. What happens
when a few hundred such children are put in the same room is the closest thing
linguistics has to a natural experiment, and it happened in Nicaragua around
1980, when new schools gathered deaf students who had previously been isolated
in their families. The first cohort pooled their homesigns into a rough shared
system. The younger children who arrived afterward did not merely learn it —
they regularized it, adding consistent spatial agreement and tighter morphology
that their elders did not produce. Researchers who followed the school across
its first decades watched grammar appear on a timescale usually reserved for
inference.

Elsewhere, signed languages arise in villages where hereditary deafness is
common and hearing neighbors sign as a matter of course — Kata Kolok in the
Balinese village of Bengkala, and the sign language of the Al-Sayyid Bedouin
community in the Negev, among others. These languages sometimes organize space on
different principles: rather than setting up abstract loci in front of the
signer, some point to the actual compass direction of the actual field where the
actual event happened. Martha's Vineyard worked this way too, until off-island
marriage and the pull of mainland schools dispersed the community and the
language died out in the twentieth century.

That is the fragility worth noticing. A signed language does not survive because
it is written down or taught in universities. It survives because deaf children
meet each other early and often enough to pass it on, and when the institutions
that gathered them are closed or scattered, a language can thin out inside a
single generation.
