# The Archaeology of Everyday Words

Flex your forearm and watch the long belly of the muscle slide under the skin.
Someone, a very long time ago, thought that looked like a small animal moving
under a blanket, and named it accordingly: the Latin word is *musculus*, little
mouse. Every time an anatomy student writes "muscle" they are repeating a joke
about rodents made by people who have been dead for two thousand years and who
would not recognize the word in its current spelling.

Etymology is the study of that sort of inheritance. It is often treated as a
parlor trick, a supply of dinner-party facts, and it does supply them. But the
reason it works at all — the reason word histories can be reconstructed rather
than merely guessed at — is that languages change in ways disciplined enough to
be run backwards.

## Sound Changes Keep Their Appointments

The founding realization of historical linguistics was that resemblance between
words is worthless as evidence unless it is systematic. Any two languages will
share a few accidental lookalikes. What matters is a recurring correspondence:
not "these two words sound similar" but "every word of this shape in language A
appears in this other shape in language B, without exceptions worth speaking of."

When Sir William Jones told the Asiatic Society in Calcutta in 1786 that
Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin were too closely related for chance to explain, he
was pointing at exactly this kind of pattern. Over the following decades the
correspondences were pinned down in detail, most famously by Jacob Grimm, whose
name is attached to the set of shifts that separate the Germanic languages from
their relatives. A *p* in Latin or Greek or Sanskrit shows up as an *f* in
English: *pater* and father, *pes* and foot, *piscis* and fish. A *t* becomes
*th*: *tres* and three. A *k* becomes *h*: *canis* and hound, *cornu* and horn,
*centum* and hundred. Once you know the rule, "hemp" and "cannabis" stop looking
like coincidence, and "heart" and "cardiac" fall into place.

The apparent exceptions turned out to be the best proof of all. Certain Germanic
words refused to follow Grimm's pattern, and in 1875 Karl Verner showed that the
deviations depended on where the accent had fallen in the ancestral word. The
irregular pairs in English — was beside were, seethe beside sodden, lose beside
forlorn — are the fossilized residue of that second rule. A field with two
interlocking laws that together leave almost nothing unexplained is a field
doing science, not connoisseurship.

## Meanings Slide

Sound is the easy half. Meaning wanders in ways that are describable but not
predictable, and usually the wandering is invisible to the people doing it.

Narrowing is the commonest path. Old English *deor* meant any animal, which is
still what its German cousin *Tier* means; English restricted it to one
conspicuous species of game. *Mete* meant food of any kind, which is why old
recipes have sweetmeats and why the proverb about one man's meat is not about
butchery. *Hund* meant dog in general before it shrank to a particular sort of
hunting dog, and *fugol* meant bird before it shrank to poultry. *Steorfan*
meant simply to die; the manner of dying got welded on later.

Words also slide downhill morally. A *villanus* was a farmhand attached to a
Roman country estate, a *knave* was just a boy, and *silly* began as a word for
blessed and lucky, passed through innocent and harmless, arrived at feeble, and
finished as foolish — a complete character assassination conducted over roughly
a thousand years. *Nice* traveled even further: it comes from a Latin word for
ignorant, spent the late medieval period meaning fussy or overly precise, and
only lately settled into the mild, agreeable sense that makes it the most
useless adjective in the language. *Awful* used to mean full of awe, then full
of dread, then merely bad.

And words get recruited into grammar. "I am going to leave" once described
walking somewhere with an intention; the motion drained out and left a future
tense behind, which is why "I'm going to stay right here" is not a contradiction.
The same erosion produced "will" out of a verb of wanting and "shall" out of a
verb of owing. Abstract thought runs on the same recycling: to understand is to
stand under, to comprehend is to grasp, to explain is to flatten out.

## Words Change Shape to Look Like Words You Know

Speakers dislike opaque material inside a familiar word, and they will quietly
reshape it into something recognizable even if the result makes no sense.

The French *escrevisse* came into English and got remodeled around the word
"fish", which is how a freshwater crustacean became a crayfish. Old English
*brydguma* was a bride-man, but *guma* fell out of use and was swapped for the
nearest familiar noun, giving a bridegroom whose first job now appears to be
tending horses. *Shamefast* meant held fast by shame, on the model of
steadfast; it became shamefaced, relocating the emotion to the face. An
*angnaegl* was a painful spot by the nail, and is now a hangnail, which sounds
like an explanation and is not one.

A subtler version attacks the boundary between the article and the noun. English
lost the initial *n* of *nædre* and *napron* because "a nadder" and "a napron"
were reanalyzed as "an adder" and "an apron" — the napkin keeps the original
consonant as evidence. The same process built "umpire" out of a word meaning
the odd one, not equal, and it works in reverse too: "a newt" acquired its *n*
from the article in "an ewt". Spanish speakers handed English "alligator" as
*el lagarto*, the lizard, definite article included. And the orange lost its
first letter somewhere in the Mediterranean, the Sanskrit-derived *naranga*
surviving intact in Spanish *naranja* while Italian's *un'arancia* quietly
donated the *n* to the article that preceded it.

## The Layers of English

English is unusually easy to read historically because its borrowings arrived in
distinct waves that never fully mixed.

The Norse settlements left the strangest deposit: not just vocabulary but core
grammar. "They", "them", and "their" are Scandinavian imports that displaced the
native pronouns, which is remarkable, because pronouns are normally the last
thing a language surrenders. The Norse also gave English "sky", "skin", "law",
"knife", "egg" — which pushed out an older native word — and "window", literally
a wind-eye. Because Old Norse and Old English were related, many pairs survive
as doublets with split duties: shirt and skirt, shatter and scatter, church and
kirk.

The Norman conquest layered a French-speaking administration over an
English-speaking population, and the vocabulary still shows the class structure.
The animal in the field keeps its English name and the meat on the table takes
a French one: cow and beef, pig and pork, sheep and mutton, calf and veal. Law,
government, art, and cuisine run heavily on French roots, while the words for
bodies, weather, family, and farm work stayed Germanic. Later, scholars
borrowed directly from Latin and Greek, producing the characteristic English
triplet, three registers for one idea: kingly, royal, regal; ask, question,
interrogate; holy, sacred, consecrated. The first is plain, the second is
formal, the third is technical, and no one taught you that ranking explicitly.

There are stranger doublets. Norman French and central French handled the same
Latin word differently, so English ended up with warranty and guarantee, warden
and guardian, wage and gage. "Cattle" and "chattel" both descend from the Latin
for capital, which tells you what livestock meant to a medieval household.

## What Etymology Cannot Tell You

Two cautions, both routinely ignored.

The first is that a word's history does not license or forbid its present use.
The Latin *decimare* meant to kill one in ten, so a certain kind of pedant
insists that "decimate" cannot mean to destroy most of something — but English
took the word and generalized it, exactly as it generalized dozens of others,
and the pedant is applying a rule they would never apply to "nice" or "muscle".
Origins explain how a word got here. They do not adjudicate what it means now.

The second is that most of the etymologies people love are false. "Posh" is not
an abbreviation of port out, starboard home; "golf" is not an acronym about
excluding ladies; "news" has nothing to do with the compass points; "sincere"
does not come from a phrase about wax; the distress signal was chosen because
three dots, three dashes, three dots is easy to hear in Morse, and the phrases
built around the letters came afterward. Acronymic origins are vanishingly rare
for any word older than the twentieth century, for the simple reason that
literate wordplay of that kind is a modern habit. A confident story with a tidy
moral is nearly always invented.

What the good dictionaries offer instead, when they are honest, is silence.
Nobody knows where "dog" came from — it appears late in Old English, displaces
the perfectly serviceable *hund*, and has no convincing relatives anywhere.
"Boy", "girl", "big", "bad", and "pig" are all similarly murky. The most
ordinary words in the language, the ones a two-year-old learns first, include
some of the ones whose ancestry is most thoroughly lost, and that is worth
sitting with: the vocabulary is not a well-kept archive with a few gaps. It is
mostly gaps, with a well-lit corridor running through the middle where the
correspondences happen to hold.
