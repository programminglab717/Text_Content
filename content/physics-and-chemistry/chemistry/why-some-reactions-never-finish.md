# Why Some Reactions Never Finish

There is a demonstration that chemistry teachers have been running for well over a century, and it still surprises people. Two sealed glass tubes hold the same gas mixture, nitrogen dioxide and its dimer, dinitrogen tetroxide. One tube goes into a beaker of ice water and fades to nearly colorless. The other goes into hot water and deepens to a dirty reddish brown, the color of smog. Swap the tubes and the colors swap with them, within seconds, as many times as you care to repeat it. Nothing is being added and nothing is being consumed. The same atoms are in each tube throughout. All that has changed is where a molecular tug-of-war has settled.

That tug-of-war is chemical equilibrium, and it is the normal condition of most reactions rather than an exception. The tidy arrows in a textbook equation suggest that reactants march over to become products and the story ends. In reality, the moment any product exists, it can fall apart again into the things it came from. Both directions run at once. What looks like a finished reaction is usually a reaction that has arrived at a standoff.

## A Standoff, Not a Stop

The word equilibrium invites the wrong mental picture, something settled and inert. Chemically it is the opposite. In a sealed flask where hydrogen and iodine have been sitting long enough to reach equilibrium with hydrogen iodide, molecules are still colliding, still breaking apart, still recombining, billions of times a second. The concentrations hold steady because the forward reaction and the reverse reaction are converting material at exactly the same rate. Nothing accumulates on either side, not because nothing is happening, but because everything that happens is being undone at the same pace.

Chemists confirmed this rather than assuming it. If you build a batch of the products using an unusual isotope, then let the mixture sit at equilibrium and analyze it later, you find the label scattered across both reactants and products. The atoms have been trading places the whole time, even though a measurement of concentration alone would have shown a system doing nothing at all.

Where the standoff lands is captured by a single number, the equilibrium constant. Multiply the concentrations of the products together, each raised to the power of how many of it appear in the balanced equation, and divide by the same construction for the reactants. At equilibrium, at a given temperature, that ratio takes a fixed value, and it does so no matter what quantities you started with. Begin with pure reactants, begin with pure products, begin with a lopsided mixture of both, and the system slides until the ratio matches. A large constant means the standoff sits far to the product side and the reaction looks, for practical purposes, complete. A tiny one means the reactants barely bother. Values in between describe the awkward and commercially important middle ground where you get some of what you want and a flask full of leftovers.

The same expression evaluated on a mixture that is not yet at equilibrium is called the reaction quotient, and comparing it to the constant tells you which way the system is about to move. Too few products and the reaction runs forward. Too many and it runs backward. It is one of the few pieces of chemical bookkeeping that behaves like a compass.

## Le Chatelier's Rule of Thumb

In the late nineteenth century Henri Louis Le Chatelier, a French engineer whose interests ran to cements, metallurgy and the practical problems of furnaces, summarized the behavior of disturbed equilibria in a principle that has been taught ever since. Disturb a system at equilibrium, and it shifts in the direction that partially offsets the disturbance. Push, and it pushes back.

The principle earns its keep because it is fast to apply. Add more of a reactant and the reaction consumes some of the excess by running forward. Pull a product out of the mixture as it forms, and the reaction runs forward again to replace what was taken. Squeeze a gas-phase equilibrium into a smaller volume and it shifts toward whichever side has fewer gas molecules, because fewer molecules mean less pressure. Heat a mixture, and the shift goes in whichever direction absorbs heat. That last one explains the two tubes in the water baths: splitting the colorless dimer into brown nitrogen dioxide takes energy in, so warmth drives the mixture brown and cold drives it pale.

Temperature is the odd one out in that list, and the distinction matters more than it first appears. Changing concentration or pressure moves a system to a new position along the same curve; the equilibrium constant itself is untouched, and the ratio comes back to its old value once the system re-settles. Changing temperature changes the constant. It is the only common handle that moves the destination rather than merely displacing the system from it.

Le Chatelier's principle is a rule of thumb rather than a law, and it can be pushed past its limits. Pumping an unreactive gas such as argon into a rigid vessel raises the total pressure without changing any of the partial pressures that matter, so nothing shifts, though the principle stated loosely seems to predict otherwise. In a mixture held at constant total pressure, adding more of one reactant dilutes the others, and beyond a certain point the yield of product can fall rather than rise. A catalyst, likewise, does not move the balance at all; it accelerates both directions in equal measure and simply gets the system to the same standoff sooner.

## The Compromise Inside a Fertilizer Plant

The industrial synthesis of ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen is where these ideas stop being academic. Three molecules of hydrogen and one of nitrogen combine to make two of ammonia, releasing heat as they do. Count the gas molecules on each side: four going in, two coming out. High pressure therefore favors ammonia, and plants run at pressures of a few hundred atmospheres, with all the engineering that implies in vessel walls, compressors and seals.

Temperature is the cruel part. Because the reaction gives off heat, cooling it would push the equilibrium further toward ammonia. But nitrogen is a stubborn molecule, held together by one of the strongest bonds in ordinary chemistry, and at low temperature the mixture would take geological time to reach equilibrium. The yield would be excellent and entirely theoretical. So the operating temperature is a compromise, several hundred degrees Celsius, hot enough for the reaction to move at a useful speed on an iron catalyst and cool enough that the equilibrium has not abandoned ammonia altogether. Even then, only a modest fraction of the gas converts on each pass through the reactor.

What rescues the economics is the third handle. The gas stream leaving the converter is cooled until ammonia, which liquefies far more readily than nitrogen or hydrogen, condenses and drains away. The unreacted gases are recycled to the front of the loop. By continuously removing the product, the plant never lets the mixture reach the standoff at all. A reaction whose single-pass yield looks disappointing can still consume nearly all of its feedstock, one unsatisfying pass at a time.

## Equilibria You Live Inside

Open a bottle of sparkling water and you disturb an equilibrium between dissolved carbon dioxide and the gas above the liquid. The pressure drops, the dissolved gas is suddenly in excess of what the new conditions support, and it leaves in a hurry. Leave the bottle open and the process continues slowly until the water is flat, which is to say until a new standoff has been reached with a room that contains very little carbon dioxide.

Limestone caves are carved by an equilibrium and decorated by the same one running backward. Rainwater picks up carbon dioxide from the air and from soil, becomes mildly acidic, and dissolves calcium carbonate as it seeps through bedrock. When that loaded water emerges into a cave, where the air holds less carbon dioxide, the gas escapes from solution, the equilibrium shifts, and calcium carbonate comes back out of solution as a deposit. Stalactites are precipitation driven by a change in a single partial pressure, accumulating at the rate of a thin film per drip.

Your bloodstream runs a related equilibrium as a buffer. Carbon dioxide from metabolism dissolves, forms carbonic acid, and partly dissociates into hydrogen ions and bicarbonate. Breathe too fast and you strip carbon dioxide out of the blood faster than it is made; the equilibrium slides back, hydrogen ions are consumed, and blood pH rises, which is why hyperventilation produces tingling and light-headedness. Breathing into a bag works, when it works, by raising the carbon dioxide available to push the equilibrium back where it belongs. Hemoglobin does something more sophisticated still, its affinity for oxygen tuned by the local acidity and carbon dioxide concentration, so that working muscle, which is acidic and carbon dioxide rich, pulls oxygen off the protein more aggressively than resting tissue does.

None of these systems has finished reacting. They are all sitting in the middle of a reaction that runs both ways, held in place by the balance of the two, and easily moved by anyone who understands which way to lean.
