# Entropy, Enthalpy, and Why Reactions Happen

Snap the pouch inside an instant cold pack and the thing gets cold in your hand. Nothing is burning, nothing is being cooled from outside, and no power source is attached. A salt is dissolving in water, that is all, and the temperature of the mixture drops by enough to numb a sprained ankle. The process starts the moment the barrier breaks and continues without any encouragement until the salt is gone.

This small drugstore object quietly demolishes the first rule most people are taught about chemical change. The rule says that reactions run downhill in energy, that things happen because the products are more stable than the reactants, that heat comes out. Fire obeys it. Rust obeys it. Explosives obey it emphatically. But the cold pack absorbs heat rather than releasing it, and it still happens on its own. So does ice melting on a countertop, and water evaporating from wet pavement, and the faint chill you feel when acetone dries off your fingers. Something other than energy release is deciding what is allowed to happen.

## Enthalpy Is Only Half the Ledger

The energy half of the ledger is real, and it has a name. Enthalpy is essentially the heat content of a substance measured under the ordinary condition of constant atmospheric pressure, and the change in enthalpy over a reaction is the heat that flows in or out while the reaction runs. Chemists give it a sign convention that reads backward to most people: a negative enthalpy change means heat leaves the system and the surroundings get warmer.

Where that heat comes from is bond bookkeeping. Breaking a chemical bond always costs energy, because a bond exists precisely because the bonded arrangement sits lower in energy than the separated atoms. Forming a bond always pays energy back. A reaction is a demolition followed by a construction, and its enthalpy change is simply the difference between the bill for the bonds that were broken and the refund from the bonds that were made. Methane burning in oxygen breaks four carbon-hydrogen bonds and a couple of oxygen-oxygen bonds, then builds two very strong carbon-oxygen double bonds and four oxygen-hydrogen bonds. The refund dwarfs the bill, and the difference leaves as heat and light.

This is why the energy-downhill rule works as often as it does. Most reactions that people notice are noticeable precisely because they dump energy into their surroundings. But enthalpy alone cannot explain why a gas expands to fill a room, why two miscible liquids mix on their own and never separate again, why a hot cup and a cold room drift toward the same temperature and never back. Those things happen with barely any energy change to speak of, and nothing ever reverses them spontaneously.

## Counting, Not Tidiness

The second half of the ledger is entropy, and it is routinely mistranslated as disorder, which does more harm than good. Entropy is a count. For any state of a system described by the bulk properties you can measure, temperature, pressure, volume, composition, there is some enormous number of distinct microscopic arrangements of the molecules that would all look identical from the outside. Entropy is a measure of how many. States that can be realized in vastly more ways have higher entropy, and systems left alone drift toward them for a reason that borders on the tautological: there are simply more ways to be in such a state than not.

Deal a shuffled deck and you will not get the cards in suit order. Not because the ordered arrangement is forbidden, but because it is one arrangement out of an astronomical number, and essentially all of the others look jumbled. Molecules face the same arithmetic with far larger numbers. A gas occupying half a container and then released into the whole container does not expand because expansion is favored by some force; it expands because almost every way of arranging those molecules has them spread throughout the available volume. The chance of all of them wandering back into one half at the same moment is not zero, it is just so small that the universe has not existed long enough to make it worth mentioning.

Entropy generally rises when solids become liquids and liquids become gases, when a reaction produces more molecules than it consumed, when a crystalline solid dissolves into freely wandering ions, and when temperature rises and energy has more ways to distribute itself among molecular motions. It is that last item, entropy as a way of counting the distributions of energy itself and not just of positions, that makes the whole framework work.

## Why the Cold Pack Works

Now the two halves can be put together, and the crucial move is to widen the frame. The law that governs what happens is not that a system's own entropy must rise. It is that the total entropy, system plus surroundings, must rise. Those two can point in opposite directions, and frequently do.

When a reaction gives off heat, that heat flows into the surroundings and increases their entropy, because energy dispersed among the molecules of the room can be arranged in more ways than energy concentrated in a flask. This is the hidden reason exothermic reactions tend to be spontaneous: the enthalpy release is an entropy gift to everything nearby. Conversely, a reaction that absorbs heat lowers the entropy of its surroundings, and can only proceed if it raises its own entropy by enough to more than compensate.

That is the cold pack exactly. Pulling an ionic solid apart and shoving the ions between water molecules costs energy, which the mixture takes from its own thermal store and from your hand, and the surroundings lose entropy accordingly. But an orderly crystal lattice has been converted into ions wandering freely through a liquid, an enormous increase in the number of available arrangements, and that increase wins. The same accounting explains melting ice, which takes in heat while converting a rigid lattice into a liquid that can be arranged in far more ways.

Chemists fold both halves into one number so they do not have to think about the surroundings at all. The Gibbs free energy change is the enthalpy change minus the absolute temperature multiplied by the entropy change of the system, and it is negative exactly when the total entropy of the universe would increase. A negative value means the process can run on its own. A positive one means it cannot, though the reverse can. It is a bookkeeping trick, not a new principle, but it turns a question about the entire universe into a calculation involving only the flask in front of you.

The temperature multiplying the entropy term is the interesting part, because it acts as a dial that can flip a reaction's verdict. At low temperature the enthalpy term dominates and energy-releasing reactions win, which is why water freezes in a cold room. At high temperature the entropy term dominates and processes that spread things out win, which is why ice melts in a warm one. There is a temperature where the two exactly cancel, the free energy change is zero, and neither direction is favored; for water at ordinary pressure that temperature is zero degrees Celsius, and it is why ice and water can sit in a glass together indefinitely.

Industry lives on that dial. Limestone will not decompose into lime and carbon dioxide in a quarry, because the process is strongly endothermic and the free energy change is positive at ambient temperature. Heat a kiln past eight hundred degrees and the entropy gained by liberating a gas from a solid, multiplied by that large temperature, finally overwhelms the enthalpy cost. The reaction turns spontaneous, and has been the basis of lime and cement production since antiquity, long before anyone could say why the fire had to be so hot.

## Spontaneous Is Not the Same as Soon

One caution keeps this framework honest. A negative free energy change says a reaction is allowed, not that it will happen in your lifetime. Diamond is thermodynamically less stable than graphite at room temperature and pressure; the conversion is permitted and proceeds at a rate indistinguishable from never. Thermodynamics rules on destinations, and says nothing whatsoever about travel time.

Living things exploit the gap between what is allowed and what is easy with remarkable discipline. Building a protein out of amino acids, or a strand of DNA out of nucleotides, lowers entropy locally and costs energy, and by itself would never happen. Cells pay for it by coupling each uphill step to the breakdown of molecules such as ATP, whose own decomposition is downhill by more than enough to cover the debt. The organism becomes more organized; the universe, counting the heat shed into the surroundings and the small molecules scattered as waste, ends up further along than before. Nothing is cheated. The books balance, which is the whole point, and it is why a body that stops running those reactions cools to room temperature and comes apart.
