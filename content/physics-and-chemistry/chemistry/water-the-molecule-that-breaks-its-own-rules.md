# Water, the Molecule That Breaks Its Own Rules

A pond freezes from the top down. This is so familiar that it barely registers as strange, but it should: it means the solid form of water is less dense than the liquid form, which is backward from almost every other substance on earth. Solid wax sinks in melted wax. Solid iron sinks in molten iron. Almost anything you can name settles when it solidifies, because packing molecules into an orderly crystal usually squeezes them closer together than they were as a disordered liquid. Water does the opposite, and that single reversal is the reason a lake can freeze over in winter while fish, frogs, and insect larvae go on with their lives in the liquid water underneath, insulated from the air by a lid of ice that floats rather than a floor of ice that would otherwise pile up from the bottom and eventually freeze the whole lake solid. It's one of a long list of ways that water, the most familiar substance most people ever touch, behaves nothing like its size and simplicity would suggest it should.

## A Molecule That Shouldn't Behave This Way

Water is a small molecule: one oxygen atom, two hydrogens, nine protons and nine neutrons and ten electrons all told, barely more complicated than the simplest molecules that exist. Judged purely by size, it should be a gas at room temperature, the way similar small molecules are. Hydrogen sulfide, water's chemical cousin one row down the periodic table, with sulfur standing in for oxygen, is a gas so far below room temperature that you'd need serious refrigeration to see it as a liquid at all. Ammonia and methane, comparably small molecules, are also gases under everyday conditions. Water, alone among its size class, is a liquid across a wide and, for us, extremely convenient range of temperatures, and it takes a genuinely large amount of energy to either freeze it or boil it away. Every one of these anomalies traces back to the same feature: a bond that isn't quite a bond, weaker than the ones holding the molecule together internally but strong enough, in vast numbers, to control almost everything about how water behaves in bulk.

## The Bent Shape and the Sticky Bonds

Inside a single water molecule, oxygen shares one pair of electrons with each hydrogen, an ordinary covalent arrangement, except that oxygen pulls harder on those shared electrons than hydrogen does. The electrons spend more of their time near the oxygen end of each bond, leaving oxygen with a slight negative charge and each hydrogen with a slight positive one, even though the molecule as a whole carries no net charge at all. This lopsidedness would matter less if the molecule were a straight line, with the two slightly positive ends canceling each other out, but water isn't straight. Oxygen's remaining, non-bonding electron pairs push the two hydrogens into a bent shape, roughly the angle of a wide-open pair of scissors, which means the positive and negative ends don't cancel. The whole molecule ends up polar, with a distinctly negative side and a distinctly positive side.

That polarity lets water molecules grab onto each other in a way most molecules can't. The slightly positive hydrogen on one water molecule is drawn to the slightly negative oxygen on a neighboring one, forming what chemists call a hydrogen bond: not a full covalent bond, much weaker and constantly breaking and re-forming, but a real attraction with real consequences when a huge number of molecules are all doing it to each other simultaneously. Each water molecule can form hydrogen bonds with up to four neighbors at once, two through its own hydrogens and two more by accepting bonds from other molecules' hydrogens at its oxygen's two lone pairs. Liquid water is, at any instant, a shifting, restless network of these bonds, constantly breaking under thermal jostling and reforming a fraction of a second later, giving water both the fluidity of a liquid and a persistent, underlying structure that a truly simple liquid wouldn't have.

## Ice Floats, and Everything Depends on It

That structure becomes rigid and permanent when water freezes. Cooled enough, water molecules stop jostling fast enough to keep breaking their hydrogen bonds and instead lock into a fixed lattice, each molecule bonded to exactly four neighbors in a fairly open, hexagonal arrangement. The word "open" matters: this lattice, built to maximize the number of stable hydrogen bonds, actually holds molecules farther apart on average than they manage to get in the liquid, where thermal motion lets them jumble more efficiently despite the same underlying attraction. Liquid water near its freezing point is, paradoxically, slightly more tightly packed than the ice it's about to become, which is why ice floats and why a full glass bottle of water cracks if left in a freezer: the water inside is trying to expand into a more open structure and has nowhere to go.

The ecological consequences of this one reversal are hard to overstate. Because ice floats, it forms an insulating cap rather than sinking to accumulate at the bottom, which means bodies of water freeze from the surface inward rather than from the bottom up, and a lake of any real depth essentially never freezes solid even through a long winter. Life underneath is protected by the very ice that looks, from above, like a barrier keeping life out. Very few of water's peculiarities have done more work for the planet's biology than this single accident of hydrogen-bond geometry.

## The Solvent Everything Dissolves In

Water's polarity also makes it remarkably good at pulling other substances apart, which is why chemists half-jokingly, half-seriously call it the universal solvent. Drop a crystal of salt into water and the crystal's positive sodium ions and negative chloride ions, normally locked together by mutual attraction, each get individually surrounded by a cluster of water molecules oriented to face their oppositely charged end toward the ion, oxygen facing sodium, hydrogen facing chloride. Enough water molecules crowd around each ion to outcompete the ion's attraction to its old neighbors in the crystal, and the ion drifts away into solution, thoroughly wrapped in its new water shell. The same trick works on any molecule with enough polarity of its own, which is why sugars, many acids and bases, and a huge share of the molecules biology depends on are water-soluble, while oils and fats, built from long, nonpolar hydrocarbon chains with nothing for water to grab onto electrically, are famously not.

This solvent behavior is not a side detail; it's close to the whole reason water is the medium life runs on. Chemical reactions inside a cell depend on reactants being able to find each other by diffusing freely through a fluid, on ions being able to move independently to carry electrical signals along a nerve, and on large molecules like proteins folding into shapes that expose their water-loving surfaces to the surrounding fluid while tucking their water-avoiding surfaces inward. None of that works in a solvent that can't take things apart and hold them, individually, in suspension.

## Holding Heat Without Losing Its Temper

Water also resists changing temperature more stubbornly than almost anything else that's common on Earth's surface, a property called specific heat capacity, and the reason is again the hydrogen-bond network. Raising water's temperature means making its molecules jostle faster, but a good portion of any added heat gets spent first on breaking hydrogen bonds rather than on speeding the molecules up, so it takes considerably more energy to warm a given mass of water by one degree than it takes to warm the same mass of most other liquids or solids by the same amount. This is a large part of why coastal climates run milder than inland climates at the same latitude: the ocean absorbs enormous amounts of solar heat through the summer with only a modest rise in its own temperature, then releases that stored heat slowly through the winter, smoothing out swings that inland air, lacking a comparable reservoir, cannot resist nearly as well.

The same hydrogen-bond network makes evaporation an unusually effective way to shed heat, since turning liquid water into vapor means breaking essentially all of a molecule's hydrogen bonds at once, which demands a large infusion of energy that has to come from somewhere. When sweat evaporates from skin, it draws that energy directly out of the body as heat, cooling the surface it leaves behind. It's a remarkably efficient system built entirely on the stubbornness of a bond that isn't quite a bond, repeated across more molecules than there is any real hope of counting, all because oxygen happens to be a little greedier for electrons than hydrogen, and the resulting molecule happens to be bent rather than straight.
