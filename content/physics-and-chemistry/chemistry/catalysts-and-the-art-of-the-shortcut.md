# Catalysts and the Art of the Shortcut

Slide a hunk of platinum wire into a jar of hydrogen and oxygen gas and, within moments, water starts condensing on the glass, sometimes with enough heat given off to make the wire glow. Take the platinum back out, and the reaction that just ran to completion stops cold; hydrogen and oxygen will sit together in a sealed jar for years, by all appearances doing nothing, unless something intervenes. The platinum itself is unchanged at the end, not a single atom of it consumed or transformed, ready to be dropped into the next jar and do the same trick again. This is the essential strangeness of a catalyst: it can be the difference between a reaction that never happens and one that happens almost instantly, without itself being used up in the process, and without changing what the reaction ultimately produces.

## The Hill Every Reaction Has to Climb

Chemists distinguish between whether a reaction is thermodynamically favorable, meaning the products end up at lower energy than the starting materials, and whether it's kinetically accessible, meaning it actually proceeds at a noticeable rate. These sound like they should be the same question, but they aren't. Hydrogen and oxygen combining into water releases a substantial amount of energy, and the products are far more stable than the reactants, so the reaction is enormously favorable on paper. Yet a jar of the two gases can sit indefinitely without reacting, because getting from reactants to products requires passing through an unstable, high-energy intermediate arrangement first, a kind of hill the reacting molecules have to climb over before they can roll down into the lower-energy valley of the finished product. The height of that hill is called activation energy, and it exists because breaking the original bonds in hydrogen and oxygen molecules takes energy up front, even though forming the new bonds in water will pay that energy back with interest once the new bonds are actually made.

At room temperature, only a small fraction of molecules in any sample happen to be moving fast enough, and colliding at the right angle, to have enough energy between them to make it over that hill unassisted. Raise the temperature and more molecules qualify, which is the ordinary way to speed up a sluggish reaction, but heating has costs and limits: it takes energy, it can degrade delicate reactants, and past a certain point it starts favoring unwanted side reactions just as much as the one you want. A catalyst offers a different kind of solution entirely. Rather than giving more molecules the energy to leap the existing hill, it opens up an alternate route around the mountain, a different sequence of steps to the same destination, in which no single step demands nearly as much activation energy as the original, uncatalyzed path required.

## Lowering the Hill Without Joining the Trip

The alternate route usually works by having the catalyst temporarily bind to one or more of the reactants, holding them in a strained or reactive configuration, or holding two separate reactant molecules close together and correctly oriented so they're far more likely to actually collide productively than they would be drifting freely in a gas or solution. Once the reaction proceeds through this intermediate stage, the catalyst releases the product and returns to its original form, free to grab the next molecule and repeat the whole cycle, often billions of times over before it's finally worn out by some unrelated side process like physical fouling or poisoning by an impurity.

Because the catalyst comes back unchanged at the end of each cycle, it doesn't appear in the reaction's overall equation, and, crucially, it doesn't change where the reaction's equilibrium ends up. A catalyst speeds up the forward reaction and the reverse reaction by exactly the same proportion, so if a reaction was destined to convert, say, most but not quite all of its starting material into product given infinite time, a catalyst gets it there in seconds rather than centuries but doesn't push the final balance any further than it would have gone anyway. This is a frequently misunderstood point: a catalyst is a matter of speed, not of destination. It cannot make an unfavorable reaction favorable, and it cannot squeeze more product out of a reaction than the underlying thermodynamics allow. What it can do is make the difference between a reaction that would take place over geological time and one that takes place over a coffee break.

## Surfaces That Do the Work

Many industrial catalysts are solids that act on gases or liquids passing over them, a setup called heterogeneous catalysis because the catalyst and the reactants exist in different physical phases. The catalytic converter bolted underneath a car's engine is the example most people own without ever thinking much about it. Exhaust gas, laden with unburned hydrocarbons, carbon monoxide, and various nitrogen oxides, passes through a honeycomb structure coated with a thin layer of metals, typically platinum, palladium, and rhodium, spread out enough to expose a large surface area within a physically small unit. Molecules in the exhaust stream stick temporarily to that surface, an event called adsorption, and while stuck, they sit close enough to neighboring adsorbed molecules, and in a reactive enough state, for reactions to occur that wouldn't happen nearly as readily in free-flowing gas. Carbon monoxide combines with adsorbed oxygen to form carbon dioxide; nitrogen oxides get stripped of their oxygen and released as harmless nitrogen gas; unburned hydrocarbons get oxidized into carbon dioxide and water. All three reactions happen essentially at once, on the same honeycomb, converting a genuinely toxic exhaust stream into one that's merely undesirable rather than dangerous, and the metal itself is still there, unconsumed, on the day the converter is finally removed from a scrapped car.

Because heterogeneous catalysis depends on surface area, industrial catalysts are almost always engineered into a form with as much exposed surface as physically possible: fine powders, porous pellets riddled with microscopic channels, or thin coatings spread across an inert honeycomb or bead support, all in service of exposing the maximum number of catalytic atoms to the reactant stream flowing past.

## Enzymes: Catalysis Refined by Evolution

Biology solved the same problem long before any refinery existed, and arguably solved it better. Enzymes are catalysts built from folded proteins, and they achieve levels of speed and precision that industrial chemists still can't fully match with metal surfaces and high heat. An enzyme's active site, a pocket shaped by the precise folding of its amino acid chain, is often so specifically matched to one particular reactant molecule that the enzyme will catalyze that one reaction and effectively ignore molecules that are chemically very similar but not quite the right shape or charge distribution. This selectivity lets a cell run thousands of different reactions simultaneously in the same crowded fluid without those reactions interfering with each other, something a chemist running a comparable mixture of reagents in a flask could never manage without the products turning into an unusable mess.

Industry has increasingly borrowed enzymes directly rather than trying to replicate their tricks from scratch. Laundry detergents contain enzymes that break down protein and fat stains at temperatures far lower than would otherwise be needed. Cheesemaking depends on an enzyme that clots milk proteins. Some biofuel production uses enzymes to break tough plant fiber down into simpler sugars that can then be fermented. In each case, the appeal is the same one that first made platinum wire interesting to nineteenth-century chemists: a substance that makes a desired reaction run fast, cleanly, and repeatedly, while walking away from every single cycle exactly as it arrived.

## Catalysis at Industrial Scale

Scale up from a single catalytic converter to the chemical industry as a whole, and catalysis turns out to be sitting quietly underneath an enormous share of manufactured goods. Sulfuric acid, produced in greater bulk than almost any other industrial chemical, depends on a vanadium oxide catalyst to make one key step happen fast enough to be practical. Most plastics begin as small molecules stitched into long polymer chains with the help of catalysts that control not just the speed of the reaction but the exact structure of the resulting chain, determining whether a plastic ends up rigid or flexible, transparent or opaque. And the single most consequential catalytic process of the last century or so, one that reshaped how the planet feeds itself, uses an iron-based catalyst to force nitrogen gas, otherwise nearly inert, to react with hydrogen and form ammonia, a story substantial enough to deserve its own telling, but which starts from exactly the same principle as the platinum wire in a jar: find the right surface, and a reaction that refused to happen suddenly can't stop.
