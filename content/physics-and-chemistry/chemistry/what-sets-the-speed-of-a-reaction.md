# What Sets the Speed of a Reaction

Crack a glow stick and drop it into a mug of hot water, and it blazes. Drop an identical one into ice water and it glows dimly, sulkily, for hours. The two sticks contain the same chemicals in the same amounts, and both will eventually run through every molecule they have and go dark. They differ only in how fast they spend themselves. The hot one is brilliant and brief; the cold one is faint and patient. You can move a glow stick between the two baths and watch it trade brightness for lifespan in either direction.

That trade is the whole subject of chemical kinetics. Thermodynamics will tell you whether a reaction is allowed and where it will end up, and it will tell you nothing at all about whether the journey takes a microsecond or a century. Rate is a separate question with its own rules, and in practice it is usually the more urgent one. Gasoline is thermodynamically doomed in the presence of air; it is the rate, not the direction, that decides whether that fact is an engine or a disaster.

## What a Rate Actually Measures

A reaction rate is a change in concentration divided by a change in time, and the first thing to notice about it is that it almost never holds still. Watch magnesium ribbon dissolve in acid and the bubbling is vigorous at the start, slower in the middle, and barely perceptible at the end. Nothing has been switched off. The acid has simply been partly consumed, its molecules are further apart, and encounters between acid and metal have become correspondingly rarer. Rate falls as reactants are used up, which means a single number for the rate of a reaction is meaningless unless you say at what moment it was measured. Chemists usually settle this by measuring the initial rate, the slope right at the beginning before concentrations have moved much, and by repeating that measurement with different starting mixtures.

Do that systematically and a pattern emerges. Double the concentration of one reactant and the initial rate might double, or quadruple, or not budge at all. The relationship, written out, is the rate law, and each exponent in it is called the order of the reaction with respect to that reactant. A reaction that doubles in rate when you double a reactant is first order in that substance. One that quadruples is second order. One that ignores the change entirely is zero order in it.

The single most common mistake in this territory is assuming you can read those exponents off the balanced equation. You cannot. The balanced equation is an inventory of what goes in and what comes out; the rate law is a fingerprint of the path taken in between, and the two are related only by experiment. A reaction with three molecules on the reactant side may turn out to be first order overall, because the slow step of the actual mechanism involves only one of them.

Zero-order behavior is the strangest to meet for the first time, since it means the reaction proceeds at a flat rate regardless of how much material is left. It normally signals that something other than concentration is the bottleneck: a fixed surface with only so many active sites, a lamp supplying only so many photons per second, or an enzyme already working flat out. Alcohol metabolism is the example most adults have personal data on. The liver enzyme that handles ethanol is saturated at concentrations well below those reached after a single drink, so the body clears alcohol at a roughly constant rate rather than a proportional one. Drinking twice as much does not double the elimination rate; it doubles the time.

First-order reactions behave in the opposite and more elegant way. Their rate is proportional to what remains, which produces a constant half-life: whatever fraction is left, half of it will be gone after the same interval as last time. Radioactive decay is the purest case, and most drug clearance approximates it well enough that dosing schedules are built on half-lives. It is a useful shape to recognize because it never quite finishes and never quite needs to.

## Collisions, and Why So Few of Them Count

Underneath the rate laws is a picture simple enough to sketch on a napkin. Molecules in a gas or a solution are in constant, violent motion, and a given molecule undergoes billions of collisions every second. If every collision produced a reaction, all chemistry would be over instantly. Almost none of them do.

Two filters explain the shortfall. The first is energy. Collisions vary enormously in violence, and only those above a certain threshold carry enough punch to break existing bonds and let the rearrangement proceed. In any sample at any temperature, molecular speeds are spread across a wide distribution, with the very fast molecules occupying a thin tail at the high end. It is only that tail that reacts. The second filter is geometry. Molecules are shaped objects, and a hard collision at the wrong angle, hitting an unreactive part of a large molecule, accomplishes nothing but a change of direction. The bigger and more elaborate the reacting molecules, the more of their encounters are wasted on bad aim.

Temperature works on the first filter with a leverage that people consistently underestimate. Warming a mixture does raise the average speed, but the important effect is on the shape of the tail, which grows dramatically faster than the average does. A modest temperature increase can shift a large fraction of the population across the reacting threshold, which is why the rule of thumb for many ordinary reactions near room temperature is that a rise of ten degrees Celsius roughly doubles the rate. Twenty degrees quadruples it. That exponential sensitivity is why a refrigerator buys days of freshness, a freezer buys months, and a warm kitchen counter buys an afternoon, for the same milk with the same bacteria in it.

The second filter can be attacked physically rather than chemically. A steel nail will not burn in a campfire; steel wool from the same alloy catches readily and can be lit with a battery, because dividing the same mass into fine fibers multiplies the surface available for oxygen to attack. Push that further and it becomes dangerous. Flour, sugar, sawdust and coal dust are all ordinary combustible solids that burn sedately in a heap, and all of them have destroyed grain elevators and mills when a suspended cloud of fine particles gave the oxygen so much surface at once that the entire cloud reacted essentially simultaneously. Stirring and grinding do the same thing more modestly in a lab or a kitchen, which is why recipes and procedures are so insistent about mixing.

## Slow Steps and the Shape of a Mechanism

Most reactions do not happen in the single collision the balanced equation implies. They proceed through a sequence of elementary steps, each involving one or two particles, with short-lived intermediates appearing and disappearing along the way. The overall rate is then governed by the slowest step in the chain, the same way a checkout queue is governed by the slowest cashier. Speeding up any other step changes nothing you can measure.

This is what makes kinetics a genuine investigative tool rather than a bookkeeping exercise. By measuring how the rate responds to each reactant, a chemist is looking directly at which species are involved before or during that slow step, and can rule out proposed mechanisms that predict the wrong dependence. A species that appears in the balanced equation but not the rate law is entering after the bottleneck. A species that appears in the rate law but not the balanced equation, which happens, is a catalyst or a participant in an equilibrium preceding the slow step.

Some mechanisms have feedback built in, and they behave alarmingly. In a chain reaction, one reactive step generates more reactive fragments than it consumes, so each cycle widens rather than repeating. A hydrogen and oxygen mixture is stable for years and then, once branching gets going, converts itself in milliseconds. Thermal runaway is the same idea using heat as the courier: a reaction that releases heat and speeds up as it warms can feed its own acceleration until it is out of control. Industrial reactor design is largely the art of ensuring that heat can leave faster than the reaction can make it.

## Watching Something That Refuses to Sit Still

Measuring all this requires catching a moving system in the act. For a leisurely reaction, you can pull samples at intervals and stop the chemistry cold before analyzing, by plunging the sample into ice or adding something that quenches it. Where a reactant or product is colored, a beam of light through the vessel gives a continuous readout without touching the mixture. Reactions that produce gas can be followed with a pressure gauge or an inverted graduated cylinder; reactions that create or destroy ions can be followed by conductivity; reactions that release heat can be followed with nothing more than a well-insulated cup and a thermometer.

The fast end has driven a century of instrument building. Stopped-flow devices slam two solutions together and begin recording within a couple of milliseconds. Flash techniques start a reaction with a burst of light so the clock has a sharp zero. Pulsed lasers have pushed the resolution down to the femtosecond scale, where the interesting object is no longer a rate constant but the actual motion of atoms across the fleeting arrangement between reactants and products, a structure too short-lived to isolate and, until recently, too short-lived to see.
