# Polymers and the Molecules That Grew Too Long

Pull a plastic shopping bag taut between your hands and it stretches before it
tears. Pull a sheet of aluminium foil the same way and it just rips. The
difference is not strength in any simple sense — the aluminium is stronger by
most measures — but architecture. The bag is made of molecules so long that
they are tangled around each other like cooked spaghetti, and when you stretch
it you are not breaking bonds so much as persuading those strands to slide and
straighten. That sliding is the whole story of plastics, and of rubber, and of
the proteins in the tendon connecting your thumb to your wrist.

A polymer is a molecule built by repeating a small unit over and over. The unit
is called a monomer, and in most industrial plastics it is something modest: a
two-carbon fragment, a ring, a short chain with a reactive group at each end.
What makes the resulting material interesting is not the chemistry of the unit
but the sheer number of them strung together. A polyethylene chain in a milk
jug might contain tens of thousands of carbon atoms in an unbroken line. That
single molecule, stretched out, would be visible under a good optical
microscope. It never is stretched out, of course. It is coiled, and knotted
through its neighbours, and that entanglement is what turns a waxy substance
into something you can drop on a kitchen floor.

## Two Ways to Build a Chain

Chemists make long chains by two broadly different routes, and the distinction
still organises how the industry thinks.

The first is addition polymerisation. Start with a monomer containing a carbon
double bond — ethylene, propylene, styrene, vinyl chloride. Attack it with a
reactive species that opens the double bond and leaves a new reactive site at
the end. That site attacks the next monomer, opens it, and moves the
reactivity along one unit. The chain grows from one end, monomer by monomer,
sometimes thousands of times per second, until something quenches it. Nothing
is left over; every atom of the monomer ends up in the polymer. Polyethylene,
polypropylene, polystyrene and PVC all come from this family, and between them
they account for the overwhelming majority of plastic made by weight.

The second route is condensation polymerisation. Here the monomers carry
reactive groups at both ends — an acid at one end and an alcohol at the other,
say — and they join by expelling a small molecule, usually water. Polyester and
nylon are made this way, and so are the proteins in your body, which link amino
acids by expelling water between them. Condensation is slower and fussier
because the chain grows from both ends and in the middle at once, and because
the expelled water has to be removed or it will drive the reaction backwards.
The reward is control. You can alternate two different monomers strictly,
which addition polymerisation struggles to do, and you can build in aromatic
rings and polar groups that give the finished fibre stiffness and the ability
to hold dye.

## Why Some Plastics Melt and Others Burn

The most consequential division in plastics is not chemical composition but
whether the chains are connected to each other.

In a thermoplastic, they are not. The chains are long and entangled, but the
only thing holding them to their neighbours is the weak, collective attraction
between adjacent stretches of molecule. Heat the material and those attractions
lose against thermal motion. The chains begin to slide past one another, the
solid softens, and eventually it flows. Let it cool and the attractions
reassert themselves. This is reversible more or less indefinitely, which is why
a thermoplastic can be injection moulded, ground up, and moulded again. It is
also why a polypropylene container deforms in a dishwasher and why a
polystyrene cup collapses around hot liquid.

In a thermoset, the chains are chemically bonded to each other at intervals,
forming one enormous three-dimensional network. There is no longer any
meaningful distinction between one molecule and the next; the entire object is,
in a formal sense, a single molecule. Such a material cannot melt, because
melting would require breaking covalent bonds, and by the time you supply
enough energy to do that you are simply destroying the substance. Epoxy,
phenolic resins, the rubber in a car tyre and the resin in a fibreglass hull
are all thermosets. They are moulded once, while the cross-linking reaction is
still running, and after that their shape is permanent. This is a genuine
advantage for a boat hull and a genuine problem for recycling.

Vulcanisation, Charles Goodyear's contribution, is the classic example of
controlled cross-linking. Raw natural rubber is a sticky, temperature-sensitive
thermoplastic that goes gummy in summer and brittle in winter. Heating it with
sulfur produces short sulfur bridges between chains at scattered points. Not
many — if you put in too much sulfur you get ebonite, a hard black solid used
for bowling balls and fountain pen bodies. At the right density the bridges
prevent the chains from sliding permanently past one another while still
letting them uncoil and recoil. That is precisely what elasticity is: a
material that deforms a great deal, stores the deformation as a loss of
molecular disorder, and springs back when released because disorder is
preferred.

## Crystals in a Tangle

Long chains would seem too unwieldy to organise themselves, and mostly they
are. But stretches of a chain can line up neatly with stretches of neighbouring
chains, folding back and forth to form small, ordered crystalline domains
embedded in a disordered matrix. Most common plastics are semi-crystalline in
this way, and the proportion of ordered to disordered regions determines a
great deal about how the material behaves.

High-density polyethylene has chains with few side branches, so they pack
closely and crystallise readily. The result is stiff, opaque, and comparatively
resistant to solvents — milk jugs, pipe, cutting boards. Low-density
polyethylene, made under conditions that produce frequent branches, cannot pack
as well. It crystallises less, stays floppier, and transmits light better,
which is why it becomes cling film and squeeze bottles. Same monomer, same
element ratios, entirely different materials, and the only difference is how
often the chain sprouts a branch.

The disordered regions have their own transition. Below a certain temperature
the tangled chains are frozen in place and the material is glassy and brittle.
Above it, segments gain enough freedom to wriggle and the material becomes
leathery or rubbery. This glass transition is why an outdoor plastic chair
survives a summer and cracks in a hard frost, and why the rubber compound in a
tyre is formulated with the local climate in mind. It is a property of the
disordered fraction only, which is why a semi-crystalline plastic can pass
through its glass transition and still hold its shape: the crystallites are
still doing structural work.

## The Property That Made Them Ubiquitous, and the One That Won't Let Go

Plastics displaced wood, glass, metal and leather across an enormous range of
uses in a remarkably short span, and the reason is not cheapness alone. It is
that a polymer can be tuned. Change the monomer, the chain length, the branch
frequency, the cross-link density, the additives, and you move continuously
across a property space that older materials occupied only at isolated points.
You can have a material that is transparent and shatter-resistant, or one that
is flexible at low temperature and stiff at high, or one that resists a
particular solvent while dissolving in another. Additives extend this further:
plasticisers that slip between chains and lubricate their sliding, turning
rigid PVC into flexible tubing; stabilisers that absorb ultraviolet photons
before those photons can break a backbone; carbon black that blocks light
entirely and doubles as reinforcement.

The durability that makes the material useful is the same property that makes
its disposal difficult. A carbon backbone with hydrogen and chlorine hanging
off it is not a shape that soil bacteria have much enzymatic experience with.
Cellulose and protein are attacked readily because there has been a long
history of organisms making a living from them. Polyethylene mostly just sits,
fragmenting under ultraviolet light and mechanical abrasion into smaller and
smaller pieces without the chain chemistry changing much at all. Mechanical
recycling helps but degrades quality: each melting cycle shortens chains
slightly and mixes in contamination, so recycled material tends to migrate
downward through applications rather than circulating at the same level.

The more interesting responses work on the chemistry rather than the logistics.
Chemical recycling aims to break chains back to monomers that can be
repolymerised to original quality, which is straightforward for condensation
polymers like polyester — you can hydrolyse them back to their starting
materials — and much harder for addition polymers whose carbon backbones have
no convenient weak point. Designing weak points in deliberately, so a chain
holds together under use but comes apart under a specific trigger, is an
active line of work. So is building polymers from monomers that plants make,
which changes where the carbon comes from without necessarily changing what
happens at the end of the object's life. Those are separate problems, and
conflating them has caused a good deal of confusion about what any particular
material actually accomplishes.
