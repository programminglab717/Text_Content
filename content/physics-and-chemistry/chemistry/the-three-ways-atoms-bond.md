# The Three Ways Atoms Bond

Drop a spoonful of salt in water and it vanishes, silently, into a clear liquid that will now conduct electricity. Try the same trick with a copper penny and nothing happens; the penny sits there, inert, until you melt it down or dissolve it in something considerably harsher than water. Try it with a diamond and you could wait a very long time indeed, since diamond shrugs off nearly every solvent and most acids as well. Three solids, three completely different responses to the same simple test. The difference isn't about hardness or color or anything visible on the surface. It comes down to how the atoms inside each substance are holding onto each other, and there are really only three fundamental ways for them to do it.

## The Deal at the Heart of Every Bond

Atoms bond for the same underlying reason chemical reactions happen at all: a lower-energy arrangement is more stable than a higher-energy one, and left to their own devices, systems drift toward stability. For most atoms, the specific target is a full outer shell of electrons, the same comfortable configuration that makes the noble gases so chemically lazy. An atom with one electron too many in its outer shell, or one electron too few, is under a kind of low-grade tension, and bonding is how that tension resolves. What differs from element to element is the strategy: give the extra electron away entirely, share it with a neighbor, or throw it into a communal pool with a great many other atoms at once. Those three strategies are ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding, and nearly every solid, liquid, and gas you'll ever touch is built from one of them, or some blend of the three.

Which strategy an element reaches for depends heavily on where it sits on the periodic table, specifically on a property called electronegativity, which measures how strongly an atom's nucleus pulls on electrons, including ones that technically belong to a neighboring atom. Metals, sitting on the left and in the middle of the table, hold their outer electrons loosely and have low electronegativity. Nonmetals, clustered on the right, hold theirs tightly and have high electronegativity. Put a low-electronegativity atom next to a high-electronegativity one and you get one behavior. Put two high-electronegativity atoms together and you get another. Put a great many low-electronegativity atoms together and you get a third.

## Giving It Away: Ionic Bonds

Sodium is a soft, silvery metal that reacts so violently with water it has to be stored under oil. Chlorine is a pale green gas so toxic it saw use as a chemical weapon. Combine them and you get table salt, a substance you sprinkle on eggs without a second thought. The transformation happens because sodium's single outer electron is barely held at all, while chlorine is one electron short of a full shell and pulls hard on anything nearby. Put the two together and sodium simply surrenders its electron to chlorine outright. Sodium becomes a positively charged ion, chlorine a negatively charged one, and the two ions then hold together purely through electrostatic attraction, the same force that makes a balloon stick to a wall after you rub it on your hair, just organized with far more discipline.

That electrostatic attraction doesn't stop at one pair. Each sodium ion attracts every nearby chloride ion, and vice versa, so the result is not a collection of molecules but a single, vast, repeating lattice, ions stacked in a precise geometric pattern that extends through the entire crystal. This is why ionic solids tend to be brittle rather than bendable: strike the crystal hard enough to shift one layer relative to the next, and you suddenly line up ions of the same charge face to face. They repel violently, and the crystal shatters along a clean plane rather than deforming. It's also why ionic compounds tend to have high melting points, since melting means overcoming the electrostatic grip of the entire lattice at once, and why they conduct electricity only when melted or dissolved, at which point the ions are finally free to move and carry charge from place to place. Solid salt doesn't conduct; salt water does, and so does molten salt in an industrial electrolysis cell.

## Sharing Custody: Covalent Bonds

Put two atoms together that both pull hard on electrons, neither willing to surrender, and outright transfer isn't an option. Instead they compromise: they share a pair of electrons, each atom counting the shared pair toward its own outer shell without the electrons actually belonging to either one exclusively. This is the covalent bond, and it's the bond behind most of the molecules that make up living things and most of the compounds a chemist would recognize as having a definite, countable formula, like water, carbon dioxide, or glucose.

Covalent bonds come with a subtlety ionic bonds don't have to worry about: sharing isn't always equal. Two identical atoms, like the two oxygens in an oxygen molecule, share perfectly evenly. But in water, oxygen pulls harder on the shared electrons than the hydrogens do, so the electrons spend more time near oxygen's end of the bond than hydrogen's, leaving oxygen slightly negative and each hydrogen slightly positive despite the whole molecule carrying no net charge. That lopsidedness, called polarity, is responsible for a huge share of water's unusual behavior and for the way covalent molecules in general interact with each other even after the bonding within each molecule is settled.

Covalent bonding also splits into two very different architectures depending on scale. Most covalent compounds form discrete molecules, a fixed, countable number of atoms bonded together and then done, which is why water is properly written as two hydrogens and one oxygen rather than an open-ended chain. But some elements build covalent networks instead, extending the sharing indefinitely in every direction rather than stopping at a tidy molecule. Diamond is the cleanest example: every carbon atom forms four covalent bonds to four other carbons, and those carbons bond to four more, on and on through the entire crystal, so that a diamond ring is, strictly speaking, one single molecule containing an almost unimaginable number of atoms. That's why diamond is so hard and has such an extreme melting point compared to a molecular solid like sugar: breaking it apart means breaking actual covalent bonds throughout the lattice, not just overcoming weak forces between separate molecules.

## Electrons on Loan: Metallic Bonding

Metals solve the same problem with a strategy unavailable to nonmetals: since every atom in a chunk of copper or iron or gold is equally willing to give up its loosely held outer electrons, none of them bother holding on tightly to any particular one. Instead, the outer electrons detach from their home atoms altogether and move freely through the whole structure, forming what chemists sometimes call a sea of electrons, while the atoms themselves settle into a lattice of positively charged cores sitting in that sea. Nobody owns any particular electron. Everybody benefits from all of them.

This arrangement explains nearly everything distinctive about metals. They conduct electricity exceptionally well because the electron sea is already mobile, ready to carry current the instant a voltage is applied, unlike ionic solids where the charge carriers are frozen in a lattice until melted. They conduct heat well for the same reason, since a mobile electron sea transmits vibrational energy efficiently. And they're malleable rather than brittle, which is the real giveaway that distinguishes a metal from an ionic solid of similar hardness: hammer a piece of gold and the positive cores slide past each other into a new arrangement, but the electron sea simply flows to accommodate them, maintaining the bonding the whole time. There's no moment where like charges suddenly face off and repel, the way there is in an ionic crystal, so the metal deforms smoothly instead of cracking. A goldsmith can beat gold into a sheet thin enough to see through; try that with a crystal of salt and you'll produce a small pile of dust.

## When the Categories Blur

Textbooks present these three bond types as clean categories because it makes for a tidy chapter, but real bonds sit on a continuum defined by how unevenly two atoms share their electrons. A bond between two atoms of very different electronegativity, like sodium and chlorine, is essentially fully ionic. A bond between two identical atoms is essentially fully covalent, with perfectly even sharing. Most real bonds fall somewhere in between, polar covalent bonds that share unevenly without going all the way to a full transfer, and chemists use electronegativity difference as a rough, continuous guide to where any given bond sits rather than treating the three categories as walls.

Graphite makes the blurring especially vivid. It's built from carbon, the same element as diamond, and held together within each layer by covalent bonds nearly as strong as diamond's. But graphite's carbons bond to only three neighbors each instead of four, leaving one outer electron per atom undedicated to any single bond. Those spare electrons delocalize across each entire sheet, much like the electron sea in a metal, which is why graphite conducts electricity along its layers while diamond, with every electron locked into a specific bond, is an excellent insulator. Two solids, the same element, and two entirely different bonding stories layered on top of each other in the same material, which is a fair reminder that the three-part scheme is less a set of rigid boxes than a description of three ends of a single underlying spectrum.
