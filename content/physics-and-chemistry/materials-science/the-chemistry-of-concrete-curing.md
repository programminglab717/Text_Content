# The Chemistry of Concrete Curing

Ask someone how long it takes concrete to dry, and you will usually get an answer measured in a day or two — enough time, in the popular imagination, for the water to evaporate and the slab to harden, the same basic process as a puddle disappearing off a driveway. This is almost entirely wrong, and the correction matters, because it explains both why concrete can gain strength for years after it's poured and why a contractor spraying a fresh sidewalk with water seems to be doing the opposite of what common sense suggests. Concrete does not harden by drying. It hardens through a chemical reaction with water, a reaction that needs water present to keep going, and depriving fresh concrete of moisture too early is one of the most common ways to ruin it.

## What Cement Actually Is

Concrete is a mixture of aggregate — sand, gravel, crushed stone — bound together by a paste made from cement and water. The aggregate is doing very little chemically; it is filler, chosen for cost and for how well it packs together, and most of the finished material's volume is aggregate rather than paste. The paste is where all the interesting chemistry happens, and the cement inside it starts its life as limestone and clay, ground together and fed into a rotating kiln and heated to a temperature high enough to partially fuse the mixture into hard nodules called clinker. That clinker is a blend of several distinct calcium-based mineral compounds — chief among them two different calcium silicates, along with smaller amounts of calcium aluminate compounds — and the clinker is then ground into the extremely fine gray powder that gets bagged and sold as Portland cement.

None of that powder does anything interesting until water is added. The moment cement and water meet, a set of hydration reactions begins immediately, and it does not stop after the concrete has visibly set and can bear weight — it continues, more slowly, for a very long time afterward.

## The Gel That Holds Everything Together

The calcium silicate compounds in cement react with water to form a material called calcium-silicate-hydrate, usually abbreviated C-S-H, and this gel — not the sand, not the gravel, not the cement powder itself — is the actual glue holding a piece of concrete together. It is not crystalline in any simple sense; it forms as an extremely fine, densely packed structure with an enormous amount of internal surface area for its volume, growing outward from each cement grain until neighboring growths interlock with each other and with the surfaces of the sand and gravel, knitting the whole mass into a continuous solid. Calcium hydroxide forms alongside it as a byproduct, and the aluminate compounds react on their own faster timeline, contributing to the concrete's earliest stiffening.

This hydration reaction releases heat as it proceeds, which is easy to overlook in a small sidewalk pour but becomes a serious engineering concern in large ones. Pour a dam or a thick building foundation, and the released heat, unable to escape quickly from the interior of such a large mass, can raise the internal temperature substantially above the surface temperature. As the interior later cools back down and contracts while the already-hardened surface does not contract in step with it, the mismatch can crack the concrete from the inside. Engineers manage this on large pours by circulating cooling water through embedded pipes during curing, using cement blends formulated to release heat more slowly, or simply pouring in stages rather than all at once.

## Why Keeping It Wet Matters

Hydration can only proceed where there is water available to react with unreacted cement, and it can only proceed within the tiny, water-filled pore spaces of the paste. If the surface of fresh concrete is allowed to dry out in the first days after it is poured — sun and wind pull moisture out of the surface far faster than it can be replenished from deeper in the slab — hydration in that surface layer stalls before it has gone very far. Since the surface is exactly the layer of the concrete under the most abrasion and weathering later, and exactly the layer most people ever touch, this half-finished hydration produces a chalky, dusting, weaker surface prone to fine cracking, even though the interior may have cured just fine.

This is why curing, properly understood, means actively keeping the concrete moist, or at least sealing moisture in, for an extended period rather than letting the surface dry out early. In practice this can mean covering a slab with wet burlap or plastic sheeting, misting it periodically with water, ponding a shallow layer of water on a horizontal surface, or spraying on a curing compound that forms a temporary membrane to trap the concrete's own mix water inside. Concrete cured underwater, or in a sealed form where it cannot dry out at all, can develop excellent strength precisely because it never has the chance to starve the surface reaction of water. Strength gain follows a predictable curve: most of it develops within the first week or so, but the reaction continues at a diminishing rate for a long time afterward, which is why the concrete industry conventionally rates strength at twenty-eight days after pouring as a practical benchmark, even though the material keeps gaining a bit more strength for a long time after that milestone passes.

## Tuning the Mix

Modern concrete rarely relies on plain Portland cement alone. Supplementary materials such as fly ash, a byproduct of coal combustion, or ground slag from iron production are often blended in; these materials do not react with water directly the way cement does, but they react with the calcium hydroxide left over from cement hydration in a slower secondary reaction called a pozzolanic reaction, filling in additional C-S-H gel over a longer timescale and often producing a denser, more durable, more chemically resistant final paste. Chemical admixtures adjust nearly every other practical aspect of the mix: plasticizers make wet concrete flow more easily without needing extra water that would weaken the finished paste, retarders slow the set on a hot day or a long pour, accelerators speed it up in cold weather, and air-entraining agents deliberately whip microscopic air bubbles into the mix to give any water trapped in the pores room to expand when it freezes, rather than cracking the concrete from the inside during winter freeze-thaw cycles.

Roman engineers, working with none of this modern chemical vocabulary, discovered their own version of the pozzolanic reaction by mixing lime with volcanic ash, producing a concrete durable enough that structures like the dome of the Pantheon have stood for roughly two millennia, still one continuous mass of set paste and aggregate, outlasting a fair amount of the more modern concrete poured in the last century using ordinary Portland cement without particular attention to durability. The chemistry has been refined enormously since, but the basic act — mixing a reactive powder with water and aggregate, and then giving that mixture the time and moisture it needs to finish reacting — has not fundamentally changed.

## A Reaction, Not a Drying

It is worth returning to the plain fact underneath all of this: a concrete slab is, chemically, still working long after it looks finished. Walk across a driveway a week after it was poured and it already feels and sounds like solid stone, but the calcium silicate gel inside it is still slowly filling in around every remaining grain of unreacted cement, and it will keep doing so, at an ever-slackening pace, for years. Nothing is evaporating away to leave that strength behind. It is being built, one hydration reaction at a time, out of a powder and the water it was never allowed to lose too soon.
