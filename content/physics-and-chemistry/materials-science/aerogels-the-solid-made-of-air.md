# Aerogels: The Solid Made of Air

Hold a block of silica aerogel up to the light and it looks like something that shouldn't exist: a pale, faintly bluish, almost smoky solid that you can see through in places, sitting in your palm with so little apparent substance that people who handle it for the first time often check twice to be sure they're actually holding anything at all. It has been called frozen smoke often enough that the phrase has become the standard way to describe it to someone who hasn't seen one, and the description is more accurate than most nicknames given to unusual materials — the material genuinely is, by volume, overwhelmingly empty space, held together by a solid framework so sparse and so fine that it barely interrupts the passage of light, sound, or heat.

## A Bet About a Jelly

The material's origin traces back to the chemist Samuel Kistler, working in the late 1920s, and the story usually told about how he arrived at it is a bet with a colleague over whether the liquid inside an ordinary gel, the same general category of substance as fruit jelly, could be replaced entirely with a gas without collapsing the delicate solid network suspended inside it. It's the kind of origin story materials science likes to tell about itself, a stubborn question chased for its own sake rather than for any obvious application, and whatever the precise details of the wager, Kistler did work out how to do exactly that, publishing his results and giving the resulting material its name.

The problem he was solving is easy to demonstrate badly at home: take a wet gel — a three-dimensional network of solid particles or polymer strands with liquid filling nearly all the space between them, often occupying the overwhelming majority of the gel's total volume — and simply let the liquid evaporate on its own. As the liquid recedes through the gel's network of extremely fine pores, the surface tension at each shrinking liquid-air boundary pulls inward on the delicate solid structure surrounding it, and that pulling is more than the fragile network can resist. The whole structure collapses in on itself as it dries, shrinking down into a dense, cracked, unremarkable lump that has lost essentially all of the fine internal architecture it started with. This is exactly what happens if you let a water-based gel simply air-dry, and it is the reason nobody had produced anything like aerogel before Kistler, despite gels themselves being nothing new.

## Drying Without a Liquid Surface

Kistler's solution, supercritical drying, sidesteps the problem instead of fighting it. Every liquid has a critical point — a specific combination of temperature and pressure above which the usual distinction between liquid and gas disappears entirely, replaced by a single supercritical fluid phase that has no surface, and therefore no surface tension, because there is no boundary between two distinct phases left to have tension across. By carefully heating and pressurizing the liquid trapped inside a wet gel past its critical point, then slowly venting off pressure while staying above the critical temperature, the fluid can be converted straight into a gas without ever passing back through an ordinary liquid state and without a liquid surface ever forming inside any of the gel's pores. There is nothing left to pull the delicate solid network inward as the fluid leaves, because at no point does a liquid-gas interface with surface tension exist inside the material at all. What remains, once all the fluid has been vented away, is essentially the original solid network, structurally intact, now filled with gas instead of liquid.

## What's Left Behind

The resulting material, most commonly made from a silica-based wet gel produced through ordinary sol-gel chemistry, is astonishing to hold precisely because of what the drying process preserved: a solid skeleton making up only a small fraction of the material's total volume, built from particles or strands only a few nanometers across, branching and connecting into a structure with enormous internal surface area packed into very little actual mass. The pores throughout that structure are themselves nanometer-scale, smaller than the wavelength of visible light in some dimensions, which is part of why aerogel scatters light with a faint blue cast reminiscent of the sky — a similar kind of scattering by structures much smaller than the light passing through them, though arising from a solid nanostructure rather than gas molecules.

That same nanoscale pore structure is responsible for the material's most useful property, exceptional thermal insulation, arguably better than almost any other solid material. Heat moving through an ordinary solid travels via a continuous path of touching atoms; aerogel's solid skeleton is so sparse and its connecting strands so thin and tortuous that this conductive path is about as interrupted as a solid material's can be. Heat moving through an ordinary trapped gas, meanwhile, relies on gas molecules bouncing off each other to relay energy along; aerogel's pores are frequently smaller than the average distance a gas molecule would otherwise travel before colliding with another molecule, which forces most of the molecules inside the material to collide with the solid pore walls instead of with each other, substantially suppressing ordinary gas-phase heat conduction as well. Blocked on both the solid conduction path and the gas conduction path at once, aerogel ends up insulating far better than its ingredients would suggest on their own.

## From Laboratory Curiosity to Specialty Material

Despite these remarkable properties, aerogel spent decades mostly as a laboratory novelty, and even now it remains a specialty material rather than an everyday one, for reasons that trace directly back to the same structure that makes it so effective. A network this sparse is also mechanically fragile — pure silica aerogel can crumble or shed dust under fairly modest handling, which limits where it can be used without some kind of protective housing or reinforcement. Supercritical drying also remains a relatively slow, energy-intensive, specialized process compared with simply letting something air-dry, which keeps manufacturing costs well above what a bulk insulation material like foam or fiberglass costs to produce.

Where its properties genuinely matter more than its cost or fragility, aerogel has found real use: thin insulating blankets and jackets marketed for extreme cold, insulation panels in construction and industrial piping where space is tight and performance matters more than price, and a memorable role in space exploration, where a block of aerogel was used to capture microscopic dust particles arriving at extremely high speed from a comet, the material's extreme sparseness allowing fast-moving particles to decelerate gradually through the depth of the block instead of vaporizing instantly on a denser, harder surface.

## Beyond Silica

Researchers have since extended the same basic sol-gel-and-supercritical-drying approach well beyond silica, producing aerogels from carbon, various metal oxides, and even polymers, each inheriting some version of the same essential trick: build a delicate solid network in liquid, then remove the liquid so carefully that the network never notices it's gone. Carbon aerogels conduct electricity in a way silica versions cannot, opening up uses in energy storage. Some newer polymer-based aerogels trade a little thermal performance for a considerable gain in flexibility and toughness, addressing the brittleness that has kept the original silica version out of anything that needs to survive being flexed or knocked around. The underlying idea, though, has not changed since a chemist decided to find out whether a jelly could keep its shape after its liquid was taken away — a question that turns out to open onto one of the strangest and most efficient insulating structures a laboratory has ever produced.
