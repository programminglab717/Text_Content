# Nuclear Fission and Fusion Compared

The sun and a nuclear reactor generate heat by doing exactly opposite things to atomic nuclei. Deep in the sun's core, immense pressure and heat force light atomic nuclei to merge into heavier ones, releasing energy in the process. Inside a reactor core, comparatively gentle conditions coax heavy atomic nuclei to split apart into lighter fragments, also releasing energy in the process. Fusion joins; fission divides. That the universe manages to extract usable energy from both directions of that ledger, splitting and combining, seems like it should be a contradiction. It isn't, and the reason it isn't reveals something elegant about how nuclear matter is held together in the first place.

## The Curve That Explains Both

Every atomic nucleus is held together by the strong nuclear force acting between its protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons, working against the electrical repulsion of all those positively charged protons packed into a tiny volume. The amount of energy that would be required to pull a nucleus completely apart into its separate, individual nucleons is called its binding energy, and dividing that figure by the number of nucleons gives a useful per-particle measure of how tightly, on average, a given nucleus is bound together.

Plot that measure against atomic mass across the periodic table and a distinctive shape emerges: binding energy per nucleon rises steeply for the lightest elements, climbs to a broad peak somewhere around iron and its close neighbors, and then declines slowly again through the heaviest natural elements like uranium. That single curve is the reason both fission and fusion can release energy despite pulling nuclei in opposite directions. Fusing light nuclei, from the low, rising left-hand side of the curve, into a heavier nucleus closer to the peak increases binding energy per nucleon, and that increase has to come from somewhere, released as usable energy. Splitting a heavy nucleus, from the gently declining right-hand side of the curve, into two medium-sized fragments closer to the peak also increases binding energy per nucleon, releasing energy by the same logic in reverse. Iron sits at the summit precisely because it is the one point on the curve where neither splitting nor joining offers any further energy to extract; every reaction on either side of it, fission on the heavy end or fusion on the light end, is rolling downhill toward that same peak.

## How a Reactor Splits Atoms on Purpose

Fission's practical usefulness rests on a further coincidence: certain heavy nuclei, notably a particular isotope of uranium, are not just capable of splitting but split readily when struck by a slow-moving neutron, and critically, each split releases a handful of new neutrons along with its energy. Physicists in the late 1930s, working through puzzling experimental results from bombarding uranium with neutrons, first showed that the uranium nucleus was breaking into smaller fragments, and it was quickly recognized that a released neutron from one splitting nucleus could go on to trigger the splitting of a neighboring nucleus, which would release further neutrons in turn.

That chain reaction is the entire operating principle of a nuclear reactor. Left completely unchecked, with every released neutron going on to trigger another fission, the reaction rate would escalate at explosive speed, which is exactly what happens, deliberately, in a nuclear weapon. A power reactor instead carefully manages the chain reaction to proceed at a steady, controlled pace, using control rods made of neutron-absorbing material that can be inserted or withdrawn to soak up just enough excess neutrons to hold the reaction rate level rather than letting it run away, alongside a moderator material, often ordinary water, that slows fast-moving neutrons down to the slower speeds at which they trigger further fission most efficiently. The heat released by all this controlled splitting boils water into steam, and the steam turns a turbine, which is a strangely mundane ending for a process that begins with splitting the nucleus of an atom.

## How a Star Fuses Atoms Under Its Own Weight

Fusion demands a far more hostile environment to get started than fission does, because two positively charged nuclei have to be forced close enough together for the short-range strong force to take over and bind them, and their mutual electrical repulsion fights that approach every step of the way. Only extreme temperature, meaning extreme average particle speed, gives nuclei enough kinetic energy to smash through that electrical barrier often enough for fusion to occur at any appreciable rate, which is why fusion happens readily only in environments as hot as stellar cores.

The sun fuses hydrogen into helium through a sequence of reaction steps known as the proton-proton chain, in which individual protons gradually combine, with a couple of intermediate transformations along the way, into a stable helium nucleus, releasing energy at each step. This proceeds at a pace that, atom for atom, is actually quite leisurely; the sun's staggering total energy output comes not from any single reaction being especially fast but from the sheer, almost incomprehensible number of nuclei undergoing fusion simultaneously throughout its enormous, dense core. The sun's own gravity does the job of confinement, compressing its core to the density and temperature fusion requires and holding it there for as long as fuel remains, which for a star like the sun is a very long time indeed.

## Why One Powers Cities and the Other Still Mostly Doesn't

Fission's practical head start over fusion comes down to a difference in what each process demands of an engineer rather than a physicist. A fission chain reaction, once you have enough of the right uranium isotope packed together, essentially wants to happen, and the entire engineering challenge is slowing it down and controlling it safely, a problem solved well enough decades ago that fission reactors have supplied a meaningful share of electricity in many countries ever since, alongside the unresolved difficulties of long-lived radioactive waste and the risk that reactor technology or fuel could be diverted toward weapons.

Fusion's challenge runs the opposite direction: getting hydrogen or its heavier isotopes to fuse at a useful rate requires recreating stellar core conditions, tens of millions of degrees at minimum, without a star's gravity available to hold the superheated material in place. Laboratory approaches instead use powerful magnetic fields, in doughnut-shaped devices called tokamaks, to trap the hot ionized gas, or plasma, away from any physical wall it would instantly destroy on contact, or alternatively use powerful lasers to compress a tiny fuel pellet so rapidly and symmetrically that it briefly reaches fusion conditions before it can fly apart. Both approaches have made steady, genuine progress, and in the early 2020s a laser-based experiment finally produced a fusion reaction that released more energy than the lasers delivered directly into the fuel, a long-sought milestone. That achievement is still a long way from a fusion power plant delivering electricity to a grid at a competitive cost, since the energy required to run the entire facility, not just the lasers striking the fuel, remains far larger than what any current experiment gets back.

The appeal that keeps fusion research funded despite the difficulty is easy to state even if the engineering is not: its fuel is abundant, extractable from ordinary water, its reactions produce no long-lived radioactive waste comparable to fission's, and there is no credible path from a fusion reactor to a weapon. Fission proved, within a few years of being discovered, that the energy locked inside a nucleus could be released on demand. Fusion has spent the decades since trying to prove that the same energy, gathered from the opposite direction, can be released on demand too, cheaply and safely enough to matter, rather than only inside stars and bombs.
