# The Double-Slit Experiment

In a darkened room in London in the early 1800s, a physician with a restless curiosity about light cut two narrow, closely spaced slits into an opaque screen and let a beam of sunlight pass through both at once. On a second screen placed some distance behind, Thomas Young expected, if light behaved the way most of his contemporaries assumed, to see two bright bands, one behind each slit, with darkness everywhere else. What he saw instead was a series of alternating bright and dark stripes spread across the screen, far more than two, fading gradually toward the edges. That striped pattern, unglamorous as it looks, did more to settle a long argument about the nature of light than any single result before or since, and more than a century later, when experimenters ran a version of the same setup using particles of matter instead of light, it would end up raising a stranger question than the one it had originally answered.

## A Wave Wins an Old Argument

By Young's time, the dominant view of light in English-speaking science leaned heavily on Isaac Newton's authority, which held that light consisted of a stream of tiny particles, or corpuscles, traveling in straight lines. Rival theories treating light as a wave, disturbing some medium the way ripples disturb a pond, had circulated on the European continent but struggled against Newton's reputation and a corpuscular model's easy success at explaining reflection and straight-line shadows.

Waves and particles behave very differently when you make two of them cross paths. Two streams of particles simply pass through each other, or collide and scatter, but they do not cancel each other out. Two overlapping water waves, by contrast, can either reinforce each other into a taller wave, where a crest from one aligns with a crest from the other, or cancel into flat stillness, where a crest from one lines up with a trough from the other. This canceling and reinforcing is called interference, and it is a signature that only waves produce.

Young's twin slits acted as two new sources of light waves, spreading outward from each slit like ripples from two stones dropped near each other in a pond. At some points on the far screen, a wave crest from one slit arrived exactly alongside a crest from the other slit, adding up to a bright band. At other points, a crest from one arrived alongside a trough from the other, canceling out to darkness. The striped pattern was interference, plain and unmistakable, and it strongly favored a wave picture of light over Newton's corpuscles. The wave theory of light, bolstered further later in the century when James Clerk Maxwell showed that light was an electromagnetic wave, held the field for the remainder of the nineteenth century.

## The Pattern That Should Not Have Come Back

Light's story got complicated again around the turn of the twentieth century, when phenomena like the photoelectric effect suggested light also delivered its energy in discrete packets, later called photons, behaving in some experiments distinctly like particles after all. That reopened the particle-versus-wave argument in a new and stranger form, this time with evidence solidly on both sides depending on which experiment you asked.

The plot thickened further when physicists began asking whether matter, ordinarily the poster child for "particle," might also carry some wave-like character. In the 1920s, Louis de Broglie proposed exactly that: every particle of matter, not just light, has an associated wavelength related to its momentum. Experiments soon backed him up. Beams of electrons fired at crystal targets produced diffraction patterns, the signature of wave behavior, closely matching what de Broglie's formula predicted, confirming that even something as solidly particle-like as an electron carries a wave nature.

Once that was established, an obvious question followed: what happens if you fire electrons, rather than light, through Young's two slits?

## Firing Them One at a Time

The result, reproduced many times since with steadily improving equipment, is the double-slit experiment's genuinely unsettling core. Fire a broad beam of electrons at two slits and, exactly as with light, an interference pattern of stripes builds up on the detector screen behind, direct evidence that electrons behave as waves passing through both slits simultaneously and interfering with themselves.

The strangeness intensifies when the beam is throttled down so far that only one electron passes through the apparatus at a time, with enough delay between each that there is no possibility of one electron interfering with another. Common sense suggests that a single, solitary electron ought to go through one slit or the other, landing on the detector as one small point, the way a thrown pebble goes through one open door or another. Instead, over many individually fired electrons arriving one at a time, minutes or hours apart, the accumulated pattern of dots on the screen slowly builds up into the same banded interference pattern seen with a full beam. Each electron, alone, somehow behaves as though it had passed through both slits at once and interfered with itself, contributing to a pattern that only makes sense as a wave phenomenon, even though each individual arrival registers as a single, particle-like point.

The standard interpretation, developed alongside the broader framework of quantum mechanics, describes each electron as governed by a wave of probability that genuinely does pass through both slits and interfere with itself, with the wave's intensity at any point on the screen determining the likelihood that the electron will be detected there. The particle does not settle on which slit it took until, in some sense, it is detected; before that, only the spread-out probability wave has a definite story.

## Watching Changes What Arrives

Physicists, understandably suspicious of a story this strange, tried the obvious next experiment: place a detector at the slits themselves, capable of registering which of the two slits each electron actually passes through, and see whether the interference pattern survives once the mystery of "which path" is resolved.

It does not. The moment a measurement determines which slit an electron went through, even in principle, the interference pattern vanishes, replaced by two simple bands, one behind each slit, exactly the naive particle prediction Young's experiment ruled out for light more than a century earlier. This holds regardless of how gently or cleverly the which-path measurement is made; the mere existence of information, somewhere in the universe, about which slit the electron used is enough to destroy the interference.

Niels Bohr folded this into his principle of complementarity: a quantum system can display wave-like behavior or particle-like behavior, but the experimental setup itself forces a choice about which question you are allowed to ask, and no single setup answers both at once. Later refinements of quantum theory explain the disappearance of interference in terms of decoherence, the way a measuring device becomes entangled with the particle and effectively erases the delicate overlap between the two paths that produced the interference in the first place. The mathematics of decoherence is precise even where the underlying interpretation of what is "really" happening to the electron between emission and detection remains a subject physicists still debate.

What Young's simple striped pattern started, in other words, never fully resolved into a tidy picture of what light or matter actually is at the smallest scales. It resolved instead into a set of extraordinarily reliable rules for predicting what will show up on a screen, bolted onto a philosophical puzzle about measurement and reality that the original nineteenth-century experiment could not have anticipated and that the twenty-first century has still not put to rest.
