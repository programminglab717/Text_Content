# The Discovery of Superconductivity

In a physics laboratory in Leiden in 1911, a strip of frozen mercury sat wired into a simple circuit, submerged in liquid helium cooled to within a few degrees of absolute zero, while a researcher watched a galvanometer needle that was supposed to show how the mercury's electrical resistance changed as the temperature crept lower. Resistance had been falling gradually as the mercury cooled, exactly as expected. Then, over a temperature range too narrow to properly measure with the equipment on hand, the needle dropped to a reading indistinguishable from zero. Not lower. Zero, or close enough to zero that repeated, careful measurement could not detect any resistance at all. Heike Kamerlingh Onnes, who ran the laboratory, checked his equipment for a fault, found none, and reluctantly accepted that mercury, cooled below a sharp threshold temperature, had stopped resisting the flow of electric current altogether.

## The Race to Absolute Zero

Kamerlingh Onnes had not set out looking for this. His laboratory had spent years pursuing a narrower, more mechanical goal: liquefying every gas that could be liquefied, pushing steadily toward the coldest temperatures achievable in a laboratory. Helium was the last holdout, resisting liquefaction until colder conditions than any other known gas required, and Kamerlingh Onnes's team finally succeeded in producing liquid helium a few years before the mercury experiment, opening up a previously inaccessible range of extremely low temperatures for any physicist willing to build the equipment to reach it.

Having the coldest laboratory in the world, he turned it toward an open theoretical question of the day: what happens to a metal's electrical resistance as its temperature approaches absolute zero? Resistance in an ordinary metal comes largely from electrons colliding with the vibrating atoms of the metal's crystal lattice as they try to flow through it, and since cooling a metal reduces how violently its atoms vibrate, resistance was known to decrease with falling temperature well before Kamerlingh Onnes's experiments. The open question was what happened at the very end of that trend, as temperature approached its absolute floor. One reasonable guess held that resistance would simply fade smoothly toward zero as atomic vibrations died away completely. Another camp, worried that electrons themselves might freeze into a fixed, immobile arrangement at extreme cold, guessed that resistance might instead rise sharply as the temperature bottomed out.

## An Abrupt Cliff, Not a Gentle Slope

The mercury result matched neither prediction. Instead of a smooth glide toward zero or a rise, resistance held roughly steady as the metal cooled, then plunged essentially instantaneously to an immeasurably small value once a specific, sharply defined temperature was crossed. This behavior looked much less like ordinary cooling and much more like a phase transition, the same category of abrupt qualitative change seen when water freezes into ice at a precise temperature rather than gradually thickening into solid form over a wide range. Kamerlingh Onnes called the new state superconductivity, and the sharp temperature at which a given material dropped into it became known as its critical temperature.

Other metals soon showed the same behavior, each with its own characteristic critical temperature, generally only a handful of degrees above absolute zero, cold enough that superconductivity remained for decades a laboratory curiosity rather than a practical technology, since maintaining liquid helium temperatures outside a specialized lab was neither cheap nor simple.

## Currents That Refuse to Stop, and Magnetic Fields That Get Expelled

Zero resistance implies a startling practical consequence: a current, once started flowing around a closed loop of superconducting wire, should keep flowing indefinitely with no battery or power source needed to sustain it, since there is no resistance dissipating its energy away as heat. Experiments confirmed exactly this. Currents induced in superconducting rings persisted for as long as researchers were willing to keep watching, with no detectable decay, behavior utterly unlike any ordinary circuit, where resistance bleeds current away as heat within moments of removing a power source.

A second, subtler signature of superconductivity emerged more than two decades later, when Walther Meissner and Robert Ochsenfeld discovered that a superconducting material does not merely resist an applied magnetic field passing through it; it actively expels any magnetic field from its interior as it crosses the critical temperature, a phenomenon now called the Meissner effect. This distinction mattered enormously to theorists, because a hypothetical material that was merely a perfect conductor, with zero resistance but otherwise behaving like ordinary metal, would be expected to trap whatever magnetic field happened to be present at the moment it became resistance-free, rather than pushing an existing field back out. Superconductivity, the Meissner effect showed, was not simply "resistance turned off." It was a genuinely distinct state of matter with its own rules, one dramatic enough that a small, sufficiently strong magnet can be made to hover, stably levitating, above a cooled superconducting disk, suspended by the very field it is being pushed out of.

## Explaining the Unexplainable

For nearly half a century after Kamerlingh Onnes's discovery, superconductivity resisted theoretical explanation, a genuinely embarrassing gap given how precisely it could be measured. The eventual answer, worked out by John Bardeen, Leon Cooper, and John Robert Schrieffer in 1957 and now known by their initials as BCS theory, required an idea that seemed backward at first glance: electrons, which repel each other directly since they carry the same electric charge, could nonetheless form loosely bound pairs, called Cooper pairs, through an indirect attraction mediated by the surrounding crystal lattice. One electron passing through the lattice momentarily tugs nearby positively charged atoms slightly out of position, and a second electron passing shortly afterward feels a faint attraction toward that distorted, slightly positive wake before the lattice springs back into place.

Individually, this attraction is far too weak to matter at ordinary temperatures, where thermal jostling overwhelms it easily. Cooled low enough, however, vast numbers of these loosely paired electrons condense into a single collective quantum state, all moving in coordinated lockstep through the material. Scattering a single electron out of this collective flow, the microscopic event that produces ordinary resistance, would require disrupting the entire coordinated state at once rather than deflecting one particle in isolation, and below the critical temperature there simply is not enough random thermal energy available to do it. The current flows, effectively, as a single coherent quantum entity too well organized for the lattice to knock off course.

## Chasing Warmer Superconductors

BCS theory explained the superconductors known at the time, all of which required cooling with expensive liquid helium, but it also predicted, discouragingly, an upper limit on how high a critical temperature any such conventional superconductor could plausibly reach. That ceiling made a 1986 discovery by Georg Bednorz and Karl Müller all the more startling: a brittle ceramic compound built from copper, oxygen, and other elements, a class of material nobody expected to conduct electricity well at all, turned out to become superconducting at a temperature well above what BCS theory seemed to allow. Within about a year, related ceramic compounds pushed the critical temperature high enough to be reached using liquid nitrogen instead of liquid helium, a far cheaper and more practical coolant, opening the door to applications that had previously been economically unthinkable.

What causes superconductivity in these high-temperature ceramic materials is still not settled the way BCS theory settled the original, low-temperature case; electron pairing appears to be involved again, but the mechanism binding the pairs together resists the simple lattice-vibration story that worked for mercury and its conventional cousins. Meanwhile, more conventional superconductors, humbler and better understood, already do serious work in the world: the powerful, stable magnetic fields inside an MRI scanner, the steering and focusing magnets that bend particle beams around accelerator rings, and experimental magnetically levitated trains all lean on wires that, cooled sufficiently, simply refuse to resist.
