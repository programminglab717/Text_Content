# Pompeii: A Town Under the Ash

Nobody in Pompeii knew they were living under a volcano. Vesuvius was a mountain with good soil on its flanks, vines to the treeline and woodland above; the Greek geographer Strabo had noticed its burnt-looking rocks and wondered, but the connection was not common knowledge, and no eruption lay within living memory or local legend. When the ground shook hard enough to crack the town's buildings some seventeen years before the end, the damage was repaired as earthquake damage, which is what it was. Houses were still being rebuilt when the mountain opened.

The town it destroyed was a provincial place of perhaps ten or twelve thousand people, old enough to have layers. Its earliest street grid is pre-Roman, laid out by Oscan speakers; Greeks and Etruscans passed through it; the Samnites held it and gave it its finest houses; and after it backed the wrong side in the Italian war against Rome it was besieged, taken, and settled with Roman veterans as a colony. The mixture shows in the archaeology and in the language of its inscriptions. It sat above the mouth of the Sarno with a harbour, exported wine from the volcanic slopes, and made a great deal of money from fermented fish sauce, the pungent condiment poured over half of Roman cooking, which Pompeian producers shipped in labelled jars across the western Mediterranean.

## Two Days in Autumn

The eruption is one of the few natural disasters of antiquity described by someone who watched it. Pliny the Younger, a teenager staying across the bay at Misenum, wrote two letters about it decades later at the request of Tacitus, who was working on his history. His uncle, the encyclopaedist Pliny the Elder, commanded the fleet there, watched the cloud rise over the bay, and set out by ship — first out of curiosity, then to take people off the shore. He never came back; he died on the beach at Stabiae, apparently overcome while waiting for the wind to change.

The nephew's description of the cloud is the reason volcanologists now call this kind of eruption Plinian. He compared it to an umbrella pine, a single trunk rising to a great height and then spreading into branches, pale in places and in others stained dark with earth and cinders. The physics behind the shape is a column of gas and fragmented magma rising by its own buoyancy until it reaches a level where it can rise no further and flattens out.

What fell on Pompeii first was pumice, light stones of frothy volcanic glass, and it fell for most of a day — deep enough by the end to bury doorways and, more lethally, to load roofs until they gave way. People who left in the first hours with a pillow tied over their head, as Pliny describes the crowds at Misenum doing, mostly survived. People who sheltered indoors and waited it out were making a reasonable decision that turned out to be fatal, because the second phase of the eruption was of a different kind altogether. When the column faltered and collapsed, it sent avalanches of hot gas and ash racing down the mountain at ground level. Those surges reached Herculaneum first, in the night, and Pompeii in the early morning. They killed instantly by heat and they buried what they killed.

The date everyone learns, the twenty-fourth of August, comes from manuscripts of Pliny's letter, and there is now a serious case against it. Fruit and nuts found in the town suggest autumn; braziers had been set out; the victims' clothing looks heavy for high summer; and a charcoal inscription uncovered in a house under excavation appears to record a date in October. The argument is not settled, but the season has quietly shifted for most people who work there.

## What Ash Keeps

Volcanic burial is a strange kind of preservation. It is not gentle — roofs fell, upper storeys were sheared off, and the surges scoured and flattened — but it is fast and it is dry, and it seals what is left from air, water and scavengers.

So Pompeii holds the things that never survive elsewhere. Loaves of bread carbonised in the oven, scored into the wedges Italians still divide flat loaves into. Counters in fast-food shops with big jars sunk into the masonry, one of them recently excavated with a painted frieze of the animals on the menu. Bakeries with hourglass-shaped mills of hard volcanic stone that a blindfolded donkey walked around all morning. Wax tablets recording a banker's loans. A gladiator barracks. Latrines, lead pipes, taps, a water tower where the aqueduct's flow was divided between fountains, baths and private houses, with the public fountains deliberately plumbed to be the last to fail.

And the writing. Pompeians wrote on their walls constantly. Professional sign-painters covered street frontages with election notices in red — so-and-so's supporters urge you to vote for him, sometimes naming the trade guild or the neighbours or the women of the household who backed the candidate, though women could not vote themselves. Below and between them, scratched into the plaster, is everything else: tallies, insults, prices, declarations of love, lines of poetry misremembered, obscenities, a schoolboy's alphabet. One scratched message marvels at a wall for not having collapsed under the weight of so much tedious scribbling. There is nothing else like it from the ancient world, because everywhere else the plaster was washed, repainted or eventually fell off.

## Digging, Badly and Then Well

Herculaneum was found first, in the early eighteenth century, when a well-digger struck marble and a Bourbon prince turned the discovery into a mining operation, driving tunnels through the rock-hard deposit to pull out statues for the royal collection. Pompeii, easier to dig, was opened a few decades later, and for a century it was treated as a quarry for objects rather than as a place. Paintings were cut out of walls and the rest left to weather. Trenches were dug where finds seemed likely and backfilled when they were not. Winckelmann, the founder of classical art history, visited and was scathing about the methods.

The change came with Giuseppe Fiorelli, who took charge in the eighteen-sixties and imposed order that still governs the site. He divided the town into numbered regions, blocks and doorways, so that any object could be tied to an address. He stopped the tunnelling and began removing the deposit from the top down, so that upper walls and roofs were recorded as they emerged instead of being smashed from below. And he understood what the cavities in the hardened ash were.

A body buried in fine ash rots away and leaves a void with the shape of the body pressed into the surrounding material — the folds of clothing, the hair, the soles of the feet. Fiorelli had workmen pour liquid plaster into such voids and then chip away the matrix, producing casts of the dead in the positions in which they died. The results are the most disquieting objects in archaeology: a man propped on one elbow, a family together, a dog on a chain twisted onto its back. The technique was later extended to voids left by wooden doors, shutters, furniture and tree roots, and the roots have let excavators replant Roman gardens with something like the original layout.

## Keeping It

An excavated ruin is a ruin with the lid taken off. Everything at Pompeii has been deteriorating faster since it was uncovered than it did in the previous eighteen centuries, and the site's modern history is largely a history of that fight. Rain gets into wall tops. Sun fades the frescoes that survived precisely because they were in the dark. Early restorations used cement and iron, and the iron has rusted and split the masonry it was meant to hold. Allied bombing in 1943, aimed at German positions in the area, hit the site repeatedly. In 2010 a building that had housed gladiatorial equipment collapsed overnight after heavy rain, which produced enough political embarrassment to fund a major, and by most accounts successful, programme of drainage, roofing and consolidation.

Roughly a third of the town has never been dug at all, and the current policy is to leave it that way. The reasoning is straightforward: excavation is destruction that cannot be undone, the site already has more standing structure than it can maintain, and future archaeologists will have techniques nobody has thought of yet. Recent work in the unexcavated zone has been driven mostly by the need to stabilise the ragged edge where old digging stopped — which is how the charcoal date inscription turned up, and a fresco of a flatbread that half the internet decided was a pizza.

What makes the place matter is not the disaster. It is that Roman history was written almost entirely by and about the top of the social pyramid, and here the pyramid is preserved sideways: the banker's records and the barmaid's graffiti, the senator's garden and the mill where the donkey walked, all sealed on the same morning.
