# What the Hoplite Carried

Start with the shield, because everything else follows from it. The aspis was
round, about the width of a man's outstretched arms from elbow to elbow, and
dished like a shallow bowl. Its core was wood, usually poplar or willow,
carved from a single piece or glued up from planks, faced on the outside with a
thin sheet of bronze and lined inside with leather. It weighed something on the
order of seven kilograms, which is a great deal to hold at arm's length.

So it was not held at arm's length. Inside the bowl were two fittings: a bronze
armband at the center, the porpax, through which the forearm passed, and a
leather grip near the rim, the antilabe, held in the hand. The weight therefore
rested along the whole forearm rather than in the wrist, and the concave rim
could be propped on the shoulder during the long waits before contact. The
consequence of that arrangement is the central fact of Greek warfare for three
centuries. A shield gripped at its center can be swung freely to cover a man on
any side. A shield strapped to the forearm cannot. It covers its bearer's left
side and, by geometry, the exposed right side of whoever stands next to him.
The aspis is a piece of equipment that becomes useful only when men stand
shoulder to shoulder, and it is close to useless for a man fighting alone.

## The Rest of the Panoply

The primary weapon was the dory, a thrusting spear of ash roughly two and a
half meters long with an iron head at one end and a bronze spike at the other.
The spike served as a counterweight, let the spear be planted upright in the
ground, and gave the rear ranks something to finish a fallen enemy with as the
line moved over him. It also meant a man whose shaft snapped — a common enough
event — could reverse what was left and keep fighting. Beyond that he carried a
short iron sword, either a straight double-edged xiphos or the heavy
forward-curving kopis, and both were weapons of last resort.

Armor varied more than the schoolbook picture suggests. The Corinthian helmet,
beaten from a single sheet of bronze with cheekpieces and a narrow nasal, gave
superb protection and made its wearer nearly deaf and severely tunnel-visioned;
men wore it pushed back on the crown of the head until the moment of contact.
Later generations preferred open designs that let a man hear an order. Body
armor began as a bronze bell corslet and shifted, over the fifth century,
toward a lighter composite of glued linen layers, sometimes scaled, which was
cooler and cheaper. Bronze greaves clipped onto the shins by their own springiness.

All of it was private property. There was no state armory and no uniform, and a
man equipped himself, which put hoplite service squarely inside the census
class that could afford a panoply — farmers with land enough to produce a
surplus, not the poorest citizens who rowed in the fleet and not only the
aristocracy. Equipment was inherited, repaired, captured, and handed down.
Shield blazons were sometimes civic, like the Theban club or the Spartan lambda,
and sometimes purely personal, a gorgon or a bull or a family joke.

## Eight Deep

A phalanx was a rectangle. Depth of eight ranks was a normal default, though
commanders varied it freely; the Thebans in particular took to stacking far
deeper on one wing. The best and steadiest men stood in the front rank and at
the rear, the front because they did the fighting and the rear because their
job was partly to prevent the ranks ahead from leaking backward.

Two things reliably happened when two such rectangles approached each other.
The first is that each line drifted to its right as it advanced, because every
man edged toward the shield of the neighbor on his right, seeking cover for his
own exposed side. Thucydides noticed this and explained it exactly that way.
The practical result was that the right wing of each army tended to overlap and
defeat the enemy's left, so that battles were often won by whichever side could
wheel its victorious right inward before its own left collapsed. The second is
that the last stretch of the approach was frequently made at a run, to cross
the zone where arrows and javelins fell and to arrive with momentum.

What happened at contact is one of the longest-running arguments in ancient
history. One reading takes the word othismos — shoving — literally: the front
ranks locked shields, the rear ranks put their weight into the backs of the men
ahead, and the battle became a mass push, decided when one side's cohesion
broke and it was physically driven backward. The other reading points out that
a man crushed between a hundred kilos of pressure and an enemy spearpoint can
do very little fighting, that the sources describe spear-work and wounds
consistent with individual combat, and that othismos in Greek is also used
metaphorically for any hard-pressed struggle. The honest position is that both
kinds of moment probably occurred, at different points along a line and at
different stages of an engagement.

## The Shape of a Battle

Sources that describe the experience rather than the outcome agree on a few
details. Armies sang a paean as they advanced, which kept the step and gave men
something to do with their fear. Wine was issued beforehand. The dust raised by
thousands of feet on dry summer ground blotted out everything beyond a few
ranks, so that a hoplite generally had no idea how the battle was going
anywhere except directly in front of him, and commanders in the front rank knew
barely more. Men fouled themselves; the sources say so without embarrassment.
The noise of bronze on bronze was reportedly audible at a considerable
distance.

Most hoplite battles were short. Once one side gave way the formation
dissolved, and men who turned to run threw away their shields first because a
shield is the heaviest thing you carry and the one thing you cannot run with.
That is why losing a shield was the specific mark of cowardice in Greek
insult. Casualties fell overwhelmingly on the defeated, and even then they were
not catastrophic by later standards; heavy infantry pursuing heavy infantry
cannot pursue far, and cavalry was scarce in most Greek armies.

The aftermath followed an understood ritual. The winners set up a trophy — a
captured panoply hung on a post at the spot where the enemy first turned — and
the losers sent a herald to ask for their dead. Asking implied defeat, and
granting the request was obligatory. The whole apparatus of agreed flat ground,
short campaigns timed around the harvest, and a single decisive afternoon made
war between neighboring city-states into something expensive but survivable.

That framework has fed a durable theory: that the phalanx was the military
expression of a rising class of independent small farmers, that it made them
politically indispensable, and that hoplite warfare and citizen self-government
grew together. The theory is attractive and probably half true. It has also
been picked apart. Early hoplite equipment appears in aristocratic graves
before it appears anywhere else, adoption was gradual rather than revolutionary,
and no ancient source describes the constitutional bargain the theory requires.

## How the Formation Lost Its Monopoly

The phalanx was superb at exactly one thing and mediocre at everything else. It
could not hold broken ground, could not turn a flank quickly, and was helpless
against an enemy who refused to close. It was also an instrument for a
single afternoon, not a campaign. Armies carried little and stayed in the field
for weeks rather than seasons, because the men in the ranks had farms that
needed them. Against a city willing to shut its gates, a phalanx could do
nothing but trample and burn the crops outside the walls and hope the insult
provoked a fight — and when Athens simply declined the invitation year after
year during the Peloponnesian War, sheltering behind its fortifications and
feeding itself by sea, the whole logic of decisive infantry battle stopped
working. Light troops learned this. An Athenian
commander named Iphicrates famously destroyed a Spartan regiment with javelin
troops who withdrew whenever the hoplites charged and returned the moment they
stopped, and the lesson circulated fast. The professional mercenary — the sort
of man who marched into Mesopotamia with Cyrus and walked back out again with
Xenophon — became a fixture, and the amateur farmer-soldier lost his monopoly
on the battlefield.

Then came two battles that ended the era. At Leuctra the Theban commander
Epaminondas massed his left wing to an absurd depth, led with it rather than
with his right, held the rest of his line back, and broke the Spartan king's
wing before the weaker parts of his own army were engaged. It was the first
clear demonstration that the rectangle could be used asymmetrically as an
instrument rather than merely aligned and released. A generation later, Philip
of Macedon took the idea further with a pike five meters long held in both
hands, a smaller shield slung from the neck, and heavy cavalry trained to
exploit the gap that the pikes opened. At Chaeronea the Greek city-states met
that combination and lost.

What survives of all this is mostly the shield. In the Agora at Athens
archaeologists found a battered bronze shield facing, punched and torn,
inscribed to say that the Athenians took it from the Lacedaemonians at Pylos.
It was one of the shields surrendered by the Spartiates trapped on Sphacteria —
men who, everyone had assumed, did not surrender. The Athenians nailed it up
where the whole city could walk past it.
