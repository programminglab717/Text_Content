# The Roman Legion

Start with the load. A legionary on the march carried his mail shirt, helmet, shield, sword, dagger and two javelins, and on a forked pole over his shoulder he carried a cloak, a cooking pot, a mess tin, a saw, a basket, a length of rope, a turf-cutter, a pickaxe or stake, and several days of grain that he would grind and bake himself. Soldiers called themselves Marius's mules, after the general said to have made them carry it rather than trail a wagon train. Whether or not he did, the joke describes the institution accurately: the Roman army's advantage over its enemies was rarely a matter of individual ferocity. It was that a legion could arrive somewhere inconvenient, dig itself in by nightfall, and still be there in the spring.

## From Phalanx to Chessboard

The earliest Roman army was a phalanx on the Greek model, a block of propertied citizens with spears and round shields, fighting as a single dense mass. Massed spearmen work well on flat ground against similar formations and badly against almost everything else, and the long, grinding wars in the hills of central Italy taught Rome that lesson repeatedly.

What emerged was the manipular legion, and it is one of the few genuinely original tactical ideas of the ancient world. Instead of one line the legion formed three, each made of small self-contained blocks called maniples, arranged with gaps like the dark squares on a chessboard, the blocks of the second line covering the gaps in the first. The youngest and poorest men, lightly equipped skirmishers, screened the front and then filtered back through the gaps. Behind them stood the hastati, young men in their first campaigns; behind those the principes, men in their prime; and at the rear the triarii, veterans who still fought with the long spear and who knelt behind their shields waiting. Romans had a proverb for a hopeless situation: it has come down to the triarii.

The gaps did the work. A line with intervals could advance over broken ground without dissolving, absorb a charge that had spent itself on the front rank, and replace tired men with fresh ones mid-battle — something a phalanx simply cannot do. Against Hannibal it was not enough, because Hannibal was better at everything else, but against the Macedonian successor kingdoms it was decisive. A phalanx that lost its cohesion on uneven ground was finished, and Roman maniples were designed to find exactly that seam and push into it.

## The Cohort Legion

By the last century of the Republic the maniple had been superseded by the cohort: roughly 480 men, ten cohorts to a legion, each cohort made of six centuries of about eighty. The property classes that had defined the old battle lines were gone, along with the requirement that a soldier equip himself. The state now supplied the kit and the pay, and recruited from citizens who had no land to go back to. That change, conventionally credited to Marius and probably spread over a longer period than the story allows, altered Roman politics more than Roman tactics. A soldier who owned a farm wanted the campaign over. A soldier who owned nothing wanted a general who could get him land at discharge, and would follow that general a long way to get it.

The cohort was large enough to hold a piece of line on its own and small enough to be manoeuvred, and it made the legion modular. Commanders detached cohorts to hold a bridge, garrison a town, or form a reserve line at an angle to the main front, as Caesar did at Pharsalus against Pompey's cavalry. The first cohort, at double strength, contained the senior centurions and stood on the right of the line beside the eagle.

The eagle mattered enormously. Each legion had one, a gilded standard in the care of a senior soldier, kept in a shrine in the camp headquarters and carried into battle; losing it was the worst disgrace available, and recovering eagles lost decades earlier in Parthia was treated as a triumph in its own right. Individual centuries had their own standards too, which were practical as much as sentimental: in dust and noise, with no reliable way to shout orders across a battlefield, men found their place by looking for a pole.

## Kit Built for One Kind of Fight

Roman equipment was specialised and slightly odd, and it only makes sense as a system.

The javelin, the pilum, had a small pyramidal head on a long thin iron shank. Thrown at short range it punched through a shield, and the thin shank then bent or the wooden shaft worked loose, so the weapon could not be thrown back and the shield it was lodged in became an encumbrance the enemy usually had to drop. A man with no shield facing a Roman line was in serious trouble.

That trouble was the short sword. The gladius, adopted from Spanish models, was a stabbing weapon with a lethal point and a blade barely long enough to be called a sword by the standards of Rome's Gallic enemies. Worn on the right hip, drawn without raising the shield arm, it was made for a man pressed shoulder to shoulder in a line who would step in behind his shield boss and put six inches of iron into an abdomen. The shield, a curved rectangle of laminated wood faced with hide and linen and rimmed in metal, was itself a weapon: a legionary punched with the heavy iron boss to open a gap, then stabbed.

Body armour changed over the centuries — mail first, then in the early imperial period the articulated iron plate armour familiar from Trajan's Column, then mail again — while helmets acquired ever-deeper neck guards and broad cheekpieces, protection against downward cuts from above. The hobnailed boots were the least glamorous and most important item, because the campaign was mostly walking.

## Eighty Men and a Vine Stick

The unit a soldier actually lived in was the century, and within it the tent party: eight men who shared a leather tent, a mule, a hand quern and a cooking pot, who dug their stretch of ditch together and were punished together. Armies since have rediscovered the same arithmetic. Loyalty operates at the scale of a squad.

The century's commander was a centurion, promoted from the ranks and identifiable by a transverse crest and by the vine stick he carried and used. Centurions were graded in a strict order of seniority within the legion, culminating in the first spear, the senior centurion of the first cohort, a man typically in his forties or fifties who had spent his entire adult life in the army and whose advice the aristocratic officers above him ignored at their peril. Beneath the centurion an optio brought up the rear of the century and kept the men from drifting backwards, a standard-bearer carried the signum, a watch officer distributed the nightly password on a wooden tablet, and a hornblower relayed signals.

The officers above this level were politicians. A legion under the emperors was commanded by a senator, with six tribunes, one of them a young senator getting his first taste of the army and five from the equestrian order. The one professional in that group was the camp prefect, usually a former first spear, who ran the machine.

Alongside the legions, and eventually as numerous, were the auxiliaries: non-citizen units raised in the provinces, supplying nearly all the cavalry, the archers, the slingers and much of the light infantry the legions lacked. They served long terms and received citizenship at discharge, which made the army one of the great engines of Romanisation. A Batavian or a Syrian who survived his service handed his sons a different legal status than his own.

## Digging In

Every night on campaign, at the end of a day's march, a Roman army built a fort. Surveyors went ahead and staked out the same plan it always used: a rectangle, a ditch and turf rampart topped with the stakes the men had carried, gates on each side, two main streets crossing at the headquarters, the units always in the same relative positions. A soldier woken in the dark knew the way to his post without thinking.

The effort was enormous and it paid for itself constantly. An army that fortifies every night cannot be surprised, cannot be forced to fight before it chooses, and can retreat into its own camp in the middle of a bad day. The same skills produced the siege works around Alesia, where Caesar's men built a double ring of fortifications facing inwards and outwards at once; the bridge thrown over the Rhine in a few days for no strategic reason other than to demonstrate that it could be done; and, over centuries, a great many roads, canals, aqueducts and city walls. Josephus, who watched the legions work in Judaea from the losing side, remarked that their drills were bloodless battles and their battles bloody drills.

Service under the emperors ran to twenty-five years, ending in a discharge payment or a plot of land, funded from a treasury Augustus established for the purpose because he understood exactly where the danger lay. Soldiers were forbidden to marry for most of that period, a rule they ignored in practice, raising families in the civilian settlements that grew outside every permanent fortress and that in several cases grew into cities still inhabited today.

The legion's real strength was never its equipment, which its enemies could copy and sometimes improve on. It was replaceability. Centurions were interchangeable, cohorts were interchangeable, the camp was the same camp in Scotland and in Syria, and a legion that lost most of its men could be rebuilt around the survivors and the eagle in a season. Rome lost battles routinely, including some of the worst defeats suffered by any ancient state. It very rarely lost the following year.
