# Sparta and the Spartan Mirage

Thucydides made a prediction about ruins. If Sparta were ever deserted, he
wrote, and a future visitor saw only the foundations, he would refuse to
believe the place had been as powerful as it was said to be — whereas the same
visitor at Athens would guess the city twice as strong as it really was. He was
right about the stones. Sparta was not a city in the Greek sense at all: five
unwalled villages strung along the Eurotas under Mount Taygetus, with no
monumental gateway, no fortification circuit for most of its history, and
public buildings a visitor from Corinth would have found embarrassing.

What Thucydides did not anticipate is that posterity would be misled in the
opposite direction, and not by the ruins. Almost nothing written by a Spartan
about Sparta survives. Everything we have comes from outsiders, and the most
detailed accounts come from men who were not merely outsiders but enthusiasts.
Xenophon the Athenian sent his own sons to be educated there and wrote an
admiring treatise on the Spartan constitution. Plutarch, writing perhaps five
hundred years after the system he describes was already dead, assembled the
biography of a lawgiver, Lycurgus, whose dates he could not establish and whose
existence modern scholars widely doubt. Even hostile witnesses were working
from a legend. Aristotle criticized Sparta sharply, but he criticized the
Sparta that Greek political theory had already constructed.

Scholars call the resulting distortion the Spartan mirage: a system described
as if it were fixed, ancient, and designed all at once, when it was in fact
assembled over generations, changed considerably, and was already
half-fictional to the Greeks who wrote it down. Roman-era Sparta made the
problem worse by leaning into it, restaging archaic-sounding rituals for
tourists, including a brutal flogging contest at the sanctuary of Artemis
Orthia that drew spectators from across the empire and eventually acquired
seating.

## The Arrangement That Actually Existed

Strip away the legend and a real and very unusual arrangement remains. Full
Spartan citizens called themselves the Homoioi, the Similars, and their
similarity was enforced materially. Each held an allotment of land that he did
not farm. Farming was done by helots, an enserfed population bound to the soil,
who handed over a fixed portion of the produce and kept the rest — an
arrangement that, unlike chattel slavery elsewhere in Greece, let helot
families persist across generations with their own communities, marriages, and
memory.

Helots were not the only people doing the work. The Laconian towns of the
surrounding hills and coast were inhabited by the perioikoi, the dwellers
around: free men, self-governing in local matters, who had no vote in Sparta
and no say in its foreign policy but who owed military service and made
virtually everything the Spartiates used. Somebody had to forge the spearheads,
beat out the bronze, cut the timber, weave the famous red cloaks, and trade
with the outside world for what Laconia did not produce. A citizen body
forbidden to engage in any craft or commerce could only exist on top of a class
that did, and the perioikoi are the least discussed and most necessary layer of
the whole structure.

Because he did not work, the Spartiate could do something no other Greek could:
train, full time, for his whole adult life. He ate his evening meal not at home
but in a mess of perhaps fifteen men, to which he contributed a fixed monthly
quota of barley, wine, cheese, figs, and a little money for relish. The
contribution was not symbolic. A man who could not meet it lost his place in
the mess, and a man without a mess was no longer a citizen. The most
egalitarian-looking institution in Sparta was therefore also a property
qualification with a sharp edge.

Boys left home at seven for the public upbringing, the agoge, which ran them in
age-cohorts through years of deliberate hunger, minimal clothing, competitive
violence, and instruction in obedience delivered largely by slightly older
boys. Theft of food was encouraged and punished if detected, the point being
detection rather than honesty. Spartans did not use coined money, keeping
instead to iron bars that were worthless outside Laconia — a policy usually
presented as a guard against luxury, and equally effective as a guard against
Spartiates accumulating private wealth that could buy influence.

What all of this bought was drill. Other Greek armies were assemblies of brave
amateurs; the Spartan army was subdivided into small units with a chain of
command running down to squads of a few dozen men, each with an officer who
could receive an order and act on it. That is why Spartans could change
formation under pressure, wheel, and countermarch while everyone else could
only advance. They went forward slowly and in step to the sound of pipes rather
than at a run, which other Greeks found unnerving, and before a battle they
combed out their long hair with some care, on the grounds that a man about to
die should look well.

## Standing on a Volcano

None of this makes sense until you count the helots. In the eighth and seventh
centuries Sparta conquered Messenia, the fertile region on the far side of
Taygetus, and enslaved its population wholesale rather than absorbing or
expelling it. That decision solved the land problem permanently and created a
subject population that outnumbered its masters by a wide margin — and, unlike
slaves bought individually from a dozen different peoples, the Messenian helots
spoke one language, occupied one territory, and remembered being free.

Sparta's entire strange apparatus reads differently once you see it as an
occupation. The ephors were said to declare war on the helots formally each
year, so that killing one carried no religious pollution. The krypteia sent
selected young men out into the countryside to live rough and murder helots
found abroad at night, singling out the strong ones. Thucydides records that
the Spartans once invited helots who claimed to have served bravest in war to
put themselves forward for liberation; about two thousand did, were garlanded
and paraded round the temples, and then disappeared, and no one ever learned
how each of them died. The manoeuvre was designed to identify exactly the men
most likely to lead a rising.

The Spartans also lived with the knowledge that this could fail. When a severe
earthquake struck in the middle of the fifth century, the Messenians rose, and
Sparta was fighting on its own territory for years. Spartan foreign policy —
the reluctance to campaign far from home, the caution that infuriated allies,
the refusal to send a full army abroad for long — was not temperament. It was a
garrison commander's arithmetic.

## Two Kings and Five Overseers

The constitution was genuinely odd, and other Greeks found it fascinating
because it seemed to mix monarchy, oligarchy, and democracy in a single
structure. There were two hereditary kings from two separate royal houses, who
commanded armies, performed the major sacrifices, and watched each other. A
council of elders, the gerousia, held twenty-eight members elected for life
from men over sixty, plus the two kings, and prepared business. And five
ephors, ordinary citizens elected annually and barred from repeating, held
extraordinary powers: they could arrest a king, prosecute one, and did.

The assembly of citizens voted, but it voted by shouting, with officials in a
nearby building judging which roar was louder. Aristotle called the procedure
childish. It is also almost impossible to audit, which may have been the point,
and there was a standing provision allowing the elders to set aside a decision
the people had made "crookedly."

Spartan women lived under rules that scandalized other Greeks. Girls exercised
in public, ate better than girls elsewhere, and married in their late teens
rather than at puberty. Women could inherit and control land, and because male
citizens died in war while property passed through daughters, holdings
concentrated over time in female hands — Aristotle complained that a startling
proportion of Spartan territory had ended up owned by women, and treated this
as a flaw in the design rather than a consequence of it.

## The Numbers Ran Out

Sparta's decline is one of the clearest cases in ancient history of a system
failing on its own terms. The Spartiate body shrank, generation after
generation. Herodotus could speak of eight thousand Spartiate men in the
generation that fought the Persians; by the early fourth century the number of
full citizens was a small fraction of that. Land concentrated in fewer hands as
heiresses married heirs and families died out, and every man who dropped below
the mess contribution dropped out of the citizen body permanently. The Greeks
had a word for it: oliganthropia, a shortage of men.

Reputation masked the problem for a while, and then stopped. At Pylos an
Athenian force cut off a Spartan detachment on the island of Sphacteria, and
after weeks of siege the survivors — including a substantial number of
Spartiates — surrendered instead of dying. The shock ran through Greece. A
generation later, having won the Peloponnesian War and acquired an empire that
flooded the city with exactly the coined wealth its rules were built to
exclude, Sparta met a Theban army at Leuctra and lost, along with hundreds of
the few remaining citizens it could not replace.

Epaminondas then did the thing no invader had ever done. He marched into
Laconia, and rather than sacking the villages he crossed into Messenia, freed
the helots, and built them a walled city with a citizen body and a constitution
of their own. Sparta lost, in a single campaigning season, the agricultural
base that had paid for three centuries of full-time soldiering. It never got
Messenia back, never recovered its numbers, and spent the following centuries
as a second-rank state increasingly in the business of performing its own past
for visitors who had read about it.
