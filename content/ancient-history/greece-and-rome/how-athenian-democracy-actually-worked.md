# How Athenian Democracy Actually Worked

Among the objects dug out of the Athenian Agora are broken slabs of stone cut
with rows of narrow slots, arranged in columns, with a bronze tube fixed down
one side. These are kleroteria — allotment machines. A citizen reporting for
jury duty carried a small bronze or boxwood ticket stamped with his name, his
father's name, and his deme, and that ticket was slotted into one of the
columns. Black and white balls were then poured into the tube and released one
at a time. A white ball meant that the entire row of tickets beside it was
selected; a black ball meant that row went home. The machine existed for one
reason: to make the composition of a jury impossible for anyone, including the
officials running the draw, to arrange in advance.

That instinct explains most of what is strange about Athenian democracy to
anyone raised on the modern elected kind. The Athenians did not think of
elections as democratic. Elections favored the well-known, the well-spoken, and
the rich, and everybody understood this. The lot, by contrast, treated citizens
as interchangeable, which was precisely the point. Nearly every office in the
state was filled by drawing names, held for a single year, and barred from
repetition. The handful of jobs that genuinely required expertise — above all
the ten generalships — were elected and could be held again and again, which is
how Pericles could dominate the city for decades without ever being anything
grander than one of ten annually elected officers.

## The Assembly on the Pnyx

Sovereignty lived on a hillside west of the Acropolis. The Pnyx was a terraced
open-air meeting place with a speaker's platform cut from the rock, and any
adult male citizen could walk up it, sit down, and vote. By the fourth century
the Assembly met about forty times a year on a fixed calendar, with extra
sessions when circumstances demanded. It decided war and peace, treaties, grain
supply, public building, the awarding of honors, and the fate of individual
men.

Two features made this more than a mob. The first was that debate was
genuinely open: any citizen had the right to speak, and the herald's formula
asking who wished to address the people was not a formality. In practice a
recognizable class of politicians did most of the talking, because speaking
well to six or eight thousand people in the open air is a skill, but the right
of an unknown farmer from Acharnae to stand up and disagree was real and
occasionally exercised. The second was that the Assembly did not set its own
agenda. It could only vote on business prepared in advance by the Council, a
constraint that kept the day's discussion from dissolving into whatever
happened to be on people's minds.

Voting was by show of hands, estimated rather than counted, except when a
decision concerned a single named individual, in which case secret ballot and a
quorum of six thousand were required. In the fourth century attendance came to
be paid, at a rate that rose over time, for the same reason jury pay had been
introduced earlier: without it, only men who could afford to lose a working day
would show up, and the democracy would quietly become a democracy of the
leisured.

The same quorum governed the strangest of Athenian procedures. Once a year the
Assembly was asked whether it wished to hold an ostracism. If it said yes, a
vote was held some weeks later in the Agora, and each citizen scratched a name
on a potsherd. The man with the most votes went into exile for ten years, kept
his property, and lost no rights beyond the right to be in Attica. No charge
was brought and no defense was offered, because ostracism was not a punishment;
it was a way of resolving a deadlock between two powerful rivals by removing
one of them without bloodshed. Thousands of the used sherds have been found,
some of them written in the same hand in batches, which suggests that helpful
people stood ready to supply a pre-inscribed ballot to voters who could not
write.

## The Council, the Tribes, and the Clock

The Council of Five Hundred did the state's continuous work. Fifty men came
from each of the ten tribes, and the tribes themselves were an artificial
creation — Cleisthenes' most ingenious piece of engineering. Instead of
grouping Athenians by the old kinship divisions that aristocratic families
controlled, he divided Attica into three regions (the city, the coast, the
inland) and built each new tribe from demes drawn out of all three. A tribe was
therefore a cross-section of the whole territory rather than a local bloc, and
a regional magnate could no longer deliver his neighbors as a voting unit. Deme
membership became hereditary, so a family that moved to the city kept voting
with its ancestral village.

Each tribe's fifty councillors served as the standing executive for one tenth
of the year, a period called a prytany. They took their meals at public expense
in a round building called the Tholos, and a third of them slept there every
night so that the state was never without someone authorized to act. Their
chairman was chosen by lot for a single day and night, held the keys to the
treasuries and the state seal, and could never hold the position again. It is
one of the sharper illustrations of the Athenian attitude to power that the
nearest thing the city had to a head of state changed every twenty-four hours
by lottery. Socrates held the job once, on the day the Assembly voted
illegally to try the generals of Arginusae as a group, and refused to put the
motion.

## The Courts

The law courts, the dikasteria, were in some ways the more powerful body. Six
thousand citizens over thirty were sworn in as jurors each year, and panels
were drawn from them daily — two hundred and one for a private suit, five
hundred and one or more for a public one, with even larger panels for cases of
real weight. There was no judge in the modern sense, no professional
prosecutor, and no lawyer. A magistrate kept order and enforced the timetable.
Prosecution was brought by a private citizen, and both sides spoke for
themselves, often using speeches written by hired professionals whose surviving
work gives us much of what we know about ordinary Athenian life.

Speaking time was measured by a klepsydra, a clay jar with a bronze outlet that
drained in a fixed interval; when the water ran out, the speaker stopped. The
jury heard both sides in a single sitting and voted immediately, by dropping
bronze ballot discs into an urn, without deliberating together. There was no
appeal, because a jury of five hundred citizens chosen by lot was understood to
be the people itself, and there was nothing above the people to appeal to.

The courts were also the mechanism by which officials were held to account.
Before taking up any post a man underwent dokimasia, a scrutiny of his
qualifications and conduct; on leaving it he underwent euthynai, an audit of
his accounts and actions. And the Athenians built in a check on their own
Assembly through the graphe paranomon, a prosecution brought against the
proposer of a decree on the grounds that it contradicted existing law. A decree
could be passed by the full Assembly and then struck down in court, with the
man who moved it fined. It made politicians cautious, and it meant that the
sovereign people, having changed its mind, had a legal way of saying so.

## Who Was Not in the Room

The scale of the exclusion is not a footnote. Women of citizen families had no
political existence at all; they could not attend the Assembly, own substantial
property in their own right, or go to law without a male guardian. Resident
foreigners, the metics, might live in Athens for generations, pay a special
tax, serve in the fleet, and run much of the city's trade and manufacture
without ever voting. And slaves, who were everywhere — in households, in the
workshops, and in appalling numbers in the silver mines at Laurion — were
property.

A law carried under Pericles narrowed the citizen body further by requiring
both parents to be of citizen descent, which turned Athenian citizenship from a
status into something closer to a closed hereditary corporation with a valuable
shared asset: the empire, the courts, the pay, the grain. The adult male
citizens who actually held the franchise were a modest fraction of the people
living in Attica, likely somewhere in the tens of thousands at the city's
height, against a total population several times larger.

## What It Survived

Athens lost the Peloponnesian War, and twice during the crisis the democracy
was overthrown — once in 411 by an oligarchic council, and again in 404 when
the victorious Spartans installed the regime remembered as the Thirty Tyrants,
which killed and expropriated its way through the city for the better part of a
year. Both times the democracy came back, and after the second restoration the
Athenians did something genuinely unusual. They swore an amnesty: no
prosecutions for acts committed under the Thirty, excepting only the Thirty
themselves and a few of their immediate agents. The word they used for the oath
means, more or less, not to recall past wrongs.

It held, mostly. It did not save Socrates, who was tried and executed a few
years later by a jury of ordinary citizens under charges that everyone involved
understood to carry political weight the amnesty formally excluded. The system
that produced the kleroterion, the water clock, and the twenty-four-hour
chairman also produced that verdict, and Plato never forgave it. Both facts
belong to the same institution, and the Athenians would not have seen any
contradiction in that: the people had voted.
