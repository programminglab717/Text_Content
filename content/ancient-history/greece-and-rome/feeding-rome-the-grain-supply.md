# Feeding Rome: The Grain Supply

Seneca describes a scene at Puteoli on the Bay of Naples: the whole town out on the moles, watching the horizon, because a particular kind of ship had appeared. The grain fleet from Alexandria was the only shipping permitted to carry a topsail into the bay, so the rig identified it at a distance, and the small fast vessels that ran ahead of the convoy brought word that the rest were coming. People went out to meet the mail boats. In a city that depended on ships it had no control over, arriving from a coast a thousand miles away across a sea that was closed for months of the year, the sight of grain on the horizon was worth a holiday.

Rome at its height held something close to a million people, a size no European city would reach again for many centuries, and it sat in a region that could not come close to feeding it. Italy grew grain, but the land around the capital had shifted towards vines, olives and stock, and the soil could never have supplied a population of that order in any case. Everything came in by sea. The quantities involved are argued over, because ancient figures are unreliable and the dole rolls are only part of the picture, but every serious estimate lands in the hundreds of thousands of tonnes of wheat a year. Moving that much bulk cargo without railways or steam is the real achievement, and it was done by exploiting the one advantage the Mediterranean offers: water transport is cheap, and everything worth conquering is near water.

## Where It Came From

Sicily and Sardinia fed the Republic. They were the first provinces, taxed in grain, and for two centuries a Roman politician thinking about food supply thought about them.

The empire's scale demanded more. North Africa — the rich wheat country behind Carthage and westward into what is now Tunisia and Algeria — became the single largest supplier, and Egypt, annexed after Actium, became the most politically sensitive. A speech preserved in Josephus claims Africa fed the city for eight months of the year and Egypt for four, which is probably too neat, but the ranking is right. Both regions sent grain as tax in kind rather than as ordinary trade, which meant the state's food supply was not a purchase but a levy, and the shipping was a service contracted from private owners rather than a fleet the state operated.

Augustus understood the leverage this created. Egypt was administered as a special case, governed by a knight rather than a senator, and senators were forbidden to set foot in it without the emperor's permission. The province was not merely rich. It held the bread of Rome, and whoever held Egypt could starve the capital in a season.

## The Sea, and the Season

Ancient ships sailed by the weather, and Mediterranean winter weather was regarded as an unacceptable risk. From late autumn until spring the sea was effectively closed; cargoes wintered in port and the grain fleets ran in the summer months, which meant the entire year's supply had to be carried, unloaded and stored in a compressed season.

The route from Alexandria was worse than the map suggests. Prevailing summer winds from the northwest made the direct passage to Italy a long beat, and captains ran instead up the Levantine coast and along the south of Asia Minor, past Rhodes and Crete, before the run west. Going out could take a month or more; coming home with the wind behind them took a fraction of that. The account in Acts of a grain ship from Alexandria caught by weather off Crete, driven for days, and wrecked on Malta is the best surviving description of what could go wrong when a sailing decision was made too late in the year. The ships themselves were large by ancient standards — Lucian describes visiting one that had been blown off course into Piraeus and was clearly a wonder to the crowd — capable of carrying cargoes measured in many hundreds of tonnes.

Because the state needed private owners to take these risks, it bought them. Claudius, after storms destroyed a substantial part of a season's shipping, offered shipowners public indemnity against loss and dangled legal privileges — exemptions and advantages in Roman law — for those who built ships above a certain size and committed them to the grain run for a term of years. Shippers who did well by this arrangement advertised it. The forum behind the theatre at Ostia is ringed with small offices whose mosaic floors show ships and name the ports their owners worked out of, one of them accompanied by an elephant.

## The Last Twenty Miles

Rome is not a port. It sits inland on a river too shallow and twisting for seagoing ships, and the last stretch of the journey was the most awkward part of the whole system.

For a long time the big ships unloaded at Puteoli, well to the south, and the cargo came up the coast in smaller vessels. Claudius attacked the problem directly, building an artificial harbour near the Tiber mouth with curving moles — its foundations anchored, according to the story, on the enormous ship that had brought an obelisk from Egypt, deliberately sunk and filled with concrete. That basin proved dangerously exposed, and Trajan added a great hexagonal inner harbour behind it, lined with warehouses, whose outline is still visible today next to Rome's airport.

From there, grain was transferred into flat-bottomed river barges and hauled up the Tiber, towed from a path along the bank by teams of men or oxen, a journey of some twenty miles against the current that took several days. It was unloaded at the Emporium below the Aventine, where gangs of porters carried sacks up from the quays into the warehouse district.

Those warehouses were serious engineering. Grain in bulk is a difficult commodity: it sweats, heats, sprouts, and feeds insects and rodents enthusiastically. The Roman answer was long narrow chambers with thick walls, small high openings for ventilation, and floors raised on low piers so that air circulated underneath and damp could not rise. Measurers checked each consignment with standard measures and levelling sticks, and clerks recorded it, because from the moment it left the ship the grain was accounted for against the ration lists.

## Bread as Politics

The distribution at the Roman end began as a reform and became an entitlement nobody could withdraw. In the late Republic a tribune carried a law selling grain to citizens at a fixed low price; a later tribune made it free; Caesar found the rolls so inflated that he cut them drastically; Augustus fixed the number of recipients at something like two hundred thousand and put the whole system under a prefect of the grain supply, an equestrian official who reported to him.

Two things about the dole are routinely misunderstood. It was not poor relief — eligibility ran to adult male citizens on a list, a privilege of citizenship and residence rather than a test of need, and men of some means were on it. And the monthly ration, five measures of wheat, was roughly enough bread for one adult, not for a household; it was a foundation to be topped up by work, not a living.

Grain, moreover, is not bread. For most of Roman history recipients took wheat and dealt with milling and baking themselves or through the neighbourhood bakery. Rome's bakers formed a powerful guild tied ever more tightly to the state, and one of them, a freedman contractor who supplied the public, built himself a tomb outside the city gate decorated with a frieze of the whole process, its façade covered in what appear to be stylised grain measures — a baker announcing his trade in stone with no embarrassment at all.

The political stakes were personal. When supplies ran short the crowd did not petition; it rioted, and it rioted at whoever was in reach. Claudius was mobbed in the Forum during a shortage and pelted with stale bread, and the memory of it is one reason he put so much money into the harbour. Tacitus records ships arriving during another scarcity loaded not with wheat but with sand for the arena, an insult the city did not forget. Juvenal's bitter shorthand for a population that had traded political rights for handouts — bread and circuses — is quoted endlessly, usually without noticing that the man who coined it was describing an arrangement the elite had built deliberately and depended on.

The system's end is a clean demonstration of how load-bearing it had been. When Constantinople was founded it was given a grain supply of its own, and Egypt's harvest was redirected east. When the Vandals took the African provinces in the fifth century the western supply was cut at the source. No emperor could conjure a substitute, because there was no substitute; the population of Rome fell through the following century until a city built for a million held a fraction of that, camped among its own monuments. Down by the old river quays there is a low hill made entirely of broken oil jars, discarded one by one as cargoes were emptied. It is the residue of the supply system, and it is the only part of it still standing.
