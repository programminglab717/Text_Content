# How the Romans Built a Road

A few miles outside Rome the Appian Way still runs as it was left: great irregular slabs of grey basalt fitted together like a dry stone wall laid flat, worn into a shallow trough down the middle, with ruts cut into the stone by iron-tyred wheels passing over the same line for centuries. Umbrella pines stand along it. Tombs stand behind them, because burial inside the city was forbidden and the approach roads became cemeteries by default. The surface is uncomfortable to walk on and would have been worse to ride over, which is a useful corrective to the postcard. Roman roads were not built for comfort. They were built to move soldiers, and everything else they did followed from that.

The first of them, the Appian, was pushed south towards Capua in the late fourth century BC by the censor Appius Claudius Caecus, in the middle of Rome's long wars with the Samnites. The pattern held for the next four hundred years. A road arrived in a region shortly after the legions did, was named for the magistrate who commissioned it, and turned a conquest into an administrative fact. By the empire's height the network is usually reckoned in the tens of thousands of miles of engineered main road, with a far larger web of secondary and local tracks hanging off it, running from the Scottish border to the edge of the Sahara and from Portugal to the Euphrates.

## Choosing the Line

The reputation for straightness is deserved but frequently misread. Roman surveyors were not obsessed with straight lines for their own sake; they were minimising the length of a route they intended to build very heavily, and a straight line is the cheapest thing to build when the ground allows it. Where the ground did not allow it they compromised without embarrassment. Alpine sections climb in hairpins. Roads skirt marshes, take the firm gravel terrace above a flood plain, and bend several miles out of their way to reach a place where a river can be crossed.

What you actually see on the ground is a series of straight segments, each aimed at a fixed point, with abrupt changes of direction at high ground where one alignment ends and the next begins. That is the signature of the method. Surveyors worked from summit to summit, sighting between poles or fires set on hilltops, and interpolated the line across the intervening country by setting up intermediate marks until the sighting closed. Their main instrument was a simple cross of arms hanging plumb bobs, used to lay out right angles and check that a row of markers was true; levels were taken with a long水 trough or a weighted frame of the kind Vitruvius describes. None of it was sophisticated. All of it depended on patience and on labour that the army had in quantity.

## The Layers

There was no single Roman recipe, whatever the neat cross-section diagrams in textbooks suggest. Excavation across the empire turns up roads of wildly differing construction: two feet of material in one place and five in another, paved stone here, rammed gravel there, timber corduroy laid over peat in the wetter parts of Britain and the Rhineland. The often-quoted four-layer scheme comes from a passage of Vitruvius about laying floors, and later writers applied it to roads with more confidence than the evidence supports.

The consistent elements are simpler and more revealing. First, the builders dug down to something they trusted, stripping off topsoil until they reached subsoil or bedrock, and they dug ditches along both sides. Second, they built up in courses, coarse material at the bottom — broken stone, rubble, sometimes set in lime mortar — with progressively finer material above, and the whole mass compacted as it went. Third, they raised the road above the surrounding ground on an embankment, the agger, and cambered its surface so that water ran off to the sides. Fourth, wherever stone paving was used, the slabs were set with a raised kerb to keep the mass from spreading sideways under load.

Drainage is the whole trick. Any road surface will survive traffic; almost none will survive saturation, because water in the foundation turns the bearing layer into a slurry that pumps out under each passing wheel. A raised, cambered, ditched road with permeable layers beneath it stays dry, and a dry foundation carries an astonishing amount of punishment. The Romans overbuilt to a degree that makes no economic sense for ox carts, which is another reminder of the client they were building for. A road that can take a siege train in February is a road that never becomes an excuse.

Paving in dressed polygonal blocks was expensive and largely reserved for approaches to cities and for stretches near quarries with the right stone. Most of the network, for most of its length, was well-drained compacted gravel — perfectly good, and much easier to repair.

## Rivers, Ridges and the Occasional Mountain

Where a road met an obstacle the Romans generally went through rather than around. Masonry bridges on semicircular arches carried roads over rivers across the whole empire, with cutwaters set against the current and, on the larger examples, piers founded inside timber cofferdams pumped dry for the purpose. Several are still carrying traffic. The small bridge linking the Tiber island to the left bank in Rome has been in continuous use since the late Republic.

On steep ground, cuttings were blasted out with iron tools, fire and water, and terraces were carried along cliff faces on retaining walls. On the Flaminian Way north of Rome a tunnel driven through a limestone spur under Vespasian is still the road, a short dark slot with its tool marks visible on the walls. Marshes were crossed on causeways of brushwood and timber piles; the road running north from Rome over the Pontine marshes was accompanied for a stretch by a canal, and travellers preferred to be towed along it overnight rather than endure the paving.

## Measuring and Using the Network

Every Roman mile — a thousand paces, a little short of a modern mile — was marked by a stone cylinder on a plinth. Milestones carried the distance to the nearest significant town, and above that, in larger letters, the name and titles of the emperor or magistrate responsible for building or repairing the stretch. They were as much political advertisement as navigation aid, and because they are dated by imperial titles they are now among the most useful documents historians have for the chronology of road building. Augustus set up a gilded marker in the Forum from which distances on the main roads out of Italy were reckoned.

On top of the physical network ran a state courier service, organised under Augustus, with relay stations at intervals along the main routes: small posts where a rider changed horses, and larger ones with stabling, accommodation and repair facilities for an overnight stop. Use required a permit, and the permits were abused constantly; the obligation to feed, house and supply animals to official travellers fell on the communities along the route and was a genuine burden, a form of taxation collected in hay. A courier changing mounts could make perhaps fifty miles a day as routine business, and far more when the news justified killing horses.

Travellers navigated with written itineraries rather than maps — lists of stations and the distances between them, the ancient equivalent of turn-by-turn directions. One surviving late Roman map, known from a medieval copy, is drawn as an enormously elongated strip with the road network stretched across it and geography squeezed out of the way entirely, which tells you what its users cared about. A set of silver cups found at a spa north of Rome, shaped like milestones and engraved with the stations and mileages from southern Spain all the way to the capital, appears to have been a pilgrim's or traveller's souvenir of a long journey.

## What Became of Them

The roads outlasted the state that built them, partly because they were absurdly durable and partly because nobody had a better idea. Medieval traffic used them, rulers taxed the traffic, and churches and farmhouses were built out of their paving where the stone was worth robbing. In Britain the old routes acquired English names and became the spine of the later road system; long stretches of modern highway across northern France, the Po valley and central Anatolia still follow alignments chosen by a surveyor with a plumb bob.

What did not survive was maintenance. A Roman road was not a monument but a managed asset, patched by local communities under legal obligation, repaved by emperors advertising their generosity, its ditches cleared and its surface renewed. Remove that and the mechanism that made it work inverts: ditches silt, water sits in the foundation, frost lifts the slabs, and a cart finds it easier to run on the verge alongside. Many old Roman routes survive today as a hollow way beside a field, or as a hedge line, or as a name — the physical road dismantled, the decision about where the road should go still in force two thousand years later.
