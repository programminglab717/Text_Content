# The Constitution of the Roman Republic

There was no document. A Roman asked to show a visitor his city's constitution would have had nothing to hand over — no charter, no founding text, no numbered clauses. He could only have pointed at behaviour. At the twelve attendants walking ahead of a consul with bundles of rods on their shoulders, and at the axes that were taken out of those bundles when the procession crossed into the city. At the fact that the man they escorted, who a month earlier had commanded an army in Spain, would be a private citizen again in December and could be prosecuted like anyone else. At a bench of several hundred former magistrates whose collective opinion was technically only advice, and was almost never disregarded.

Romans called the arrangement the mos maiorum, the custom of the ancestors, and they treated departures from it as something close to impiety even as they altered it constantly. Rules acquired authority by being old. A practice that had been followed for three generations was binding in a way that no statute could quite match, and much of what looks in retrospect like constitutional law began as a precedent that nobody had the nerve to break. What follows is therefore a description of a machine that was never designed, assembled over three or four centuries out of concessions extracted in crises and never rationalised afterwards.

## Offices Divided, and Timed

Two principles run through nearly every Roman magistracy: collegiality and annuality. Offices came in pairs or larger boards, and they expired after a year.

The two consuls held imperium, the power to command and to compel, and each could veto the other. In the field this could be catastrophic — at Cannae the two consuls are said to have alternated days of command — but it was precisely the point. A single man with unchecked authority was what the Republic had been founded to prevent, and the founding story of the expulsion of the kings was rehearsed often enough to keep the anxiety fresh. Praetors, originally one and eventually a half-dozen or more, also held imperium; the senior of them ran the courts at Rome while others took provincial commands. Quaestors handled money, in the treasury and with the armies. Aediles looked after the streets, the markets, the water supply and the public games, an office with no real power that was nonetheless worth bankrupting yourself for, because the games were how a young politician bought a reputation.

Above all of these, and intermittent, were the censors: two men elected every few years, holding office for about eighteen months, who conducted the census, awarded the state's contracts, and revised the roll of the Senate. Censors could strike a man off that roll for conduct they judged disgraceful, which gave two elderly ex-consuls a disciplinary power over the entire governing class that no annual magistrate possessed.

The dictatorship was the emergency valve. In a genuine crisis the consuls could appoint a single commander with authority overriding theirs, but for six months at most, and with a deputy called the master of horse appointed alongside him. For most of the Republic the office was used and then laid down, sometimes within weeks. Its later career, under Sulla and Caesar, was a perversion of an institution that had originally worked exactly as intended.

Ambition was channelled through a customary sequence of these offices, the cursus honorum, with minimum ages and compulsory intervals eventually fixed by law: quaestor, then aedile or tribune, then praetor, then consul, with ten years required before a second consulship. The effect was that nobody reached the top of Roman politics before his early forties, and nobody stayed there.

## A Senate That Could Not Command

The Senate had no power to legislate and no power to give orders. Its resolutions were advice, formally addressed to a magistrate who had asked for it. In practice it governed.

Its membership, a few hundred until Sulla roughly doubled it, consisted of men who had held office, enrolled for life by the censors. This gave it the one thing every other part of the system lacked: continuity. Consuls came and went annually; the Senate contained every consul of the past thirty years. It managed relations with foreign states, received embassies, assigned provinces and commands, extended commands beyond their year when a war was unfinished, and controlled the treasury, which meant it controlled whether an army was paid. A magistrate who defied it found the funds for his campaign mysteriously unavailable.

Debate was strictly by rank. The presiding consul called on the senior members first, and the man listed at the head of the roll spoke before anyone else, so that the tone of a session was often set before most of those present had any opportunity to speak at all. Junior senators frequently never spoke; they voted by walking to one side of the chamber. Reputation, accumulated over decades and quantifiable in a way Romans took seriously, did most of the work that formal authority might have done elsewhere.

## The Assemblies and the Arithmetic of Voting

Roman citizens did vote, and their votes decided elections, laws and wars. They did not vote as individuals. They voted in blocs, and the composition of the blocs determined the outcome more reliably than anything said in the debate.

The centuriate assembly, which elected consuls and praetors and formally declared war, was descended from the army muster and organised by wealth. Citizens were sorted into property classes, each class holding a fixed number of voting units, and the richest classes together with the cavalry centuries held a majority of those units between them. Voting proceeded from the top down and stopped as soon as a majority was reached, so in a decisive election the poorest centuries were simply never called. Nobody concealed this. It was understood that men who paid for the army's equipment should weigh more heavily in deciding whether to fight.

The tribal assemblies, which passed most legislation, divided citizens by tribe — thirty-five of them, but only four covering the city of Rome itself. Since freedmen and much of the urban poor were enrolled in those four, an enormous population controlled four votes out of thirty-five, while a thinly settled rural tribe controlled one. Voting also required attendance in person at Rome, which quietly excluded most rural citizens in practice.

More restrictive still: no assembly could meet unless a magistrate summoned it, no proposal could be put unless a magistrate put it, and nothing could be amended from the floor. The people said yes or no to a text drafted for them. Public discussion happened at informal meetings beforehand, where speakers were invited by whoever had called the meeting. There was no initiative from below.

## The Tribunes

Set against all of this was the strangest institution in the Republic: ten tribunes of the plebs, elected annually, who were not magistrates of the Roman people at all but officers of the plebeians, protected by an oath that made killing them sacrilege. The tribunate emerged from the long political struggle between patricians and plebeians and it retained the character of a weapon throughout.

A tribune could physically interpose himself to rescue a citizen from a magistrate. He could veto — a single word sufficed — almost any public act: an election, a law, a levy, a Senate resolution. He could convene the plebs and put proposals to them, and after a law passed early in the third century those resolutions bound the whole community, which made the tribunate a route to legislation that bypassed the Senate entirely.

The check on this enormous power was its own multiplicity. Any one of the ten could block the other nine. The Senate therefore rarely needed to defeat a hostile tribune; it needed only to find a friendly one, and among ten ambitious young men there was usually one available.

## What Polybius Saw, and What He Missed

Polybius, a Greek hostage who spent years in Rome watching the system work, explained Roman success by the mixture: consuls supplying the monarchic element, the Senate the aristocratic, the assemblies the democratic, each able to obstruct the others and none able to dominate. Written after Rome had absorbed the disaster of Cannae and come back to win the war anyway, the analysis was not flattery. Something in the arrangement really did produce a resilience that better-generalled states lacked.

What the model understates is how narrow the governing group was. A handful of families supplied consuls generation after generation; a man without consular ancestors who reached the office was called a new man, and the phrase existed because the phenomenon was rare. Elections were shaped by inherited networks of obligation running from great houses down through hundreds of dependants, and by the money that funded games, feasts and public building.

The deeper problem was scale. The constitution was built for a city-state whose army was its citizen body and whose magistrates went home after a year. Provinces, long commands far from any supervision, and armies that looked to a general for land at discharge fitted into it badly. When the Gracchi demonstrated that a tribune with the assembly behind him could override the Senate, and the Senate answered by declaring emergencies in which killing citizens became defensible, both halves of the machine learned that the other could be broken. After that the veto was something to be intimidated out of a man rather than obeyed, and armies became arguments.

Augustus did not abolish any of it. He held the offices, or the powers of them, and left the forms standing — consuls still elected, still giving their names to the year, presiding over a Senate that still deliberated. The shell proved remarkably durable. Men were still being appointed consul at Rome five centuries after the last consul decided anything.
