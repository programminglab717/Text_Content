# A Day at the Theatre of Dionysus

You would want to arrive before dawn. The seats on the south slope of the
Acropolis were wooden for most of the fifth century, set into the natural
hillside above a beaten earth circle, and there were nowhere near enough of
them for everyone who wanted one. People brought cushions, bread, wine, and
dried figs, because they were going to be there until the light went. Somewhere
in the front rows were reserved stone thrones for priests and officials, and
the priest of Dionysus sat in the best seat in the house, directly facing the
circle, because the entire day was a religious occasion and he was the god's
representative at it.

The god's own statue was there too, carried in from a shrine outside the city
on the opening day and set up to watch the plays. Nobody in Athens thought of
theatre as a night out. It happened twice a year, at festivals of Dionysus, and
the performances were competitive offerings to him in the same sense that a
sacrificed bull was an offering.

## The Machinery of the Festival

The great festival was the City Dionysia, held in early spring when the sailing
season reopened and Athens filled with foreigners. It began with a procession
that hauled the statue through the streets accompanied by baskets of offerings,
sacrificial animals, and large carved phalloi carried upright by delegations
from allied states. There was a parade in the theatre itself in which the
tribute paid by the empire was displayed, and in which the sons of men killed
in war, raised at public expense, were presented in armour when they came of
age. Then several days of contests: first choruses of fifty men and fifty boys
from each of the ten tribes competing in the dithyramb, then the plays.

Getting a play staged meant persuading one man. The chief magistrate of the
year reviewed applications from playwrights and "granted a chorus" to three of
them for tragedy, and to a further set for comedy. We do not know what he read
or heard in order to decide, which is a real gap: the single most consequential
act of literary judgment in Athens has left no description of its procedure.

Funding came from a rich individual rather than the treasury. A citizen with
property above a threshold could be assigned the choregia, an obligation to pay
for the chorus — their training, their months of rehearsal, their costumes,
their piper, and their keep. It was expensive and it was public, and a choregos
whose production won put up a monument saying so, some of which still stand
along the street below the theatre. The system got the city its festival
without taxation and got the wealthy a socially acceptable way to spend money
competitively.

## The Space

The performance area was the orchestra, a flat circle of packed earth with a
small altar at the centre. Behind it stood the skene, originally a hut for
changing and later a painted wooden facade with a central door, which could
represent a palace, a cave, or a tent. Two long open ramps called parodoi ran
in on either side between the seating and the skene, and the chorus entered
along them in formation. Everything about the layout assumes the chorus is the
centre of gravity. It is dancing in a circle with the audience wrapped most of
the way around it, not standing on a stage facing forward.

Sound carried remarkably well, helped by the slope, the hard surfaces, and the
open ring of hillside. At Epidaurus, the best preserved of the later stone
theatres, a coin dropped in the orchestra can be heard near the top rows, an
effect that has been traced partly to the limestone seating filtering out
low-frequency crowd noise while passing the higher frequencies of the voice.
There was no artificial light and no roof, and the plays ran through whatever
the weather did.

## Three Actors and a Chorus

Everyone on the ground was male and everyone wore a mask. Masks were light
constructions of stiffened linen, cork, or wood, covering the whole head with
the hair attached, with an open mouth. They did two practical jobs beyond
characterization: they let a small number of performers play many parts, and
they made a figure legible from sixty rows up, where a human face is a smudge.

Tragedy worked under a rule that looks arbitrary until you watch what it
produces. There were three speaking actors, no matter how many characters the
play contained, so parts were distributed among them and each actor changed
mask and costume behind the skene between scenes. Aeschylus is credited with
introducing the second actor and Sophocles the third, and each addition opened
a new kind of scene — a second actor makes conflict possible, a third makes
it possible for someone to be caught between two other people.

The chorus numbered a dozen or fifteen in tragedy, twice that in comedy, and it
sang and danced in unison to a double-reed pipe. Choral odes divide the action,
comment on it, remember the myths that rhyme with it, and frequently misread it
in ways the audience can see through. Comedy gave the chorus a set-piece called
the parabasis in which it stepped forward, dropped the pretense of the plot,
and addressed the audience directly on behalf of the poet.

Stagecraft was minimal and deliberate. Violence happened offstage and was
reported by a messenger, often at length and in the play's most vivid verse — a
convention that puts the horror in the audience's head rather than on the
ground. Two devices broke the rule. The ekkyklema was a wheeled platform rolled
out through the central doors to display an interior tableau, typically the
bodies. The mechane was a crane that lifted an actor over the skene, used for
gods arriving in the air, and used often enough by Euripides that the Latin tag
for a plot resolved by divine intervention still names the machine.

## The Contest

A tragedian competed with four plays performed in a single day: three tragedies
and then a satyr play, a short burlesque with a chorus of the god's horse-tailed
attendants, which treated heroic material as farce and sent the audience to
lunch in a different mood. Sometimes the three tragedies formed a continuous
story, as in the only connected trilogy that survives entire, Aeschylus'
account of the murder of Agamemnon and its consequences, which ends with the
foundation of an Athenian homicide court.

Judging was rigged against being rigged. Ten judges were selected by lot, one
from each tribe, from lists of names sealed in urns before the festival. At the
end each wrote a ranking, all ten went into a container, and only some were
drawn out and counted. No judge knew whether his vote would be among them, and
no one could buy a majority he could identify in advance. The crowd made its
preferences loudly known regardless, and judges were not immune to that.
Aeschylus won repeatedly, Sophocles was said never to have come third, and
Euripides — the one the later world copied most — rarely won at all in his
lifetime.

## What Came Down to Us

Something like a thousand tragedies were performed in the great century of
Athenian drama. Roughly thirty survive, by three authors, and their survival is
largely an accident of school syllabuses: at some point in antiquity a small
selection was chosen for teaching, copied repeatedly, and everything outside
the selection gradually stopped being recopied. Most of what we have of
Euripides beyond the school set comes from a single manuscript containing an
alphabetical run of his plays that happened to escape.

Comedy changed more. Aristophanes wrote in a tradition that put living
Athenians on stage under their own names, mocked the war policy while the war
was on, and staged a version of Socrates in a basket studying the heavens. By
the time Alexander was in Asia, that kind of comedy was finished, and the
comedy of Menander had replaced it: no political abuse, no chorus to speak of,
just households, lost children, mistaken identities, and young men in love with
unsuitable women. Roman playwrights adapted it, and through them it became the
shape of nearly every comic plot in European theatre.

Menander himself was admired for centuries and then vanished, known only
through quotations, until excavators began pulling papyrus out of ancient
rubbish mounds in Egypt. Fragments came first. Then, in the twentieth century,
a codex surfaced carrying a nearly complete comedy — a grumpy old man, a
country estate, a well someone falls into — and a playwright the world had lost
for well over a thousand years could be read again, almost entire.
