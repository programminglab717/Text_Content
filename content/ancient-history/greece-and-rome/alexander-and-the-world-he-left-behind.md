# Alexander and the World He Left Behind

In the spring of 334 BC a Macedonian army of something over thirty thousand men
crossed the Hellespont into Asia. Its king was twenty-two, had been on the
throne for less than two years, and was carrying a debt large enough that the
campaign had to succeed early or fold. He was also inheriting a war. The
invasion of the Persian Empire had been his father's project, advertised as
revenge for the burning of Greek temples a century and a half earlier, and an
advance force was already in Asia Minor when Philip of Macedon was stabbed to
death at a wedding celebration in his own capital.

The army mattered more than the grievance. Philip had spent twenty years
turning a fractious highland kingdom on the edge of the Greek world into the
best military organization in the eastern Mediterranean, and he had done it by
changing almost everything. His infantry carried a pike roughly twice the
length of a Greek spear, gripped in both hands, with a small shield slung from
the shoulder — a weapon useless for single combat and devastating in a block,
presenting five ranks of points to anyone in front. He drilled that infantry
year-round and paid it, which meant it could campaign in winter and in mountains
while citizen militias went home for the harvest. He built a siege train with
torsion artillery, so that walled cities stopped being invulnerable. And he
developed the Companion cavalry into a shock arm that charged in wedge
formation at the seam the pikes had opened. At Chaeronea in 338 this combination
beat the combined armies of Athens and Thebes, and the Greek city-states became
members of a league whose commander-in-chief was the king of Macedon.

## Four Years

Alexander won his first battle in Asia at the Granicus river within weeks of
landing, against a Persian satrapal army that made the mistake of putting its
cavalry on the riverbank and its Greek mercenary infantry uselessly behind. A
year later he met the Great King himself at Issus, on a narrow coastal plain
where Darius's numbers could not deploy, and broke the Persian centre by riding
straight at the royal chariot. Darius fled and left behind his mother, his
wife, and his daughters, whom Alexander treated with a conspicuous courtesy
that was worth a province in propaganda.

Instead of pursuing inland he turned south along the coast, on the reasoning
that a fleet he could not beat at sea could be beaten by taking away its
harbors. Tyre, on an island half a mile offshore, refused to open its gates.
Alexander built a causeway out to it, lost it to storms and sorties, rebuilt it
wider, brought up towers on ships, and after a siege of many months took the
city and sold the survivors. Egypt then surrendered without a fight, welcomed
him as a liberator from Persian rule, and gave him the site of Alexandria and a
long detour to the oracle of Ammon at Siwa, from which he returned satisfied in
a way he never fully explained.

The decisive battle came at Gaugamela in 331, on ground Darius had chosen and
levelled for his chariots. Alexander advanced obliquely to his right, dragging
the Persian left out of position, and charged the gap that opened. Darius fled
again and was murdered the following year by his own satrap Bessus. Babylon and
Susa opened their gates. At Persepolis, the ceremonial heart of the empire,
Alexander took a treasury so large it required thousands of pack animals to
move, and then burned the palace complex — whether as deliberate policy or,
as one ancient account has it, during a drunken party, remains an open
question.

## The Far Side

What followed was harder than everything before it. Bactria and Sogdiana, the
mountainous northeast of the empire, were held by local barons who did not
fight pitched battles, and Alexander spent two brutal years storming rock
fortresses and chasing raiders. He married Roxane, the daughter of a Sogdian
noble, in what looks less like romance than a treaty.

He was also changing, and the Macedonians did not like it. He began wearing
elements of Persian dress, appointed Persians to office, enrolled Iranian
troops, and experimented with proskynesis, the Persian court gesture of
obeisance, which Greeks regarded as something you did to a god. Old officers
objected. Philotas, son of Alexander's most senior general, was executed for
failing to report a conspiracy; Parmenion, his father, was murdered in Media
afterwards on Alexander's orders, because a man whose son had been killed could
not be left in command of the army's supply lines. At a drinking party in
Samarkand, Alexander killed Cleitus, who had saved his life at the Granicus,
with a spear, for saying in public that the king's achievements were really his
father's.

In 326 he crossed into the Punjab and beat the Indian king Porus at the
Hydaspes in a monsoon river crossing complicated by war elephants, which the
Macedonian horses refused to approach. It was his last great battle. A few
weeks later, at another river, the army stopped. The men had been marching for
eight years, had been told repeatedly that the ocean was just beyond the next
range, and declined to go further. Alexander shut himself in his tent for days,
and then turned back. The return route through the Gedrosian desert killed
more of his people than any battle had.

Back in the imperial heartland he pressed the fusion of the two elites
further. At Susa he married a daughter of Darius and had dozens of his senior
officers married to Persian noblewomen on the same day, at enormous expense, in
a ceremony most of them quietly unwound after his death. He had also ordered
thirty thousand Iranian youths trained in Macedonian weapons and drill, and
when he announced at Opis that his oldest veterans were being sent home with
bonuses, the army took it as a threat and mutinied outright. Alexander had the
ringleaders executed, withdrew into his quarters, and began conferring
Macedonian titles on Persians. The soldiers came and stood outside until he
relented. It was a display of exactly how far the relationship between this
king and this army had drifted from what it had been at the Hellespont.

He reached Babylon and died there in the summer of 323, after a fever that
followed a long night of drinking, at thirty-two. He left no arrangements. A
story circulated afterwards that he had bequeathed his empire to the strongest;
it is far too convenient to be trusted.

## The Funeral Games

His only legitimate heir was unborn, and his half-brother was mentally
incapable of ruling. What his generals did over the next forty years was
described by one of them as funeral games. They fought each other across three
continents, through a sequence of alliances that dissolved as soon as anyone
looked like winning. Ptolemy took Egypt immediately and made his claim
concrete by hijacking Alexander's funeral cortege and installing the body in
his own capital. Antigonus came closest to reassembling the whole and was
killed at Ipsus in 301 by a coalition of everyone else, an engagement decided
by Seleucus and several hundred Indian elephants acquired by treaty from the
Mauryan empire.

By the early third century the settlement had hardened into three main
kingdoms: Ptolemaic Egypt, the enormous and permanently overstretched Seleucid
realm running from the Aegean into Iran, and Antigonid Macedon holding the old
homeland and a grip on Greece. Smaller powers — Pergamon, Rhodes, the Greek
leagues — survived in the gaps by playing the big three against one another.

## What the Kingdoms Made

The political failure produced a cultural result nobody had designed. Greek
became the working language of government, trade, and scholarship from the
Adriatic to the Hindu Kush — not classical Attic but koine, the simplified
common dialect that soldiers and clerks actually spoke, and in which, three
centuries later, the New Testament would be written. Cities founded or refounded
on the Greek model appeared across the east, with gymnasia, theatres, and
councils, and a Greek-speaking administrative class staffed them.

Alexandria became the intellectual capital of the world, largely because the
Ptolemies decided to buy it. They funded the Museum, a research institution
with salaried scholars, and the Library attached to it, and the effect on
learning was immediate: Euclid systematized geometry there, Eratosthenes
measured the circumference of the earth from the angle of shadows at midsummer
in two Egyptian cities, and Aristarchus proposed that the earth went round the
sun. Archimedes, working in Syracuse, corresponded with the Alexandrians.
Mathematics, astronomy, anatomy, and textual criticism all took recognizably
modern shape in these two centuries.

The far east of Alexander's conquests went its own way and produced something
stranger. Greek-speaking kings ruled in Bactria for generations, struck some of
the finest portrait coinage of antiquity, and pushed into northwest India,
where one of them, Menander, appears in a surviving Buddhist text as a king who
questions a monk and is convinced. At Kandahar the Indian emperor Ashoka had
his moral edicts carved in Greek and Aramaic for the local population. The gods
travelled too, in both directions and in hybrid forms, and the Ptolemies
deliberately engineered a new one, Serapis, to be worshipped by Greeks and
Egyptians at once.

Rome arrived in stages. Macedon was defeated at Pydna, Corinth was destroyed,
the Seleucid remnant was annexed, and Egypt came last. Cleopatra, who lost it,
was a Macedonian Greek descended from one of Alexander's bodyguards, ruling a
country her family had held for nearly three hundred years — and she is said to
have been the first of the line who bothered to learn Egyptian.
