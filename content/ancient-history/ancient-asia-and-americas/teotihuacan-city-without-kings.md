# Teotihuacan, the City Without Kings

Stand at the south end of the great avenue and look north. The paving runs
straight for something like five kilometres, rising in shallow stages, flanked
by platform after platform, and ends against the Pyramid of the Moon with the
bulk of Cerro Gordo standing directly behind it like a larger version of the
same shape. The avenue does not point at the pole. It is rotated about fifteen
and a half degrees east of north, and every wall, plaza, compound and drain in
the city is laid out on that rotation, including the canalized stretch of the
river where it crosses the grid. Something dictated the angle — a sunset on a
particular date, the setting of the Pleiades, a sightline to a peak — and
whatever it was, the city committed to it for five centuries.

The avenue also does not go anywhere. It is not a road out of town. It is an
axis, with the city's largest monuments arranged along it, and the name it
carries now is a misunderstanding: Nahuatl-speaking visitors arriving a
thousand years after the place was abandoned took the platforms lining it for
tombs and called it the Avenue of the Dead. They were wrong about that, and
about most of the rest of the city, and they were the ones who named it.

## A Plan Imposed on a Valley

Teotihuacan sits in a side valley of the Basin of Mexico, well watered by
springs, with obsidian sources within reach and the volcanic landscape of the
highlands all around. It began as one settlement among several, and grew
explosively in the first couple of centuries CE, at a period when a lava flow
from a small volcano on the southern edge of the basin buried the rival centre
of Cuicuilco and seems to have pushed its population somewhere else. At its
height the city held perhaps a hundred thousand people, possibly a good many
more, making it one of the largest cities anywhere in the world at the time and
by a wide margin the largest in the Americas.

What makes it strange among ancient cities is the degree of central design.
Most old cities accrete: lanes wander, blocks are irregular, the plan records
centuries of individual decisions. Teotihuacan was gridded, and the grid was
enforced. The monumental core — the Sun Pyramid, the Moon Pyramid, the walled
precinct called the Ciudadela — went up within a few generations, an
extraordinary mobilization of labour and materials. Residential districts were
then built out along the same axes, and the standard building type across the
whole city was a single architectural idea repeated about two thousand times.

## Inside the Mountains of Stone

The Pyramid of the Sun is a rubble-and-earth mass faced in stone, roughly
sixty-five metres high, with a base as broad as the great pyramid at Giza. Under
its centre runs a lava tube, modified in antiquity into a chamber with lobed
side rooms, which was almost certainly the reason the pyramid stands where it
does. Caves in Mesoamerican thought were openings into the earth, sources of
water, and points of emergence for ancestral peoples. The pyramid was built over
a doorway.

The Moon Pyramid grew in a series of enclosing stages, each new shell built over
the last, and modern excavation of those stages has found dedicatory burials
placed at each expansion: men with their hands tied behind them, some
decapitated, together with eagles, pumas, wolves and rattlesnakes, several of
the animals caged and kept alive long enough to leave signs of captivity on
their bones, along with offerings of greenstone, obsidian blades and pyrite
mirrors.

The most explicit deposit is at the Feathered Serpent Pyramid inside the
Ciudadela, whose facade carries alternating sculpted heads of a plumed serpent
and a second, blockier creature, set against a field of seashells. When it was
built, more than two hundred people were buried in and around its foundations in
arranged groups, many of them dressed as warriors with collars of shell plates
carved to look like human teeth, hands bound. Chemical analysis of their teeth
and bones indicates that a substantial share of them had grown up somewhere
else. Beneath the same pyramid, a tunnel driven into the bedrock and sealed in
antiquity was reopened in this century and found to contain thousands of
offerings, pools of liquid mercury, and walls that had been dusted with pyrite
to glitter in torchlight.

## Two Thousand Apartment Blocks

For all the violence in the foundations, the ordinary architecture of
Teotihuacan is domestic and, by ancient standards, remarkably comfortable. Most
of the population lived in walled residential compounds: single-storey
rectangular blocks, blank on the outside, with no street-facing windows, opening
inward onto sunken patios that admitted light and drained rainwater into an
underground system. Inside were suites of rooms for multiple families, plastered
floors, small shrines, and painted walls. Several compounds housed particular
crafts — obsidian knapping, figurine moulding, pottery, lapidary work — which
suggests that residence and occupation were bound together, something like a
guild quarter organized as an extended household.

The compounds were also where people buried their dead, under the floors, so
the city's cemeteries are its houses. That has allowed isotope and diet studies
of large numbers of ordinary residents, and the results point to a population
drawn from all over Mesoamerica. Two districts advertise it openly. On the
western edge, a neighbourhood built and used for centuries contains Zapotec-style
tombs, funerary urns and pottery from the Oaxaca valley, maintained in a
Teotihuacan matrix by people who kept their own burial customs for generations.
On the eastern side, a cluster of round adobe structures — a form used nowhere
else in the city — is full of imported ceramics from the Gulf coast and the Maya
lowlands, and looks like a compound of foreign traders.

## Nobody's Portrait

Here is what the city does not have. No royal portraits. No named rulers. No
dynastic monuments, no inscribed stelae recording accessions and conquests, no
tombs identified as belonging to a particular king. This is not because writing
was unknown: Teotihuacan used a system of signs, painted in murals and on
pottery, and it names places and perhaps people. It has not been deciphered, and
there is very little of it.

Compare the contemporary Maya cities, which are practically wallpapered with
the biographies of individual kings, and the absence becomes conspicuous. The
art of Teotihuacan shows processions of identical figures in profile, masked and
costumed, scattering offerings; it shows a storm deity with goggled eyes and a
front-facing figure, sometimes read as a great goddess and sometimes as
something else entirely, from whose hands or body water and seeds pour; it shows
coyotes and jaguars in ritual dress. What it does not show is a man on a throne
with his name attached.

Two readings compete. Either rulers existed and were deliberately kept out of
public imagery, their power expressed through institutions and lineage houses
rather than personal display, or the city was actually governed by some kind of
council or coalition of the great compounds. The apartment compounds, which look
like the architecture of powerful corporate households, are the strongest
evidence for the second view, and the mass sacrifices at the Feathered Serpent
Pyramid — an event that looks like a single ruler's statement — are among the
best for the first.

## Reach

Teotihuacan's presence turns up all over Mesoamerica in the fourth and fifth
centuries. Its green-tinged obsidian, from a source north of the city,
travelled a thousand kilometres and more. Its distinctive cylindrical tripod
vessels and its characteristic building profile — a sloping apron surmounted by
a framed vertical panel — were copied, adapted and sometimes imported wholesale
into highland Guatemala and the Maya lowlands.

The sharpest evidence is a date. Maya inscriptions at Tikal record the arrival
of an outsider on a day in 378 CE, the death of Tikal's own king on exactly that
day, and the installation shortly afterward of a new ruler described as the son
of a lord whose name-glyph shows a spearthrower and an owl, a figure associated
with the distant highland city. Whether that was conquest, a coup with foreign
backing, or the arrival of a legitimizing dynasty is argued over, but the
Teotihuacan connection was clearly something Maya kings afterward wanted on
record, because their descendants kept invoking it in costume and text for
generations.

## Burning

Sometime around the middle of the sixth century, the centre of the city burned.
The destruction was not general: it concentrated on the temples and elite
buildings along the central avenue, where sculpture was smashed, floors were
broken up and fires were set hot enough to leave the fill reddened. Ordinary
compounds away from the core show much less of it. That pattern reads less like
a sack by outsiders than like an attack aimed at the institutions of the state
by people who knew exactly which buildings mattered, possibly after a stretch of
drought and failing authority.

The city did not empty overnight; people went on living in parts of it for a
long time afterward. But it never again ran anything, and its name did not
survive. Centuries later the Mexica found the monuments standing in the valley,
concluded that the gods had made the sun and moon there at the beginning of the
present world, buried offerings in the ruins and sent their rulers to the site on
pilgrimage. They called it Teotihuacan, which is roughly the place where the
gods were born, and that is still what everyone calls it, because whatever its
builders called it remains unread.
