# The Olmec and the Mother Culture Question

In the nineteenth century a worker clearing land on a hacienda in southern
Veracruz struck something solid in the ground and assumed he had found an
upturned iron kettle, perhaps buried treasure. Digging around it exposed a
stone head taller than a man, with heavy lidded eyes, a broad flat nose and
thick lips, wearing what looked like a close-fitting helmet. The traveller who
published a note about it a few years later thought the face was African. That
was the first colossal head to be described, and it set a pattern: everything
about this culture arrived out of context, and almost everything about it
became an argument.

Serious excavation waited until the late 1930s, when Matthew Stirling began
working at Tres Zapotes and then at La Venta and San Lorenzo. He found more
heads, enormous stone thrones, caches of jade, and at Tres Zapotes a broken
stela carrying a Long Count date with its uppermost digit missing. Reconstructed
as a seventh-cycle date, it fell in the first century BCE — older than any dated
Maya monument then known. Maya specialists, whose whole framework had the Maya
inventing the calendar and everyone else borrowing it, objected sharply, and the
dispute ran for years. It was settled twice over: first by radiocarbon dates
from La Venta in the 1950s, which put the site many centuries before the Classic
Maya, and then in 1969 when the missing top of the stela was found and the
reading confirmed.

We do not know what these people called themselves. Olmec is a Nahuatl word
meaning something like rubber people, used by the Aztecs for the inhabitants of
the same wet Gulf lowlands more than two thousand years later, borrowed by
archaeologists for want of anything better. The label has stuck to a culture
that flourished in southern Veracruz and western Tabasco from roughly 1500 BCE
to around 400 BCE, and to an art style that turns up far outside that region.

## San Lorenzo

The earliest of the great centres sits on a plateau above the floodplain of the
Coatzacoalcos river, and the plateau itself is partly manufactured: ridges
extending from it were built up with enormous quantities of imported fill. It
flourished for about three centuries in the earlier part of the second
millennium BCE, which makes it the oldest urban centre known in Mesoamerica by a
comfortable margin.

Its inhabitants laid a drainage system of U-shaped basalt troughs, each one
carved, fitted with a cover stone, and joined into lines running hundreds of
metres — which means they were cutting and shaping hard volcanic rock for a
utility system, not just for sculpture. Ten colossal heads have come from San
Lorenzo. There are seventeen or so known in total, the largest over three metres
tall and weighing well beyond twenty tonnes, and all of them are basalt from the
Tuxtla mountains, sixty kilometres and more to the northwest across country with
no draught animals, no wheeled vehicles, and a great deal of swamp. The blocks
were moved by raft along the coast and rivers and by sheer organized human
effort overland.

Each head is a different face. They have individual features, distinct
headgear, and a naturalism that is rare in Mesoamerican art of any period; they
are almost certainly portraits of rulers. Some appear to have been recarved from
older monuments — a few show traces of earlier surfaces — which suggests that
stone of that quality was too valuable to leave alone and that reworking a
predecessor's image may itself have meant something. Many monuments at San
Lorenzo were found broken, defaced and buried in deliberate arrangements. That
was long read as evidence of a violent overthrow. It might equally be a ritual
retirement of objects that had become dangerous or spent.

## La Venta

As San Lorenzo declined, La Venta rose, on an island of high ground in the
Tabasco wetlands, and it held its position for several centuries. Its centre is
organized around a long north-south axis with an earthen mound at one end,
thirty-odd metres high, one of the oldest large pyramids in the Americas and
made of packed clay rather than stone.

The remarkable finds at La Venta are the things nobody was meant to see again.
Beneath the plaza floors, excavators uncovered massive mosaic pavements of
serpentine blocks — hundreds of them, imported from a great distance, laid out
in a stylized mask or jaguar-face design, and then immediately covered over
with tonnes of coloured clay fill. The labour involved in acquiring and placing
that stone is comparable to the labour of a substantial building, and the object
was buried the moment it was finished.

The same instinct produced one of the most affecting deposits in American
archaeology: a group of small figurines of jade and serpentine, standing in a
semicircle facing one figure with its back to a row of upright celts, arranged
like a scene in a play, buried under a floor. Later builders dug a narrow shaft
down through subsequent floor layers to check on the arrangement, then filled it
in again. Someone remembered exactly where it was, generations afterward.

## What They Made, and What It Meant

Olmec art has a small, insistent vocabulary. Infants with downturned mouths,
cleft heads and heavy jowls, often held in an adult's arms in the niche of a
throne. Figures caught between human and jaguar, or human and toad, or human and
harpy eagle, mid-transformation. Standing figures with the flattened skulls
produced by cradleboard binding. Seated men holding bars or bundles, their eyes
half closed, in postures that later Mesoamerican imagery associates with
trance.

Their prized materials came from far away. Blue-green jadeite was quarried in
the Motagua valley in what is now Guatemala and worked into celts, beads, ear
flares and figurines using string saws and abrasive sand, since they had no
metal. Polished concave mirrors of iron ore, capable of throwing a focused spot
of light or starting a fire, came from Oaxaca. Rubber, tapped from local trees
and processed with the juice of a morning-glory vine to make it elastic, was
formed into balls; waterlogged deposits at a spring site have preserved some,
along with carved wooden busts and the remains of infants, laid in a place where
water came out of the ground.

Whether they wrote is unresolved. A serpentine slab turned up in a gravel quarry
near San Lorenzo carrying more than sixty incised signs in horizontal rows, with
repeated sequences that look like language. Its supporters call it the earliest
writing in the Americas. Its critics point out that it was recovered from a
quarry pile rather than an excavation, which leaves its date resting entirely on
associated material. What is beyond dispute is that the region's later
inhabitants, in the centuries after the Olmec centres faded, produced genuine
long texts in a related script alongside Long Count dates, and that the
calendrical machinery the Maya later perfected was already circulating in the
isthmus.

## Mother or Sister?

Here is the argument that has run for half a century. Olmec-style objects and
imagery appear all over Mesoamerica between roughly 1200 and 800 BCE — hollow
white-slipped baby figurines, pottery incised with abstracted jaguar-paw and
flame-eyebrow motifs, carved reliefs on a cliff face in Morelos, monumental
stone at a site in Guerrero, jade caches. One reading, long associated with
Michael Coe, makes the Gulf coast the mother culture: the first place in
Mesoamerica to develop hereditary rulers, monumental art and a state-scale
religious system, which then radiated outward and set the terms for everything
later.

The other reading, argued most forcefully by Kent Flannery and Joyce Marcus,
denies the primacy. On their account, a number of regions — the Gulf lowlands,
the Valley of Oaxaca, the Valley of Mexico, the Pacific coast of Chiapas and
Guatemala — were developing ranked societies in the same centuries, in contact
with one another, exchanging goods and ideas among peers. Shared symbolism in
that situation does not require a single source, and Oaxaca in particular has
its own long sequence of villages, public buildings and early carved monuments
that owes nothing obvious to Veracruz.

Chemical sourcing of pottery was supposed to settle it. Studies of the clay in
Olmec-style vessels found at sites across Mesoamerica concluded that a
substantial number had actually been made in the San Lorenzo region and carried
outward, while the reverse traffic was slight — which would make the Gulf coast
an exporter of style as well as a producer of it. The response was another round
of sampling and statistics reaching different conclusions, and the dispute is
now partly about analytical method.

The honest position is that both sides are describing something real. Nothing
anywhere else in Mesoamerica at that date matches the scale of San Lorenzo, its
earthworks or its sculpture, and the debt later cultures owed to the imagery of
the Gulf coast is visible in their rain gods and their maize gods and their
ballcourts. And the regions receiving that imagery were not empty vessels; they
took what suited them and made something local out of it, which is what
societies generally do.

Either way, the thing that ought to be startling is the basalt. A twenty-tonne
block cut from a volcanic slope, floated and dragged sixty kilometres through
rivers and swamp, and carved into the face of a particular man with a particular
expression, by people who had no metal tools, no pack animals, and no wheel —
three thousand years ago, in a rainforest, at the start of everything.
