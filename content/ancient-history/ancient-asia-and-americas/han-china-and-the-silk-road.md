# Han China and the Silk Road

A court attendant named Zhang Qian left Chang'an around 138 BCE with something
over a hundred companions and an assignment that was close to a long shot.
Emperor Wu wanted an alliance against the Xiongnu, the nomadic confederation
north of the frontier that had been extracting silk, grain and imperial brides
from the Han court for two generations under the diplomatic fiction of a
marriage treaty. Court intelligence held that a people called the Yuezhi, driven
westward by the Xiongnu a few decades earlier, might be nursing enough of a
grudge to strike from behind. Zhang Qian's job was to find them and make the
offer.

He was captured almost at once and spent roughly a decade in Xiongnu custody,
where he was given a wife and fathered a son. When he finally escaped he went
west rather than home, crossed the mountains, and located the Yuezhi in the
rich country south of the Oxus. They had settled well, were prospering, and had
no interest at all in a war of revenge two thousand miles from their new
pastures. He was captured a second time on the return leg. He reached Chang'an
after about thirteen years, with one surviving companion and his Xiongnu wife,
and nothing to show diplomatically.

What he brought instead was a description of a world the Han court had not
known existed in any detail: Dayuan in the Ferghana valley, Kangju on the
steppe beyond it, Anxi — Parthia — with its cities and its silver coins stamped
with the king's face, and beyond that, rumours of an empire at the far western
edge of everything. He had seen bamboo from Sichuan and cloth from the
southwest for sale in a Bactrian market, which meant goods from Han territory
were already moving by routes nobody in the capital had mapped. And he had seen
the horses.

## Heavenly Horses and the Cost of Cavalry

Han armies had a structural problem. The Xiongnu fought as mounted archers on
tough steppe ponies and could disengage whenever a battle went badly, which
meant a Chinese infantry victory settled nothing. Building a cavalry arm big
enough to answer them required horses that Chinese pastures could not easily
produce and that the Xiongnu had no reason to sell. Zhang Qian reported that
Dayuan bred a larger, faster animal, said to sweat blood — probably a parasitic
skin infection, though the phrase stuck and the beasts entered Chinese poetry as
heavenly horses.

Emperor Wu sent envoys with gold to buy breeding stock. Dayuan refused and, by
one account, killed the mission. The response was a punitive expedition under Li
Guangli that marched several thousand kilometres across desert and mountain,
failed catastrophically the first time, was reinforced and sent back, and in the
end besieged the Ferghana capital and came home with a few dozen superior
animals and a few thousand ordinary ones. Judged as economics it was lunacy.
Judged as a demonstration that Han power could reach across the Pamirs and
break a city, it worked exactly as intended. The oasis kingdoms in between drew
the obvious conclusion and began sending envoys of their own.

## The Corridor, the Wall and the Paperwork

Between the Yellow River bend and the oases of the Tarim Basin runs the Hexi
Corridor, a narrow strip of watered land pinned between the Qilian mountains and
the Gobi. Whoever held it separated the Xiongnu from the Tibetan-plateau peoples
to the south and controlled the only practical land route west. Han armies took
it in a series of campaigns from the 120s BCE onward, and the state then did
what it did best: it organized. Four commanderies were established along the
corridor — Wuwei, Zhangye, Jiuquan and Dunhuang — and settlers, convicts and
garrison troops were moved in to farm them. The rammed-earth wall was extended
out into the desert, not as a continuous barrier but as a line of beacon towers
and walled posts spaced within signalling distance, with mounted patrols between.

What survives from those garrisons is not glory but administration. Thousands of
inscribed wooden and bamboo slips have been recovered from rubbish pits at
Juyan, Dunhuang and other frontier sites, and they are the most vivid thing we
have from the period: duty rosters, receipts for millet, inventories of crossbow
bolts counted down to the individual arrow, medical notes, a soldier's request
for leave, drafts of letters home, the regulations for how many bundles of brush
to light on a beacon tower depending on how many raiders had been sighted.
Travellers carried passes recording their name, age, height, complexion, the
colour of their oxen and the contents of their cart, and the passes were checked
and logged at each barrier. The road west existed because a bureaucracy was
willing to feed and audit men standing in a desert for decades.

## A Relay, Not a Route

Almost nobody travelled the whole distance. The long-distance trade of the Han
period worked as a relay, with goods changing hands repeatedly among Tarim oasis
towns, Kushan territory in the south, Parthian middlemen further west, and the
merchant communities of the Mediterranean ports. A bolt of silk that reached
Rome had passed through many hands, each taking a margin, which is why a
material that was ordinary clothing in Chang'an was a luxury priced against
gold by the time it arrived.

Silk moved west in quantity partly because the Han state produced it as a fiscal
instrument. Households paid part of their tax obligation in woven cloth, bolts
of standard width and length functioned as a kind of currency in regions where
bronze coin was scarce, and soldiers and officials on the frontier were
sometimes paid in it. When the court bought peace from the Xiongnu or rewarded a
loyal oasis king, it did so in bales of silk, and much of that silk then moved
onward through steppe and desert markets without the court's involvement.
Lacquerware, bronze mirrors and iron went west as well. Coming east were horses,
woollen textiles, glass — which Chinese craftsmen did not make at Roman quality
— jade from Khotan, furs, aromatics, and eventually grapes, alfalfa for the new
horses, and other crops that took root in northwestern China.

Much of this moved under a diplomatic cover story. A foreign mission arriving
at the Han court with tribute was lodged, fed at state expense, and sent home
with gifts calculated to exceed in value whatever it had brought, because the
gifts expressed the emperor's magnanimity and the tribute expressed submission.
The arithmetic was obvious to everyone involved. Han officials complained in
memorials that the envoys crowding the western road were merchants who had
acquired a letter and a few curiosities, that many of them represented kingdoms
that had not sent them, and that some of the same men came back year after year
under different names. The court kept receiving them anyway. Trade dressed as
ritual was easier to justify in a political culture that regarded profit as a
low motive and the submission of distant peoples as a high one.

The silk itself was made by households. Mulberry trees grew on field margins
across the north and the Yangtze provinces, and the work of feeding the worms,
killing the pupae, reeling the filament and weaving it fell largely to women,
whose labour was assessed and taxed in bolts. State workshops produced the
elaborate polychrome weaves that show up in frontier tombs and, centuries later,
in desert graves along the Tarim rim. The technology was not a guarded secret so
much as an accumulated body of domestic practice, difficult to transplant
because it required the trees, the worms, the climate and the skills together —
which bought China several centuries of near monopoly before sericulture took
hold further west.

The relay also kept the two ends of it ignorant of each other. Parthia sat in
the middle and had no incentive to introduce its suppliers to its customers. In
97 CE an envoy named Gan Ying, sent west by the general Ban Chao, got as far as
the shore of a great western sea — most likely the Persian Gulf — and was told
by sailors there that the voyage onward could take two years and that many men
died of longing for home. He turned back. Roman writers meanwhile complained
about the drain of coin eastward for transparent cloth and knew the producers
only as the Seres, the silk people, a name attached to almost no reliable
information.

## What Else the Corridor Carried

Ban Chao's career shows how thin the Han grip on the west actually was. Over
roughly three decades he controlled the Tarim oases largely by manipulating
local rivalries with a handful of men and a reputation, installing friendly
kings and executing hostile envoys, rather than by garrisoning the region in
force. The Protectorate of the Western Regions existed on and off for two
centuries, lapsing whenever the court in Chang'an or Luoyang was distracted or
broke, and being reasserted when an energetic official pushed for it.

The corridor carried more than freight. Buddhism came east along it, arriving
first with Central Asian merchants and monks rather than as a state import; the
earliest translators working in China were men from Parthia, Sogdiana and the
Kushan lands, rendering Sanskrit concepts into a Chinese vocabulary borrowed
partly from Daoism because no other vocabulary existed. Centuries later the
cliffs at Dunhuang, at the western end of the Han commanderies, would be honeycombed
with painted cave shrines paid for by travellers and local families — the visible
residue of a religion that had walked in along a military road.

Nor was the desert corridor the only channel. Goods went south from Sichuan
through the mountains toward Burma and eastern India along tracks the court
barely controlled — the route that had put Sichuan bamboo in a Bactrian market
before any Han official set foot there. Ships worked the coast from the ports of
the far south to the Malay peninsula and on to India and Sri Lanka, meeting
vessels riding the monsoon out of the Red Sea. An embassy that reached the
southern Chinese coast in the second century CE claiming to come from the ruler
of Da Qin, the Roman empire, probably consisted of merchants who had made the
voyage from Egypt and improvised a title on arrival.

Disease moved too, and so did technology in both directions, and so did people:
hostages, brides, defectors, prisoners, and the mixed populations of the oasis
towns who spoke several languages because their livelihood required it.

The name is modern. A German geographer surveying routes for possible railways
in the 1870s coined Seidenstrasse — silk road — and it has been impossible to
dislodge since, though it flattens a shifting braid of desert tracks, mountain
passes and sea lanes into a single highway that never existed. The Han had no
such word. They spoke of the Western Regions, and their records of the place are
mostly about grain rations, horse fodder and the number of arrows in a tower.
