# The Qin Unification of China

Among the bronze weapons buried with the terracotta army outside Xi'an are
hundreds of crossbow triggers. They are small multi-part mechanisms — a sear, a
catch, a lever, pinned together — and the striking thing about them is that
parts from one will fit another. They were made to a specification, in
different workshops, by different hands, and they interchange. Many of the
weapons also carry short inscriptions naming the office that ordered them, the
foreman who ran the shop, and sometimes the individual worker. If a blade
failed inspection, the state knew exactly whose name to send for.

That is the Qin state in miniature: standardized output, documented
accountability, and an unmistakable implication that somebody would be punished.
The kingdom that used these methods to conquer the other six Chinese states and
declare an empire in 221 BCE lasted, in that form, about fifteen years. Almost
everything it built outlived it.

## A State Built for One Purpose

Qin sat in the Wei river valley at the western edge of the Chinese world, and
the older states of the east regarded it as half-barbarian — a frontier place
with rough manners and too many neighbours who rode horses. In the middle of
the fourth century BCE its ruler hired a reformer from abroad, usually known as
Shang Yang, and gave him the authority to rebuild the kingdom from the ground
up.

The reforms were systematic and brutal. Households were registered and grouped
into units of five and ten whose members were legally answerable for each
other's crimes; failing to report a neighbour's offence carried a penalty of its
own. Hereditary aristocratic rank was abolished and replaced by a ladder of
some twenty grades that a man climbed principally by presenting the heads of
enemy soldiers, with the accompanying land, tax exemptions, and legal
privileges attached to the grade rather than the bloodline. Agriculture and
textile production were rewarded; commerce and anything the state classified as
idleness were penalized. Old fiefs were dissolved and the territory
reorganized into counties run by appointed officers who could be removed. Law
was written down, published, and applied without regard to rank, which was
presented as fairness and functioned as terror.

The point of all this was not prosperity in any general sense. It was to
convert the entire population into two outputs — grain and soldiers — and to
strip out every intermediate loyalty that might absorb some of either. Shang
Yang's own end illustrated the system's indifference to persons: when his
patron died, his enemies moved against him, and the man who had written the
statutes was executed under them.

Qin also got lucky with water and geography. Its conquest of the Sichuan basin
early in the third century BCE gave it a second agricultural heartland, made
enormously productive by a diversion works on the Min river that split the flow
without damming it and irrigated the plain around Chengdu. A large canal cut
across the Wei valley added more. Two granaries, protected by mountains, feeding
an army that recruited by lottery from registered households.

## The Conquest

By the 230s BCE the balance had tipped. Qin had already broken Zhao's field army
in a catastrophic campaign a generation earlier, in which the surrendered
soldiers were reportedly killed en masse — the figures in the sources are not
believable, but the strategic result was. The six remaining states negotiated,
allied, betrayed each other, and were taken one at a time over about a decade.
Han fell first. Zhao followed. The Wei capital was reduced by diverting a river
into it. Chu, the largest, required a reported six hundred thousand men and a
commander who refused to move until he had them. Yan, whose crown prince had
gambled on an assassin carrying a dagger rolled inside a map — the attempt
failed farcically, with the king of Qin dodging around a pillar because court
protocol kept his guards' weapons out of the hall — was destroyed shortly
after. Qi, which had stayed out of every coalition in the hope of being left
alone, surrendered in 221 BCE without a serious fight.

The king of Qin then decided that no existing title fit what he had become. He
assembled a new one from two words with divine and archaic connotations,
producing a term best rendered as emperor, and called himself the First. His
successors would be the Second, the Third, and so on for ten thousand
generations.

## Making One Country Out of Seven

What followed in the next decade was an administrative reconstruction with no
real precedent. The court debated re-establishing princely domains for the
imperial family, the arrangement that had structured Chinese politics for
centuries, and the chancellor Li Si argued it down: enfeoffment was precisely
what had produced five hundred years of war. Instead the whole empire was cut
into commanderies, subdivided into counties, each with a civil administrator, a
separate military commander, and an inspector reporting independently to the
centre. Officials were appointed, salaried, transferred, and evaluated on
paperwork.

Then the standardization, which is what Qin is actually remembered for. Weights
and measures were fixed, with official bronze and pottery standards distributed
carrying the text of the imperial edict stamped into them, so that the rule
travelled with the object. Currency was unified on a round bronze coin with a
square hole. The script was regularized: regional variants that had drifted
apart over centuries were pruned toward a single official form, which made a
document written in one commandery legible in another. Axle widths were
specified, so that carts would run in the same ruts on the same roads — a
mundane-sounding rule that mattered enormously for moving grain.

Highways radiated from the capital. A canal was cut through the southern hills
to link the Yangtze watershed with the rivers flowing toward the Pearl,
allowing supplies to follow the armies south. In the north, the general Meng
Tian drove nomadic confederations out of the Ordos loop and connected and
extended the defensive walls the northern states had built against each other
into one long rammed-earth barrier — an ancestor of the Great Wall, not the
stone one visitors climb today. The walls that had divided the former states
internally were pulled down, their weapons confiscated and melted, and tens of
thousands of aristocratic families were relocated to the capital where they
could be watched.

## The Price

All of this ran on conscripted labour, taken from farming households by a legal
system with no interest in excuses. The First Emperor's own tomb complex east
of the capital absorbed an enormous workforce over decades: a stepped earthen
mound, and beyond it pits holding thousands of life-size ceramic soldiers in
formation, individually finished, originally painted, armed with real bronze
weapons. They were not found until farmers digging a well hit them in the
1970s.

The regime's intolerance of alternative authority extended to books. On Li Si's
proposal, privately held copies of historical records from the conquered states
and of the old poetry and documents were ordered burned, with exemptions for
technical works on medicine, divination, and agriculture and for the copies
held in the imperial library. Scholars who cited antiquity to criticize the
present were treated as a political problem, and tradition holds that several
hundred were executed, in a story later writers made more lurid than the
evidence supports.

It is worth noticing what did not happen, though. Caches of Qin legal documents
recovered from officials' tombs show a bureaucracy preoccupied with procedure —
how to assess a grain store, how to handle a runaway conscript, what
compensation applies to an injured ox. The penalties are harsh, but the system
is a system, not caprice. Qin's cruelty was not lawlessness. It was law with
nothing above it.

## Four Years

The First Emperor died in 210 BCE while touring the east. His chancellor and
his chief eunuch concealed the death long enough to forge the succession in
favour of a pliable younger son, reportedly packing the imperial carriage with
rotting fish to disguise what was happening inside it, and sent orders to the
capable elder son and to Meng Tian instructing them to kill themselves. They
obeyed.

Within a year the empire was in revolt. The traditional account begins with a
party of conscripts halted by rain, facing execution for arriving late and
deciding they might as well rebel — though the recovered Qin statutes prescribe
fines rather than death for exactly that offence, which suggests the famous
story has been sharpened somewhere in transmission. What is not in doubt is
that the rising spread immediately, that the old regional identities the empire
had spent fifteen years suppressing came straight back as rallying points, and
that the court destroyed itself in parallel: the chancellor executed, the
Second Emperor forced to suicide, the last Qin ruler surrendering in 206 BCE
to a rebel commander of peasant origin. The capital was sacked and burned.

And then the Han dynasty that emerged from the wreckage kept nearly all of it.
The commanderies and counties, the appointed officials, the registers, the
standard weights, the unified script, the codified law, the conscription, the
roads — retained, softened at the edges, and justified in a different
vocabulary. Han writers made an entire genre of denouncing Qin harshness while
governing through Qin institutions. The dynasty that gave its name to the
country in every European language lasted a decade and a half. The machinery it
assembled ran, with modifications, for the next two thousand years.
