# The Mauryan Empire and the Edicts of Ashoka

In the fourteenth century a sultan of Delhi had a polished sandstone column
dug out of the ground near a village in the Punjab, wrapped in padding, loaded
onto an enormous wheeled cradle, dragged to the Yamuna, floated downriver on
lashed boats, and re-erected on the roof of his hunting palace. It was an
extraordinary piece of engineering for a piece of stone whose purpose nobody
could explain. The column carried lines of writing. The sultan assembled the
learned men of his capital, and none of them could read a word of it.

They were looking at Brahmi, and the text was a public letter from a king who
had died more than fifteen hundred years earlier. It would be another five
centuries before anyone could say what it said.

## The Empire Chandragupta Built

The Mauryan state began in Magadha, on the middle Ganges, in the turbulent
decades after Alexander's army reached the Indus and turned back. Around 321
BCE a man named Chandragupta displaced the ruling Nanda dynasty and took the
throne at Pataliputra, a city at the junction of the Ganges and the Son whose
timber fortifications — enormous teak palisade beams, some of which have been
excavated at Patna — impressed the foreign visitors who saw them.

Chandragupta expanded fast, northwest into the territories Alexander's
successors held and south into the Deccan. When Seleucus, who had taken the
eastern share of Alexander's empire, came down through the passes around 305
BCE, the war ended in a treaty rather than a conquest: Seleucus ceded the
provinces along the Indus frontier and received five hundred war elephants,
which he used a few years later at the battle of Ipsus in Anatolia. He also
sent an ambassador, Megasthenes, whose account of India survives only in
quotations by later Greek and Roman writers — a mixture of sharp observation
about city administration and road measurement with material that was clearly
travellers' gossip.

The state Chandragupta and his successors ran was, by the standards of the
time, heavily administered. A political manual attributed to his minister
Kautilya, surviving in a text that was probably compiled and revised over
several later centuries, describes a kingdom governed through registered
villages, licensed guilds, state-run mines and salt works, controlled prices,
and a dense network of informers reporting on officials and on each other. Some
of that is idealized theory. But the Mauryan empire minted punch-marked silver,
maintained a trunk road running from the northwest across the Ganges plain, and
by the third century BCE administered territory from Afghanistan to Karnataka,
which is not something a light-touch government does.

Chandragupta's grandson came to the throne around 268 BCE after what later
tradition insists was a violent succession struggle. His name was Ashoka.

## Kalinga

Eight years into his reign, Ashoka invaded Kalinga, the coastal country
corresponding roughly to modern Odisha, and won. What makes the campaign
unusual is not the conquest but what he did afterwards, which was to have an
account of his own remorse carved into rock faces around the edges of his
empire.

The text — the thirteenth of his major rock edicts — states the cost in
figures: a hundred and fifty thousand people deported, a hundred thousand
killed in the fighting, and many times that number dead of other causes. It
then says that the king felt profound sorrow and regret at the slaughter, that
what weighs on him most is the suffering of the blameless — the brahmans and
ascetics, the householders, the people whose relatives and friends were
destroyed — and that he now considers the conquest of people by right conduct
to be the only conquest worth having. He adds that he hopes his sons and
grandsons will not think new conquests worth making, and that if they do
conquer, they should at least do it gently.

No other ancient ruler published anything remotely like this. Assyrian kings
itemized their atrocities as achievements. Egyptian pharaohs put the
smiting-of-enemies pose on temple walls for two thousand years. Here is a
sovereign spending money and skilled labour to inform his subjects, in
permanent stone, that his great military victory made him sick.

Whether the remorse was entirely sincere is a fair question. Kalinga was
conquered and stayed conquered; the same edict ends with a reminder that the
king still has power and that forest peoples who make trouble should consider
his capacity for punishment. Ashoka was renouncing further wars of expansion,
not the empire he already had.

## What Dhamma Asked For

The edicts talk constantly about dhamma, a word Ashoka uses to mean something
broader than a sectarian creed. He was personally a Buddhist — one inscription
records his visit to the Buddha's birthplace and his reduction of that
village's tax, and another warns monks and nuns against splitting the community
— but the public programme he promoted is mostly ethical rather than doctrinal.
Respect parents and elders. Be generous to ascetics and brahmans of every
sect. Treat servants and the poor decently. Do not kill living creatures
needlessly. Speak well of other people's beliefs rather than praising your own
at their expense, because praising your own sect immoderately damages it.

He backed this with administration and with public works. Officers of dhamma
were appointed to tour the provinces and check on welfare, on prisoners, on
the treatment of the old. Hunting expeditions were replaced by pilgrimage
tours. Long lists of animals were declared protected, and the royal kitchen,
which had been consuming large numbers of peacocks and deer daily, was reduced
to almost nothing. Wells were dug along the roads, shade trees planted, rest
houses built, medicinal plants procured and cultivated for the use of people
and animals alike. Envoys carrying the message were sent abroad — to Sri Lanka,
to the far south, and, according to the same thirteenth edict, to five
Hellenistic kings whom Ashoka names, which conveniently dates his reign against
Mediterranean chronology.

## Stone, Polish, and Lions

The physical form of the edicts matters. Most of the rock edicts sit on natural
boulders and cliff faces near roads and frontiers, where travellers would pass.
The pillar edicts are something else: monolithic shafts of fine sandstone
quarried near Varanasi, some of them twelve metres or more and weighing tens of
tonnes, transported hundreds of kilometres, and finished with a lustrous
mirror polish that later Indian stoneworkers never quite reproduced. On top sat
a bell-shaped lotus capital carrying a sculpted animal — a bull, an elephant, a
lion, or the four addorsed lions from Sarnath that independent India adopted as
its state emblem.

The language is Prakrit, the everyday speech rather than Sanskrit, and the
script varies with the audience: Brahmi across most of the subcontinent,
Kharoshthi in the northwest where Aramaic-derived writing was familiar, and at
Kandahar in southern Afghanistan a bilingual version in Greek and Aramaic for
populations left over from the Achaemenid and Macedonian periods. Ashoka was
not simply carving in the imperial language and hoping. He was translating.

## Losing and Recovering a King

The empire did not long survive him. Ashoka died around 232 BCE; within about
fifty years the last Mauryan was assassinated by his own army commander, and
the territory fragmented into the regional kingdoms that would characterize the
next several centuries. The edicts stayed where they were, increasingly
unreadable as Brahmi evolved into its many descendant scripts, until the
letters became merely decorative marks on old stones.

The recovery was the work of nineteenth-century Calcutta. James Prinsep, assay
master at the mint and an obsessive student of coins and inscriptions, cracked
Brahmi in the 1830s using short repetitive donation records at Buddhist stupas,
where the same formula — this is the gift of so-and-so — recurred with only the
donor's name changing. Once the values were fixed, the edicts spoke. They
turned out to be signed not with a dynastic name but with a title: Beloved of
the Gods, He Who Looks On With Affection.

Matching that title to a known king took decades more, drawing on Sri Lankan
Buddhist chronicles that preserved traditions about a great Mauryan patron.
The question was settled only in the twentieth century, when an inscription
found in the Deccan gave both the epithet and the personal name together. The
king who had asked to be remembered for what he did after Kalinga had spent two
thousand years anonymous on his own monuments.
