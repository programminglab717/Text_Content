# The Cities of the Indus Valley

Pick up a fired brick from the ruins at Mohenjo-daro, then pick up another from
Harappa, roughly six hundred kilometres upriver. They are, to a very close
approximation, the same brick. The proportions run one to two to four —
thickness, width, length — which is the ratio that lets a mason lay a wall in
alternating courses without cutting anything. The same ratio turns up at
Dholavira in the salt flats of Kutch and at Lothal near the Gulf of Khambhat.
Nobody has found the document that specified it. Nobody has found the official
who enforced it. The brick is simply the same everywhere, for something like
seven centuries, across a territory larger than Egypt and Mesopotamia combined.

That is the characteristic frustration of the Indus civilization. It left an
enormous amount of physical evidence and almost nothing that speaks. The
mature phase — the period when the big cities were functioning — ran from
around 2600 to 1900 BCE, contemporary with the Old Kingdom pyramids and the
Sumerian city-states. Its people built in baked brick on a scale nobody in the
region would match again for two thousand years. And they wrote, on seals and
tags and pottery, in a script that remains unread.

## A Grid, a Drain, and a Well on Every Block

Mohenjo-daro sits on the Indus floodplain in Sindh, and what survives of it
is startling to walk through because it looks planned in a way that ancient
cities usually do not. The main streets run roughly north-south and east-west,
wide enough for carts, dividing the lower town into large blocks. Lanes branch
off them. Houses turn blank walls to the street and open inward onto
courtyards, which is a design that answers heat, dust, and the desire for
privacy all at once, and which is still the ordinary house form across much of
Pakistan and northwest India.

The drainage is the part that tends to stop visitors. Wastewater from bathing
platforms and latrines ran out of houses through terracotta pipes or brick
channels into covered drains under the streets. The drains had inspection
covers and sediment traps — sumps where solids could settle and be shovelled
out. Somebody was emptying them. Somebody was replacing the covers. This is
municipal maintenance, sustained over generations, and it implies a level of
civic coordination that the rest of the archaeological record stubbornly
refuses to explain.

Wells are everywhere, many of them private, built of specially shaped wedge
brick that makes a strong circular shaft. Mohenjo-daro alone had hundreds. On
the raised western mound stands the Great Bath, a sunken brick tank about
twelve metres long, waterproofed with bitumen between two brick skins and
drained by a corbelled outflow. It is tempting to read ritual purification into
it, and most archaeologists do, cautiously, because the alternative
explanations are thin. But that is an inference, not a reading.

Dholavira shows the same instincts working in a harsher place. It sits on an
island in what is now a seasonal salt desert, and its builders cut enormous
reservoirs into the bedrock, channelling two seasonal streams into a cascade of
stone-lined tanks. The city's water storage capacity is out of all proportion
to its size. In that landscape it had to be.

## The Seals and the Silence

The most familiar Indus artefact is a small square seal of steatite, fired to a
hard white surface, usually carved with an animal in profile above a short line
of signs. The commonest animal is a bull-like creature with a single curving
horn — the so-called unicorn, almost certainly a stylized representation rather
than a claim about zoology — standing before an object that might be a feeding
trough or an incense burner. Zebu bulls, elephants, rhinoceroses, tigers, and
water buffalo appear too. A few seals show figures seated in what looks like a
yogic posture, surrounded by animals, and these have generated a century of
argument about continuity with later Indian religion that the evidence cannot
settle either way.

The signs above the animals are the problem. There are somewhere around four
hundred distinct ones, which is too many for an alphabet and too few for a
system where every sign is a word, suggesting a mixed script of the kind used
in early Mesopotamia and Egypt. But the inscriptions are brutally short. The
average is about five signs. The longest known text runs to a few dozen. There
is no bilingual — no Indus equivalent of the Rosetta Stone, no Persian king
helpfully repeating himself in three languages. Without a long text, patterns
cannot be tested; without a known related language, sound values cannot be
guessed. Proposals have linked the script to Dravidian, to Indo-Aryan, to
nothing linguistic at all. None has convinced the field.

What the seals were for is slightly clearer. Many have a boss on the back
pierced for a cord, and clay sealings survive bearing their impressions,
sometimes with the marks of the bags, jars, or bales they once secured. They
look like the tools of an economy that needed to identify ownership, contents,
or authorization along a supply chain.

## Weights, Beads, and the Voyage West

That economy reached a long way. Indus weights, made of cut and polished chert
in neat cubes, follow a consistent standard from tiny stones used for weighing
something precious up through large ones for bulk goods, with the smaller steps
doubling. The same system appears at every major site and at Indus-linked
trading posts abroad. A merchant in Gujarat and a merchant in the Punjab were
using compatible scales.

Craft production was specialized and, in places, industrial in character.
Carnelian was heated to deepen its colour, drilled with remarkably hard stone
bits to produce beads up to ten centimetres long, and etched with an alkaline
paste to burn white patterns into the red stone. These long barrel beads are
the signature Indus luxury good, and they turn up in the Royal Cemetery at Ur
in southern Mesopotamia. Mesopotamian texts of the period mention a distant
land called Meluhha that supplied carnelian, ivory, timbers, and copper, and
Meluhha is very probably the Indus. There was even, at one point, an
interpreter of Meluhhan language attested in Mesopotamia — which tells us
someone there could speak it, and that the knowledge died with him.

Lothal, on a tidal creek in Gujarat, has a large brick basin that its excavator
identified as a dock, with a spillway to maintain water level. The
interpretation is debated; it may have been a reservoir. Either way the site
was a bead-making and shell-working centre with clear maritime connections, and
Indus goods moved along the Gulf coast through Oman and Bahrain.

## Nobody Obviously in Charge

Here is the anomaly that keeps the Indus interesting. Contemporary Egypt and
Mesopotamia announce their rulers constantly: royal tombs stuffed with grave
goods, palaces, temples with priesthoods and land holdings, carved reliefs of
kings smiting enemies, king lists, boasting inscriptions. The Indus cities have
none of that. No structure has been confidently identified as a palace or a
temple. No burial stands out as royal — Harappan cemeteries are modest and
fairly uniform, with pottery and some ornaments. There is no monumental art
glorifying a ruler, no depiction of a battle, and remarkably little weaponry
compared with what you would expect.

This has produced a wide range of explanations: a merchant oligarchy, a
priestly elite whose authority was expressed through ritual purity rather than
display, a federation of city-states sharing standards the way trading partners
share a currency, even something closer to a collection of corporate lineages.
The honest position is that the absence of evidence is itself the finding. A
society that could standardize a brick across half a million square kilometres
and maintain sewers for centuries did not lack organization. It lacked, or
declined to produce, the particular habit of making power visible in stone.

## When the Rivers Moved

Around 1900 BCE the cities begin to fail. The process was not a single
catastrophe. Drains stopped being cleared and were built over. Houses were
subdivided. Craft standardization loosened, seals and weights went out of use,
long-distance trade thinned out. People kept living in many of these places,
but they stopped living in them as cities.

The most persuasive causes are environmental and slow. A major river system
east of the Indus — the seasonal Ghaggar-Hakra in India and Pakistan, along
whose banks an enormous number of Harappan sites cluster — appears to have lost
much of its flow as tributaries were captured by neighbouring drainages. At
roughly the same period, evidence from lake sediments and ocean cores points to
a weakening of the summer monsoon across the region. Agriculture that had
supported dense urban populations on floodplain silt became less reliable.
Settlement shifted east and south, toward the Ganges-Yamuna region and Gujarat,
into smaller villages growing crops better suited to a drier, more variable
climate, including millets and rice.

An older story, that invading chariot-borne Aryans sacked the cities, has not
survived scrutiny. The scattered unburied skeletons at Mohenjo-daro that once
served as evidence of massacre came from different levels and show no
convincing pattern of battle wounds. The decline was under way before any
plausible arrival date, and it looks like drought and dispersal rather than
conquest.

What persisted was quieter than the cities: crops, cattle, bead-working, house
plans, perhaps water rituals, certainly the long trade routes. A whole
civilization did not vanish. It de-urbanized, and it did so while still
refusing to tell us its name.
