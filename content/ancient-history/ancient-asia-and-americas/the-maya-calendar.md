# How the Maya Calendar Works

A Classic Maya monument usually opens by telling you what day it is, and it
takes most of a paragraph to do it. First an oversized introductory glyph, then
five numbers stacked in a column, then the name of the day in one count, then a
run of glyphs giving the lord presiding over that night, the age of the moon,
how many days the current lunation will run, and only then the day's position in
the solar year. Twenty or more glyph blocks can pass before the text gets around
to the king and what he did. The date is not preamble. For the people who
commissioned these stones, fixing an event in several interlocking cycles at
once was a large part of the point of carving it.

Three systems are doing the work, and they were not invented by the Maya. The
260-day count and the 365-day year were shared across Mesoamerica, and the Long
Count appears on monuments in the Gulf lowlands and Chiapas before it shows up
in the Maya heartland. What the Maya did was push the machinery further than
anyone else, and write far more of it down.

## Two Wheels of Different Size

The 260-day count, usually called the tzolk'in from a modern Yucatec coinage,
runs twenty named days against thirteen numbers. The day names cycle — Imix,
Ik', Ak'bal, K'an, and on to Ajaw — and the numbers cycle independently
alongside them. After 13 Ben comes 1 Ix, because the numbers have run out while
the names have not. Since 13 and 20 share no factors, every combination occurs
before any repeats, and the count closes after 260 days. It is tied to nothing
astronomical. It is a divinatory almanac, each day carrying a character that
shapes what should be done on it, who should be named on it, and what a child
born under it can expect. Day-keepers in the Guatemalan highlands have kept it
running without interruption ever since, which makes it one of the longest
continuously observed calendars anywhere.

Why 260? Nobody knows. The interval is close to human gestation, which is the
explanation Maya day-keepers themselves have offered. It is also, at the
latitudes where the count may have originated, the number of days between the
two passages of the sun through the zenith in one direction — a real observable,
marked by shadowless noon. It may simply be thirteen and twenty multiplied
together because both numbers were already loaded with meaning.

The second wheel is the haab, a 365-day year of eighteen months of twenty days
each, plus a tail of five. The months have names — Pop, Wo, Sip, and so on — and
the days within them are numbered from zero, the first day being described as
the seating of the month, the way a lord is seated in office. The five leftover
days, Wayeb, were dangerous: a gap in the machinery, a time to stay home, not
travel, not wash, not comb your hair.

The haab has no leap day. It drifts against the actual solar year by roughly a
quarter of a day annually, slipping a full month every hundred and twenty years
or so. The Maya certainly knew — their astronomical tables are accurate enough
that they could hardly not have — and chose not to correct it. A calendar used
to align ritual with cycles of its own does not need to track the seasons, and
the seasons could be watched directly.

## The Calendar Round

Run the two wheels together and a full date looks like 4 Ajaw 8 Kumk'u: a
tzolk'in position and a haab position. That pairing repeats only after both
cycles return to their starting alignment, which takes 18,980 days — 73 runs of
the 260-day count, 52 runs of the 365-day year, a little under fifty-two of our
years. This is the Calendar Round, and across most of Mesoamerica it was the
whole system. It works well for a human lifetime and badly for anything longer.
A date given in Calendar Round terms is ambiguous the way "the fourteenth of
March" is ambiguous: fine if everyone knows which century you mean.

## The Long Count and a Shell for Zero

The solution was to count days straight from a fixed starting point. The Long
Count is positional and mostly base-twenty: the k'in is one day, twenty k'in
make a winal, and then the pattern breaks. Eighteen winal, not twenty, make a
tun of 360 days — a deliberate distortion to bring the unit near the solar
year. Twenty tun make a k'atun of about twenty years, and twenty k'atun a
b'ak'tun of 144,000 days, near enough four centuries. A date like 9.12.11.5.18
is simply that many of each unit elapsed since the start.

The numerals are written with dots for ones and bars for fives, and the Maya
had a sign for zero — a shell, or a hand, or a variant shaped like a flower —
centuries before the concept reached medieval Europe. Scribes who wanted to show
off replaced the bar-and-dot numbers with the heads of the gods who personified
each number, and the most ambitious replaced the whole column with full-figure
portraits of numbers carrying period-glyphs on their backs, which turns a date
into a procession.

The starting point is a Calendar Round date, 4 Ajaw 8 Kumk'u, the day a previous
world ended and the current one was set in motion. On our calendar it falls in
August of 3114 BCE, a time when nobody in Mesoamerica was writing anything. The
match between the two systems rests on a correlation constant worked out from
colonial-era documents, eclipse records and, later, radiocarbon dates on
inscribed wooden lintels, and the most widely used version puts the two
calendars in a relationship that has held up well.

## The Machinery Underneath the Date

The glyphs that follow the Long Count on a monument are where the system gets
genuinely intricate. One of them names which of nine Lords of the Night was
ruling, a cycle that turns over every nine days regardless of everything else.
Several more make up a lunar series: how many days since the last new moon,
whether the current lunation belongs to a six-month set and which position it
occupies in that set, and whether this particular moon would run twenty-nine or
thirty days. Since a lunation is a bit over twenty-nine and a half days,
scribes had to alternate, and different cities alternated differently until
they synchronized their practice — an agreement reached between kingdoms, visible
in the inscriptions as a sudden convergence.

There is also an 819-day count, whose number is seven times nine times thirteen,
associated with the four directions and their colours and with a god of
lightning and royal descent. It rotated a station through the directions, and
the scribes recorded which station was current and how many days had passed
since it began.

Once a date was fixed, movement between dates was handled with distance numbers:
so many days, winal and tun forward or backward, with a glyph specifying the
direction of travel. This let a text lay out a reign as a chain — accession,
first captive, dedication of a temple, death — each link a counted interval, and
let a king assert that his accession fell a round number of cycles after an
ancestor's, or a mythological event's. Kingship in the Classic period ran on
period endings. A k'atun closing was an occasion for a new stela, a scattering
rite, and a portrait of the ruler who happened to be holding the office when
the cycle turned.

## Tables and Predictions

The four surviving Maya books, all of them post-Classic and all of them
survivors of an otherwise near-total destruction, show what the calendar was for
once the monuments stopped. The best-preserved contains almanacs organized on
the 260-day count for agriculture, beekeeping, rain and hunting, and behind them
two remarkable pieces of astronomy.

One is a Venus table. Venus returns to the same position relative to the sun
every 584 days on average, and five of those cycles equal eight haab years
almost exactly, which lets the planet be slotted into the calendar. The table
tracks the planet through morning star, disappearance, evening star and
disappearance again, assigns omens to each station, and includes instructions
for shifting the table when the accumulated error grows too large — the
correction is the impressive part, because it means the scribes were checking
their predictions against the sky and knew the average period was not quite 584.

The other is an eclipse table, built on the fact that eclipses can only happen
near the nodes where the moon's path crosses the sun's, at intervals of five or
six lunations. It does not predict which eclipses would be visible from Yucatan
— that is a harder problem — but it flags the dates on which one was possible,
which is exactly what you need if the danger is ritual rather than optical.

Nothing in any of this points to an ending. The thirteenth b'ak'tun completed on
a winter solstice in 2012, and the one Classic text that seems to mention that
date is damaged, brief, and reads like an ordinary anniversary note. Elsewhere
the scribes ran the other way: a text at Palenque projects an anniversary of a
king's accession thousands of years into the future, and a monument at Coba
writes out the creation date with more than twenty higher periods stacked above
the b'ak'tun, all of them full. The count was designed to keep going.
