# Reading the Oracle Bones

Start with a shoulder blade. An ox scapula is a broad, slightly dished plate of
bone with a ridge running down one face and a thick knob at the joint end,
which makes it useless for the job until a craftsman has worked on it. Somebody
saws the ridge flat, trims the socket, scrapes away the remaining tissue, and
polishes the surface until it is smooth and pale. Turtle shells get the same
treatment — specifically the plastron, the flat belly plate, sawn free along
its edges and flattened. In the workshops attached to the late Shang court at
Anyang this was routine production work, and the raw material arrived as
tribute: inscriptions on the edges of some shells record how many were sent,
and by whom.

What happened next is the best-documented divination procedure of the ancient
world, and it left behind the oldest substantial body of Chinese writing —
somewhere around a hundred and fifty thousand inscribed pieces, most of them
broken, recovered from a stretch of farmland in northern Henan.

## Preparing the Bone

The preparation that matters is invisible from the front. On the reverse of the
polished bone, a worker chisels a series of hollows: an elongated oval gouge,
and beside it or overlapping it a round bore, repeated in orderly columns
across the whole surface. Dozens of these pits might be cut into a single large
plastron. Their function is mechanical. Bone is tough and cracks
unpredictably, but bone thinned almost to translucency in a controlled shape
will fail along a controlled line. The hollows are stress concentrators. They
guarantee that when heat is applied, the bone will break in a way the diviner
can read rather than shattering or doing nothing at all.

That the Shang went to this trouble tells you something about what they thought
they were doing. A divination that produced random noise would have been easier
to arrange. They wanted a legible answer, which meant engineering the medium
until it answered legibly.

## The Crack

Heat goes into the hollow — a glowing point of some kind, a brand or a heated
rod, held against the thinned bone from behind. The bone pops. On the front
face a crack appears, and because of the shape of the gouges it appears in a
consistent form: a long vertical line from the oval, with a shorter branch
running off it sideways from the round bore. The resulting figure is a
right-angled T.

The Chinese character for divination is that shape. It is still in the writing
system, a vertical stroke with a small tick off its side, and it still appears
as a component in characters having to do with foretelling. The word for it may
well have started as the sound of the bone cracking.

What the diviners read in the crack is the part we have lost. The vertical was
fixed by the gouge, so the variable was the angle of the transverse branch, and
probably its length, sharpness, and whether it turned up or down. Somewhere
there was a body of interpretive knowledge that made one angle favourable and
another not. None of it was written down on the bones, and none of it survives.
We have the apparatus and the results and no manual.

## The Anatomy of an Inscription

After the cracking, a scribe incised a record beside the crack, and these
records follow a consistent format that makes them unusually easy to work with.

The preface gives the day and the diviner. Days were named by a cycle of sixty,
generated by running a list of ten signs against a list of twelve, and this
sexagenary cycle has been counting days in China without interruption ever
since — one of the longest continuously operating calendrical devices anywhere.
Then comes the charge: the proposition being put to the bone. Charges are
frequently stated in complementary pairs, one positive and one negative,
cracked on opposite sides of the same shell or on matching bones, as though the
procedure worked by testing a statement against its denial. Then, on many
pieces, the prognostication, almost always attributed to the king himself: the
king read the cracks and said that there would be trouble, or that it would
rain, or that the coming ten days would pass without misfortune. Finally, on a
minority of pieces, the verification — added afterwards, recording what
actually happened.

The verifications are what make the corpus feel alive. A king predicts trouble
within the coming period, and the scribe later notes that on such-and-such a
day there was indeed bad news, arriving from a particular direction. A
well-known sequence concerns the childbirth of a royal consort: the divination
asks whether the delivery will be good, the king reads the cracks and concludes
that it will be good only if the child comes on a certain day, and the
verification records that the birth came on the wrong day and that it was a
girl — therefore, in the scribe's flat notation, not good.

The subjects run from the cosmic to the domestic. Sacrifices to particular
ancestors, on the days named for them, with specified numbers of cattle, sheep,
and sometimes human victims. Whether to attack a named enemy people this
season, and whether the campaign will receive supernatural assistance. Whether
the harvest will be adequate. Whether it will rain, and whether the rain will
come from the north or the west. Hunting plans. Dream interpretation. The
king's toothache. And, week after week, the standing question about the next
ten days: will there be disaster?

Over the roughly two centuries of the Anyang record the practice changes in a
revealing direction. Early on, a corps of named diviners handles a wide range
of open questions. By the final reigns, the king is effectively the only
diviner, the topics have narrowed to routine sacrificial scheduling and
hunting, and the charges have stopped being questions at all — they are
statements that the coming period will be auspicious, cracked apparently as a
formality. The oracle had become a liturgy.

## From Chinese Pharmacies to Anyang

The bones re-entered history through a drugstore. Ground fossil bone, sold as a
traditional remedy under the name dragon bone, had been dug up by farmers near
the village of Xiaotun for a long time, and some of the pieces carried scratch
marks that the sellers occasionally scraped off to improve the product. In
1899 a senior scholar in Beijing, buying medicine, is said to have recognized
the scratches as archaic writing. Collectors moved in, prices rose, and dealers
concealed the source for years to protect their supply. The first published
collection of rubbings appeared in the early 1900s, and scholars began the slow
work of identifying graphs.

The decisive result came when ancestor names appearing on the bones were
matched against the list of Shang kings preserved in the great Han-dynasty
history written more than a thousand years after the dynasty fell. They lined
up. A king list that sceptics had treated as legend turned out to have been
transmitted accurately, which changed how everyone thought about the reliability
of early Chinese historiography.

Systematic excavation at Anyang began in the late 1920s and revealed that the
site was not merely a bone source but the last Shang capital: palace and temple
foundations of rammed earth, bronze foundries, huge shaft tombs with ramps,
chariot burials, and sacrificial pits containing hundreds of human victims. In
the 1970s the intact tomb of a royal consort was found — the same consort who
appears repeatedly in the divination records leading troops, conducting rituals
and, in that one sequence, giving birth. Her name on the bronzes matched her
name on the bones. Text and burial confirmed each other.

## A Script That Never Broke

Around four thousand distinct graphs appear in the corpus, of which perhaps a
third to a half can be read with confidence. The rest are mostly names of
people and places, which is exactly the category hardest to crack.

What is remarkable about the script is how finished it already is. It contains
straightforward pictures, abstract indicators, characters borrowed purely for
their sound, and — most tellingly — compounds that pair a meaning element with
a sound element, which is the principle that generates the overwhelming
majority of Chinese characters to this day. A writing system does not arrive at
that structure immediately. Whatever came before Anyang, on materials that did
not survive burial, must have been developing for a long time.

And unlike cuneiform or hieroglyphs, this system never went out of use. The
graph for a tripod cooking vessel, the graph for a person, the graph for the
sun: draw them the way a Shang scribe did, and a literate reader today will
often recognize them. The bones were unreadable for three thousand years
because nobody had seen them, not because the writing had died.
