# The Vedas Before Writing

The oldest surviving literature of India was composed by people who had no
alphabet, and it was preserved for well over a thousand years by men who
refused to use one after it became available. The Rigveda — 1,028 hymns
arranged in ten books, addressed mostly to gods of fire, storm, dawn, and an
intoxicating pressed plant — was carried from mouth to mouth across roughly
sixty generations before anyone thought it proper to write it down. When it was
finally committed to manuscript, comparison of the recitation traditions with
the written versions showed that the memorizers had been the more reliable
medium.

This is not an anecdote about impressive feats of memory. It is a fact with
consequences, because it shaped what the text is, who was allowed near it, and
how a society organized itself around a body of sound.

## The Machinery of Memory

An oral tradition that merely wants to be remembered can tolerate drift. Epics
change; storytellers improvise; each generation adjusts the emphasis. The Vedic
tradition could not work that way, because the hymns were understood to be
effective only if pronounced exactly — right syllables, right pitch, right
pauses. A mispronounced formula was not a stylistic lapse but a ritual failure,
and there are Brahmana passages warning about the consequences of getting a
word's accent wrong. Preservation therefore had to be engineered.

The basic version of the text, recited as continuous speech with the ordinary
sound changes that happen where words meet, is the samhita-patha. From it the
tradition derived a set of increasingly unnatural recitations whose purpose was
error-checking. The pada-patha breaks the stream into separate words, undoing
those sound changes and exposing each word in its dictionary form. The
krama-patha recites overlapping pairs: first and second word, second and third,
third and fourth, so that every word appears twice, in two different contexts.
Beyond that lie the woven recitations — jata and ghana — which take those pairs
and run them forward, backward, and forward again in fixed patterns, so that a
short line of verse expands into a long chant in which every word is spoken
many times, always bracketed by the words that must surround it.

The effect is a checksum. A word that slipped out of place in one recitation
would collide audibly with the pattern in another. A mastered reciter could not
easily forget a word without the structure telling him so. Alongside these
techniques the tradition developed a formal science of phonetics — descriptions
of where in the mouth each sound is articulated, rules for the changes that
occur at word junctions, treatises specific to each school's version of the
text. Indian grammatical analysis, which reached extraordinary sophistication
in Panini's compressed rule-system for Sanskrit sometime in the middle of the
first millennium BCE, grew directly out of this conservation work. Grammar in
India began as a branch of ritual maintenance.

## Cattle, Chariots, and Seven Rivers

The world the earliest hymns describe is not urban. There are no cities in the
Rigveda worth the name, no bureaucracy, no coinage, no writing. Wealth is
cattle, and the ordinary word for a raid or a battle is built from the root for
cow-seeking. Horses matter enormously — fast horses, and the light spoked-wheel
chariot they pull, which appears in the hymns as both a weapon and an image of
speed and prestige. Barley is the grain. Patrons give poets cows, horses, and
gold ornaments, and the poets say so in the hymns, at length.

The geography points to the northwest: the Indus and its tributaries, the land
of seven rivers, with the Ganges barely mentioned. Society is organized in
tribes — Bharatas, Purus, Yadus and others — led by chiefs whose authority
rests on success in raiding and generosity in distribution rather than on
inherited office. One long hymn celebrates a battle of ten kings in which a
coalition attacked the Bharatas at a river crossing and lost. These are the
politics of a cattle-keeping people with good horses and no fixed borders.

The gods match the society. Indra, who gets more hymns than anyone, is a
drinker and a fighter who smashes the serpent Vritra and releases the pent-up
waters. Agni is fire, and therefore the priest of the gods, because it is fire
that carries an offering upward. Varuna watches over oaths and order. Soma is a
plant, pressed and filtered and drunk, and also a god, and the whole ninth book
is addressed to it in the act of being strained. Ushas, the dawn, gets some of
the most purely beautiful verse in the collection.

## Moving East, and Finding Iron

Over the following centuries the centre of gravity shifts southeast, out of the
Punjab and into the plain between the Yamuna and the Ganges. The later Vedic
texts know this country well. They name new tribes and new confederations —
the Kurus and Panchalas above all — and they know rice, which the Rigveda does
not, along with a dark metal that the earlier hymns lack. Archaeologically the
period lines up with a distinctive thin grey pottery painted in simple designs,
found across the doab, and with the spread of iron working in the early first
millennium BCE.

Iron is usually credited with making this expansion possible, on the argument
that clearing the dense monsoon forest of the middle Ganges required iron axes.
The claim is probably overstated — fire clears forest more cheaply than any axe
— but the combination of iron tools, rice cultivation on wet alluvial soil, and
settled villages changed the economic base. Cattle-wealth on the move gave way
to grain surplus in one place. Chiefs became kings with territory. The rituals
grew accordingly: the rajasuya consecration, the year-long horse sacrifice in
which a stallion was released to wander and whatever land it entered was
claimed, elaborate offerings that took months and required teams of specialists.

It is in this later period that the four-part division of society into priests,
warriors, producers, and servants is first stated plainly, in a late hymn that
describes the categories as emerging from the dismembered body of a cosmic
being. The hymn reads as a justification written after the fact. Earlier
material shows a much looser arrangement, with poets and patrons and clan
membership mattering more than any fixed hereditary rank. What hardened over
the following centuries was not only the ranking but the idea that it was built
into the structure of the world.

## The Sacrifice as Engine

Later Vedic religion is best understood as a technology. The fire altar is a
machine for producing effects — rain, cattle, sons, long life, victory,
eventually a good afterlife — and the sacrifice works because it is performed
correctly, not because a god chooses to be generous. The Brahmana texts, prose
commentaries on ritual practice, are relentlessly technical: which brick goes
where, what formula accompanies which gesture, what to do when something goes
wrong, what any given action stands for in the larger system of
correspondences. They connect syllables to seasons, altar dimensions to years,
body parts to cosmic regions, in a dense web of equivalences that made the
ritual an operating model of the universe.

This gave enormous leverage to the specialists who knew the operating
instructions. A great sacrifice required several classes of priest working
simultaneously from different collections — one reciting verses, one chanting
melodies, one muttering the formulas that accompanied the physical acts, one
supervising the whole performance and correcting errors. Their fees, often
described in the texts themselves, were substantial. The elaboration of the
ritual and the elaboration of priestly authority advanced together.

## The Turn Inward

Somewhere in the later part of this period, a different line of thought
surfaces in texts appended to the ritual literature — the Aranyakas, meant for
forest study, and then the Upanishads. Their tone is unmistakably new. Instead
of specifying which formula accompanies the pouring of clarified butter, they
ask what the ritual means, and then whether the external performance is needed
at all. The important fire, one line of argument goes, is the one in the body.
The important offering is breath. What underlies the self is what underlies
everything.

Out of these dialogues come the ideas that would dominate later Indian
religious thought: an ultimate reality beneath appearances, an inner self
identical with it, action producing consequences that carry across lives, and
release from the cycle as the highest goal. They also produce a new social
type, the renouncer who walks away from household, property, and sacrificial
duty altogether — a figure that the earlier system had no place for and that
would within a few centuries anchor whole movements, including the ones founded
by the Buddha and by Mahavira.

None of this displaced the older text. The hymns went on being memorized,
syllable by accented syllable, by boys who began the work young and finished it
after years, in schools that transmitted particular recensions through
particular lineages. Writing arrived in India, and the reciters kept reciting.
A written Veda was, for a long time, regarded as a thing of low value —
something to be distrusted, because a page cannot be examined on its accent and
a page does not have a teacher standing behind it.
