# Early Japan and Korea Across a Narrow Sea

From the hills above Busan on a clear day you can see Tsushima. From the
southern end of Tsushima you can see Iki, and from Iki the hills of northern
Kyushu. The water between the Korean peninsula and the Japanese islands is
about two hundred kilometres at its narrowest, broken into three manageable
legs by islands that a rowed and sailed boat could cross in a day each,
weather permitting. For most of the period covered here that strait was not a
border. It was the busiest road in the region, and almost everything that
changed life in early Japan came across it.

## Rice, Bronze and the End of the Jomon World

The Jomon people had been in the archipelago for thousands of years, making
some of the oldest pottery in the world, living well on acorns, chestnuts, deer
and an extraordinarily productive coastline. They were not farmers in any
serious sense and did not need to be. Then, in the first millennium BCE —
conventionally the last few centuries of it, though radiocarbon work has pushed
the earliest paddies back considerably further — wet rice cultivation appeared
in northern Kyushu, arriving as a package. It came with the irrigated field
itself, with the wooden spades and stone reaping knives to work it, with
raised-floor granaries, with new pottery, with weaving, and shortly afterward
with bronze and iron.

Nothing in the Jomon record leads up to this. The package is Korean, and the
skeletons change too: the people buried in the new farming districts of
northern Kyushu and western Honshu tend to be taller and differently
proportioned than their Jomon neighbours, and the likeliest reading is a real
movement of people, absorbed over centuries into the existing population rather
than replacing it. The archipelago's later population looks like a mixture, and
the mixture happened unevenly, which is part of why the far north and the
Ryukyus remained culturally distinct for a very long time.

Farming brought stored surplus, and stored surplus brought the rest of it.
Yayoi villages acquired ditches, palisades and watchtowers; at Yoshinogari in
Saga, the defended enclosure is enormous. Burials from the period include
skeletons with stone arrowheads embedded in bone and, more than once, bodies
missing their heads. Bronze arrived first as Korean-style slender daggers and
mirrors, then diverged: Japanese smiths began casting bells, the dotaku, which
grew steadily larger and thinner until they could not have been rung at all and
were buried in hillsides in caches, apparently as ritual deposits. That is a
recognizable pattern — an imported object detaching from its original use and
becoming something local and sacred.

## What the Chinese Records Saw

The earliest written descriptions of the islands are Chinese, and they exist
because the Han empire had a foothold on the peninsula. After conquering the
northern Korean state of Joseon around the end of the second century BCE, Han
established commanderies there, the most durable of them centred near modern
Pyongyang, which survived for four hundred years as a Chinese provincial
outpost, a market and a conduit for prestige goods. A gold seal dug up in the
eighteenth century on an island in Hakata Bay, granted by the Later Han court to
a small Wa kingdom, shows that envoys from northern Kyushu were making the trip
to that world by the first century CE.

The fullest early account describes Wa as dozens of small communities, some of
them sending tribute, ruled at one point by an unmarried woman named Himiko who
governed through her command of spirits, kept a thousand female attendants, was
seen by almost no one, and was succeeded after a period of disorder by a young
kinswoman. Her envoys reached the Wei court and came home with a title and a
consignment of bronze mirrors. Where her domain actually lay — in Kyushu or in
the Kinai plain near the later imperial heartland — has been argued over for
three centuries, and the argument has never been purely archaeological, because
the answer bears on how old the Yamato line really is.

## Tombs and Horses

From the third century onward, the elites of the Kinai and beyond began burying
their dead under enormous earthen mounds. The distinctive Japanese form is the
keyhole: a round tumulus joined to a trapezoidal forecourt, surrounded by one or
more moats, with the slopes revetted in stone and the terraces lined with
haniwa — unglazed clay cylinders, some plain, some topped with models of
houses, boats, shields, horses, warriors and shamanesses. The largest of them,
in the Osaka plain, are among the biggest tombs ever built anywhere by volume of
earth moved. They required organized labour on a scale that implies a state, or
at least a confederation with a dominant partner.

The grave goods change over the period, and the change is diagnostic. Early
mounds hold mirrors, beads and objects of ritual authority. Fifth-century ones
hold iron armour, iron weapons, and above all horse trappings: bits, stirrups,
gilt-bronze ornaments, saddle fittings. Horses were not native to the islands in
any useful number. Riding gear, the techniques that went with it, and the
armourers who made the plate all came from the peninsula, along with the potters
who built sloping tunnel kilns into hillsides and fired the hard grey stoneware
called sue ware at temperatures Japanese kilns had never reached.

## Gaya Iron and the Three Kingdoms

The peninsula in these centuries was not one country. Goguryeo held the north
and much of Manchuria, a cold, mountainous, militarized state that pushed south
whenever it could; a stone stele raised for one of its greatest kings still
stands beside the Yalu, recording campaigns that reached deep into the south and
mentioning the Wa as an enemy. Baekje held the southwest, a maritime kingdom
oriented toward the sea and toward Japan. Silla, in the southeast, started as
the least of them and finished as the one that won. Between Baekje and Silla, in
the lower Nakdong valley, lay Gaya — never a single kingdom, but a cluster of
polities sitting on some of the best iron ore in the region.

Gaya iron is the hinge. Bundles of iron ingots turn up in Kyushu tombs and Gaya
tombs alike, and the ceramics, armour and burial customs of the two shores
converge so closely in this period that particular objects are hard to assign to
one side or the other. Yamato interests in the peninsula were real, and the
Japanese chronicles later described them as a colony; Korean scholarship reads
the same evidence as trade and mercenary service. What is clear is that a
Yamato elite that wanted iron, horses, armourers and literate administrators
had to deal with Gaya and Baekje to get them, and paid in troops.

## Buddhism, Writing and the Immigrant Families

Buddhism came to the peninsula from China in the late fourth century, taken up
first by Goguryeo and Baekje and only much later, and reluctantly, by Silla.
From Baekje it crossed to the Yamato court in the sixth century as a diplomatic
gift: an image, some sutras, and a covering letter recommending the new teaching
in terms of the benefits it would bring. The court split. One faction backed the
foreign deity, others blamed it for plague and threw the image in a canal. The
dispute was settled by a short civil war, and the winners built the first
permanent temple in the islands using architects, tile-makers, carpenters and
an image-caster sent over from Baekje.

Writing arrived the same way, in the hands of people. Scribal lineages
descended from peninsular immigrants — the Hata, the Aya and others — kept the
Yamato court's records, managed its treasuries, and supplied its weavers,
saddlers and smiths. The tradition that a scholar named Wani brought the
Confucian Analects to the court is tidier than whatever actually happened, but
the shape of it is right: literacy in Japan began as a foreign professional
skill practised by resident families in a language that was not the spoken one.

## The Year the Traffic Reversed

In the middle of the seventh century Tang China allied with Silla and destroyed
the other two kingdoms. Baekje fell first. Yamato, with long obligations to the
Baekje house, sent a fleet to restore it and lost catastrophically at the mouth
of the Geum river in 663 to a smaller, better-handled Tang and Silla force.
Goguryeo was gone within five years.

The consequences on the Japanese side were immediate and constructive. Refugee
Baekje nobles and engineers arrived in numbers and were given rank and land.
They built Korean-style fortifications across northern Kyushu and along the
Inland Sea — ring walls on hilltops, and an earthwork with a water-filled ditch
guarding the approach to the regional headquarters — against a Tang invasion
that never came. The court, badly frightened, spent the following half-century
turning itself into something that could not be picked apart so easily: land
and population registers, provincial governors, a legal code modelled closely on
Tang statutes, a permanent capital laid out on a Chinese grid, and an official
history.

That history, compiled in the early eighth century by scribes whose own
ancestors had come across the strait, was written in Chinese and organized
around an unbroken imperial line descended from the sun. It kept a great deal of
peninsular material because it had to, and it recast the rest. The sea that had
been a road for eight centuries became, in the telling, a moat.
