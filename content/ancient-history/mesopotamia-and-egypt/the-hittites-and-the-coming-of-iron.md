# The Hittites and the Coming of Iron

Among the objects wrapped into the bandages of Tutankhamun's mummy was a dagger with an iron blade. In a tomb stuffed with gold to the point of vulgarity, that blade was arguably the rarer thing. Analysis of its metal has found the nickel content characteristic of meteoric iron, which means it was not smelted from ore at all. It fell out of the sky, someone recognized that the lump was workable, and it was forged into a weapon fit to accompany a king into the afterlife. A dagger made of sky-metal is not a military technology. It is a curiosity of immense value, closer in status to a gemstone than to a sword.

Hold that image while considering the story usually told about iron and the Hittites: that the kings of Hattusa discovered how to smelt it, guarded the secret as a state monopoly, used iron weapons to dominate Anatolia, and that when their empire fell the secret escaped and the Iron Age began. It is a tidy story with a clear mechanism and a satisfying collapse, and it has been repeated in textbooks for well over a century. Nearly every part of it is wrong, and the way it went wrong is more interesting than the story itself.

## What the Bronze Age Actually Ran On

Bronze is copper with roughly a tenth part tin, and the two ingredients are wildly unequal in their distribution. Copper is common. Cyprus alone supplied so much of it that the island's name and the metal's are tangled together, and there were workings in Anatolia, the Sinai, the Balkans and the Alps. Tin is not common. There is essentially none in the eastern Mediterranean basin. It had to come from a long way off — from sources in Central Asia, and probably in time from the far west of Europe — along routes that crossed the territory of every polity in between.

The material consequences of that imbalance are visible in the archaeology. A merchant ship that went down off the Turkish coast at Uluburun in the late Bronze Age was carrying ten tons of copper in flat four-handled ingots and about a ton of tin, along with Canaanite storage jars, ebony, ivory, glass ingots and a scatter of goods from half a dozen cultures. That ratio is not a coincidence; it is the recipe, loaded as cargo. Centuries earlier, Assyrian merchants operating out of a trading quarter at Kanesh in central Anatolia left behind an archive of their correspondence, and much of it concerns donkey caravans hauling tin and textiles inland and silver back home. Bronze was a diplomatic achievement as much as a metallurgical one. To keep an army equipped you needed treaties, safe roads, credit, and a functioning international system.

The Hittites lived inside that system rather than above it. Their empire, run from the fortified upland capital of Hattusa in the bend of the Halys river, was one of the great powers of the age, capable of fighting Ramesses II to a standstill at Kadesh and then concluding with Egypt the earliest peace treaty of which we have both parties' texts. Their language had been an enigma until a Czech scholar, Bedřich Hrozný, cracked it during the First World War by noticing that a ritual sentence about eating bread and drinking water contained words that looked uncannily like their Indo-European equivalents. The archives at Hattusa turned out to be enormous: treaties, edicts, prayers, oracle reports, festival calendars, and letters to and from every court that mattered.

## The Letter That Started the Myth

Somewhere in that archive is a letter from a Hittite king to a foreign monarch — most likely an Assyrian one — dealing with a request for iron. The king's reply is a polite deferral. Good iron is not available at the moment in the place where it is produced, a district in the southeast of the empire; the season is wrong, the work is not finished; in the meantime here is an iron dagger blade as a gift, and the rest will follow later.

Read that letter for what it says rather than what it has been made to say. Iron is being handled as a scarce prestige commodity, sent in single small pieces between kings, and even the Hittite king cannot produce it on demand. This is not the correspondence of a state sitting on a strategic weapons monopoly. It is the correspondence of a state that has a modest and unreliable supply of something fashionable and is managing a diplomatic request as gracefully as it can. The rest of the Hittite evidence agrees: iron in their texts turns up as thrones, statuettes, ritual implements, ceremonial daggers, objects whose function is to be impressive. Nobody was arming infantry with it.

## Why Iron Is Hard

The difficulty is not finding the ore. Iron ore is one of the most widespread useful substances on the planet; it can be dug out of hillsides, scraped off the bottoms of bogs, and picked up as loose nodules in a hundred landscapes. The difficulty is everything after that.

Copper melts at a temperature a good charcoal furnace with bellows can reach, and molten bronze can be poured into a mould and cast into a finished shape. Iron melts far higher, beyond what any ancient furnace could manage. What a bloomery furnace produces instead is a bloom: a spongy, porous lump of iron mixed through with glassy slag, formed in the solid state at a temperature well below melting. That lump is not usable. It has to be reheated and hammered, repeatedly, to squeeze the slag out and weld the metal together, and a great deal of the original mass is lost in the process. There is no casting, no pouring, no mould. Every iron object has to be beaten into existence.

Worse, what you get at the end of that labour is wrought iron, which is soft. A well-made bronze blade will hold an edge better than a plain iron one. Iron only becomes the superior metal once smiths learn to add carbon to it — soaking the hot metal in charcoal so the surface absorbs carbon and becomes steel — and then to quench it in water to harden it, and then to temper it so that the hardened edge does not shatter. Each of those steps is a separate piece of knowledge, none of them obvious, all of them acquired slowly and unevenly across generations of workshops. The reason iron took so long to displace bronze is not that nobody thought of it. It is that for a long stretch iron was simply worse, and expensive in effort besides.

## The Collapse Did the Work

Around the end of the thirteenth century BC the eastern Mediterranean came apart. Hattusa was burned and abandoned. Ugarit, the great Syrian port, was destroyed and never reoccupied, its last tablets baked in the fire that ended it. The Mycenaean palaces fell, Cyprus was disrupted, and Egypt recorded desperate fighting against coalitions of seaborne raiders and then withdrew from its Asian possessions. The causes are still argued over — drought, earthquake, migration, internal revolt, the brittleness of top-heavy palace economies, most likely several of these feeding one another — but the effect on trade is not in doubt. The long, fragile supply lines that carried tin across continents were among the first things to break.

That is what made iron attractive. A smith in a diminished Levantine town or a Greek village could not obtain tin and might not obtain much copper either, but there was iron ore within a day's walk, and the knowledge of how to work it had been accumulating for centuries in a small way. Iron did not win because it was better. It won because it was local. It required no treaty with a distant king, no caravan crossing three frontiers, no credit arrangement that depended on the survival of a palace bureaucracy. An empire is a prerequisite for bronze. Iron will tolerate a dark age.

The archaeology bears this out in the details. Iron objects become common first in Cyprus and the Levant, in the generations on either side of the collapse, and they are ordinary objects — knives, tools, fittings — rather than royal gifts. True carburized steel weapons show up later still, and patchily, as the technique spread from workshop to workshop. The transition took centuries and looked nothing like a released secret.

So where did the monopoly story come from? Largely from reading the evidence backwards. Nineteenth and early twentieth century scholars inherited the convenient three-age scheme of stone, bronze and iron, then went looking for the agent of the third transition. The Hittites were conveniently placed in Anatolia, which has iron ore; they had vanished dramatically at exactly the right moment; and their archives contained a letter that mentioned iron. The pieces fit if you do not press them. Press them, and what the Hittites turn out to have possessed is not a secret of metallurgy but a normal share of a difficult new craft, practised mainly on objects meant to be admired, in an empire whose real strength rested on chariots, fortifications, treaties, and the ordinary alloyed copper that everybody else was using too.
