# The Phoenician Alphabet

A stone sarcophagus lifted out of a tomb shaft at Byblos by French excavators in the early 1920s carries a line of writing cut along the edge of its lid. The text is short and businesslike, as funerary curses tend to be: it names the man inside, names the son who buried him, and promises trouble for anyone who comes to disturb the box. What matters about it is not the threat but the signs. There are twenty-two of them in the system, they run from right to left, they are built from strokes and hooks and small triangles that a competent mason could cut in an afternoon, and there is not a single vowel among them. The date has been argued over for a century, but it sits somewhere near the turn of the first millennium BC. Anyone who has learned to read English is looking at a distant ancestor of their own letters.

The thing worth noticing first is how few signs there are. The scripts that dominated the literate Near East before this one were large. Cuneiform in its working form required a scribe to control several hundred signs, each of which might function as a word, a syllable, or a silent classifier depending on where it sat. Egyptian hieroglyphic writing was comparably demanding, and the hieratic shorthand used for everyday documents did not reduce the underlying inventory so much as speed up its execution. Both systems produced a professional class: men who trained for years, who held status because of that training, and who had an obvious interest in the difficulty of what they did. Twenty-two signs dissolve that arrangement. A merchant can learn them in a week and keep his own accounts.

## One Sound, One Sign

The Phoenician script is not quite an alphabet in the sense we now use the word. Specialists usually call it an abjad, a system in which every sign stands for a consonant and the vowels are left for the reader to supply. This sounds like a crippling defect until you look at the language it was designed for. Phoenician, like Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, and their relatives, builds most of its vocabulary on skeletons of three consonants. The root k-t-b has to do with writing; slot different vowels and prefixes into it and you get the verb, the agent, the document, the act. The consonants carry the lexical meaning and the vowels carry the grammar, and a reader who knows the language and the situation recovers the grammar without much effort, in the same way that an English reader silently supplies the missing sense in a telegram or a headline.

Because the system is phonetic all the way down, it has no boundary. A cuneiform scribe writing an unfamiliar foreign name had to hunt for signs that approximated its syllables. A Phoenician writing the same name simply wrote the consonants he heard. This is the property that makes the script portable in a way that no earlier system had been. It does not encode a vocabulary or a body of learning. It encodes speech, and any speech will do.

## The Ox and the House

The signs did not appear from nothing, and their names give the game away. The first letter is aleph, which is also the ordinary word for an ox. The second is bet, a house. Further along come mem, water, and ayin, an eye, and resh, a head, and kaph, the palm of a hand. Turn the archaic form of aleph on its side and you can still see a horned head; the early bet is a simple floor plan with a doorway. The principle behind this is acrophony: draw the thing, then use the drawing for the first sound of its name. An ox head no longer means ox. It means the glottal catch that begins the word.

The prototypes for these pictures came out of Egypt. Egyptian scribes had long possessed a set of uniliteral signs, each standing for a single consonant, sitting inside their larger apparatus of logograms and multi-consonant signs and classifiers. They never took the obvious step of throwing away the rest and writing with the small set alone, presumably because the rest was the tradition and the tradition was the point. Somebody else took that step. Scratched inscriptions found at the turquoise mines of Serabit el-Khadim in Sinai, and a pair of rough texts cut into a rock face at Wadi el-Hol in the Egyptian desert, show a handful of Egyptian-looking pictures being used for their Semitic sound values by people who were plainly not trained Egyptian scribes. The letters were made by outsiders: Semitic-speaking labourers, soldiers, or traders working in or near Egypt, who borrowed the shapes without inheriting the rules.

Several centuries separate those scratchings from the tidy twenty-two of the Byblos lid, and the record across that gap is thin and hard to read. What the surviving material shows is a long process of reduction and standardization: the pictures losing their detail, rotating, settling into stances, and the inventory shrinking to fit the consonants Phoenician actually distinguished. By the time the script reaches its mature form the pictures are gone. What remains is a set of abstract marks with picture names, the way a modern keyboard has a key called Escape.

## A Script Built for Cargo

The Phoenicians themselves were the inhabitants of a string of cities along the Levantine coast — Byblos, Sidon, Tyre, Arwad and their neighbours — hemmed in by mountains and facing the sea, which shaped their economy for them. They sold cedar to treeless Egypt, and the purple dye extracted at enormous trouble from murex shells, and glass, and finished metalwork, and they carried other people's goods besides their own. Their settlements and trading posts eventually reached Cyprus, Sicily, Sardinia, North Africa and the Atlantic coast of Iberia. Carthage began as one of these foundations and outlived the homeland.

That network did more to spread the alphabet than any deliberate campaign could have. A script that can be learned quickly and written fast on a scrap of anything travels with the people who use it, and it turns up wherever they stop. Its portability came at a cost we still pay: because Phoenician scribes wrote their letters, contracts, and literature on papyrus, and papyrus rots in a damp coastal climate, almost none of it survives. A people who gave the Mediterranean its writing left behind a corpus consisting largely of what happened to be cut into stone or metal — dedications, tomb warnings, votive stelae, bowls, inscribed arrowheads. We read their neighbours' accounts of them far more often than we read them. Even the Greek word for a papyrus roll, and so ultimately the word Bible, seems to preserve the name of Byblos, the port the material came through.

## What the Greeks Changed

Somewhere around the eighth century BC, and probably in a trading context where the two peoples were in daily contact, Greeks took the script over. The borrowing is not a theory to be argued about; it is written into the letters themselves. Alpha, beta, gamma, delta mean nothing whatever in Greek. They are Greek mouths repeating Semitic words for ox, house, camel and door, kept as labels long after the labels stopped describing anything.

The Greeks also made one change of real consequence. Greek is an Indo-European language in which vowels do heavy lexical work and cannot be inferred from the consonants, so a vowelless script would have been close to unusable. The solution was economical: Phoenician had several consonants that Greek did not use, and those orphaned signs were reassigned to vowels. Aleph, a sound Greek speakers did not produce, became alpha. He became epsilon, ayin became omicron, yod became iota, waw split into two descendants, one of which is upsilon. With that move the script became a full alphabet, able to represent any spoken word in the language unambiguously, and able to be read aloud by someone who did not already know what it said.

Early Greek writing kept the old right-to-left habit for a while, and often ran boustrophedon, alternating direction line by line the way an ox turns at the end of a furrow, with the letters themselves flipping to face the way the line was going. Left-to-right won out eventually and the letter shapes froze accordingly. From Greek the alphabet passed to the Etruscans, and from the Etruscans to Rome, where it acquired the forms that this sentence is made of. Meanwhile the eastern branch of the family went its own way: Aramaic, written in a close cousin of the Phoenician letters, became the working language of empires, and its descendants include the square Hebrew script, Syriac, Arabic, and quite possibly, through routes that are still debated, the writing systems of South and Southeast Asia.

No one owned any of this. There is no Phoenician king who decreed the alphabet, no academy that regulated it, no moment at which it was declared finished. It spread because it was cheap, because it required no institution to maintain it, and because anyone who saw it working could copy it and bend it to a language it had never been designed for. That indifference to its own origins is the whole reason it is still here.
