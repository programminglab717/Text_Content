# Hammurabi and His Code of Laws

The stone is about two and a quarter meters tall, dark, and shaped like an
enormous blunt finger. At the top, carved in relief, a seated god hands a rod
and a coiled measuring line to a standing man in a fringed robe; the man's hand
is raised to his mouth in a gesture of respect. Below that, running around the
pillar in dense columns of archaic-looking cuneiform, are roughly three hundred
paragraphs of Akkadian, each one following the same grammatical shape: if a man
does this, then that follows. The pillar was not found in Babylon, where it was
made. It was found at Susa in western Iran, dragged there as loot six hundred
years after it was carved, by an Elamite king who chiselled out a panel of the
text to make space for an inscription of his own. He never got around to adding
it. The blank scar is still there.

Hammurabi's laws are the most famous legal document of the ancient world and one
of the most consistently misunderstood. They were not the first law collection
in Mesopotamia. They were probably not, in any modern sense, law at all. And the
king who put them up spent most of his long reign doing something quite
different from legislating.

## Thirty Years of Waiting

He came to the throne of Babylon in the eighteenth century BCE, inheriting a
respectable but unremarkable kingdom of perhaps a hundred kilometers' reach.
Babylon at that point was one player among several, and not the strongest. Larsa
under the durable Rim-Sin held the south. Eshnunna controlled the Diyala.
Shamshi-Adad had assembled a substantial northern realm around Ashur and Mari.
Aleppo commanded the west. Elam, from beyond the Zagros, had the means to tip
any of these balances and the habit of doing so.

We know an unusual amount about the diplomacy of this world because the palace
at Mari, on the middle Euphrates, burned down with its correspondence inside it.
Thousands of letters survive: envoys reporting on rivals, kings complaining
about unreturned troops, officials sending assessments of who was allied to whom
this month. One of them contains a line often quoted in summaries of the period,
to the effect that no king in the region was strong on his own — that ten or
fifteen lesser rulers marched behind Hammurabi, a comparable number behind
Rim-Sin, behind Eshnunna's king, behind Qatna's, and twenty behind the king of
Aleppo. It is a portrait of a system in which power meant clientage, and in
which everyone was watching everyone.

For his first three decades Hammurabi mostly built canals, repaired temples,
signed treaties, and waited. Babylonian year-names, which label each year after
its most notable event, record wall-building and irrigation work far more often
than conquest. Then, in his twenty-ninth year and after, something broke loose.
He defeated a coalition that included Elam, turned on Rim-Sin and took Larsa
after a siege, absorbed Eshnunna, and — having been allied to Mari for
years — captured it, then went back two years later and demolished its walls and
palace. Within roughly a decade he had gone from one king among several to the
only one left standing in Mesopotamia.

## The Text on the Pillar

The laws belong to the end of that career. The prologue and epilogue are written
in a high literary register quite unlike the clipped legal paragraphs between
them, and they are pure royal self-presentation: the gods chose Hammurabi to
make justice appear in the land, to prevent the strong from oppressing the weak,
and here is a long list of cities whose shrines he restored and whose people he
protected. The epilogue instructs any wronged man to come and have the stone
read aloud to him, and calls down an extravagant catalogue of curses on any
future ruler who defaces it — which, given the Elamite chisel marks, was not an
idle worry.

The provisions themselves cover a startling range of ordinary life. There are
rules for false accusation, theft, kidnapping, and harboring runaway slaves; for
burglars, who are to be executed at the breach they made in the wall; for
soldiers who send a substitute instead of serving; for the tenancy of fields and
orchards, the rental of oxen and boats, the wages of hired men, the fees of
surgeons and veterinarians and boatbuilders. There are clauses on marriage
contracts, dowries, divorce settlements, adoption, inheritance among sons of
different mothers, and the obligations of a wife whose husband has been taken
prisoner. There are clauses about tavern keepers, who appear to have been
women and who are held to standards involving both honest measures and not
letting outlaws meet on the premises.

Two mechanisms recur and both feel foreign. One is the river ordeal: in certain
accusations that cannot otherwise be resolved, the accused is thrown into the
river, and the river's verdict — whether the person sinks or comes out — settles
the matter. The other is talion, the principle that the penalty should mirror
the injury. It produces the famous eye for an eye and tooth for a tooth, and
also its more alarming extensions. If a builder builds a house badly and it
collapses and kills the householder, the builder is put to death. If it kills
the householder's son, the builder's son is put to death.

## Justice Was Not the Same for Everyone

That last clause points at the structure that organizes the whole document.
Hammurabi's world had at least three legal grades of person: the *awilum*,
roughly a free man of standing; the *mushkenum*, a dependent of lower status
whose exact position is still argued over; and the slave, who was property but
could hold some assets and could be freed. The penalty for an offense depended
on the rank of both parties.

Put out the eye of an *awilum* and you lose your own. Put out the eye of a
*mushkenum* and you pay silver. Put out the eye of a man's slave and you pay the
owner half the slave's value, because the injury is to property. A surgeon who
kills an *awilum* during an operation loses his hand; if the patient was a
slave, he simply replaces the slave. The same asymmetry runs through the fees:
the surgeon who successfully opens an abscess earns ten shekels from a man of
standing, less from a commoner, less again from a slave's owner. Talion was not
a doctrine of universal human equivalence. It was a doctrine of equivalence
between social equals, with cash settlements downward.

## Was Any of This Enforced?

Here is where the document gets genuinely strange. Mesopotamia has left us tens
of thousands of legal records from this period and the centuries around it:
contracts, loan agreements, records of disputes brought before judges, the whole
paperwork of a litigious society. Judges cite the king, they cite custom, they
cite the oath sworn by the parties. As far as anyone has been able to establish,
not one of them cites the stele. No verdict says: by paragraph such-and-such of
the laws of Hammurabi. And some surviving verdicts reach outcomes the stele
would not permit.

This is not because the collection was unknown. Copies were made on tablets for
centuries afterward, and student scribes were still laboriously reproducing
sections of it more than a thousand years later, which is how the erased portion
has been partly reconstructed. The text was famous. It simply does not appear to
have functioned as a statute book.

The best reading available is that Hammurabi's laws belong to a recognized genre
of royal monument rather than to legislation. Earlier collections exist: one in
Sumerian associated with the founder of the Third Dynasty of Ur, another from
Lipit-Ishtar of Isin, a third from Eshnunna, all with the same if-then structure
and some of the same subject matter. Kings in Mesopotamia periodically issued
edicts cancelling debts and releasing people from bondage, and those edicts were
acted on immediately and demonstrably. The great stone collections seem to have
done something else: they demonstrated that the king had a grasp of justice
across every domain of life, from goring oxen to unfaithful wives, and they
preserved that demonstration in a durable medium addressed to the gods and to
posterity.

Which does not make them fiction. The cases are too specific and too closely
matched to the real texture of Babylonian life to be invention, and they
probably reflect the judgments a competent court actually reached. Read the
pillar as a record of how judges were expected to think, rather than a rulebook
they consulted, and the contradictions ease.

Hammurabi's empire lasted barely longer than he did. His son faced revolt across
the south within a decade, lost the old Sumerian cities, and watched the
southernmost region break away entirely. Babylon survived as a city and
eventually gave its name to the whole civilization, but the kingdom Hammurabi
spent thirty patient years assembling was gone inside a generation. The stone
outlasted it, then outlasted the Elamites who stole it, and turned up in a
French excavation trench in the winter of 1901.
