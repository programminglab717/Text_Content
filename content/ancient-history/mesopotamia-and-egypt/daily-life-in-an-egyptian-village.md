# Daily Life in an Egyptian Village

The first thing to understand about an Egyptian village is that it sits on a lump. Generations of mudbrick houses, collapsing and being rebuilt on top of their own rubble, raise the ground under a settlement a little higher every century, and in the Nile valley that accumulation is not merely incidental — it is survival. The river came up every summer and spread across the floodplain, and a village built at field level would have been standing in water. So the houses crowd together on a mound at the desert margin or on a natural rise, the fields begin where the mound ends, and nobody wastes good black soil on a building. Most of these mounds are still out there, flattened, dug into for fertilizer in the nineteenth century, or buried under a modern village doing the same thing in the same place.

What follows is a composite. Ordinary villages were built of mud and left few archives, so much of what can be said with confidence comes from two unusual settlements that happened to sit on dry desert ground and survive: the artisans' village at Deir el-Medina, near the Valley of the Kings, and the workmen's village at Amarna. Neither was typical — both were state-founded, state-fed communities of specialists. But the houses, the pots, the bread ovens and the household gods were much the same everywhere, and Deir el-Medina in particular left behind so much casual writing that it lets us hear a village talking.

## The Village's Year

The calendar a village actually lived by had three seasons of four months each: akhet, the inundation; peret, the coming-forth, when the water withdrew and the crops went in; and shemu, the dry season of harvest and low water. What changed between them was not only the work but the shape of ordinary life — who was at home, who was owed labour, what there was to eat, and whether there was anything to celebrate.

When the flood was up, the village on its mound became an island among other islands, with movement across the drowned fields done by boat or not at all. Nobody could farm, and that made akhet the season when the state and the great temple estates called in their labour. Men went off in gangs to reinforce dikes, clear canals, quarry, haul, and build, fed from granaries for the duration, and they might be gone for weeks. A household ran short-handed while its men were away, and the women, the old and the half-grown kept the animals, the garden plots and the grinding going. The arrangement was compulsory, but it was also the season in which a poor family ate at someone else's expense, which is not nothing.

The falling water left a problem behind it. Every boundary mark in the floodplain had spent weeks under silty water, and the mud came back smooth and anonymous. Field edges had to be re-established each year out of memory, the testimony of neighbours, fixed points on ground the flood never reached, and whatever the estate's scribes had written down the year before. Disputes over exactly where one man's strip ended are among the most persistent themes in Egyptian village litigation, and Egyptian wisdom literature returns again and again to the man who shifts a boundary marker or leans on the measuring cord, treating him as a standard specimen of the dishonest. Greek writers later claimed that this annual re-surveying was where geometry came from, which is too neat to believe, but it does indicate how routinely Egyptians found themselves measuring land.

Underneath all of it sat a simple exposure. A flood that came up short meant a thin harvest, and a thin harvest meant borrowing — seed grain, or food to get to the next one — on terms set by whoever had a full granary. Much of the land was not owned by the people working it but held by temples, royal foundations and large households and farmed by tenants who owed a share of the yield, so a bad year could roll a family into obligations that took several good ones to work off. The household granary in the back court was not storage so much as insurance, and the anxiety about it runs right through Egyptian writing, which keeps coming back to years of hunger and to the virtue of the man who feeds his neighbours in them.

The compensations were the festivals, and there were many. Local feast days interrupted the working calendar constantly, and the gang records from Deir el-Medina show work simply stopping for them. At Thebes the great occasions were spectacular: the images of the gods left their sanctuaries and travelled in procession, once a year crossing the river to visit the mortuary temples on the west bank, and families went out to the tombs of their own dead with food and drink and spent the night there. Meat, which almost never appeared in a village diet, appeared then.

## The House

A village house was mudbrick, made from the same mud and chopped straw the walls of the granary were made from, moulded in a wooden frame and dried in the sun. Roofs were palm logs or bundled reeds covered with matting and mud. Walls were plastered and often whitewashed, and interior surfaces might be painted. Windows were small and set high, because the point was to exclude the sun, and interiors were dim and cool.

The plan varied, but a common arrangement ran two to four rooms deep from the street. The front room frequently held a niche or a low platform associated with household religion and, in one well-preserved case at Deir el-Medina, an enclosed brick bed that may have been used for childbirth or for the seclusion that followed it. Behind that came a main living room, often with a column supporting the roof and a low bench along one wall, then a smaller back room for storage or sleeping, and finally a walled kitchen court, open to the sky, with a domed clay oven and a grinding emplacement and a cellar dug under the floor for jars. The flat roof was usable space: a place to dry dates, keep grain, sleep on hot nights.

Furniture was sparse even in comfortable homes. Reed mats, low stools, a chest or two, a bed frame strung with rush, a wooden headrest instead of a pillow, and a shallow pottery lamp with a twist of linen for a wick and a little oil. Most of the working day happened outdoors or on the roof anyway.

## Bread, Beer, and Grit

Grain arrived at the house whole and left the household's hands twice transformed. It was ground on a saddle quern — a woman kneeling and pushing a stone back and forth over a larger stone — and the abrasive dust of that process, plus sand blown in from the desert, ended up in the flour. Egyptian teeth from every social level show the result: molars worn flat, sometimes into the pulp, with the abscesses that follow. Bread was the staple and the wage, baked flat on hot surfaces or in moulds, and it varied from coarse everyday loaves to the sweetened, fruit-filled kinds recorded for festivals and offerings.

Beer was the other half of the grain economy: thick, lightly alcoholic, strained, and closer to a liquid food than a recreational drink. Both men and women drank it daily, children included. Onions and dates and figs supplemented the diet, pulses supplied protein, and fish from the river was the cheap meat, caught in nets and traps and split and dried in the sun. Waterfowl came out of the marshes by clapnet and throwstick. Pigs, goats and sheep were kept around the settlements; beef was for temples, festivals, and people with land. Most households also kept a donkey, the universal pack animal of Egypt long before camels arrived.

## What Deir el-Medina Overheard

The men of Deir el-Medina cut and decorated royal tombs. They were organized as a gang divided into a left side and a right side, each under a foreman, with a scribe keeping the records, and they were paid in rations — grain, plus fish, vegetables, oil, and firewood, delivered by outside servants of the state. Because they were literate to an unusual degree and because limestone flakes were free, they wrote constantly on ostraca: notes, receipts, letters, laundry lists, drawings, dirty jokes.

Among the survivals are attendance registers noting why a man missed work, and the reasons are wonderfully human. Illness. Brewing beer. A festival. Building his house. A scorpion sting. Wrapping a dead relative for burial. Attending a mother or a wife. Working, on the quiet, for one of his superiors.

Late in the reign of Ramesses III, when the grain rations had been repeatedly late, the workmen walked off the job, sat down behind a mortuary temple and refused to move until they were paid — the earliest labour action anyone has documented. Their scribe recorded it, including their complaint that they were hungry and that this was happening in the reign of a great king.

Disputes inside the village went to a local council of respected residents, and when a case was too knotty or the evidence too thin, they consulted an oracle: the image of the deified king Amenhotep I, carried in procession by bearers, which answered yes or no by moving forward or back as questions were put or written statements held up. A woman named Naunakhte used the village court to settle her estate, declaring which of her children had supported her in old age and would therefore inherit her property and which had not and would not. Women in Egypt could own, inherit, buy and dispose of property in their own names, and she did.

## The Other Side of the Mound

Beyond the houses, past the last cultivated strip, lay the cemetery, because the dead belonged on the dry ground that would preserve them and the living belonged on the wet ground that fed them. Ordinary burials were modest — a pit, a mat or a plain coffin, a few pots, some beads, an amulet — but the relationship was not thought to end with the interment. Families brought food to the tomb. They wrote to their dead, sometimes on the inside of an offering bowl, asking a father or a husband to intervene against an illness, a legal opponent, or a grievance persisting on the other side of death.

Against the smaller troubles — scorpions, snakes, fever, difficult births, the evil eye of a neighbour — the household relied on amulets, spoken spells, and the two deities who turn up in village houses far more often than the great gods of the temples: Bes, the dwarf with the lion's mane and the aggressive grin, and Taweret, the pregnant hippopotamus standing upright. Neither had a grand cult. Both stood guard over the parts of life that actually frightened people.
