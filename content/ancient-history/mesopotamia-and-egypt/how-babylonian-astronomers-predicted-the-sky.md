# How Babylonian Astronomers Predicted the Sky

Somewhere in Babylon, on the roof of a temple, a man is watching the western
horizon at dusk, waiting to see whether a thread of new moon will appear in the
afterglow. If it does, tomorrow is the first day of the month. If it does not,
the old month gets a thirtieth day and he will be back at the same parapet
tomorrow evening. He has a water clock beside him to measure how long the
crescent stays up after sunset, and a wax or clay tablet to write the result on.
Behind him, in a room he has access to, are records of people doing exactly this
on the same roof for two hundred years.

That continuity is the whole story. Babylonian astronomy was not built on a
theory of how the heavens are arranged — the Babylonians never produced anything
like the nested spheres of the Greeks, and seem not to have wanted one. It was
built on an extraordinarily disciplined habit of writing down what happened,
maintained across dynasties, conquests, and changes of ruling language, until
the record itself was long enough to reveal its own patterns. Out of those
patterns they got prediction, and the predictions were good.

## The Sky Was a Message Before It Was a Mechanism

The motive, at the start, was not curiosity. Mesopotamian kings governed
surrounded by signs. Anything unusual — the shape of a sacrificed sheep's liver,
a birth defect in a lamb, a fox running into a city — carried information about
the king's prospects, and the sky was simply the largest and most public source
of such signs. Scholars compiled these into vast reference series, of which the
celestial one ran to some seventy tablets. Its entries have a rigid shape: if
the moon is surrounded by a halo and a particular star stands within it, then
the king will be besieged; if a planet appears in a certain month, then the
harvest will fail or the crown prince will rebel.

This was national rather than personal. Omens concerned the king, the army, the
harvest and the city, not private individuals. Only quite late — well into the
Persian period — do we find the first horoscopes cast for the birth of an
ordinary named child, and by then the technical machinery was so good that the
astrologer could compute planetary positions for a birth date without anyone
having observed the sky that night at all.

The practical consequence of the omen tradition was that watching the sky became
a salaried, institutional activity with an obligation to report. And an omen is
much more useful if you know it is coming. An eclipse announced three months in
advance gives the court time to perform the countermeasures; an eclipse that
arrives unannounced is a failure of the profession. Prediction had a customer.

## The Diaries

From the middle of the first millennium BCE onward, Babylonian scribes kept what
modern scholars call the Astronomical Diaries: month-by-month records, compiled
over a run of centuries, of everything worth noting. Each entry gives the moon's
behaviour at the start and end of the month, the intervals between sunset and
moonset measured in time-degrees, the positions of the planets against fixed
stars, conjunctions, eclipses, and any unusual appearance.

Then, in the same tablet, the scribe records the weather. And the height of the
Euphrates. And the market rate that month for barley, dates, mustard, cress,
sesame and wool. And whatever happened in the city — a festival, a plague, a
riot in the streets, the arrival of a foreign army. The diaries are the reason
we have independent confirmation of the date of certain battles of the ancient
world, because someone in Babylon noted them down between the river level and
the price of dates.

The mixture looks eccentric until you remember the point. If celestial events
foretell earthly ones, then the responsible thing to do is record both in the
same column and see how they correlate. It is a research program, pursued with
the wrong hypothesis, that happened to produce the longest continuous series of
dated scientific observations anyone has ever compiled — running, by the best
estimates, for something like seven centuries with only patchy gaps.

## The Arithmetic of Return

What a long record gives you is periods. Celestial events recur, not on tidy
round numbers, but on intervals that become visible once you have enough
repetitions to compare.

Eclipses were the first real prize. The Babylonians established that the pattern
of eclipse possibilities repeats after 223 lunar months — about eighteen years
and eleven days — and organized eclipses into cycles of that length, each
subdivided into a schedule of dates where an eclipse might fall. That
arrangement lets you look up, rather than calculate, when the next one is due.
Not every listed possibility produces a visible eclipse from Babylon, but the
schedule tells you which nights to watch, and that is most of the battle.

The planets got the same treatment. Venus returns to nearly the same position
and phase after eight years. Saturn takes fifty-nine. Jupiter has useful
recurrences at seventy-one and eighty-three years, and combining the two gives
better accuracy than either alone. A category of tablet called the Goal-Year
text exploits this directly: to forecast what the planets will do in a coming
year, the scribe pulls the observations from exactly one period ago for each
planet — eight years back for Venus, fifty-nine for Saturn, and so on — copies
them onto a single tablet, and applies a small correction for the drift. No
theory of orbits is required. The sky is treated as a set of overlapping cycles
whose lengths have been measured.

Calendar-keeping worked the same way. Twelve lunar months fall about eleven days
short of the solar year, so an extra month has to be slotted in periodically to
keep the harvest festivals in the right season. For a long time the decision was
made ad hoc, by royal order, sometimes at short notice, which must have been
maddening for anyone with a contract falling due. Eventually it was regularized:
seven extra months inserted over each nineteen-year cycle, at fixed positions.
Nineteen solar years and 235 lunar months agree to within a couple of hours,
which is why the same cycle still governs the date of Easter and the Jewish
calendar.

## Zigzags Instead of Circles

The last phase is the impressive one. By the middle of the first millennium,
Babylonian scholars stopped merely cataloguing recurrences and began computing.

Their tool was the numerical table. They divided the band of sky the sun and
planets move through into twelve equal signs of thirty degrees each — the zodiac
in the form we still use, invented as a coordinate system rather than a
fortune-telling device — and worked entirely in a base-sixty place-value
notation that made multiplication and subdivision easy. Then they modelled
variable motion arithmetically. The sun does not cross the zodiac at a constant
rate; neither does the moon. Rather than explain why, they represented the
changing velocity as a sequence of numbers.

Two schemes survive. One assigns the sun a fixed fast velocity through part of
the zodiac and a fixed slow velocity through the rest, with an abrupt step
between — crude-looking, but accurate enough over a year. The other makes the
velocity increase by a constant amount each month up to a maximum, then decrease
by the same amount to a minimum, then turn again: a linear zigzag, a triangular
wave, approximating a smooth oscillation with straight segments. From these,
scribes produced ephemerides — tables giving, month by month for years ahead,
the moon's position, the length of daylight, the dates when Jupiter would turn
retrograde, the predicted magnitude of a coming eclipse. These are calculated
values. Nobody watched them happen; they were written before the fact.

There is a striking piece of evidence that this arithmetic was more
sophisticated than it first appears. Among the tablets dealing with Jupiter's
motion are several that compute the distance the planet travels over an interval
by treating a graph of its velocity against time as a trapezoid and taking the
area — and then divide that trapezoid into two equal areas to find the moment at
which Jupiter has covered half the distance. That is a geometrical procedure
applied to an abstract quantity, velocity plotted against time, rather than to
anything physically shaped like a trapezoid. The technique was not recognized
for what it was until long after the tablets had been excavated, because nobody
expected to find it there.

## What Traveled West

Greek astronomers inherited all of this and knew it. Hipparchus used Babylonian
eclipse records, which reached back far enough to let him measure the slow shifts
that a human lifetime cannot reveal, and he adopted Babylonian period relations
and the sexagesimal notation that astronomers everywhere then kept for the next
two thousand years. Ptolemy's great synthesis quotes Babylonian observations
directly and dates them by a king list that begins with a Babylonian reign. The
Greek word for a Babylonian — Chaldean — became the ordinary term for an
astrologer throughout the Mediterranean, which tells you where the reputation
lay.

What the Greeks added was the thing the Babylonians had declined to attempt: a
physical geometry of circles and spheres meant to explain the motion, not merely
reproduce it. That turned out to be the more fertile approach in the long run.
But it was built on numbers gathered by men on temple roofs who wanted to know
whether a halo around the moon meant trouble for the king, and who wrote down
the price of barley in the same breath.
