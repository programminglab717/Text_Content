# The Race to Read the Hieroglyphs

Sometime in the late fourth century AD, on the island of Philae near the first cataract of the Nile, a priest cut a short inscription in hieroglyphs into stone. It is the last dated example anyone has found. Within a generation or two the temples were closed, the priesthoods that had kept the old script alive broke up, and the knowledge of how to read it disappeared completely. Egypt was still covered in writing — on obelisks, temple walls, coffins, papyri, statue bases, the doorways of tombs — and for roughly fourteen hundred years nobody on earth could read a word of it.

This was not for want of trying. The failure was unusually instructive, because the wrong answer was available early, it was elegant, and it held the field for centuries.

## Fourteen Centuries of Wrong

The source of the trouble was a late antique Greek text attributed to one Horapollo, which purported to explain the hieroglyphs and treated each sign as a symbolic emblem. A hare meant opening, because hares sleep with their eyes open. A vulture meant mother. A goose meant son. Some of the entries were not far off the actual meanings of certain signs, which made the book more damaging rather than less, because it confirmed the underlying premise while getting the mechanism entirely wrong. When the manuscript reached Renaissance Italy it landed among readers already inclined to believe that Egypt held a deep and wordless wisdom, and it settled the matter for them: hieroglyphs were not writing in the ordinary sense but a philosophical picture-language, each sign a compressed idea, immune to the accidents of any particular tongue.

Athanasius Kircher, the seventeenth-century Jesuit polymath, gave this view its most confident expression. He produced long, ornate translations of obelisk texts full of divine emanations and cosmic principles, all of it invented. The inscriptions he was working on mostly said things like the name and titles of a king. And yet Kircher made one contribution that turned out to be indispensable: he argued, correctly, that Coptic — the liturgical language of Egyptian Christians, written in Greek letters plus a handful of extra signs, and still chanted in churches — was the surviving descendant of the ancient Egyptian language. Whoever eventually read the hieroglyphs would be reading Coptic in disguise. Kircher could not use that insight because he did not think the signs recorded sounds at all. Someone else would.

## A Stone in the Mud at Rosetta

In the summer of 1799, French soldiers strengthening a fort near the town of Rosetta in the western Delta turned up a slab of dark stone with three blocks of writing on it. An officer of engineers, Pierre-François Bouchard, recognized what it might be worth and had it sent on. Napoleon's expedition had brought a small army of scholars along with the actual army, and the scholars understood immediately that a text in three scripts, one of them Greek, was the best chance anyone had been given.

The Greek turned out to be a decree issued by a council of priests assembled at Memphis in honour of the young Ptolemy V, listing his benefactions and the honours voted to him in return. Near the end it instructed that the decree be inscribed in sacred writing, in native writing, and in Greek. That single clause did the crucial work. It meant the three blocks were not three different texts sharing a monument but three versions of the same text, which is what made the stone a key rather than a curiosity.

The slab itself went to Britain under the terms of the French capitulation in Egypt and ended up in the British Museum, but this mattered less than it might have. Ink impressions and plaster casts had already been taken and distributed, so scholars in Paris, London, Stockholm and elsewhere were all working from good copies of the same text at roughly the same time. What followed was a race, and its participants knew it.

## Getting Halfway

The first real progress came on the middle block, the cursive script now called demotic, which looked more like ordinary handwriting than the pictures above it. Johan David Åkerblad, a Swedish diplomat with a strong grasp of Coptic, picked out the proper names in the demotic by matching their positions against the Greek and assembled an alphabet from them. He could read several words correctly. Then he over-extended the method, assumed demotic was alphabetic throughout, and ran into a wall. Silvestre de Sacy, the leading French orientalist of the day and later Champollion's teacher, made careful progress on the demotic and then, characteristically, declared the problem probably insoluble.

Thomas Young took it considerably further. Young was an English physician of formidable range — he had done fundamental work on the wave nature of light and on the elasticity of materials — and he came to the Egyptian problem as one more puzzle. He established that demotic was not a separate system but a cursive derivative of the hieroglyphic script, related to it rather than independent of it. He worked out that the oval rings now called cartouches enclosed royal names, which had been suspected but not demonstrated. He matched groups of demotic signs to the Greek words they must correspond to, and he arrived at a partly correct phonetic reading of the name Ptolemy. He also noticed that the signs face the direction from which a line should be read, which is the first thing anyone learns about hieroglyphs today.

Then he stopped short, and the reason he stopped is the whole story. Young believed that phonetic writing in hieroglyphs was a special contrivance, adopted reluctantly to spell out foreign names like Ptolemy and Berenice because Egyptian symbols could not otherwise accommodate them. The rest of the script, in his view, remained symbolic. He had opened the door and then decided it led to a cupboard.

## Champollion and the Coptic Advantage

Jean-François Champollion had been preparing for this since adolescence. Born in Grenoble in 1790, he collected languages compulsively — Hebrew, Arabic, Syriac, Persian, Chinese and others — but the decisive one was Coptic, which he studied until he could think in it. He copied Coptic manuscripts, drilled its vocabulary, and by his own account used it for private notes. When the moment came, he was not translating into Coptic. He already had the language in his head and was waiting for the spelling.

He worked on the cartouches first, using not only the Rosetta Stone but a lithograph of an obelisk that William Bankes had brought from Philae to his estate in Dorset. The obelisk carried two cartouches and its base had a Greek inscription naming Ptolemy and Cleopatra. Because the two names share several letters in different positions, the comparison gave a cross-check: a sign that had to be p in one name had to be p in the other, and the values held. He extended the method across cartouches of Greek and Roman rulers until he had a working alphabet.

That alphabet, presented to the Académie des Inscriptions in Paris in September 1822 in a letter addressed to its secretary, Bon-Joseph Dacier, is what made him famous. But the alphabet was not the breakthrough, because on Young's reading it applied only to foreigners. The breakthrough came from copies of reliefs at Abu Simbel that reached Champollion shortly before. In one cartouche he saw a sun disc, followed by a sign he could not yet read, followed by two identical signs he had already established as s. He knew the Coptic word for sun: re. He also knew that Greek writers rendered certain Egyptian royal names as Thutmosis and Ramesses, and that Coptic had a verb meaning to give birth, mise. Ra — mes — s. A pharaoh from centuries before any Greek had set foot in Egypt, his name spelled out phonetically, sign by sign, in the sacred script.

There was another argument, simpler and just as fatal to the old view. Champollion counted. The Greek text of the Memphis decree runs to a few hundred words; the hieroglyphic version, even damaged, contains well over a thousand individual signs. If each sign stood for a whole word or idea, the numbers could not possibly work out. There had to be several signs per word.

## What the Script Turned Out to Be

The system Champollion uncovered, and that Karl Richard Lepsius and others put on a firm footing over the following decades, is a mixed one, and its mixedness is exactly why it defeated people who insisted it must be one thing or the other. Most signs are phonograms standing for one, two or three consonants — vowels went unwritten, which is why Egyptologists insert a conventional e to make the words pronounceable. Some signs are logograms, standing for the word they picture, often marked with a small stroke to say so. And at the end of most words sits a determinative: a sign that is never pronounced at all and exists to tell the reader what category the word belongs to. A pair of walking legs for motion, a seated man for a personal name, a scroll for abstractions, a hill country sign for foreign places. They function as a built-in disambiguator for a script that writes no vowels, and they are one of the more elegant solutions any writing system has found to that problem.

The priority quarrel that followed was bitter and largely national, with British opinion insisting on Young's share and French opinion on Champollion's. Both had one. Young's demonstration that cartouches held names, and that the two scripts were relatives, was necessary; Champollion's recognition that the phonetic principle ran through the whole language was the thing that opened it. He went to Egypt himself in 1828 and spent a year and a half reading inscriptions off the walls in front of him — the first person to do so in fourteen centuries — and died four years later at forty-one, his grammar and dictionary unfinished and published afterwards by his brother.

What came out of it was not mainly the royal propaganda anyone had expected from obelisks. It was letters, court records, tax assessments, medical texts, love poetry, school exercises, and the grumbling notes of workmen at Deir el-Medina about their rations and their foremen.
