# How the Achaemenid Empire Was Administered

A traveller arriving at a way station on the road through Fars around the end of the sixth century BC would hand over a sealed clay document and receive, in exchange, a measured quantity of flour, some wine or beer, and fodder for the animals. A clerk then recorded the transaction on a small tablet: who received it, how much, on whose authority, on what day. Tens of thousands of those tablets were found in the fortification wall at Persepolis, where they had been dumped once the accounting period they covered was closed. They are almost entirely tedious. They are also the best surviving view into how the largest empire the world had yet seen actually moved its resources around, and what they show is an administration obsessed with authorization and receipts.

That is not the picture the empire's enemies handed down. Greek writers gave posterity a Persia of tribute caravans, golden thrones and despotic whim, and because the Greeks wrote the books that Europe kept reading, the paperwork was forgotten. The Achaemenid state that ran from the Indus to the Aegean for roughly two centuries was held together less by terror than by a set of practical arrangements: divided provincial authority, a road system built for messages, a chancery language that nobody in the ruling house spoke at home, and a settled policy of leaving local institutions alone as long as the revenue arrived.

## Satrapies and the Problem of the Viceroy

The empire was divided into something on the order of twenty provinces, each under a satrap — a word that means roughly "protector of the realm." A satrap was a viceroy in the fullest sense. He held court, collected revenue, administered justice, raised troops, and in the western provinces conducted something close to his own foreign policy with the Greek cities on his frontier. Many satraps were members of the royal family or of the handful of noble houses that stood closest to it, which made the post both a reward and a risk.

The risk was obvious, and the Persians knew it. A province the size of Egypt or Lydia, governed by a royal relative with an army and a treasury, is a rebellion waiting for a bad succession. The structural answer was to split the functions. The garrison commander in a province and the officer in charge of its treasury were, in principle, appointed by the king and reported to the king rather than to the satrap, so that no single man in the province controlled soldiers, money and administration at once. Greek sources also describe travelling royal inspectors, the so-called eyes of the king, who arrived unannounced with an escort and looked into the province's affairs.

These checks worked imperfectly. Western satraps did rebel, sometimes in concert, and more than one dynastic crisis turned on which provincial governors could be bought. What is striking is not that the system leaked but that it held for as long as it did across such distances, and that Alexander, having destroyed the dynasty, kept the satrapal framework more or less intact because there was nothing better available to replace it with.

## The Road and the Relay

Distance was the real adversary. The Royal Road ran from Sardis, near the Aegean coast, to Susa in the lowlands of western Iran, a journey that Greek writers reckoned at around ninety days on foot. Along it, at intervals of roughly a day's travel, stood stations with lodging, provisions and fresh horses. Ordinary traffic used them; so did the army; so, above all, did the couriers.

The courier system was the point of the whole enterprise. Rather than sending one rider the entire distance, the Persians posted men and horses at each station, and a dispatch was passed from one to the next without pause, so that the message travelled at the speed of a horse without ever waiting for a horse to rest. Herodotus, who was not disposed to flatter Persia, wrote about these riders with open admiration, describing how neither snow nor rain nor heat nor darkness kept them from their stage — a sentence that echoed down far enough to end up carved on a post office in New York. Ninety days by foot became a matter of a week or two for a piece of news. An empire that can hear about a revolt within a fortnight is a fundamentally different proposition from one that hears about it after a season.

The same infrastructure carried the sealed travel authorizations that the Persepolis tablets record. A person on official business held a document from the king or a satrap specifying his route and entitlement, and stations along the way issued rations against it and wrote down what they had issued. It is a recognizably bureaucratic instrument: the warrant, the entitlement, the counter-receipt.

## Writing in a Language the King Did Not Speak

The Persian court spoke Old Persian. The empire was administered largely in Aramaic. This was not an oversight but a sensible inheritance: Aramaic had already spread across the Near East as a language of trade and correspondence under the Assyrians and Babylonians, it was written with an alphabet rather than a syllabary of hundreds of signs, and it went on ink and parchment and papyrus rather than clay. The Achaemenids adopted it wholesale as the chancery language, producing a standardized form that scholars call Imperial Aramaic, uniform enough from Egypt to Bactria that a scribe trained in one province could read a document drafted in another.

The mechanics of this were peculiar and worth picturing. An order might be spoken in Persian, written down in Aramaic by a scribe who translated as he wrote, carried for a month, and then read aloud in the receiving province in the local language by another scribe translating back. Regional administration kept its own habits underneath: Elamite in the heartland accounts at Persepolis, Babylonian cuneiform in Babylonia, demotic Egyptian in Egypt.

We can watch the system operating because one provincial archive escaped. On the island of Elephantine at the first cataract of the Nile, a garrison of Jewish soldiers in Persian service left behind a mass of Aramaic papyri: contracts, marriage settlements, property disputes, letters about ritual observance, and a petition to the governor of Judah asking for authorization to rebuild their temple after it was destroyed in a local quarrel. The same dry sand preserved an Aramaic copy of Darius's great proclamation from the cliff at Behistun — a text the king had cut into a rock face in three languages and then, it appears, circulated in translation to the ends of the empire. The propaganda was distributed by mail.

## Revenue, Coin and the Ceremony of Tribute

Each satrapy owed a fixed annual assessment, payable in silver by weight and in kind. Darius is credited with putting this on a regular footing, and the reforms attributed to him include standardized royal weights and measures and a bimetallic coinage — the gold daric and the silver siglos, struck with the image of a running, kneeling archer. Coinage mattered mainly in the west, where the Greek and Anatolian cities already used it; through most of the empire silver continued to move as weighed bullion and obligations were discharged in grain, livestock, wine and labour. The Persepolis tablets show workers of both sexes paid in commodities at graded rates, with rations adjusted by skill and status.

The ceremonial version of all this is carved onto the stairways of the Apadana at Persepolis, where delegation after delegation files toward the king bearing the produce of their country — horses, textiles, vessels, a pair of humped cattle, a lion cub — each group distinguished by its own dress and hairstyle and led by the hand by a Persian usher. The reliefs are usually described as a tribute procession, and they may depict a specific festival. Either way they encode the theory of the empire: many peoples, each recognizably itself, bringing what it has to one throne.

## Ruling Through Other People's Institutions

The most durable Achaemenid technique was the least visible. Cyrus, on taking Babylon, presented himself in a Babylonian inscription as the restorer of neglected cults and the repairer of temples, and permitted deported communities to return home with their sacred objects — a policy remembered gratefully in the Hebrew Bible. In Egypt, an official named Udjahorresnet, who had served the last native pharaohs, went over to the conquerors and recorded on his statue how he instructed Cambyses in the proper titles and protocols of an Egyptian king and had the temple of Neith at Sais cleared of squatters. Darius is credited with having Egypt's existing laws collected and codified rather than replaced.

None of this was sentiment. A king who is crowned according to local rite, who funds the local priesthood, and who confirms the local landowners in their holdings does not need a large occupying army, and can collect his revenue through the people who were already collecting it. The cost is that the empire never produced a common culture, only a common administration. When the administration failed, at Gaugamela and afterwards, the provinces did not dissolve. They were simply handed, more or less intact, to somebody else.
