# The Sumerian City-States

Southern Iraq is flat in a way that is hard to photograph. The horizon sits
where it has always sat, and the ground offers the eye almost nothing — except,
here and there, a low brown swelling a few dozen meters high and half a
kilometer across. Those swellings are cities. Each is a tell: the accumulated
debris of mudbrick buildings put up, softened by rain, knocked flat, and rebuilt
on the rubble of the previous version, over and over, until the town was
standing on a platform made of its own past. Warka is Uruk. Tell al-Muqayyar is
Ur. Tello is Girsu. In the third millennium BCE these were the most densely
populated places on earth, and they spent most of their history fighting one
another.

There was never a Sumerian state. That is the fact most likely to be lost in a
summary. Sumer was a language, a set of gods, a shared body of literature and
scribal training, and a couple of dozen walled towns strung along the lower
Tigris and Euphrates, each with its own patron deity, its own ruler, its own
canals, and its own grudges. What held them together was mutual intelligibility
and a shared sense that their way of doing things was how civilized people
lived. What kept them apart was water.

## Water Made Them and Water Divided Them

The alluvial plain south of modern Baghdad gets too little rain to farm. Every
crop that fed those cities was irrigated, and irrigation in Mesopotamia was a
harder problem than the geography suggests. The two rivers rise with the spring
snowmelt in the Anatolian highlands, which puts the flood at almost exactly the
wrong moment: too late to water a crop sown in autumn, early enough to threaten
the barley standing ripe in the field. Farmers had to hold water back, store it,
and release it on a schedule that had nothing to do with the river's own.

The Euphrates helped, in its way. Slower and siltier than the Tigris, it
deposited sediment along its own banks until the channel ran on a raised ridge
above the surrounding plain, so a canal cut through the levee would carry water
outward by gravity alone. That made the first irrigation cheap. It also made it
fragile. Silt settles in slow water, canals clog, and a neglected channel
becomes a shallow ditch within a few seasons. Keeping a canal system alive meant
organizing gangs to dredge it every year, adjudicating who drew water first, and
maintaining the whole thing across land that belonged to different households
and different gods.

It was once fashionable to argue that this hydraulic burden produced despotism
by necessity — that only a central authority could manage the waterworks, and so
kings were the price of agriculture. The archaeology has not been kind to the
theory. The earliest canals were short, local, and well within the capacity of a
village to maintain. Large-scale state irrigation shows up after the cities and
kings already existed, not before. The rivers explain why people crowded
together on the plain; they do not by themselves explain who ended up in charge.

Water also carried a long-term penalty. Irrigating a hot flat plain with no
natural drainage raises the water table and leaves dissolved salt behind when
the water evaporates. Administrative records from the south show the proportion
of barley rising against wheat over the centuries, and barley tolerates salt
considerably better. Fields that had produced well for generations gradually
produced less. Some of the abandonments visible in the settlement record may
simply be farmers walking away from ground that had turned white.

## The House of the God

Walk into one of these cities around 2600 BCE and the largest building is not a
palace. It is a temple, and it is not a church. The Sumerian word for it is the
ordinary word for house, and that is close to literal: the temple was the god's
household, with the deity resident in a statue that was washed, clothed, fed
twice a day, and taken out on festival processions. Inanna lived at Uruk, Nanna
the moon god at Ur, Enki at Eridu, Ningirsu at Girsu, and Enlil at Nippur, which
was the one city everybody recognized as sacred and nobody could quite convert
into political dominance.

The god's household held land, and that land was worked. Tablets by the
thousand record barley, oil, and wool issued to laborers, weavers, shepherds,
fishermen, gardeners, and scribes on the temple's rolls. For a long time
scholars read these archives as showing that the temple owned essentially
everything and the city was a kind of religious corporation. The picture has
softened. Private sale documents, family landholdings, and palace estates all
turn up alongside the temple's property, and the economy looks more mixed than
the first generation of excavators assumed. But the temple remained the largest
single organization most Sumerians ever dealt with, and it did the things a
state does: it stored surplus, employed the landless, kept accounts, and lent
out silver and grain at interest.

The stepped ziggurats that illustrate every book on Mesopotamia belong mostly to
a later period. Earlier temples sat on modest raised terraces, rebuilt higher
each time the old structure was filled in. The famous tower at Ur, its lowest
stage still standing in restored brick, went up under the kings of the Third
Dynasty, well after the era of independent city-states had ended.

## En, Ensi, Lugal

Sumerian had several words for ruler and used them inconsistently, which is a
useful clue that the institution was still being invented. The *en* at Uruk
seems to have combined priestly and political authority. The rulers of Lagash
generally called themselves *ensi*, a title later used for a provincial
governor. *Lugal* means, flatly, "big man," and became the standard word for
king. To claim to be King of Kish was to claim a kind of seniority over the
plain as a whole, and rulers whose cities were nowhere near Kish took the title
anyway.

The Sumerian King List, compiled long after the events it describes, presents
this messy situation as an orderly relay: kingship descends from heaven, resides
in one city for a stretch of years, is defeated, and moves on to the next. The
early reigns run to tens of thousands of years apiece, and several kings the
list places in sequence certainly ruled at the same time in different cities.
The document is not a chronicle. It is an argument — that legitimate rule is
singular and indivisible, held by one city at a time — produced by scribes
working for rulers who very much wanted that to be true.

## Lagash Against Umma

The best-documented quarrel of the third millennium concerned a stretch of
fertile ground called the Gu'edena, lying between Lagash and its neighbor Umma.
It generated a run of inscriptions across several generations, which is why we
know about it at all, and there is no reason to think it was unusual.

By the account Lagash left, a king of Kish had once been called in to arbitrate
and had set a boundary marker. Umma moved it. Eannatum of Lagash won a battle
over the field and commemorated it on a limestone monument now called the Stele
of the Vultures, which shows his infantry advancing in a tight block behind
overlapping shields with spears projecting forward — the earliest clear image of
massed formation fighting anywhere. The reverse shows the god Ningirsu holding
the enemy in a net. His successors record the same dispute flaring again,
complete with the accusation that Umma had drained the boundary canal and owed
back rent on the field, calculated in barley, with interest.

It ended badly for Lagash. Lugalzagesi of Umma took the city and burned its
temples, and a Lagash scribe wrote a bitter little text listing the shrines
destroyed and asking the goddess to hold Umma's ruler responsible for the
sacrilege. Not long before, a Lagash ruler named Urukagina had issued a set of
reforms — cancelling debts, curbing the fees officials were extracting from
funerals and divorces, promising that the powerful would not abuse the widow and
the orphan. That phrase went on to appear in royal proclamations for the next
two thousand years.

## What Replaced Them

Lugalzagesi did not enjoy his hegemony long. Sargon of Akkad defeated him,
brought him in a neck-stock to Nippur to be displayed before Enlil, and went on
to fold the whole plain into the first empire of its kind — one king, appointed
governors, a new capital whose site has still not been located. Independent
city-states came back for a while after Akkad fell, then were absorbed again by
the Third Dynasty of Ur, whose administrators generated a paper trail so
enormous that a single provincial livestock depot has yielded tens of thousands
of tablets.

Sumerian stopped being spoken somewhere in the early second millennium. It did
not stop being written. Scribes in Babylon and Assyria were still copying
Sumerian hymns and vocabulary lists more than a thousand years later, the way a
European schoolboy in 1850 was still parsing Latin. The cities themselves lasted
longer than the language. Ur was occupied for centuries after anyone there spoke
Sumerian, and was finally abandoned when the Euphrates, which had never been
reliable, wandered off east and left the harbor sitting in dry ground.
