# How the Great Pyramids Were Built

Stand at the northeast corner of the Great Pyramid and the first surprise is
that the blocks are not all the same. The bottom courses are enormous, waist
high and several paces long. Higher up they shrink, and the stone gets rougher
and more carelessly fitted, until near the top the masonry is frankly shoddy.
The second surprise is the corner itself. Where the casing has been stripped
away, the bedrock beneath has been cut into a flat socket, and if you sight
along the base toward the far corner two hundred and thirty meters away, the
line does not visibly rise or fall. It is level to within a couple of
centimeters end to end. The pyramid was built by people who were much better at
some parts of the job than others, and who were extremely good indeed at the
part that had to be done first.

The Giza pyramids were not a mystery to the people who built them and they are
not much of one now. What they were was an enormous logistical problem —
quarrying, floating, hauling, lifting and dressing something on the order of two
and a half million blocks, most of them around the weight of a small car, on a
schedule set by the lifespan of a king. Nearly everything that looks inexplicable
about them turns out to be a question about scheduling and labor rather than
about engineering.

## Three Generations of Getting It Wrong

Nobody built the Great Pyramid on a first attempt. The form had been developed
over the preceding century, expensively and in public.

It began at Saqqara with Djoser's step pyramid, the earliest large stone
structure in Egypt, traditionally credited to the official Imhotep. It started
life as a flat rectangular tomb of the familiar kind and was then enlarged
repeatedly, upward, until it was a stack of six diminishing terraces. Its stone
is small — blocks a couple of men could shift — laid in courses that lean
inward, as though the masons were still thinking in mudbrick and not yet
trusting the material.

The decisive experiments belong to Sneferu, Khufu's father, who built on a scale
nobody had attempted. At Meidum a stepped structure was converted into a true
smooth-sided pyramid by filling the steps and casing the result; today it is a
three-stage tower standing in a skirt of its own rubble, and whether that rubble
represents a collapse during or shortly after construction or simply centuries
of stone-robbing is still argued.

At Dahshur he began again, at a steep angle of roughly fifty-four degrees, on
ground that included sand and clay rather than solid rock. Partway up, the
builders clearly got frightened. Cracks opened in the internal chambers, cedar
beams were wedged in to brace the passages, and the angle of the upper half was
abruptly reduced to about forty-three degrees, producing the Bent Pyramid's
unmistakable silhouette. Then he built a third, a short walk to the north,
laying it out at the shallower angle from the ground up and founding it properly
on rock. The Red Pyramid worked. It is the first true pyramid that stands
essentially as designed, and everything at Giza descends from it.

## Flat, Square, and Pointed North

Get the base wrong and the errors multiply all the way up: a footprint that is
not square produces edges that do not meet at a point, and an unlevel base
produces a lean. So the survey came first.

The plateau was cleared and dressed. At Giza the builders saved themselves work
by leaving a knoll of natural bedrock inside the footprint and building around
it, so an unknown fraction of the Great Pyramid's lower volume is simply the
hill it sits on. Leveling the perimeter is usually explained by trenches filled
with water — a water surface being a perfect level — with marks cut at the
waterline and the rock then trimmed to those marks. A plumb line and a wooden
A-frame would do the same job more laboriously.

The orientation is the part that still attracts arguments. The sides of the
Great Pyramid face the cardinal directions to within a small fraction of a
degree, an accuracy that no shadow cast by a stick will give you casually. The
usual candidates are astronomical: watching a circumpolar star, or a pair of
them, and marking the direction when one passes directly above the other, or
bisecting the arc between a star's rising and setting points on an artificial
horizon. Solar methods using the length and direction of shadows at equal
intervals either side of noon can also achieve it. Slight differences in the
alignment errors of successive Fourth Dynasty pyramids have been used to argue
for one method over another, since a stellar technique would drift over decades
in a predictable direction. The case is suggestive rather than settled.

## Where the Stone Came From

Most of the mass never moved far. The bulk of the core is ordinary local
limestone quarried on the plateau itself, from a large pit a few hundred meters
south of the Great Pyramid whose volume is broadly consistent with the volume of
stone above it. Blocks were freed by cutting channels around them with copper
chisels and stone picks, then splitting them off the bed below with wedges and
levers. Copper is soft, and the tools wore fast; the workforce included
coppersmiths whose job was continuous resharpening and recasting.

The fine white limestone for the outer casing came from Tura, across the river
on the east bank, and had to be ferried. The granite — for the King's Chamber
beams, the portcullis slabs, and the lowest casing courses of the third
pyramid — came from Aswan, roughly eight hundred kilometers upstream. Granite
cannot be chiselled with copper. It was worked by bashing it with hand-held
balls of dolerite, a harder stone, and sawn and drilled using copper tools fed
with wet quartz sand as an abrasive, where the copper is really just a carrier
for the grit that does the cutting.

River transport was the easy part, and the Egyptians organized it around the
annual flood, when the water came close to the plateau and barges could be
brought to a basin near the foot of the causeway. That this is not speculation
was settled by a find at a Red Sea harbor site, where a cache of papyri
included the working logbook of an inspector named Merer, who commanded a boat
crew in the last years of Khufu's reign. His journal records the crew's cycle in
plain administrative language: load limestone at Tura, sail to Giza, deliver,
return, repeat, with the days ticked off. It is the oldest inscribed papyrus
known, and it is a shipping record for the Great Pyramid.

On land, blocks went on wooden sledges. A tomb painting from a later period
shows a colossal statue being dragged on one while a man standing on the front
pours liquid onto the sand ahead of the runners, a detail long dismissed as
ritual. It is not ritual. Damp sand compacts ahead of a sledge instead of piling
up into a bow wave, and the reduction in drag is large enough to matter
enormously across two million trips.

## Up the Ramp

Nobody has found a ramp at Giza, which is unsurprising — a ramp is made of
material you want back, and would have been dismantled and reused. The debate is
therefore about geometry and volume.

A single straight ramp at a workable gradient becomes absurd as the pyramid
rises: by the upper courses it would contain more material than the monument
and extend far out into the desert. A ramp that zigzags up one face is much
smaller but has to be repeatedly rebuilt. A spiral wrapping the structure keeps
the volume modest but buries the corners and the faces, which is a serious
problem when those corners are exactly what the surveyors need to sight along to
keep the thing true. One proposal puts a narrow ramp inside the pyramid itself,
spiraling up just behind the casing, with open notches at the corners for
turning the blocks; density anomalies in the masonry have been read as support
for it, and it remains unproven.

Most likely the answer is mixed: a substantial external ramp for the lower
courses, which contain the overwhelming majority of the stone, and something
else for the last fifth, where the working surface is small and the volumes
are trivial by comparison. A quarry in Middle Egypt used in this era has
produced a steep haulage ramp flanked by staircases and rows of post holes,
apparently anchor points for ropes so that teams hauling above could be
assisted by teams pulling down against a mechanical advantage. That changes the
arithmetic of what gradient is possible. Herodotus, visiting more than two
thousand years later, was told the blocks were raised from step to step with
machines made of short timbers — which sounds like levering, and levering
certainly happened in the final placement, where a block had to be nudged the
last few centimeters into a joint you cannot slide a blade into.

## The People Who Did It

The pyramid builders were not slaves, and the evidence for that is not
sentimental. Southeast of the Sphinx, excavation has uncovered a settlement laid
out for the workforce: long galleries that appear to have been sleeping halls,
industrial bakeries with bread moulds by the hundred, a building where fish were
processed in bulk, and bone refuse indicating that cattle, sheep and goats were
being delivered and eaten in quantities that imply a deliberately provisioned
labor force rather than a starved one.

On the slope above lie the workers' own cemeteries — small mudbrick tombs, some
with inscriptions naming the occupant's role, arranged in a way that suggests
people buried where they had worked and considered it an honor. The skeletons
show what you would expect: heavy wear on the spine and joints, a high rate of
healed fractures, and evidence of real medical attention, including limbs set
well enough to knit straight and at least one amputation the patient survived.

Inside the pyramid, in the cramped relieving chambers above the King's Chamber
that nobody was ever meant to enter, workmen left gang names daubed in red
ochre. The crews had names like the Friends of Khufu, and were subdivided into
smaller units whose designations borrow the vocabulary of a ship's crew. Those
marks were made for internal bookkeeping — to record which gang had delivered
which block — and they are the closest thing we have to the builders speaking.

The workforce was probably a permanent skilled core of masons, surveyors, and
craftsmen, swollen seasonally by a rotating labor obligation drawn from the
villages, most efficiently during the inundation when the fields were underwater
and there was nothing else to do. Herodotus was told a hundred thousand men
worked in three-month shifts for twenty years. Modern estimates based on the
settlement's capacity and the ration logistics come out considerably lower, in
the low tens of thousands, which is both more plausible and more impressive.

The casing that once made these structures smooth and blinding white is almost
entirely gone, pried off across the medieval centuries and burned for lime or
built into the mosques and walls of Cairo. A cap of it survives near the summit
of Khafre's pyramid, which is why that one looks finished from a distance.
Everything else you can climb with your eyes is the rough core, never intended
to be seen at all.
