# What Broke the Bronze Age

A clay tablet found in the ruins of Ugarit, a wealthy port on the Syrian coast, carries a letter from its last king to the ruler of Cyprus. Enemy ships have appeared, he writes; they have burned his towns and done damage to his country. His own fleet is away in the waters off southern Anatolia and his army is up in Hittite territory. The city is undefended. Whether the letter was ever sent is not clear. Ugarit was destroyed shortly afterwards and never reoccupied, and the tablet stayed in the wreckage for three thousand years.

Within roughly fifty years either side of 1200 BC, something comparable happened across most of the eastern Mediterranean. The Mycenaean palaces in Greece burned or emptied. Hattusa, capital of the Hittite empire, was abandoned and the empire ceased to exist. City after city on the Levantine coast and in Cyprus shows a destruction layer at about the same horizon. Egypt survived, but as a smaller and poorer state that lost its Asiatic possessions and within a century and a half had fractured internally. Whole categories of thing simply stopped: monumental palace construction, long-distance luxury trade at the old volume, and, in Greece, writing itself.

Explaining this has been an industry for a century, and most of the explanations offered have one thing in common. They are too simple.

## What There Was to Lose

It helps to appreciate the size of the thing that fell over. The late Bronze Age in this region was not a collection of isolated kingdoms but an interlocking system, and the interlocking was the point.

Consider the metal that gives the age its name. Bronze is copper alloyed with tin. Copper was available in quantity on Cyprus and in a few other places. Tin was not available anywhere nearby; the nearest substantial sources lay far to the east, and the tin reaching Mediterranean workshops had travelled thousands of miles through intermediaries before it got there. This meant that the basic material of weapons, tools, armour and prestige goods could not be produced by any one kingdom on its own. A palace in Greece needed a trade route running across Anatolia or Mesopotamia to keep its soldiers armed.

The evidence for the resulting traffic is unusually vivid. A ship that went down off the southern Turkish coast at Uluburun, a century or so before the collapse, was carrying tons of copper ingots and a proportionate quantity of tin, along with raw glass, ebony, ivory, resin in Canaanite jars, ostrich eggshells, Mycenaean pottery, and a scarab bearing the name of an Egyptian queen. It is a single hull holding the products of at least seven or eight cultures. Meanwhile the diplomatic correspondence recovered from Amarna in Egypt shows the great kings of the period — Egypt, Hatti, Babylon, Assyria, Mitanni — addressing one another as brothers, arranging marriages, exchanging gold and lapis and horses, and complaining about the quality of the last shipment. Small states in the Levant wrote in as clients, asking for troops and denouncing their neighbours.

A system like that is efficient and it is fragile in a specific way: the pieces are not independently viable. Cut two or three of the routes and the failures propagate.

## The Sea Peoples Problem

The traditional villain is a coalition of migrating raiders known from Egyptian sources as the Sea Peoples. The fullest account is carved on the walls of Ramesses III's mortuary temple at Medinet Habu, which records a great land and sea battle in his eighth year — conventionally placed around 1177 BC — against a confederation whose component names include the Peleset, the Tjeker, the Shekelesh, the Denyen and the Weshesh. The reliefs show ox-carts with women and children among the combatants, which reads as migration rather than a raiding expedition. An earlier pharaoh, Merneptah, had already fought a Libyan invasion that included allied groups with similar-sounding names.

There are good reasons not to make these people the cause of everything. The first is that the Egyptian texts are triumphal inscriptions, a genre that does not distinguish carefully between an invasion repelled and a border skirmish inflated. The second is that the identifications of the individual groups with places — the Peleset with the Philistines who settled in southern Canaan is the most secure — remain largely conjectural. The third and most serious is chronological. Some of the destructions attributed to the Sea Peoples occurred before the events Egypt records, and others in places no seaborne raider would plausibly reach. Hattusa sits on a high plateau in central Anatolia, hundreds of miles inland; its abandonment needs a different explanation.

The most persuasive reading inverts the causation. Large groups of people moving by sea and land with their families are not usually an initiating cause. They are usually a symptom of something that made home unliveable. The Sea Peoples may be less the hammer than the sound of something already breaking.

## Drought, Earthquake, and the Sword

Three other candidates have serious evidence behind them.

Climate is the one that has gained the most ground recently. Pollen cores taken from lake sediments in the southern Levant, along with cave deposits and records of lake levels, point to a markedly drier period setting in during the thirteenth century and persisting for a long time. Similar indications have come out of Anatolia and Cyprus. Independently, the written sources describe grain shortage as a live crisis: Hittite kings ask urgently for grain shipments from Ugarit and Egypt, and Egyptian records mention grain sent north to keep Hatti alive. A Hittite empire in the Anatolian interior, with poor agricultural margins at the best of times, depending on imported grain shipped through a Syrian port, is a state with no slack in it at all.

Earthquakes are the second. The eastern Mediterranean sits on active faults, and several of the destroyed sites show damage patterns — collapsed walls fallen in a consistent direction, crushed skeletons under masonry — more suggestive of seismic failure than of sack. The geophysicist Amos Nur has argued for a clustered sequence of major quakes over several decades along connected fault segments, an "earthquake storm" that would have struck one city after another faster than any of them could rebuild. The mechanism is plausible; the difficulty is that earthquakes damage buildings but rarely end civilizations, since people generally rebuild on the rubble. The relevant question is what happens when they arrive on top of everything else.

The third is military. Robert Drews argued that the palace states of the period had built their armies around expensive chariot corps and massed archery, and that a change in infantry tactics — bodies of foot soldiers with javelins, long slashing swords and better personal armour, able to break chariotry in the open — destroyed the military advantage on which the palaces rested. The argument is elegant and probably explains part of the picture, though it works better for the fate of certain armies than for the abandonment of entire regions.

## Why One Cause Is Not Enough

None of these on its own carries the weight. A drought of a few decades is survivable for a state with reserves. An earthquake is survivable for a city with a hinterland. Raiders are survivable for a kingdom with an intact army and functioning allies. Egypt, after all, absorbed everything that was thrown at it and remained standing for another hundred and fifty years, in reduced form.

The more convincing account, argued at length by Eric Cline among others, treats the collapse as a systems failure — the kind that occurs when a network of highly interdependent parts loses enough of its connections that the remaining ones cannot carry the load. Drought reduces harvests. Reduced harvests force grain imports, which stretch the trade routes. Raiding and piracy make those routes dangerous and expensive. Insecure routes interrupt the tin supply, which degrades the army over years rather than days. A palace that cannot feed its dependants or arm its soldiers loses the authority that made it a palace, because its authority was exactly its capacity to redistribute. Internal revolt becomes likely. And every state that fails in this way removes another node from the network that its neighbours were relying on.

There is a corollary worth holding on to: the more efficient and specialized the system, the fewer shocks it takes. A village growing its own barley and smelting nothing does not care what happens to the tin route from the east. A palace with bureaucrats, scribes, chariot squadrons and a craft workforce fed from central stores cares enormously.

It is also worth resisting the word collapse where it does not apply. This was not uniform. Assyria came through weakened but intact. Several Phoenician coastal cities survived and, within a couple of centuries, were running the maritime trade of the whole Mediterranean. Cyprus continued to work copper. Egypt lost its empire but not its identity. The catastrophe was concentrated on precisely the palace-centred states whose whole organizing logic depended on the international system, and the places that were less integrated came through best.

## What Grew Back

The aftermath was genuinely dark in places. In Greece, population fell sharply, settlements shrank, the Linear B script vanished along with the administration it existed to serve, and four centuries passed before Greeks wrote anything again — this time with an alphabet borrowed from the Phoenicians, used first for personal and poetic purposes rather than for palace inventories.

But the spaces left by the empires filled with smaller and more inventive things. Iron came into general use, partly because it did not require a long-distance supply chain for tin and could be worked from ores available almost everywhere. Alphabetic writing spread, being far easier to learn than the syllabaries and cuneiform of the old order, and literacy reached people who were not professional scribes. Phoenician cities, Aramean kingdoms, the Neo-Hittite states of northern Syria, Israel and Judah, the Philistine pentapolis: a landscape of modest polities replaced a landscape of great kings, and out of it came most of what the classical world would inherit.
