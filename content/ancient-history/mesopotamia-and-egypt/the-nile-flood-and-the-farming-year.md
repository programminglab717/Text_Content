# The Nile Flood and the Farming Year

For most of the year the Nile in Upper Egypt is a clear green river running between banks of dust. Then, in the weeks after midsummer, the water begins to rise and change colour. It turns first a dull green, then a heavy reddish brown, and it keeps rising for six or eight weeks until the fields on either side are under water and the villages sit on their mounds like a chain of small islands. The brown is the point. It is soil, scoured off the Ethiopian highlands by monsoon rain and carried two thousand miles downstream, and its arrival on the fields of Egypt every summer is the reason the place could support a state at all.

Herodotus called Egypt the gift of the river, and for once the tourist slogan is a fair summary of the hydrology. Rain over the valley itself is negligible. Without the flood, the cultivable strip either side of the Nile would be desert, as the land a few hundred metres back from the water still is. The sharpness of that line — green, then nothing — is the most striking thing about the country from the air, and it was the organizing fact of Egyptian life for something like five thousand years.

## Where the Water Comes From

Two rivers make the Nile, and they behave very differently. The White Nile rises in the equatorial lakes and arrives at Khartoum with a flow that barely varies through the year, filtered and steadied by the vast swamps it passes through. The Blue Nile drains the Ethiopian highlands, where the summer monsoon dumps its rain on steep volcanic slopes, and it comes down in a violent seasonal surge carrying an enormous suspended load. The Atbara, joining further north, does much the same and then nearly dries up in winter.

So the flood is Ethiopian weather, arriving in Egypt some weeks late. The rise reaches Aswan in early summer, crests in late August or September in Middle Egypt, and drains away through the autumn. The White Nile keeps the river alive in the low season; the Blue Nile and Atbara supply the flood and almost all of the silt. Egyptians knew nothing of this — the source of the river was a standing mystery, variously explained by caverns beneath the first cataract or by the tears of a goddess — but they knew the timing with great precision, because everything else depended on it.

## Akhet: the Water Season

The Egyptian year had three seasons of four months each, and it began with the flood. Akhet, inundation, ran from roughly July to October. During akhet the land was not farmed because the land was underwater.

It was not simply left to drown, though. Over millennia the valley had been engineered into a system of basins. The Nile builds natural levees along its own banks, so the floodplain is slightly higher at the river's edge and dips away behind, and Egyptians extended and reinforced those levees with earth banks, then built cross-banks running out to the desert edge. The result was a chequerboard of basins, some of them very large, each connected to the river by a controlled breach. When the flood came up, water was admitted into a basin and held there for perhaps six weeks while the silt settled out, then released downstream or back into the falling river through a lower cut. What remained was a field soaked deep, coated with fresh sediment, and briefly free of the salts and weed seeds that plague irrigated farming elsewhere.

That annual flushing is why Egyptian agriculture escaped the salinization that damaged so much of the irrigated land in southern Mesopotamia. Water standing on a field and then drawn off carries dissolved salt away with it, rather than leaving it behind to concentrate season after season. The Egyptians did not design the system for that reason, but it is the main reason the same fields stayed productive for thousands of years without exhaustion.

Akhet was also the season with the most spare labour in the country, since the farms were under water and the farming population was not needed on them. Large state projects — quarrying, hauling stone, building — are generally understood to have drawn on that idle season, when men could be fed from the granaries and moved by boat over flooded ground that would otherwise have required hauling everything overland.

## Peret: Coming Forth

As the water fell, the land emerged, and the second season took its name from that: peret, the coming forth, roughly November to March. This was sowing and growing.

Sowing on newly drained land was simple to the point of casualness. The mud was already wet and soft, so the ground needed only breaking up rather than deep ploughing; a light wooden ard drawn by a pair of cattle was enough, and on soft ground a hoe would do. Seed was broadcast by hand from a basket, and then livestock — sheep, or in tomb paintings often pigs — were driven back and forth across the field to trample the grain down into the mud. It is the kind of technique that only works when the soil arrives pre-prepared, and it astonished foreign visitors, who were used to agriculture that required far more effort for far less return.

The staples were emmer wheat and barley. Barley made beer, which was a food rather than a luxury and a standard component of rations; emmer made bread, which along with beer accounted for most of the calories most Egyptians consumed. Flax was the other great field crop, grown for linen and for oil, and pulled rather than cut so the fibres ran the full length of the stem. Around and between these came lentils, chickpeas, onions, garlic, lettuce, cucumbers and melons, with dates, figs, pomegranates and grapes from orchards and vineyards on higher ground that needed watering by hand.

Land above the reach of the flood, or crops needing water after the basins had drained, were served by lifting devices. The shaduf — a counterweighted pole with a bucket on one end, worked by a man swinging it between river and channel — appears in Egypt during the New Kingdom and transformed what could be grown on the margins. The ox-driven waterwheel and the screw pump arrived considerably later, under Persian and Greek rule, and with them the possibility of a second crop on land the flood alone could not serve.

## Shemu: Harvest and Low Water

Shemu, the season of low water, ran from roughly March to June: harvest, then the long hot wait for the river to rise again.

Grain was cut with sickles — a curved wooden shaft set with flint teeth for most of Egyptian history, later bronze — and usually taken high on the stalk, leaving long stubble for grazing. The sheaves went to a threshing floor where cattle or donkeys were driven round in circles over them until the grain was knocked loose, then the mixture was tossed into the air with wooden scoops so the breeze could carry off the chaff. What came down clean was measured, recorded, and carried to mud-brick granaries with domed tops and a hatch at the base.

The measuring was the state's moment. Grain was the real currency of Egypt; salaries, taxes, rents and temple endowments were all reckoned in sacks of it long after coined money existed elsewhere. Scribes appear constantly in tomb paintings of the harvest, with their palettes and rolls, noting the yield, and just as often the painting shows a farmer being held or beaten over a shortfall. It is a rare instance of an ancient society depicting its own tax enforcement as a normal part of the agricultural calendar.

## Reading the River

Because everything rested on the flood, the flood itself was measured with care. Nilometers — stairways or shafts cut down to the water with graduated markings on the wall — stood at Elephantine near the first cataract and at other points downstream. A reading taken as the water rose gave weeks of warning about what the year would be like: how much land would be watered, how large the harvest would be, and therefore what taxes could reasonably be assessed. The records were kept, and lists of flood heights year by year survive from as early as the Old Kingdom.

The start of the flood also anchored the calendar. The heliacal rising of Sirius — the morning when the star first becomes visible again just before dawn after weeks lost in the sun's glare — falls close to the beginning of the rise, and it was treated as the marker of the new year. The civil calendar itself, however, ran a tidy 365 days with no leap day at all, so it slipped against the actual solar year by a day every four years and drifted all the way round over roughly fourteen centuries. Egyptian administrators kept using it anyway, and kept observing Sirius separately, which tells you something about the durability of a system that works well enough.

A flood that fell short was a catastrophe, and a succession of short floods was worse: less land watered, less grain, no reserve, and famine. A flood that came too high was also ruinous, sweeping away banks and drowning villages on their mounds. The hazard was old and remembered. A Ptolemaic-era inscription carved on a rock near Aswan tells of a seven-year famine under a king of the distant Old Kingdom, ended when the pharaoh made proper offerings to the god of the cataract — a story projected back across two thousand years, which is a reasonable indication of how long the fear had been in circulation.

## The Year That Stopped

The inundation ended within living memory. A first dam at Aswan went up at the beginning of the twentieth century and was raised twice; the High Dam, finished in 1970, put an end to the flood entirely. Lake Nasser now absorbs the summer surge and releases water through the year, which means Egyptian farmers can take two or three crops from land that once gave one, and that the catastrophic low year has been abolished.

The silt, though, settles out in the reservoir. The fields that fertilized themselves for five thousand years now require manufactured fertilizer, the Mediterranean delta is eroding without its annual resupply of sediment, and land kept permanently wet has begun to accumulate the salt that the old flushing carried away. The gift of the river turned out to be a specific mechanism, not a general benevolence, and it worked in one particular way.
