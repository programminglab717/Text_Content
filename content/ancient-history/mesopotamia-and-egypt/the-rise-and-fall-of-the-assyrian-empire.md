# The Rise and Fall of the Assyrian Empire

In a gallery of the British Museum there is a wall of carved gypsum showing the
siege of a walled town. Assyrian soldiers push a wheeled ram up a purpose-built
ramp of packed earth and timber. Archers shoot from behind wicker screens.
Defenders drop torches and try to catch the ram's head with chains while a
soldier inside works a long-handled ladle, pouring water on the flames.
Below and to the side, the aftermath: files of captives with bundles on their
shoulders and children on carts, leaving through a gate, and, off at the edge
where a modern museum label declines to dwell, men impaled on stakes.

The town is Lachish, in Judah. The king who commissioned the panels put them in
a room of his palace at Nineveh where visitors would have to walk past them. He
was not being candid by accident. Assyria spent three centuries making itself
the most efficient instrument of organized violence the ancient Near East had
produced, and it advertised the fact deliberately, in stone, in the expectation
that the advertisement would do part of the work.

## Merchants Long Before Soldiers

It did not start that way. The city of Ashur sits on a rocky bluff on the
Tigris, in a position that is defensible, commercially convenient, and
agriculturally mediocre — it lies at the dry margin where rainfall farming
becomes a gamble. In the early second millennium BCE its people solved that
problem by trading. Ashur ran a long-distance commercial network into central
Anatolia, six weeks away by donkey caravan, exporting tin and fine textiles and
bringing back silver and gold.

The archive of that trade survives, oddly enough, not at Ashur but in Anatolia,
at a merchant quarter outside the walls of Kanesh. Thousands of clay letters
turn up in the burned houses: orders, complaints, partnership accounts, disputes
over smuggled goods that avoided the local ruler's duties. Women in Ashur wrote
to husbands and sons abroad about weaving output, household debts, and how long
it had been since anyone sent money. The picture is of a small, prosperous,
litigious commercial city run by an assembly and an eponym official, with the
king as one authority among several.

Assyria's later self-image had no room for any of this. The royal annals of the
first millennium present an unbroken line of warrior kings extending the borders
by divine command, and the god Ashur — originally the deified city itself —
becomes a deity whose will is specifically that his territory should grow.

## The Machine Tiglath-Pileser Built

The Neo-Assyrian empire, the one that mattered, took shape over a few reigns in
the ninth and eighth centuries. Ashurnasirpal II campaigned annually, moved the
capital to Kalhu, and threw a dedication feast for his new palace whose guest
list and menu he had inscribed on a stele: tens of thousands of people, fed for
ten days on numbers of sheep, cattle, birds, bread and beer that read as
deliberate overstatement and may not be. Shalmaneser III pushed west into Syria
and left a black stone obelisk showing a king of Israel prostrate before him
with tribute, one of the earliest images we have of a named biblical figure.

The decisive changes came with Tiglath-Pileser III, who took the throne in the
mid-eighth century in circumstances that look very much like a coup. Before him,
Assyrian conquest often meant a campaign, a sack, a promise of annual tribute,
and a return the following decade to collect it again by force. He replaced that
cycle with administration. Conquered territory became provinces with appointed
governors reporting to the capital. Vassal kings who failed were removed and
their kingdoms annexed outright. Tribute became assessed taxation.

He also reorganized the army. The old system of seasonal levies raised from
Assyrian farmland gave way to a standing professional core, supplemented by
units recruited from conquered populations — including, notably, whole
contingents of foreign charioteers and cavalry absorbed intact. Cavalry steadily
displaced the chariot as the mobile arm over this period, and Assyrian armies
carried specialists: engineers who bridged rivers and built siege ramps, sappers
who undermined walls, quartermasters who maintained supply depots along
established routes. A road system with relay stations let messages move between
the frontier and the palace at a speed nobody else in the region could match.

Behind the army sat a paperwork habit that is the reason Assyrian history can be
dated at all. Each year was named after an official, and lists of those year-
names were kept in sequence for centuries. One entry notes that in a particular
official's year the sun was obscured — a solar eclipse, datable by calculation,
which pins the whole list to absolute years and, through synchronisms, anchors
much of the chronology of the surrounding Near East. The provincial governors
generated a matching flood of letters: grain shipments, road repairs, troop
numbers, complaints about neighbouring officials, reports of unrest on a
frontier. A good deal of it was still sitting in the palace archives when the
palaces burned.

## Deportation as Policy

The practice that defined Assyrian rule was not massacre. It was relocation.
Populations from a rebellious region were uprooted and resettled hundreds of
kilometers away, often in a part of the empire that had itself been emptied by
an earlier deportation, and the vacated land was filled with people moved in
from somewhere else entirely.

This was cold policy rather than blind cruelty, and the royal records treat it
as an administrative achievement. Deportees were counted, escorted, sometimes
provisioned; craftsmen and scribes among them were sorted out for royal
workshops. Communities stripped of their land, their temples, their neighbors
and their local aristocracy had nothing left to revolt around, and once settled
among strangers they had every incentive to become productive taxpayers. Over
generations the policy churned the ethnic map of the entire Near East and
contributed to a striking outcome: Aramaic, the language of many of the moved
populations, spread until it became the everyday speech and working script of
the empire that had done the moving.

The best-known instance is the fall of the northern kingdom of Israel to Sargon
II, whose deportation of its population produced the durable legend of the lost
tribes. Judah, further south, survived — Sennacherib besieged Jerusalem, took
tribute, and withdrew without capturing it, an outcome that both the Assyrian
annals and the biblical account manage to report as a success for their own
side.

## Stone, Water, and a Library

Assyrian kings built compulsively, and each new reign seemed to demand a new
capital. Sargon II raised an entire city from bare ground at Dur-Sharrukin, laid
out on a rigid plan with a palace, a temple complex, and guardian figures at the
gates — the *lamassu*, five-legged human-headed winged bulls carved so that they
read correctly whether seen from the front or the side. He died on campaign
before the place was properly occupied, and his son abandoned it.

That son, Sennacherib, moved to Nineveh and remade it. His engineers built a
system of canals to bring water down from hills tens of kilometers away, with a
stone aqueduct carrying the channel across a valley on corbelled arches — one of
the oldest substantial aqueducts anywhere, and still visible. He rebuilt the
city's walls, laid out gardens irrigated by that water, and filled his palace
with the relief sequences that include Lachish.

His grandson Ashurbanipal added the other thing Nineveh is remembered for. He
had been trained as a scholar, unusually for a crown prince, and could apparently
read the old languages. He sent agents across Babylonia to collect or copy
tablets, and assembled at Nineveh a library of literary, scholarly, divinatory
and technical texts running to many thousands of pieces. When the palace burned,
the fire baked the clay. Almost everything modern readers know about
Mesopotamian myth, including the fullest version of the Gilgamesh epic, came out
of that collection.

The library was not assembled out of antiquarian affection. Assyrian kings
governed under the constant supervision of omens, and the court employed
astrologers, exorcists, diviners and physicians whose job was to read the sky,
the livers of sacrificed sheep, and the behaviour of animals for signs bearing
on royal business. Their letters to the king survive in quantity and are
wonderfully human: scholars hedging an ambiguous reading, contradicting a rival,
grumbling about not having been paid, urging the king not to go out on an
unlucky day. When the signs threatened the king's life, the court could enthrone
a substitute — a stand-in installed with the regalia to absorb the predicted
misfortune, while the real king withdrew and was referred to in correspondence
as the farmer. The substitute was killed at the end of the period. A ritual that
elaborate is not superstition at the margins of government; it was part of how
the government worked.

## Seventy Years From Zenith to Ash

Ashurbanipal's Assyria looked unassailable. He had crushed a revolt led by his
own brother in Babylon, destroyed the Elamite kingdom and sacked Susa, and held
tribute-paying territory from the Persian Gulf into Egypt, which Assyrian armies
had occupied outright for a period. No neighbor could face an Assyrian field
army. And within about two decades of his death the empire ceased to exist.

The collapse was fast enough that historians still argue about its mechanism.
The proximate causes are clear: a disputed succession, civil war among rival
claimants, and the simultaneous emergence of two enemies capable of
cooperating — Nabopolassar, who made Babylon independent and then aggressive,
and the Medes under Cyaxares, coming down out of Iran. The allies took Ashur,
the old religious capital, and then in 612 BCE laid siege to Nineveh and
destroyed it. A remnant court held out at Harran for a few years with Egyptian
support, and that was the end.

The deeper causes are less settled. The empire had grown by consuming the
surplus of newly conquered provinces, and by the seventh century there was
little left within reach worth the cost of taking; Egypt had proved impossible
to hold. Decades of deporting and resettling had built an army and a
bureaucracy manned largely by people with no ancestral loyalty to Ashur.
Whatever the balance of reasons, the destruction was unusually complete. Two
centuries later a Greek mercenary army marched past the ruins of Assyrian
cities on the Tigris and recorded them as abandoned mounds whose builders nobody
locally could name.
