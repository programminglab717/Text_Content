# Making a Mummy

Before anyone in Egypt thought to embalm a body, the desert was already doing the job. A corpse laid in a shallow pit at the edge of the floodplain, curled on its side and covered over with sand, loses its water within days. Decay needs moisture, and dry sand pulls moisture out of tissue faster than bacteria can work with it. Graves from before the pharaohs have produced bodies with skin still stretched across the bones, hair on the scalp and nails on the fingers, dried to something like leather and never touched by a drop of resin.

The odd thing about the history that follows is that artificial mummification became necessary only because burial improved. Once the dead were laid in wooden coffins, then in brick-lined chambers, then in rock-cut tombs with plastered walls and doors, the sand no longer had access to them. A sealed coffin inside a stone chamber is a humid box. Bodies that would have dried out in a simple pit now rotted in comfort. Somebody had to reproduce by hand what the desert had been doing for free, and the answer arrived at over the following two thousand years was less a technique than an industry, with workshops, price tiers, specialists, and a liturgy of its own.

## What the Body Was For

Egyptian thinking about a person did not stop at the body. A human being was a set of components that death pulled apart and that funerary ritual was meant to reassemble. The ka was the vital double, which stayed near the tomb and had to be fed; offerings of bread, beer, beef and fowl were made to it in a chapel at the front of the tomb, and where real food ran out, a painted list of food on the wall and a spoken formula would stand in for it. The ba, usually drawn as a bird with a human head, was the mobile part. It could leave the tomb by day and had to be able to find its way back at night to a body it recognized. The akh was what the dead person became if the whole apparatus worked: an effective, transfigured spirit among the stars or in the following of Osiris.

Add the name and the shadow, both of which counted as parts of the person, and the practical consequence is obvious. The corpse was not a discarded shell. It was the anchor for everything else. Destroy it and the assembly came apart, which is why chiselling out a tomb owner's name and hacking away his portrait was a real and feared punishment, and why a statue of the deceased was often sealed into a small blind chamber in the tomb as a spare body in case the first one failed.

## Seventy Days

The full treatment conventionally ran seventy days, and the family handed the body over to a workshop rather than attempting any of it themselves. Greek writers who described the trade many centuries after it had matured reported several grades at different prices, from the complete job down to a cheap option amounting to little more than a purge and a salting. The surviving mummies broadly bear this out. There was a top of the range and there was a budget version, and you can tell them apart with the naked eye.

At the top of the range the first step was removal of the brain. A metal rod was worked up through a nostril, through the thin bone at the roof of the nasal cavity, and the brain was broken up and drained or drawn out in pieces. It was thrown away. Nobody appears to have thought it did anything worth keeping; it was wet tissue that would spoil, and out it went.

The heart was the opposite case. It was the organ of thought, memory, character and judgement, and it stayed in the chest, because in the hall of judgement it would be placed on a balance against the feather of Maat, the principle of truth and right order. A heart weighed down by wrongdoing tipped the scale and its owner was devoured by the waiting monster. So the heart was left alone, and when an embalmer removed it in error, it was put back.

The remaining organs came out through an incision in the left flank, usually short, cut with a blade of flint or obsidian. Lungs, liver, stomach and intestines were taken out, cleaned, dried separately in salt and stored in the four vessels now called canopic jars, each under the protection of one of the four sons of Horus. In earlier periods all four stoppers were human-headed; later they took the forms the jars are best known for, human, baboon, jackal and falcon. The kidneys were generally left in place, apparently because nobody went looking for them.

Then the drying, which was the heart of the method. Natron, a naturally occurring salt mixture of sodium carbonate and bicarbonate gathered from dried-out lake beds in the western desert, was packed into the emptied cavity in linen bundles and heaped over the outside of the body. For something in the range of thirty-five to forty days it sat in the salt while water came out of it. What was lifted off the table afterwards weighed a fraction of what had been laid on it: shrunken, hard, dark and permanently uninhabitable by anything capable of causing rot.

Everything after that was restoration. The body was washed, rubbed with oils and aromatic resins, and packed back out towards a lifelike shape with linen, sawdust, mud or resin-soaked cloth. Skin was sometimes coloured, red for men and yellow for women, following the conventions of tomb painting. Artificial eyes went into the sockets. In certain periods the embalmers grew ambitious and pushed packing beneath the skin through small slits to restore the contours of the cheeks and limbs, with mixed results.

## The Wrapping

Wrapping took the longest and carried the most ritual. It consumed enormous quantities of linen, a good deal of it recycled household cloth rather than new. Fingers and toes were bound individually, then limbs, then the trunk, layer on layer, with warm resin brushed between the layers to glue the whole thing into a rigid shell. Priests recited while they worked, and the spells that belonged to particular bandages and particular amulets were written down and copied from one generation to the next, which makes the wrapping a liturgical act as much as a craft one.

Amulets went in among the layers, each with an assigned position: the djed pillar for stability laid along the spine, the knot of Isis, the wedjat eye for wholeness and protection, and over the chest a large stone scarab carrying a spell that instructs the heart not to speak against its owner during the weighing. A mask covered the head and shoulders, cartonnage and gilding for most people, sheet gold for a king.

## What Went In With You

The mummy was one item on a long inventory. Shabtis, small mummiform figures, were included by the dozen and eventually by the hundred, each inscribed with a spell that would make it answer and go to work if the dead person were called on for agricultural labour in the next world; a well-funded burial might include a full gang with overseers to keep them at it. A papyrus of the spells for going forth by day, known now as the Book of the Dead, supplied the passwords, the names of gatekeepers and the declarations of innocence needed to get through the judgement hall. Furniture, clothing, sandals, board games, food, jars of wine and beer, boats and weapons went in according to what the family could manage.

At the door of the tomb came the decisive act, the Opening of the Mouth. A priest touched the face of the mummy or its mask with an adze and other implements while spells were recited, and the ritual restored the senses: breath, speech, sight and the ability to eat. Until it was done the mummy was preserved but inert. Afterwards it could receive offerings. Then the coffin went into the burial chamber, the shaft was filled with rubble, and the mortuary cult began, ideally supported by an endowment of farmland whose income paid a priest to bring food and recite the offering formula on festival days for as long as the arrangement held.

## The Long Tail

Mummification began as a royal privilege and worked its way steadily down the social scale. By the first millennium BC a modest wrapping and a painted coffin were within reach of a reasonably ordinary household, and the trade expanded to meet demand, sometimes producing work that would have embarrassed an earlier generation: bundles with limbs missing, bones of more than one individual inside a single wrapping, splendid outer casings over careless work within.

The same centuries saw an extraordinary volume of animal mummies. Ibises, cats, falcons, crocodiles, dogs, snakes and bulls were embalmed as votive offerings, bought by pilgrims and deposited in dedicated catacombs, some of which have yielded bundles by the million. Under Roman rule the craft took another turn. The best-known mummies of that period carry painted panel portraits set over the face, done in coloured wax on thin wood, and they look out at the viewer with a directness that nothing else from the ancient world quite matches.

Christianity brought the practice to an end gradually, less by prohibition than by making the reasoning behind it beside the point. Then Europe found a use for the leftovers. Powdered mummy, sold in apothecaries' shops as mumia, was a standard remedy for centuries on the strength of a confusion between embalming resin and medicinal bitumen, and the supply was met partly by plundering ancient cemeteries and partly by faking the product outright. The nineteenth-century fashion for unwrapping a mummy in front of a ticketed audience destroyed a great many more, usually without a record of what had been inside. A considerable number of Egyptian dead were kept intact for three thousand years and then eaten, burned or unrolled for an evening's entertainment inside of two hundred.
