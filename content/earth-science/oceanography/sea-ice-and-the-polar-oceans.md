# Sea Ice and the Polar Oceans

Stand on Arctic sea ice in late winter and it can feel as solid and permanent as the ground itself, stretching flat and white to every horizon. It isn't ground, and it isn't permanent. It is ocean water, frozen at the surface into a floating skin a few meters thick at most, riding on kilometers of liquid water below and drifting with the currents and winds like the largest, slowest raft imaginable. That distinction — frozen seawater rather than land ice — shapes almost everything unusual about how it forms, how it behaves, and what lives in and around it.

## How Ocean Water Freezes

Dissolved salt lowers the temperature at which water freezes, so seawater needs to get colder than fresh water before ice starts to form, and even then it does not freeze the way a fresh puddle does. As ice crystals form, they exclude most of the salt that was dissolved in the water, a process called brine rejection, leaving pockets and channels of concentrated, very cold salty water trapped within and beneath the growing ice, while the ice itself ends up far less salty than the water it froze from. That rejected brine is denser than the surrounding seawater and sinks, and in a handful of places around Antarctica in particular, this sinking brine is cold and salty enough to become one of the main sources feeding the deep, dense bottom water that spreads across the floor of the world's oceans.

The process starts with tiny needle-like ice crystals called frazil, which form first in the near-surface water and then clump together into a soupy layer called grease ice, recognizable by the way it dampens ripples and gives the sea surface a matte, oily sheen even in a light wind. As it consolidates further, it can pass through a stage of small rounded pieces called pancake ice, their raised rims formed from repeatedly bumping against neighboring pieces in a choppy sea, before finally locking together into a continuous sheet.

## A Zoo of Ice Types

Not all sea ice is the same age or thickness, and the distinction matters more than it might seem. Ice that survives a full summer melt season and makes it through a second winter is called multiyear ice, and it tends to be thicker, less salty, and structurally stronger than ice formed during a single winter, since repeated freeze-thaw cycles gradually flush out more of the trapped brine. First-year ice, formed and melted within a single season, dominates in places where currents and winds export ice quickly or where summer melt is thorough enough to clear the previous winter's cover entirely. Where winds or currents push ice floes against each other or against a coastline, the ice buckles and piles up into pressure ridges, jagged linear features that can rise several meters above the surrounding flat ice and extend even farther below the waterline, complicating travel across the ice for both wildlife and any ships or research teams attempting to cross it.

Ice also differs by how freely it moves. Landfast ice forms and remains attached to a coastline or grounded on shallow seafloor, staying essentially fixed in place through the winter and providing a stable platform that coastal communities and researchers alike have long used as a seasonal roadway. Pack ice, by contrast, is not attached to anything and drifts freely under the push of wind and current, constantly shifting, opening into cracks called leads, and closing again with enough force to raft one floe on top of another.

## Two Poles, Two Different Oceans

Sea ice behaves differently at each pole because the underlying geography is essentially inverted. The Arctic is an ocean basin nearly enclosed by continents, which limits how far ice can drift before running into land, and allows a persistent core of thick, multiyear ice to survive for years in the central basin, circulating slowly under the influence of a semi-permanent wind-driven gyre. Antarctica, by contrast, is a continent surrounded by open ocean, so sea ice there is free to spread outward across an enormous area each winter, unconstrained by any coastline, and just as free to melt back and disperse each summer, so that Antarctic sea ice is overwhelmingly first-year ice on a scale the Arctic does not have.

The two poles also respond to a changing climate through different physical pathways, since the Arctic's fate is tied closely to ocean heat transported in from lower latitudes and trapped in an enclosed basin, while the Antarctic's ice extent responds more to shifting winds circling an open, unobstructed ocean, along with fresh meltwater draining off the ice sheet itself, and the two systems have not tracked each other in lockstep as a result.

## Life Inside the Ice

Sea ice looks lifeless from a distance and is anything but up close. The brine channels that riddle newly formed and first-year ice, kept liquid by their high salt concentration even at temperatures well below the freezing point of fresh water, host communities of algae adapted to live in extreme cold, high salinity, and low light, tinting the underside of ice floes a visible brown or gold in places where the algae grow thick. This ice algae bloom provides a critical, predictable pulse of food at a time of year when open-water phytoplankton production has barely begun, and Antarctic krill in particular rely heavily on it, grazing directly on the underside of ice floes during the winter months when almost no other food is available. That dependency cascades upward: krill feed a huge share of the Southern Ocean's fish, penguins, seals, and whales, which makes the seasonal presence and extent of sea ice a matter of consequence far beyond the ice itself. In the Arctic, seals use the ice as a platform to rest and raise pups away from most predators, and polar bears use it as a hunting platform, walking out onto the ice to hunt seals at breathing holes in a strategy that depends entirely on ice being present and stable enough to walk on for much of the year.

## Polynyas and a Word of Caution About Icebergs

Even within an otherwise unbroken expanse of pack ice, persistent patches of open water called polynyas can form and remain open for much of the winter, kept ice-free either by strong, steady winds that push newly forming ice away as fast as it forms, or by an upwelling of relatively warmer deep water that melts ice from below faster than the cold air above can refreeze it. Far from being a minor curiosity, polynyas function as vital wintertime oases: seabirds and marine mammals that would otherwise have to travel enormous distances to find open water for breathing and feeding instead concentrate around these reliable openings, and the same locations often become disproportionately important sites of new sea ice formation once winds shift, since freshly exposed water in cold air freezes and produces brine rapidly.

It is worth being precise about a common confusion: icebergs are not sea ice at all. An iceberg is a chunk of freshwater ice that has calved off a glacier or ice shelf, land ice that formed from compacted snow over centuries before ever reaching the ocean, whereas sea ice forms directly from freezing seawater and rarely grows thicker than a few meters regardless of age. The two can appear alongside each other in polar waters and are often lumped together in casual description, but they differ in origin, salinity, structure, and scale, and a ship's navigator watching for hazards has good reason to tell them apart, since an iceberg's underwater bulk and freshwater ice structure behave quite differently from a drifting ice floe.

## A Mirror for the Sun

Beyond its role as habitat, sea ice performs a straightforward but consequential physical job: it reflects a large share of incoming sunlight back to space, rather than letting it be absorbed the way the dark open ocean absorbs it. This gives rise to a feedback loop with real teeth. Less ice exposes more dark ocean, which absorbs more heat, which warms the water, which makes it harder for ice to reform and easier for existing ice to melt further, exposing still more dark ocean the following season. This ice-albedo feedback is one of the clearest examples in the climate system of a small initial change reinforcing itself rather than being damped out, and it is a large part of why polar regions have warmed faster than the global average in recent decades, with consequences that reach the ice-dependent food web long before they reach anyone standing comfortably on solid ground at a lower latitude.
