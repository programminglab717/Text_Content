# Mapping the Seafloor

For most of the history of seafaring, the space beneath a ship's hull was the least-known part of the entire voyage. Sailors could read the stars overhead with real precision by the eighteenth century, and chart coastlines by patient triangulation, but the deep ocean floor beneath their keel might as well have been the far side of the moon: unreachable, unseeable, and left blank or simply guessed at on the charts of the day. The story of how that blank space slowly filled in is, in large part, a story about the limits of whatever tool happened to be available at each stage, and the ingenuity required to push past those limits one method at a time.

## A Single Point at a Time

The earliest method for measuring ocean depth was almost embarrassingly simple: pay out a weighted line over the side of a ship until the weight hits bottom, then measure how much line went out. This worked well enough in shallow coastal water, where sailors needed depth readings constantly to avoid running aground, and lead lines marked at regular intervals had been standard maritime equipment for a very long time. In deep water, far from any coast, the method became brutally impractical. A hemp line thick enough not to snap under its own weight would stretch and drift at an angle as it descended, introducing error, and simply hauling enough line back up after a sounding several kilometers deep, by hand, could consume the better part of a working day for a single data point. Entire ocean basins were charted, in effect, by a scattering of individual dots, each one representing enormous effort, with vast unmeasured distances assumed rather than known in between.

## Cables Beneath the Waves

Interest in the deep seafloor sharpened considerably once it became clear that a functioning transatlantic telegraph cable would need to be laid directly across it. Matthew Fontaine Maury, an American naval officer who had already built a reputation compiling wind and current charts from decades of ship captains' logbooks, took a particular interest in the depth soundings gathered across the North Atlantic and used them to argue for the existence of a relatively shallow plateau connecting the two continents, a feature he suggested calling the telegraph plateau, that made a cable route look more feasible than it might otherwise have seemed. Cable-laying itself failed repeatedly before succeeding, for reasons involving cable insulation and handling as much as seafloor terrain, but each expedition contributed additional soundings to a chart that had, until then, been drawn mostly from sparse and scattered older data.

## The Challenger Expedition

The systematic study of the ocean floor, and of the ocean more broadly, is generally traced to a single voyage: the converted warship HMS Challenger, which set out in the 1870s on a multi-year global circumnavigation dedicated entirely to scientific observation, with much of her gun deck stripped out and replaced with laboratories, winches, and sounding equipment. Over the course of the expedition, Challenger's crew took depth soundings and dredged bottom samples at stations scattered across every major ocean, discovering, among a great deal else, that the ocean floor was not the featureless basin many had assumed, but included dramatic variation in depth, and confirming the existence of extraordinarily deep trenches, including the one that would turn out to hold the deepest point in any ocean. The specimens, sediment samples, and data the voyage brought back took experts years afterward simply to process and describe, and the expedition is widely treated as the founding event of oceanography as a distinct scientific discipline, seafloor mapping included.

## Listening for the Bottom

The lead line's fundamental limitation, requiring the ship to stop and painstakingly measure one point at a time, was not solved by any improvement to the line itself, but by abandoning rope entirely in favor of sound. Echo sounding, developed and refined in the decades around the First and Second World Wars, works on a simple principle: send a pulse of sound down from the ship, measure how long it takes the echo to return from the seafloor, and use the known speed of sound in water to calculate depth directly from that travel time. Unlike a lead line, an echo sounder could take a reading continuously while the ship was underway at normal speed, transforming depth measurement from a laborious occasional sample into a continuous profile traced automatically as the ship crossed the ocean. Charts from the mid-twentieth century onward show the ocean floor's major features — the mid-ocean ridges, trenches, and abyssal plains — in something close to their true shape for the first time, all traced out by ships simply steaming along their normal routes with a sounder running.

A single ship, however, can only ever trace a line, and the space between ship tracks on even a well-traveled ocean remained unmapped in fine detail. Multibeam sonar, developed later in the twentieth century, addressed this by fanning many sound beams out simultaneously across a wide swath beneath and to either side of the ship rather than sending a single beam straight down, letting one ship's pass paint a continuous strip of seafloor rather than a single thin line, and modern seafloor surveys build up full three-dimensional coverage of an area by running parallel swaths back and forth like mowing a lawn.

## Seeing the Seafloor From Space

Even multibeam sonar, run from ships, can only cover a limited area within a human lifetime of survey time, and vast stretches of open ocean, particularly far from major shipping lanes, remain surveyed only sparsely by ship-based methods even today. The most efficient tool for filling in those gaps turned out to come from an unlikely direction: satellites. Large seafloor features like seamounts and ridges have enough mass to subtly alter the pull of gravity in their immediate vicinity, and that altered gravity pulls the ocean's surface itself into a very slight bump or dip directly above the feature, too small to notice by eye but measurable by a satellite radar altimeter tracking the sea surface's height with great precision. By scanning the height of the sea surface across an entire ocean basin repeatedly, satellites can infer the rough shape of the seafloor beneath it indirectly, through gravity, revealing the outlines of thousands of seamounts that had never been visited by any ship at all, even if the resolution of this gravity-based method falls well short of what a ship's multibeam sonar can achieve up close.

Even now, with satellites, sonar, and international efforts to combine every available survey into unified global charts, a smaller share of the ocean floor has been mapped in comparable detail to what orbiting spacecraft have achieved for the surface of the moon and Mars, a reminder of how much of the planet's own surface remains, in the most literal sense, uncharted territory.

## Robots Finish What Ships Started

The most recent chapter in seafloor mapping has involved taking the sensor off the ship entirely. Autonomous underwater vehicles, which navigate and collect data without a human pilot or a physical tether, can run pre-programmed survey patterns close to the seafloor for hours or days at a stretch, gathering multibeam sonar data at a resolution no hull-mounted system on a surface ship, standing off at a much greater distance from the bottom, could match. Remotely operated vehicles, tethered to a ship above and controlled in real time by an operator watching a video feed, trade that independence for the ability to react instantly to what the cameras show, making them the tool of choice whenever a survey needs a human judgment call in the loop, such as identifying wreckage, sampling a specific rock formation, or inspecting an undersea cable.

These vehicles have turned some of the most famous applications of seafloor mapping into media events in their own right, from locating long-lost shipwrecks resting far beyond the reach of any diver to surveying newly erupted volcanic terrain on the mid-ocean ridge within months of its formation. But the bulk of their working hours go to far less dramatic tasks: systematically filling in gaps in the bathymetric record, ground-truthing what satellite gravity data only suggested, and building the high-resolution seafloor maps that undersea cable routes, offshore wind foundations, and marine protected area boundaries increasingly depend on.
