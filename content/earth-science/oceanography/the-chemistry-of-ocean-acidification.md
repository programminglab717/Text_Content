# The Chemistry of Ocean Acidification

A pteropod is a small marine snail, sometimes called a sea butterfly for the wing-like lobes it uses to swim, and under a microscope its shell is a small marvel of engineering: thin, translucent, and precisely built from calcium carbonate extracted from seawater. Researchers who have placed pteropod shells in seawater chemically adjusted to match conditions expected to become more common in parts of the ocean found something unsettling. Within weeks, the shells visibly pitted and thinned, dissolving from the outside in the same water the animal needs to build them in the first place. The chemistry behind that experiment is not exotic or speculative. It follows directly and unavoidably from a well-understood reaction that is already happening, at some pace, in every ocean on Earth.

## An Ocean That Drinks Carbon Dioxide

The ocean has absorbed a substantial share of the carbon dioxide humanity has added to the atmosphere since industrialization began, a service it performs simply by being a vast surface in contact with the air, constantly exchanging gas across that boundary in both directions. Left alone, this absorption would seem like unambiguously good news, since it keeps that carbon dioxide out of the atmosphere, where it would otherwise contribute more directly to warming. But carbon dioxide does not simply dissolve inertly in seawater the way it dissolves in a bottle of soda. It reacts with water molecules to form carbonic acid, a weak acid that promptly sheds a hydrogen ion to become bicarbonate. That extra hydrogen ion is what acidity measures, and the ocean's average surface chemistry has already shifted measurably toward more acidic, by a fraction of a point on the pH scale, since large-scale carbon emissions began.

## The Carbonate Squeeze

The pH shift itself, while real, is not the part that threatens shell-building animals most directly. The more consequential change happens to carbonate ions, the second ingredient, along with calcium, that organisms need to build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons. Those extra hydrogen ions produced when carbon dioxide dissolves do not simply float free; they react preferentially with existing carbonate ions already present in seawater, converting them into more bicarbonate and, in the process, drawing down the pool of carbonate ions available for anything else to use. An organism trying to build a shell in this altered water is drawing from a shrinking supply of one of its two essential raw materials, and in seawater where the carbonate concentration falls low enough relative to calcium — a threshold chemists describe using a value called the saturation state — calcium carbonate structures stop merely being harder to build and start actively dissolving on their own, the same reaction that stripped the experimental pteropod shells down within weeks.

## Not All Water Is Equally Vulnerable

This chemistry does not play out uniformly across the world's oceans, and the unevenness matters enormously for which ecosystems face the earliest and sharpest effects. Cold water holds more dissolved gas than warm water for the same reason a cold soda holds its fizz longer than a warm one, which means polar and high-latitude oceans absorb carbon dioxide especially efficiently and are correspondingly among the first regions expected to cross into corrosive, carbonate-poor conditions. Deep water throughout the ocean is naturally lower in carbonate saturation than surface water even without any human influence, simply because sinking organic material decomposes as it falls, releasing carbon dioxide at depth continuously; wherever that naturally acidic deep water is brought to the surface by upwelling, as it is seasonally along parts of the Pacific coast of North America, it arrives already primed to compound whatever additional acidification has occurred from atmospheric absorption. Shellfish hatcheries along that coast have had direct commercial experience with this compounding effect, tracing unexplained die-offs of oyster larvae to upwelling events that delivered water already corrosive enough to interfere with shell formation in the larvae's first vulnerable days of life.

## Winners, Losers, and Shelled Casualties

It would be a mistake to describe every marine organism as a loser in this chemistry, and a more honest account has to hold some nuance. Certain seagrasses and some species of algae that rely on dissolved carbon dioxide directly for photosynthesis, rather than drawing from bicarbonate, may find slightly more of their preferred raw material available. But the organisms placed at a disadvantage include an outsized share of the base of marine food webs and some of its most economically important members: coral, whose reef-building calcification slows as saturation state drops even before bleaching from heat stress becomes a separate problem; coccolithophores and foraminifera, single-celled organisms whose carbonate shells form part of the biological pump that exports carbon to the deep sea; mollusks farmed commercially for food across much of the world; and pteropods themselves, which despite their small size serve as a major food source for juvenile salmon and numerous other commercially significant fish. A shift in the availability of a single class of prey animal, driven by chemistry rather than by fishing pressure or habitat loss, is a harder problem to see coming and a harder one to manage once it arrives.

## A Second Crisis Riding Alongside Warming

It has become common to describe ocean acidification as the lesser-known twin of climate warming, sharing the same root cause but operating through an entirely separate mechanism, and the framing is useful precisely because the two problems are so easy to conflate. Warming and acidification both trace back to the same rising atmospheric carbon dioxide, and both are measured, tracked, and projected using similar tools. But warming acts primarily through the physics of how the atmosphere and ocean trap and redistribute heat, while acidification acts through straightforward inorganic chemistry that would proceed on schedule even in a hypothetical world where extra carbon dioxide somehow failed to trap any additional heat at all. Treating them as a single undifferentiated problem risks missing that a solution aimed only at limiting temperature rise, such as reflecting sunlight away from the planet, would do essentially nothing to slow the ocean chemistry described here, since the carbonate system responds to the carbon dioxide itself, not to the temperature it may or may not eventually cause. Any accounting of the ocean's condition that tracks temperature alone is only reading half of the ledger.
