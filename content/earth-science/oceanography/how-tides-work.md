# How Tides Work

At low tide in the Bay of Fundy, boats that were floating that morning sit stranded on wet mud, tilted on their keels, waiting for water that will not return for six hours. Walk out onto the flats and it is possible to cover distances that were, at high tide, well offshore, out past where hulls sat afloat. Nowhere else on Earth does the same stretch of coastline swing between two such different worlds every single day, but the mechanism producing that swing is running under every coastline on the planet, just usually with less drama to show for it.

## The Pull of Two Bodies

Tides begin with gravity, and specifically with the fact that gravity's pull weakens with distance. The moon pulls on the whole Earth, but it pulls harder on the side of the Earth facing it than on the side facing away, because the near side is closer. That difference in pull — not the pull itself — is what raises a tidal bulge. Water on the near side gets tugged toward the moon slightly more strongly than the solid Earth beneath it does, so it bulges toward the moon. Water on the far side gets tugged slightly less than the Earth beneath it, so relative to the planet, it lags behind and bulges away from the moon. The result is two bulges, roughly opposite each other, rather than one.

The sun does the same thing, and because it is so much more massive than the moon, its gravitational pull on Earth is actually stronger overall. But tides depend on the difference in pull across the Earth's diameter, not the total pull, and because the sun is so much farther away, that difference is smaller than the moon's. The moon's tide-raising effect ends up considerably larger than the sun's, even though the sun wins on raw gravitational force.

## Spring Tides and Neap Tides

The Earth spins through those two bulges roughly once a day, which is why most coastlines experience two high tides and two low tides in a bit more than twenty-four hours — the extra time comes from the moon also moving along its own orbit, so the Earth has to rotate slightly farther each day to catch back up to it. But the sun's bulges are superimposed on the moon's, and their relative alignment changes over the course of a lunar month.

When the sun, Earth, and moon line up — at new moon and full moon — the solar and lunar bulges reinforce each other, producing the highest high tides and lowest low tides of the month, known as spring tides, a name with nothing to do with the season and everything to do with the water "springing" up. When the moon is at a right angle to the sun as seen from Earth — the first and last quarter phases — the solar bulge partially cancels the lunar one, producing neap tides, the smallest tidal range of the month. This cycle repeats roughly every two weeks, riding on top of the daily rise and fall.

## The Shape of the Coast Matters

If the ocean were a smooth, uniform layer of water over a featureless globe, the story could end there: two predictable bulges, modulated by the monthly spring-neap cycle. Real coastlines wreck that simplicity. The Mediterranean, largely closed off from the open Atlantic, has tides so small they are easy to miss entirely. The Bay of Fundy, by contrast, has a shape and depth that happen to match the natural resonant period of the tidal wave moving through it, the way pushing a swing at just the right rhythm builds up a much larger arc than the same effort delivered off-beat. Energy that would spread out and dissipate elsewhere gets funneled and amplified instead, and the tidal range grows accordingly.

Rotation adds another complication. As the tidal bulge moves through an ocean basin, the Coriolis effect deflects the flow, and instead of a simple wave sloshing back and forth, the tide often rotates around a point where the tidal range is essentially zero, called an amphidromic point, radiating outward in a pattern more like ripples spiraling from a stone dropped in a pond than a wave running straight across a bathtub. Charts of tidal range across an ocean basin show these points clearly, along with the concentric rings of increasing range surrounding them.

Local geography also decides whether a coastline sees two roughly equal high tides a day, one dominant high tide with a smaller second one, or effectively a single tide cycle each day. These patterns — semidiurnal, mixed, and diurnal tides — depend on how the basin's shape interacts with the different tidal frequencies contributed by the moon and sun, including the fact that the moon's orbit is tilted relative to the equator, which introduces daily inequality into the mix. The Gulf of Mexico is one of the few places with a nearly pure diurnal tide, a single high and low each day, purely as an accident of its shape and depth.

## Reading the Tide Tables

None of this variability makes tides unpredictable, which is precisely why they were among the first natural phenomena to be reduced to reliable tables well before the underlying physics was fully worked out. Coastal communities kept empirical tide records for practical reasons — fishing, navigation, harbor access — long before Isaac Newton's law of universal gravitation gave the bulges a proper explanation. Even after Newton, predicting a specific harbor's tide precisely required treating the tide as a sum of many separate periodic components, each tied to a different astronomical rhythm — the moon's orbit, the sun's apparent path, the tilt of the moon's orbital plane — added together and calibrated against years of local measurement. Harmonic tide-predicting machines, mechanical assemblies of gears and pulleys built to sum those components automatically, were producing usable tide tables for major ports well before electronic computers existed.

## Bores, Power, and Other Consequences

In a small number of rivers around the world, the incoming tide arrives not as a gradual rise but as a single abrupt wave, called a tidal bore, that pushes upstream against the river's current and can be surfed, quite literally, for long stretches inland. A bore forms when a large tidal range meets a shallow, funnel-shaped river mouth that keeps compressing the incoming tidal wave until its front steepens into something closer to a breaking wave than a normal tide, arriving as a visible, sometimes roaring wall of water that river traffic has to be timed around.

The same concentration of energy that produces a dramatic bore or an outsized tidal range in a place like the Bay of Fundy has also made certain coastlines attractive for generating electricity. Tidal power stations built across the mouths of high-range estuaries let water flow through turbines as the tide rises and, in some designs, again as it falls, extracting a share of the kinetic energy that would otherwise simply flow past unused. Unlike wind or solar power, tidal flow is entirely predictable years in advance, governed by the same astronomical mechanics that fill the tide tables, which makes it one of the few renewable energy sources that a planner can schedule against with near-total confidence, even if suitable sites with a large enough range to make the engineering worthwhile remain relatively rare along the world's coastlines.

## Life Timed to the Water

For organisms living on the shore, none of this astronomy matters directly, but its consequences shape entire ways of life. Barnacles, mussels, and intertidal snails have evolved to survive being submerged for one stretch of hours and exposed to air, sun, and predators for the next, arranging their feeding, reproduction, and even their body chemistry around a schedule set by the moon. Some marine worms and fish spawn in synchrony with spring tides specifically, timing the release of eggs to the highest water of the month so that larvae are swept as far as possible from the reef or shoreline where they hatched. The grunion runs on beaches in California and Baja California, where a small silvery fish comes ashore to spawn on the sand during the highest tides and rides the next high tide back out, are a vivid, visible version of a timing strategy that plays out more quietly in thousands of other intertidal species, all keeping time with bulges of water raised by the pull of a moon a quarter million miles away.

Even harbors and shipping channels are built around this rhythm rather than against it. Ports with large tidal ranges schedule the movement of the deepest-draft vessels around the predicted high tide, and canal locks and tidal barriers exist specifically to let engineers negotiate with a cycle that nobody has ever managed to slow down or speed up, only to predict with growing precision the further back the record of observations reaches.
