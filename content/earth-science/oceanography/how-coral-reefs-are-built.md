# How Coral Reefs Are Built

Run a hand along a piece of dead coral rubble on a beach and it feels like stone, because by that point it mostly is. Limestone, to be specific — the same basic mineral, calcium carbonate, that makes up chalk cliffs and marble quarries. It is easy to forget, looking at a reef from a boat or a beach, that the entire structure was assembled by animals, one small skeleton at a time, over a stretch of time that dwarfs a human life by orders of magnitude.

## An Animal That Builds Stone

A single coral polyp looks like a miniature sea anemone, because the two are close relatives: a soft-bodied cylinder a few millimeters across, ringed with stinging tentacles, with a single opening that serves as both mouth and anus. Reef-building corals live as colonies of thousands to millions of these genetically identical polyps, connected by living tissue, sitting atop a skeleton the colony has secreted beneath and around itself. Each polyp draws calcium and carbonate ions out of the seawater around it and deposits them as a cup-shaped structure of aragonite, a crystal form of calcium carbonate, extending its own tiny chamber outward and upward as it grows. Multiply that by a colony's worth of polyps working simultaneously for years, decades, or centuries, and the result is the branching, boulder-like, or plate-like structures recognizable as coral.

## A Partnership With Algae

None of this construction happens quickly enough to matter without help, and the help comes from an intracellular partnership most people never think about when they picture a coral. Living inside the tissue of most reef-building corals, in numbers dense enough to give the coral its color, are single-celled photosynthetic algae called zooxanthellae. These algae carry out photosynthesis using sunlight that penetrates the shallow water the coral lives in, and they pass a substantial share of the sugars and other organic compounds they produce directly to their coral host. In return, the coral provides the algae a protected home and a steady supply of some of the waste compounds, like carbon dioxide and nitrogen, that photosynthesis requires as raw material.

This partnership is what allows reef-building coral to grow fast enough, and secrete skeleton efficiently enough, to construct something as large as a reef. Photosynthesis inside the coral's own tissue appears to make calcification biochemically easier, likely by removing carbon dioxide and shifting the local chemistry in the polyp's calcifying space toward conditions more favorable for aragonite to form. It also explains why reef-building coral is so particular about where it can live: it needs water shallow and clear enough for meaningful sunlight to reach the algae, which rules out murky, sediment-choked water and most of the deep ocean, and it needs that water to stay within a fairly narrow warm temperature range, since heat stress causes the partnership to break down.

## From Polyp to Platform

A single colony, however large, is not a reef. A reef is the accumulated, cemented mass of many generations of coral skeleton, along with the skeletal remains of coralline algae, mollusks, and other calcifying organisms that live alongside the coral, bound together over time into a rigid limestone framework. Waves and grazing fish constantly break pieces off the living surface, boring sponges and worms tunnel into the skeleton, and parrotfish famously graze algae off dead coral surfaces by scraping away mouthfuls of limestone along with it, grinding much of what they ingest into the white sand that makes up many tropical beaches. Net reef growth is simply what remains after all of that erosion is subtracted from new growth, and over centuries to millennia, given the right conditions, construction wins by enough of a margin to build a structure that can stretch for miles and rise from deep water to the surface.

## Fringing Reefs, Barrier Reefs, Atolls

Charles Darwin, during the voyage of the Beagle, worked out a theory to explain three distinct reef shapes he observed around Pacific islands, and that theory has held up remarkably well. A young volcanic island newly risen from the seafloor first develops a fringing reef, growing directly off its shoreline with little or no lagoon between reef and land. As the island slowly subsides — volcanic islands cool, contract, and sink back toward the seafloor over geological time, while the crust beneath them also sags under their own weight — the reef, whose living surface must stay near sunlight, keeps growing upward and outward fast enough to stay near the surface even as the island beneath it sinks. This produces a barrier reef, standing offshore from the now-smaller island with a lagoon in between. Continue the process long enough, and the volcanic island disappears beneath the waves entirely, leaving only a ring of reef surrounding a lagoon where the island used to be: an atoll. Darwin proposed this sequence based on observation and geometry alone, decades before anyone could drill into an atoll and confirm that a sunken volcanic peak really does sit beneath the accumulated limestone, exactly as his theory required.

## A Rainforest in Nutrient-Poor Water

One of the enduring puzzles of reef ecology is how such extravagant biodiversity manages to thrive in tropical water that is, by most chemical measures, nearly as nutrient-poor as a desert. Cold, nutrient-rich water tends to be cloudy with plankton, exactly the condition that blocks the sunlight coral needs; the clear blue water reefs favor is clear largely because it is short on the nitrogen and phosphorus that would otherwise feed dense plankton blooms. Reefs get around this scarcity by recycling nutrients internally with remarkable efficiency — the coral-algae partnership itself is one form of this recycling, trading waste products back and forth rather than losing them to the water column, and the reef's structural complexity supports a dense community of other organisms, from sponges to fish to bacteria in the sediment, that capture and reuse nutrients before they can drift away.

The result is a system that behaves, functionally, like a rainforest floating in the chemical equivalent of a desert, built entirely on tight internal cycling rather than external abundance. Break that cycling — by smothering the reef in sediment, by warming the water enough to force the coral to expel its algae in the stress response known as bleaching, or by changing the seawater chemistry enough to make aragonite harder to deposit — and the whole structure stops accumulating and starts eroding, at a pace that can undo centuries of slow construction far faster than it was ever built, one polyp's calcium carbonate cup at a time.
