# Touring the Abyssal Plain and the Ocean Trenches

Descend far enough beneath any ocean's surface and the water eventually gives up all pretense of being a sunlit place. Past a few hundred meters, even in the clearest tropical water, the last usable trace of sunlight fades into permanent dark. Keep going, past the mid-water zone where much of the ocean's biomass hangs suspended in a thin fog of small drifting animals, past scattered seamounts and ridges, and the seafloor eventually flattens into some of the most featureless terrain found anywhere on the planet: the abyssal plain, a blanket of soft sediment stretching for enormous distances with barely a meter of vertical relief to interrupt it.

## The Flattest Place on Earth

It was not always flat. Beneath that sediment blanket lies the same rugged volcanic crust found everywhere else on the ocean floor, formed at a mid-ocean ridge and covered in the same jagged basalt outcrops, fault scarps, and small hills visible on younger seafloor closer to the ridge that produced it. What smooths all of that roughness away is simply time and patient accumulation. As oceanic crust ages and spreads away from the ridge where it formed, it slowly collects a cover of fine sediment raining down from the water column above, and given tens of millions of years, that sediment cover can grow thick enough to bury all but the tallest volcanic peaks completely, draping a smooth, gently sloping cover over terrain that would otherwise look just as jagged as any young seafloor near a ridge.

## What the Plain Is Made Of

The sediment itself comes from a few distinct sources, and which one dominates in a given spot depends mostly on depth, distance from land, and how productive the water is far overhead. Close to continents, fine mineral particles carried out by rivers and wind settle out even at great distances from shore, producing a fine reddish-brown or gray clay. Farther from land, in areas where surface waters support dense plankton growth, the sediment can instead be dominated by the microscopic calcium carbonate or silica shells of dead plankton, drifting down after the organisms that built them die, accumulating into a sediment type oceanographers call ooze — calcareous ooze where carbonate shells dominate, siliceous ooze where they are silica-based. Below a certain depth, calcium carbonate shells dissolve before they can accumulate at all, since cold, high-pressure deep water is chemically corrosive to carbonate; below that boundary, known as the carbonate compensation depth, only clay and silica-based ooze survive to build the sediment record, giving the deepest abyssal plains a chemistry distinct from shallower ones even when the overlying water is equally productive.

## Life on a Deep Sediment Desert

Nothing about total darkness, near-freezing temperatures, and crushing pressure sounds hospitable, and by the standards of a coral reef or a kelp forest, the abyssal plain is indeed sparsely populated. But it is not empty. Every organism there depends, directly or indirectly, on marine snow — the slow, continuous drift of dead plankton, fecal pellets, molted shells, and other organic debris falling from the productive surface layer far above, most of it consumed or recycled long before it reaches bottom, leaving only a thin trickle of food for whatever lives on the plain itself. Sea cucumbers plow along the sediment surface in surprising numbers, ingesting mud and digesting whatever organic material it contains. Brittle stars, small crustaceans, and worms burrow through the top layer of sediment, and scavengers like giant amphipods can converge with startling speed on any larger food fall, such as a dead fish or whale carcass, that happens to reach the bottom intact. Bioluminescence is common among the animals that swim above the plain rather than living directly on it, used for camouflage, communication, or luring prey in a place where producing your own light is often the only light available at all.

## Nodules on the Sediment Surface

Scattered across large stretches of certain abyssal plains, half-buried in the surface sediment, lie potato-sized lumps called manganese nodules, built up in extraordinarily slow layers as dissolved metals precipitate out of seawater or pore water onto some small starting object, a shark's tooth or a fragment of shell, over the course of millions of years. Despite the name, these nodules typically contain a mix of several metals besides manganese, including nickel, copper, and cobalt, concentrated by this slow precipitation process into a form considerably richer than the ore typically mined on land for the same metals. Because they simply sit loose on the sediment surface rather than being embedded in solid rock, nodules are, at least in principle, far easier to collect than a conventional ore deposit, requiring no drilling or blasting, only some means of gathering them up from the seafloor and lifting them thousands of meters to a ship above.

That apparent accessibility has made nodule-rich stretches of abyssal plain, particularly a well-known zone in the eastern Pacific, the focus of growing commercial and regulatory attention, as demand for the metals used in batteries and electronics has climbed. Marine biologists studying the same seafloor have raised concerns that the animal communities living on and immediately around the nodules, many of them found nowhere else, are poorly understood and could be difficult or impossible to restore once disturbed by any large-scale collection effort, since sediment plumes stirred up by mining could smother filter-feeding organisms across an area far larger than whatever patch is directly harvested.

## Where the Seafloor Bends Downward

Follow an abyssal plain far enough and, in certain parts of the ocean, the smooth sediment cover ends abruptly at the edge of a trench: a long, narrow gash in the seafloor plunging thousands of meters deeper than the surrounding plain, marking a subduction zone where one tectonic plate is bending downward and sliding beneath its neighbor. Trenches trace the western and northern margins of the Pacific Ocean especially closely, tracking the boundaries where dense oceanic plates dive beneath either continental crust or younger oceanic crust, generating the earthquakes and volcanic arcs found along those same margins on land. The deepest point in any ocean sits inside one of these trenches, in the western Pacific, at a depth that puts it farther below sea level than the height of the tallest mountain on any continent rises above it.

## Pressure at the Bottom of the World

Life inside a trench has to contend with pressure far beyond anything found on the surrounding abyssal plain, since pressure increases steadily with every additional meter of water overhead. At the greatest depths, the pressure is enough to crush an unprotected air-filled structure instantly, yet fish and invertebrates persist even there, in a zone specialists call the hadal zone, after Hades. Snailfish recovered from the deepest trenches turn out to rely on small organic molecules called piezolytes, which accumulate in their tissue and appear to stabilize proteins and cell membranes against the distorting effect of extreme pressure, allowing normal biochemistry to continue at depths where it would otherwise fail. Amphipods, tiny shrimp-like scavengers, thrive in the same trenches in large numbers, drawn by the same scarcity of food that shapes life on the plain above, but concentrated instead into the narrow, isolated trench environment, where each trench functions almost like its own island, separated from neighboring trenches by thousands of kilometers of intervening plain and ridge that its hadal specialists are poorly equipped to cross.

Sediment still drifts down into a trench the same way it settles onto the plain above, but the trench's steep walls funnel loose material toward the axis at the bottom, so trench floors often accumulate sediment faster than the flat plain nearby, along with debris shaken loose by the frequent earthquakes that these same subduction zones generate. A trench is, in that sense, both the deepest scar on the planet's surface and one of the quieter places where the sediment record of the ocean above continues to be written, undisturbed by anything faster than the occasional landslide down its own walls.
