# The Physics of Underwater Sound

A submarine crew running silent does not turn off its lights to hide. It slows its engines, secures loose equipment, and above all, stops making noise, because in the ocean, sound is the sense that matters. Light, which dominates how humans navigate and perceive the world in air, barely functions underwater beyond the first few hundred meters even in the clearest water, scattered and absorbed by the water itself long before it can travel any serious distance. Sound behaves completely differently, and understanding why explains everything from how whales find each other across entire ocean basins to how a fishing boat's sonar finds a school of fish.

## Why Sound Wins Where Light Fails

Light is electromagnetic radiation, and water molecules are simply very good at absorbing and scattering it, especially the longer, redder wavelengths, which is why deep water takes on a bluish cast: red light has already been filtered out by the time light has traveled any real distance. Even in the clearest open ocean water, usable sunlight is essentially gone within a few hundred meters, and in coastal or sediment-laden water, often within just a few meters.

Sound is a mechanical pressure wave, not an electromagnetic one, and it depends on the medium's density and stiffness rather than its transparency. Water is far denser than air and, despite feeling soft to the touch, strongly resists being compressed, and that combination of properties happens to transmit pressure waves extremely efficiently. Sound travels through water at a speed several times faster than it moves through air, and with far less energy lost to absorption per unit of distance traveled, which is why a sound loud enough at the source can, under the right conditions, be detected an ocean away, a feat no beam of light could ever manage underwater.

## What Makes Sound Speed Up or Slow Down

The speed of sound in seawater is not a single fixed number; it changes with temperature, pressure, and salinity, and all three vary constantly throughout the ocean. Warmer water transmits sound faster than cold water, higher pressure at greater depth also speeds sound up, and saltier water transmits it slightly faster than fresher water, with temperature generally exerting the strongest influence of the three across most of the ocean.

Layer these three effects on top of the way temperature and pressure actually vary with depth in a typical ocean profile, and something interesting happens. Near the surface, in a layer mixed by wind and waves, temperature stays fairly uniform, and sound speed there tends to track surface conditions, varying considerably with the weather and season. Below that mixed layer, temperature drops fairly quickly with depth through a zone called the thermocline, and since colder water transmits sound more slowly, sound speed decreases through this layer even as pressure is slowly climbing. Below the thermocline, temperature levels off and changes very little with further depth, but pressure keeps climbing steadily, and since higher pressure speeds sound back up, sound speed starts increasing again with depth even in this cold, stable deep water. Somewhere in between, sound speed hits a minimum.

## A Channel That Carries a Whisper Across an Ocean

That minimum matters enormously, because sound bends toward regions where it travels more slowly, refracting continuously in response to the gradient in speed the same way light bends passing between media of different density. A sound wave that finds itself near that speed minimum keeps getting bent back toward it from both above and below, trapped in a horizontal channel that carries it forward with remarkably little loss, since it never spreads upward or downward and rarely interacts with the losses that come from bouncing off the sea surface or the seafloor. This layer, known as the deep sound channel or the SOFAR channel, an acronym for sound fixing and ranging, can carry a sound for enormous distances, in some documented cases clear across an ocean basin, and it was originally investigated and put to practical use for exactly the purpose the acronym suggests: locating a downed aircrew's emergency charge dropped into the channel, whose precisely timed detonation could be picked up by listening stations positioned an ocean apart and triangulated back to a location.

Long before anyone built a listening station for that purpose, whales appear to have been exploiting the same channel on their own terms. Fin whales and blue whales produce extremely low-frequency calls that couple efficiently into the deep sound channel, and there is good reason to believe some of these calls can carry across enormous stretches of open ocean, offering a plausible way for the largest animals on the planet, often sparsely distributed across vast, food-poor stretches of open water, to stay in acoustic contact with each other across distances that would make any other form of communication useless.

## Listening and Pinging

Human use of underwater sound splits broadly into two approaches, distinguished by whether the user makes noise or just listens. Passive sonar involves nothing but sensitive underwater microphones, called hydrophones, picking up whatever sound is already present in the water, whether from marine life, distant ships, or geological activity, and using the direction and character of that sound to identify and locate its source without ever revealing the listener's own position. Active sonar instead sends out a deliberate pulse of sound and listens for its echo, calculating range from the travel time exactly as an echo sounder measures ocean depth, and extracting additional information like bearing and, through the pitch shift of the returning echo, even the target's speed. Active sonar reveals the operator's position to anyone else listening, which makes it a tool submariners use cautiously, but it works reliably in situations where passive listening alone would miss a silent, non-moving object entirely, like an uncharted rock or a submarine deliberately holding still and quiet.

## A Noisier Sea

Marine mammals developed their own highly refined version of active sonar long before humans built the first one. Toothed whales, including dolphins and sperm whales, generate rapid clicks and interpret the returning echoes with enough precision to hunt fish in murky water or complete darkness, distinguishing prey from clutter and from each other with a resolution that rivals or exceeds artificial sonar systems built for comparable purposes. That biological sonar depends on a naturally quiet ocean to work well, which is part of why the steady rise in background noise from shipping traffic, seismic survey airguns used in oil and gas exploration, and naval sonar exercises has drawn increasing concern among biologists. A sound field cluttered with mechanical noise can mask the calls and echoes marine mammals depend on for navigation, feeding, and finding each other, in the same way a shouted conversation becomes impossible to follow in a sufficiently loud room, except that for an animal relying on sound as its primary sense in a lightless world, the stakes of losing that conversation are considerably higher.

None of this machinery, biological or artificial, is unique to the ocean in principle — bats use a version of the same echo-ranging trick in air. What makes the underwater case distinctive is simply how well suited the medium is to carrying the signal in the first place, over distances and through darkness that would defeat any sense built around light.
