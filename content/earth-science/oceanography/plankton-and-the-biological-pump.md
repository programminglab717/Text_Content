# Plankton and the Biological Pump

Fill a glass with seawater from almost anywhere in the sunlit ocean and it looks, to the naked eye, essentially empty. Put a drop of that same water under a microscope and the emptiness dissolves into a crowd: angular glass-walled diatoms, spinning dinoflagellates, chains of cyanobacteria, and darting, jointed copepods, all going about the business of eating, photosynthesizing, and reproducing in a world sized in fractions of a millimeter. Collectively, these organisms are plankton, a word that simply means "drifter," referring not to any particular kind of organism but to anything too small or weak-swimming to make meaningful headway against ocean currents. Small as they are individually, plankton run much of the chemistry of the planet.

## An Invisible Forest

The photosynthetic half of this community, phytoplankton, includes diatoms encased in intricately patterned silica shells, dinoflagellates that in some species can produce their own light, and cyanobacteria so small that a single one is barely resolvable under an ordinary light microscope. Despite living in a layer of ocean usually no deeper than the first hundred meters or so, wherever sunlight remains strong enough to drive photosynthesis, phytoplankton collectively carry out a share of the planet's total photosynthesis that rivals all land plants combined, despite amounting to only a sliver of the planet's total living plant biomass at any given moment. They manage this through sheer turnover: a forest's leaves might last a year or more, but a phytoplankton cell often lives only days before dividing, dying, or being eaten, and that rapid cycling lets a comparatively small standing population process an enormous amount of carbon and sunlight over the course of a year.

Like land plants, phytoplankton need more than sunlight and carbon dioxide to grow; they need dissolved nutrients, especially nitrogen and phosphorus, and in large stretches of the open ocean, iron as well. Vast areas of the Southern Ocean and the equatorial and subarctic Pacific carry enough nitrogen and phosphorus to support much denser phytoplankton blooms than are actually observed, a puzzle resolved once oceanographers recognized these regions were instead limited by a scarcity of dissolved iron, a trace nutrient that arrives in the open ocean mostly via windblown dust from distant deserts, meaning the fertility of some of the largest stretches of ocean on the planet depends, indirectly, on how much dust a faraway desert happens to be shedding into the wind.

## Grazers and Their Nightly Commute

Phytoplankton feed a second tier of drifters, zooplankton, which range from single-celled protists to copepods, krill, and the drifting larval stages of fish and crabs that will eventually grow large enough to swim under their own power. Many of these grazers participate in what is likely the largest synchronized movement of biomass on the planet, repeated every single day: diel vertical migration, in which enormous populations of small animals rise from deeper, darker water toward the surface each night to feed under cover of darkness, then sink back down before dawn to avoid the visual predators that hunt more effectively in daylight. Depending on the species and the clarity of the water, this daily commute can cover several hundred meters each way, a considerable distance for an animal a few millimeters long, undertaken twice a day, every day, for an entire lifetime.

## From Sunlight to Sediment

The reason any of this matters beyond the food web itself comes down to where the carbon these organisms have processed eventually goes. When phytoplankton die, get eaten and excreted as fecal pellets, or clump together with other debris into larger aggregates, a portion of that organic material becomes heavy enough to sink out of the sunlit surface layer, drifting down through the water column as a continuous fall of particles collectively known as marine snow. Most of it never reaches the seafloor at all, consumed and respired back into dissolved carbon dioxide by bacteria and other organisms at progressively greater depths on the way down, but a portion survives the journey far enough to reach water that will not return to the surface for centuries, and a smaller fraction still reaches the seafloor to be buried in sediment more or less permanently. This entire chain of production, sinking, and partial burial is what oceanographers call the biological pump, since its net effect is to pump carbon out of the surface ocean and atmosphere and store it at depth.

The vertical migrators add a second, more active version of the same pump. An animal that feeds near the surface at night and then swims down to several hundred meters depth before dawn is carrying ingested carbon downward under its own power, and a share of that carbon gets released through respiration and waste at the depth where the animal spends its day, effectively injecting carbon directly into deeper water far faster than the passive sinking of dead cells and pellets alone would manage.

## The Pump That Cools the Planet

The cumulative effect of this pump, running continuously across the entire surface ocean, is to keep a meaningful share of the carbon dioxide that would otherwise remain in the atmosphere locked away in the deep ocean and seafloor sediment instead, a service the ocean has been performing since long before humans began adding extra carbon dioxide to the air. Without the biological pump, models of ocean chemistry suggest atmospheric carbon dioxide would settle at a considerably higher baseline concentration than it otherwise does, since the physical solubility of the gas in seawater alone, without any biology at work, would leave far more of it in the atmosphere. The pump is not a modern invention responding to modern problems; it is an ancient piece of planetary plumbing that happens to be doing humanity an enormous, largely unnoticed favor.

## When the Bloom Turns Harmful

Not every burst of phytoplankton growth is the quiet, beneficial engine described so far. Certain species, many of them dinoflagellates, can multiply explosively under the right combination of warm water, calm conditions, and nutrient enrichment, producing blooms dense enough to visibly discolor the water, often reddish or brownish, in what is commonly called a red tide. Some of these bloom-forming species produce potent toxins as a matter of ordinary metabolism, harmless to the organism itself but capable of accumulating up the food chain through filter-feeding shellfish that concentrate the toxin in their tissue far beyond the level present in the surrounding water. Shellfish harvesting closures during bloom events exist specifically to break that chain before the toxin reaches a human diner, since cooking does not reliably neutralize the compounds involved.

Other blooms cause harm through a more indirect mechanism, unrelated to toxicity: sheer biomass. A sufficiently large bloom eventually dies off all at once, and the bacteria that decompose the resulting mass of dead cells consume dissolved oxygen from the surrounding water in the process, sometimes fast enough to leave the water functionally uninhabitable for fish and other oxygen-dependent life across an entire bay or coastal shelf for as long as the decomposition continues. Coastal nutrient pollution, particularly nitrogen and phosphorus washing off fertilized farmland and through wastewater systems, has made these low-oxygen dead zones a recurring seasonal event in a number of the world's river-fed coastal waters, a direct consequence of feeding the same phytoplankton growth that, in moderation, quietly supports the rest of the marine food web.

## Written in Chalk

The slower, more permanent half of this process, the fraction of sinking material that reaches the seafloor and stays there, has been running long enough to leave a visible geological signature. Coccolithophores, single-celled phytoplankton that build elaborate shields of calcium carbonate plates around themselves, have been sinking to the seafloor in enormous numbers for tens of millions of years, and thick deposits of their accumulated shells, compressed and lithified over geological time, form the chalk that makes up formations like the white cliffs found along parts of the English Channel coastline. Every one of those cliffs is, in a very literal sense, a record of an uncountable number of organisms too small to see individually, each one having spent its short life turning sunlight and dissolved carbon into a sediment that would eventually become a landmark.
