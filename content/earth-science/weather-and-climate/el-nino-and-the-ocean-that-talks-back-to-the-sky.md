# El Niño and the Ocean That Talks Back to the Sky

Fishermen working the coast of Peru have known for a very long time that some years are simply different. Sometime around the turn of the calendar year, the cold, nutrient-rich water that normally hugs the South American coastline and feeds one of the world's great fisheries would occasionally give way to a warmer current, the anchovies would vanish, and the fishing would collapse for the season. Local sailors named the warm intruder El Niño, a reference to the Christ child, because of its tendency to arrive around Christmas. It would be a very long time before anyone realized that this local disruption off one stretch of coastline was the visible edge of a pattern that reorganizes weather across a large part of the planet.

## The Ocean and Atmosphere, Working as One System

Under ordinary conditions, the tropical Pacific runs on a fairly consistent arrangement. Trade winds blow from east to west across the ocean's surface, piling up warm water in the western Pacific near Indonesia and the Philippines until sea level there sits measurably higher than it does off South America. That same wind-driven piling exposes cooler water underneath along the eastern Pacific, where it wells up to the surface off Peru and Ecuador, the very water the anchovy fishery depends on. Warm water in the west evaporates readily and feeds a persistent zone of rising air, thunderstorms, and rainfall over the western Pacific and the Indonesian archipelago, while the eastern Pacific, sitting under cooler water, stays comparatively dry. Air completes the loop aloft, flowing east back across the Pacific before sinking again over the cooler eastern waters. This entire loop, named the Walker circulation after the scientist who first documented the pressure patterns behind it, is the normal, background state of the tropical Pacific.

## When the Ocean and Atmosphere Amplify Each Other

El Niño begins with a weakening of the trade winds, for reasons that themselves can be difficult to pin to a single cause. Once those winds relax, though, something interesting happens: the warm water piled up in the western Pacific, no longer held there by a steady push, begins sloshing back east across the ocean basin. As that warm water spreads eastward, it removes the temperature contrast that helped drive the trade winds in the first place, so the winds weaken further, allowing yet more warm water to slide east. This feedback loop, in which a change in the ocean weakens the very winds that maintain the ocean's normal state, and vice versa, is named after the meteorologist Jacob Bjerknes, who worked out in the mid-twentieth century that the ocean and atmosphere in the tropical Pacific were not separate systems responding to each other from a distance but a single coupled system, each side driving and being driven by the other.

Once this feedback gets going, the entire architecture of the tropical Pacific shifts. The zone of rising air and heavy rainfall that normally sits over Indonesia migrates east, sometimes by thousands of kilometers, following the warm water. Places that depend on that rising air for their rainy season, across the western Pacific and parts of Southeast Asia and Australia, can see drought instead, while the central and eastern Pacific, normally dry, gets drenched.

## La Niña, the Mirror Image

The system does not simply return to normal once an El Niño event fades. Often it overshoots in the opposite direction, into a state called La Niña, in which the trade winds strengthen beyond their usual intensity, pushing even more warm water than usual into the western Pacific and pulling cold water even further up along the South American coast. The rainfall pattern flips accordingly: the western Pacific gets wetter than normal, while parts of the Americas that see extra rain during El Niño can turn dry during La Niña. Neither state is more natural than the other; the tropical Pacific spends a meaningful share of its time in one or the other, with a more neutral, in-between state in the years between.

## Ripples Around the World

What makes this Pacific phenomenon worth understanding well beyond the fishing communities of Peru is how far its influence reaches. Shifting the location of the tropical Pacific's rising air changes the pattern of upper-level winds across the entire globe, including the position and strength of the jet stream over North America and elsewhere. During a strong El Niño, the southern United States often sees a wetter, stormier winter than usual, while parts of the Pacific Northwest and Canada tend toward milder, drier conditions, a pattern that follows fairly reliably from where the jet stream gets nudged by the reorganized tropical heating. Monsoon rainfall over India and rainfall patterns over eastern Africa, southern Africa, and parts of South America all show statistical links to the state of the tropical Pacific as well, though the strength of these connections, called teleconnections, varies from one event to another and is rarely as tidy as a simple on-off switch.

This is, in the end, what makes El Niño and La Niña such a compelling case study in how the climate system works: not a single cause producing a single effect, but two enormous systems, one liquid and one gaseous, locked in a feedback loop that neither one could sustain alone, with consequences that reach a very long way from the stretch of ocean where sailors first gave the pattern its name.

## Why It Is So Hard to Predict Exactly

Even with decades of ocean buoys, satellite measurements of sea surface height, and increasingly sophisticated coupled ocean-atmosphere models, forecasting the exact strength and timing of an El Niño event more than half a year in advance remains genuinely difficult. Part of the trouble is a seasonal blind spot: forecasts made in early spring, when the tropical Pacific tends to be in a more ambiguous state, are notoriously less reliable than forecasts made later in the year, a limitation forecasters have learned to warn about explicitly rather than paper over. Part of the trouble is also the sheer size of the system, since a small nudge in wind patterns thousands of kilometers from the eventual center of the action can tip the ocean toward one outcome or another. Forecasters have gotten considerably better at flagging that an event is likely coming and roughly how strong it may become, but pinning down exactly how a given year will unfold still involves real uncertainty, which is why outlooks are typically framed in terms of probabilities rather than firm predictions.
