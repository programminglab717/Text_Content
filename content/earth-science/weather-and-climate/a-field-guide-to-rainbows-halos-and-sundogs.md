# A Field Guide to Rainbows, Halos, and Sundogs

Stand with your back to the sun after a passing rain shower, shadow stretched out in front of you, and look toward the opposite horizon. If there is still rain falling somewhere out there, and the sun has broken through behind you, there is a good chance you will find an arc of color hanging in the air exactly opposite the sun, centered on the point directly across from it, a point sometimes called the antisolar point, that sits wherever the shadow of your own head falls. This geometric fact, that a rainbow is always centered on the point directly opposite the sun from the observer, explains why you can walk toward a rainbow forever without reaching it: it is not a fixed object sitting at some location, but a pattern defined entirely by the angle between the sun, the raindrops, and your own eyes, and it moves with you exactly as fast as you move toward it.

## The Bow That Follows You

Each raindrop involved in producing a rainbow acts as a tiny optical device, bending sunlight as it enters the curved surface of the drop, reflecting once off the inside of the drop's far wall, and bending again as the light exits back toward the observer. This sequence of refraction, reflection, and refraction again sends light back out at a fairly specific angle relative to the original direction of the sunlight, close to forty degrees for red light, with other colors emerging at very slightly different angles. Because countless raindrops throughout a rain shower are all doing this simultaneously, and because only the drops positioned at just the right angle relative to any given observer will actually send their bent light to that observer's eyes, the visible result is a full arc rather than a scattering of individual points, made up of a different set of drops at every point along its curve, none of which stay in the same physical location for more than an instant as new drops constantly fall through the geometrically correct position while old ones fall out of it.

## Why the Colors Separate

White sunlight is a mixture of every visible wavelength traveling together, and water, like glass and every other transparent medium, bends different wavelengths by very slightly different amounts, a phenomenon called dispersion. Blue and violet light bend a bit more than red light does when passing through a given raindrop, which sends each color out of the drop at a very slightly different angle from the others. Because the total arc formed depends on this angle, and each color needs its own slightly different geometry to reach a given observer's eye, red appears on the outer edge of the bow, where the necessary angle is very slightly larger, and blue and violet appear toward the inner edge, with the familiar spectrum of colors in between arranged according to exactly how much each wavelength bends.

## The Fainter Double and the Dark Band Between

Look carefully above a strong primary rainbow and you can sometimes find a second, fainter arc, larger in radius and with its colors reversed, red on the inside and blue on the outside. This secondary bow forms from light that reflects twice inside each raindrop rather than once before exiting, a less efficient path that loses more light at each internal reflection and so produces a noticeably dimmer arc. Between the two bows, the sky is often visibly darker than either the sky above the secondary bow or below the primary one, a region with its own name, Alexander's dark band, after the ancient writer who first described it. The darkness is not an illusion or a trick of contrast; raindrops positioned in that particular band of sky are not sending either the once-reflected or twice-reflected light back to the observer's eye at all, so genuinely less light reaches the eye from that specific strip of sky than from the regions immediately above or below it.

## Halos, Made of Ice, Not Water

A ring of light around the sun or moon, usually fainter and much closer to white than a rainbow, works on an entirely different principle, since it forms not in raindrops but in ice crystals drifting in thin, high cirrus clouds. These crystals tend to be small hexagonal plates or columns, shaped by the same six-fold ice geometry that governs snowflake growth, and light passing through two of a crystal's flat faces set at a specific angle to each other bends by a fairly consistent amount, producing a ring at a radius of about twenty-two degrees around the sun or moon, noticeably wider than it might look to a casual glance. Because so many randomly oriented ice crystals throughout a cirrus cloud are all doing this bending independently, and only crystals in just the right orientation relative to a given observer send their light to that observer's eye, the visible result, as with a rainbow, is a ring rather than scattered points, made of countless different crystals at every moment along its circumference.

## Sundogs and Other Kin

Not every ice crystal in the sky is tumbling randomly. Larger, heavier plate crystals tend to fall with their flat hexagonal faces oriented horizontally, like tiny falling coins, and light bending through crystals aligned this way concentrates preferentially at points on either side of the sun, at the same height above the horizon as the sun itself, rather than spreading evenly into a full ring. These bright patches, called sundogs or, more formally, parhelia, are common enough that most people have seen them without necessarily having a name for what they were looking at, often showing a faint hint of color, reddish on the side nearest the sun, in the same way a rainbow separates color by wavelength. Other, rarer members of this same family include the circumzenithal arc, a bright band of color arcing across the sky well above the sun, and light pillars, vertical columns of light that appear to rise from the sun or moon, or from bright ground lights on a cold night, formed by reflection off the flat undersides of falling plate crystals rather than by refraction through them at all.

## Reading the Sky Ahead of a Storm

There is a genuine, if imperfect, bit of folk wisdom behind the old idea that a ring around the sun or moon means rain is coming. Cirrus clouds, the thin, high ice clouds responsible for halos, often arrive well ahead of an advancing warm front, riding at the leading edge of the broader storm system before its lower, thicker clouds and rain arrive on the scene hours or sometimes more than a day later. Seeing a halo, in other words, can be a genuine early warning that a frontal system is approaching, even though the halo itself brings nothing but a faint ring of light. Not every halo comes good on that promise, since cirrus can drift through on its own without any storm following behind it, but the underlying logic connecting the two is real enough that generations of sailors, farmers, and other people whose work depended on the weather took the ring around the moon seriously long before anyone could show them a satellite image of the front producing it.

## Coronas and Glories, the Smaller Cousins

Two more optical effects round out a fuller field guide, and both are easy to mistake for halos at first glance despite working through an entirely different physical process. A corona is a set of small, often faintly colored rings clustered tightly around the sun or moon, much closer in than a halo, produced by diffraction, the bending of light around the tiny water droplets in a thin cloud, rather than refraction through ice. The size of a corona's rings depends on the size of the droplets doing the diffracting, which is why coronas around the moon seen through a thin, uniform cloud can look strikingly crisp, while a cloud with a wider range of droplet sizes washes the rings out into a more diffuse glow. A glory is rarer still to witness on the ground, most familiar to airplane passengers as a small ring of colored light surrounding the shadow of the aircraft cast onto a cloud layer below, produced by light interacting with droplets in a way still not completely settled in all its detail even among specialists, though it is understood to involve light re-emerging from the side of a droplet nearest the observer rather than passing all the way through as it does in a rainbow. Both effects, like the halo and the sundog, reward exactly the kind of casual looking-up that most people reserve for the occasional rainbow, evidence that the sky is doing something interesting far more often than it gets credit for.
