# Trade Winds, Westerlies, and the Coriolis Effect

Sailing ships crossing the Atlantic from Europe to the Americas learned centuries ago to sail south before sailing west, dropping down toward the latitude of the Canary Islands before turning to run before a wind that blew, with a reliability that must have seemed almost providential, steadily from the northeast. Ships returning home did the opposite, riding a different, equally dependable band of wind further north that blew from the west. Sailors named the first of these bands the trade winds, for the commerce they carried, and named a windless, storm-prone band near the equator between them the doldrums, a word that has long since escaped sailing vocabulary to describe any stretch of listless inactivity. None of these names described an accident. They described a permanent structure in the atmosphere, one that girdles the entire planet in a set of belts running roughly parallel to the equator, and that structure exists because of how the atmosphere responds to uneven heating on a rotating sphere.

## A World Girdled by Bands of Wind

Broadly, each hemisphere's surface winds fall into three belts. Nearest the equator, within roughly the tropics, the trade winds blow toward the equator and, notably, westward, out of the northeast in the Northern Hemisphere and out of the southeast in the Southern Hemisphere. In the middle latitudes, roughly where most of North America, Europe, and much of the Southern Hemisphere's populated land sits, the prevailing winds reverse, blowing from the west, and are known simply as the westerlies. Closer to the poles, a third belt, the polar easterlies, blows from the east once again, though generally weaker and less consistent than the tropical trades. Sailors gave a name to the belts between these systems as well: the doldrums mark the equatorial zone where the trade winds of each hemisphere converge and largely cancel out any consistent surface wind at all, while the horse latitudes, according to a persistent bit of maritime lore, earned their name from ships becalmed in the subtropics for so long that crews were forced to throw livestock overboard to conserve dwindling water, a grim story whose precise accuracy is hard to verify but whose underlying point, that the subtropics are notoriously calm, is well founded in the actual meteorology of the region.

## The Hadley Cell and Why the Tropics Rain So Much

The trade winds exist because of a large overturning loop of air called the Hadley cell, one of three such loops that divide each hemisphere's circulation into distinct cells stacked side by side from equator to pole. Intense solar heating near the equator warms the surface and the air above it more than anywhere else on the planet, and that warm air rises in a broad, persistent band, cooling as it goes and releasing the moisture it carried as heavy tropical rainfall, which is part of why a band of the wettest climate on Earth runs along or near the equator across multiple continents. Having risen and shed much of its moisture, that air spreads poleward at high altitude in each hemisphere before sinking back down around thirty degrees latitude, where it arrives dry and warms further as it descends, contributing to the band of subtropical high pressure and desert climate found at that latitude across the globe. Some of that sinking air flows back toward the equator along the surface to complete the loop, and it is exactly this equatorward-flowing surface air that becomes the trade winds.

## Ferrel and Polar Cells

Beyond thirty degrees latitude, the tidy picture of a single overturning loop breaks down into something messier. The Ferrel cell, occupying the middle latitudes, runs in the opposite sense to the Hadley cell beside it, driven less by direct solar heating and more by the churning influence of traveling storm systems that transport heat poleward on its behalf, which is one reason the weather in the middle latitudes feels so much more variable, day to day, than the comparatively steady trade winds of the tropics. A third, weaker polar cell occupies the highest latitudes, sinking cold, dense air near the poles and returning it toward the middle latitudes along the surface as the polar easterlies. Each of these three cells meets its neighbors at a boundary, and it is no coincidence that the polar jet stream sits close to the boundary between the Ferrel and polar cells, since jet streams in general form where neighboring cells with sharply different temperatures meet.

## The Coriolis Effect: Why Moving Air Curves

None of these wind belts would blow in the tidy, roughly east-west directions they do without the Coriolis effect bending what would otherwise be simple north-south flow into something more slanted. The Coriolis effect is not a real force in the way gravity is, but an apparent one that shows up because the Earth is rotating beneath any object moving across its surface. Imagine standing at the center of a spinning merry-go-round and rolling a ball toward a friend sitting at the edge: from your point of view outside the ride, the ball travels in a straight line, but from your friend's point of view, riding along with the rotation, the ball appears to curve away from a straight path, because the ground beneath it has been rotating out from under it the entire time. Air moving across the rotating Earth experiences the same apparent deflection, curving to the right of its direction of travel in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere, an effect that is strongest near the poles, where the rotation beneath a moving parcel of air changes most quickly, and effectively zero at the equator itself, where the local ground is not rotating out from underneath a moving object in the same way.

## How the Two Combine into Wind Belts

Put the Hadley cell's north-south flow together with the Coriolis effect's sideways deflection and the trade winds fall out almost automatically. Surface air flowing from the subtropics back toward the equator to feed the rising branch of the Hadley cell gets deflected westward as it goes, in both hemispheres, which is exactly why the trade winds blow not simply from the north or south but from the northeast and southeast respectively. The same logic applied to the Ferrel cell, with its poleward-flowing surface air, deflects that flow eastward instead, producing the westerlies. Without the Coriolis effect, the atmosphere would still have Hadley-like overturning driven by uneven heating, but its surface winds would blow straight along lines of longitude rather than settling into the diagonal, belt-like bands that sailors learned to navigate by.

## Names Sailors Gave the Wind

It is worth remembering that all of this, the Hadley cell, the Coriolis effect, the precise three-cell structure of each hemisphere's circulation, was worked out well after sailors had already learned to exploit it. Vocabulary borrowed from the age of sail still marks the map of atmospheric science today, doldrums and trade winds and horse latitudes sitting comfortably alongside more technical modern terms, a reminder that people who never had access to a satellite image of global wind patterns nonetheless built a working, practical understanding of the atmosphere's largest and most permanent features simply by paying close attention to where the wind reliably blew, and from which direction, across a lifetime of voyages that depended on getting the answer right.

## Why the Belts Shift with the Seasons

The three-cell picture in each hemisphere is not frozen in place through the year, because the entire system tracks the sun's most direct point on the globe, which migrates between the tropics of Cancer and Capricorn over the course of a year. As that point of maximum heating shifts, the rising branch of the Hadley cell, and the belts on either side of it, shift with it, generally lagging the sun's position by a matter of weeks rather than tracking it exactly. This seasonal wandering is not a minor footnote: it is the same underlying mechanism responsible for the seasonal rains of the tropics arriving at different times in different places, since a location near the edge of the deep tropics might sit under the rain-bearing rising branch of the Hadley cell for part of the year and under the dry, descending air further from the equator for the rest of it, experiencing what amounts to a wet season and a dry season governed by nothing more exotic than this same belt of global circulation sliding a few degrees of latitude north and south with the changing seasons.
