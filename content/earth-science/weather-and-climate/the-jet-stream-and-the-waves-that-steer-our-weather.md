# The Jet Stream and the Waves That Steer Our Weather

Pilots flying from Tokyo toward Los Angeles sometimes gain the better part of an hour by climbing into a ribbon of wind that most of their passengers never notice out the window. That ribbon, moving at speeds that can exceed two hundred kilometers per hour and sometimes far more, sits near the boundary between the troposphere and the stratosphere, a few kilometers below where a typical airliner already cruises. It girdles the planet in a wavering, unbroken loop, and it does more than save fuel for eastbound flights. It is one of the primary reasons weather in the middle latitudes behaves the way it does, arriving in organized systems that move from west to east rather than as random local disturbances.

## A River With No Banks

The jet stream is not a fixed feature painted onto the atmosphere so much as a description of where the wind happens to be fastest at any given moment, a pattern that reforms constantly rather than a channel with permanent walls. There are, broadly, two of them in each hemisphere. The polar jet sits at the boundary between cold polar air and milder air from lower latitudes, typically found somewhere in the middle latitudes and shifting north or south with the seasons as that boundary shifts. The subtropical jet sits higher and closer to the tropics, driven by a different mechanism tied to the poleward branch of tropical circulation. Both are strongest in winter, when temperature contrasts across each hemisphere are sharpest, and both can split, merge, or nearly disappear depending on the broader pattern.

Altitude matters here in a specific way. Near the tropopause, the temperature contrast between warm and cold air masses sitting side by side reaches its peak effect on wind speed, because wind at any level in the atmosphere responds to how sharply temperature changes horizontally beneath it, an effect that compounds with height. Close to the ground, friction and a shallower temperature gradient keep winds comparatively modest. By the time you reach ten or eleven kilometers up, the accumulated effect of that temperature contrast, undiluted by friction, produces the fastest sustained winds found anywhere in the lower atmosphere.

## Why Temperature Makes Wind

The connection between temperature and wind speed aloft follows from a relationship meteorologists call thermal wind balance, though the name describes a shear rather than a wind in its own right. Imagine two columns of air side by side, one warm and one cold. Because warm air is less dense, the warm column's pressure decreases more slowly with height than the cold column's does. That means that at some altitude, the pressure difference between the two columns has effectively reversed itself relative to the surface, and since wind blows in response to pressure differences, redirected by the planet's rotation, the wind above that boundary must be stronger than the wind below it, blowing in the direction that keeps cold air on the left and warm air on the right, looking downstream in the Northern Hemisphere. Stack up enough of these layers between the surface and the tropopause, each adding its own increment of wind, and the cumulative result at jet stream altitude is a fast-moving core sitting directly above the surface boundary between air masses, which meteorologists still call a front on the weather map.

## The Wandering Path: Rossby Waves

A jet stream that simply raced around the planet in a straight line at constant latitude would make for a fairly dull, if windy, atmosphere. It does not do this, because the Coriolis effect changes with latitude, growing stronger toward the poles and vanishing at the equator, and that changing background rotation gives the atmosphere a preference for large, slow, wavelike disturbances named after the meteorologist Carl-Gustaf Rossby, who worked out their behavior mathematically. Air pushed poleward on the jet stream encounters stronger Coriolis deflection than it had before, which curves its path back toward the equator; pushed equatorward, it experiences weaker deflection and curves poleward again. The result is a jet stream that meanders through a series of troughs, dipping toward the equator, and ridges, bulging toward the pole, marching slowly around the hemisphere.

These waves are not decoration. The trough ahead of a ridge is where surface low-pressure systems tend to form and intensify, because the pattern of spreading and converging air aloft associated with the wave's curvature pulls air upward beneath it, lowering surface pressure. This is the mechanical link between the jet stream and the parade of storms that cross the middle latitudes: the jet stream's wave pattern is not just correlated with surface weather, it is a major part of what generates it.

## Blocking Patterns and Stalled Weather

Rossby waves usually propagate, meaning their troughs and ridges drift, generally eastward, over a period of days, which is why weather in the middle latitudes tends to change on a roughly weekly rhythm as one system after another passes through. Sometimes, though, a ridge grows so large and amplified that it effectively stops moving, sitting over the same region for a week or more while the rest of the wave pattern is forced to flow around it. Meteorologists call this blocking, and it is responsible for some of the most stubborn weather extremes observed anywhere: a heat wave that will not leave because the ridge trapping warm air in place refuses to budge, or a cold snap that persists because a trough has become similarly stuck on the other side of the block.

Blocking patterns are notoriously difficult to forecast more than a week or so in advance, and difficult to explain fully even after the fact, though they tend to involve an unusual amount of wave energy converging into a narrow range of latitudes and reinforcing itself there. Some research has tied an increase in blocking frequency to changes in the temperature contrast between the Arctic and the middle latitudes, though this remains an area of active investigation rather than settled science.

## Jet Streaks and Clear-Air Turbulence

The jet stream is not uniformly fast along its entire length. Embedded within the broader river of wind are jet streaks, localized pockets where the wind accelerates well beyond the speed of the surrounding flow before slowing again further downstream, in much the same way a river narrows and speeds up through a gorge before widening and slowing again. These streaks matter for reasons well beyond fuel savings for eastbound flights. Air entering and exiting a jet streak experiences regions of convergence and divergence aloft strong enough to influence surface pressure directly beneath, so forecasters watching for where a surface storm might explosively intensify pay close attention to a storm's position relative to the exit region of an approaching jet streak, one of the more reliable signals for unusually rapid strengthening.

Jet streaks also produce one of commercial aviation's more persistent nuisances: clear-air turbulence, the bumpiness that strikes an aircraft out of an apparently cloudless sky, with no visible storm to warn pilots in advance. The mechanism is shear, a difference in wind speed or direction over a short vertical or horizontal distance, and jet streaks are exactly where that shear tends to be sharpest, particularly on their poleward side and near their exit regions. Because there is no cloud or precipitation to show up on radar, this kind of turbulence has historically been difficult to forecast precisely, and pilots have long relied partly on reports radioed in by other aircraft that already passed through a rough patch, a practical, informal reporting network built long before more sophisticated turbulence-forecasting products became available to supplement it.

## Reading the Jet Stream as a Forecaster

Long before satellites and supercomputers, forecasters already understood, in a rough way, that upper-level winds steered surface storms, from tracking balloon-borne instruments and from theoretical work connecting upper-air patterns to surface pressure changes. Today, a map of the jet stream's position and strength is one of the first things a meteorologist looks at when trying to understand where storminess will show up over the coming week. A jet stream diving south brings its associated trough and the cold, unsettled weather that comes with it; a jet stream lifting north ahead of a ridge tends to bring a lull. Because the jet stream also marks the boundary along which temperature contrasts are sharpest, it is often the difference, on any given day, between a region seeing rain, snow, or nothing at all, which is one reason the same storm system can produce a rare snowstorm on one side of a track and unremarkable rain a short distance away on the other.
