# Fog and the Inversions That Trap It

Drive into certain valleys on a still autumn morning and the fog arrives with a definiteness that feels almost like a wall: clear road, then suddenly headlights bouncing back off a gray nothing that swallows everything past twenty meters. Climb out of the valley on the same morning, up a hillside road, and there is a real chance you will pop out of the top of the fog entirely, into bright sunshine, looking down at what appears from above to be a lake of solid white filling the low ground. Fog is not a special kind of weather so much as an ordinary cloud that happens to have formed at ground level instead of a few thousand meters up, but the reasons it forms exactly where and when it does, and the reasons it can behave the way it does when you drive through it, come down to some fairly specific atmospheric conditions.

## What Fog Actually Is

A cloud, at its simplest, is nothing more than air that has cooled, or picked up enough moisture, to reach saturation, at which point water vapor begins condensing onto tiny particles of dust, salt, or pollution that are suspended in essentially all air. Fog is exactly this process happening close enough to the surface that it reduces visibility for anyone standing in it, rather than floating overhead where it only affects visibility looking upward. Because the condensation process is identical whether it happens at ground level or ten kilometers up, the real question with fog is never what it is, but why saturation happened right there, right at the surface.

## Radiation Fog

The most common variety over land, particularly in autumn and winter, forms on clear, calm nights through a straightforward process of heat loss. During the day the ground absorbs sunlight and warms, but after sunset, with no incoming solar radiation to balance it, the ground radiates its heat away to space as infrared energy, and on a clear night with no cloud cover to trap that outgoing radiation, the ground can cool substantially before dawn. Air in direct contact with the cooling ground loses heat to it and cools as well, and if that air was already fairly humid, it can reach its dew point well before sunrise. Because cold air is denser than warm air, this chilled layer tends to pool in low-lying terrain, which is exactly why valleys and river bottoms are so much more fog-prone than nearby hillsides on the same night: cold air draining downhill under gravity settles in the lowest available ground, taking the fog with it. Wind works against this process, since even a light breeze mixes the cooling surface air with the slightly warmer air above it, which is why radiation fog forms almost exclusively on calm nights and burns off quickly if wind picks up.

## Advection Fog

A different kind of fog forms not because air is cooling in place overnight, but because a mass of relatively warm, moist air is physically moving across a colder surface, cooling from below as it goes. This is advection fog, and it is the kind famous along coastlines where a cold ocean current runs close to shore, such as the fog banks that roll into parts of the California coast through much of the summer or the notoriously persistent fog over the Grand Banks off Newfoundland, where warm air moving north meets a cold current. Unlike radiation fog, advection fog does not depend on a clear, calm night at all. It can arrive at any hour, in a stiff breeze, precisely because it is being carried in rather than forming in still, radiating air, and it can persist for as long as the wind keeps pushing warm, moist air over the cold surface.

## Upslope and Evaporation Fog

A few other, less common mechanisms fill out the picture. Upslope fog forms when moist air is pushed by wind up a gradually rising terrain surface, cooling adiabatically as it rises even without any of the usual triggers, condensing into fog that clings to the rising ground itself. Evaporation fog, sometimes called steam fog, works almost in reverse: it appears over a body of water that is warmer than the air above it, often on a cold autumn morning over a lake or river, when water evaporating from the relatively warm surface immediately meets much colder air and condenses again just above the surface, producing the wisps of steam-like fog often seen rising off ponds at dawn.

## The Inversion That Holds It in Place

Ordinarily, temperature in the lower atmosphere decreases with height, which allows air near the surface, if it warms or becomes humid enough to rise, to keep rising and mixing through a reasonably deep layer. A temperature inversion reverses this arrangement, at least within some layer: instead of cooling with height, the air actually warms for some distance above the surface before resuming its normal decrease higher up. Inversions form easily on the same calm, clear nights that produce radiation fog, for the same reason, since the ground cools faster than the air above it. Once an inversion is in place, it acts like a lid. Air near the surface, being cooler and denser than the air trapped just above the inversion, has no tendency to rise through it, which means fog, once it forms beneath an inversion, has nowhere to go. Add a stagnant high-pressure system on top of this, suppressing the winds that might otherwise stir things up, and fog can persist for days, a pattern well known in low-lying valleys that trap cold air each winter beneath a lid of warmer air aloft, and in broad agricultural basins where locally famous fogs can settle in for a week or more of gray.

## Why Fog Is So Disruptive to Travel

Fog causes a disproportionate amount of trouble for transportation given how little else it actually does to the atmosphere; it brings no wind worth mentioning and often no precipitation at all, yet it can shut down an airport more completely than a moderate snowstorm. The reason comes down to how abruptly visibility can collapse and how unevenly fog distributes itself across even a small area. A layer only a few dozen meters thick can reduce visibility at ground level to a small fraction of what it is a short distance above, which is exactly the layer where aircraft need to see the runway and where drivers need to see the road. Airports measure this with instruments that estimate how far a pilot could see down the runway under the prevailing conditions, and categories of allowable landing procedure are built directly around that measurement, with the lowest-visibility approaches requiring instruments sophisticated enough to land the aircraft with the runway barely visible, or not visible at all until the last moment.

Fog has also long been tangled up with air pollution in industrial cities, where smoke and soot particles once supplied an abundance of condensation nuclei right at the moment cool, calm autumn and winter nights were already primed to produce radiation fog. The result, historically, was a thick, yellowish, choking fog sometimes called smog, a portmanteau of smoke and fog, most famously associated with London before stricter controls on coal burning cleared the air enough to let ordinary, less harmful fog return to being merely an inconvenience rather than a genuine hazard to health.

## When Fog Clears

Fog usually loses to the sun eventually, though how quickly depends on which kind it is and how thick the layer has grown. Radiation fog typically burns off within a few hours of sunrise, as the sun's energy, even filtered through the fog itself, warms the ground enough to heat the air just above it and break the stability that let the fog form in the first place. Thicker fog, or fog trapped under a particularly strong inversion, can resist this for most of a day, since a fog layer reflects a fair amount of incoming sunlight straight back up before it ever reaches the ground to do its warming work. What usually finishes the job in these stubborn cases is not the sun at all, but a change in the larger weather pattern, a new air mass or a stronger wind arriving to break the inversion apart and mix the fog away.
