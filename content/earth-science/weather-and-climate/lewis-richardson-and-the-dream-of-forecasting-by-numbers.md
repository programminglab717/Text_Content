# Lewis Richardson and the Dream of Forecasting by Numbers

In 1922, a British mathematician and Quaker named Lewis Fry Richardson published the results of an extraordinary piece of arithmetic. Working largely by hand, using observational data from a single day some years earlier, he had attempted to calculate what the weather over Europe would do six hours later by solving the fundamental equations of motion and thermodynamics for the atmosphere directly, cell by cell across a grid, rather than relying on the pattern-matching and experience that had defined forecasting up to that point. The calculation took him weeks. The answer it produced was wildly wrong, predicting a change in surface pressure many times larger than anything ever observed in the real atmosphere over so short a span. And yet the attempt itself, failure and all, is now recognized as the true beginning of numerical weather prediction, the method underlying every modern forecast.

## A Forecast That Took Longer Than the Weather It Predicted

Richardson's project had an almost absurd elegance to it. He divided the atmosphere over part of Europe into a grid of boxes, gathered what observations existed for a single starting moment, and applied the physical equations governing pressure, temperature, wind, and moisture to calculate how each quantity in each box should change over the following hours. It was, in principle, exactly what a modern computer does thousands of times a day. In practice, done with pencil, paper, and enormous patience, it consumed a large part of Richardson's spare time over an extended stretch of years, work he carried out partly while serving with a Quaker ambulance unit during the First World War, doing the calculations between duties and, by his own later account, losing the only copy of an early draft for months before finding it again under a heap of coal.

The result, when it finally emerged, predicted a rise in surface pressure over the following six hours of a magnitude that had essentially never been observed anywhere on Earth over that short a period. Richardson published the failure anyway, along with the complete method behind it, in a book that laid out in careful detail exactly how one might, in principle, calculate the future state of the atmosphere from its present state. He even sketched, half seriously, a fantasy of a "forecast factory," a vast hall filled with thousands of human computers, each responsible for one small piece of the calculation, working in a coordinated rhythm fast enough to predict the weather before it happened. It would be more than two decades before anything like the machine he imagined actually existed, in the form of the electronic computer.

## Where the Numbers Came From

Richardson did not invent the idea that weather could be calculated rather than intuited. That case had been made a few years earlier by the Norwegian scientist Vilhelm Bjerknes, who argued that if the atmosphere's state at a given moment could be measured precisely enough, and if the physical laws governing its evolution were known precisely enough, then forecasting was, in principle, nothing more than a problem in applied mathematics. Bjerknes and his colleagues in Norway did enormously influential work on the structure of fronts and cyclones using this physical, rather than purely statistical, approach to the atmosphere. Richardson took the argument at its word and tried to actually carry out the calculation, which nobody had yet attempted at any real scale.

## Why the Math Blew Up

The reason Richardson's forecast failed so badly was not, as it turned out, a flaw in the underlying physics, but a problem in how the calculation handled the passage of time. The atmosphere contains fast-moving waves, of a kind that carry very little of the weather anyone cares about but that are nonetheless a genuine, if minor, feature of the real equations of motion. Richardson's initial data, drawn from real but imperfect observations, contained small imbalances that these fast waves seized on and amplified enormously over the course of the calculation, in much the same way a small, off-center push on a swing can, if timed wrong, produce a wildly exaggerated result. Meteorologists would not fully understand and correct for this problem, through a technique for smoothing the initial data before starting the calculation, until decades later. Richardson had stumbled into one of the genuinely hard problems in numerical computation without any way of knowing it at the time.

## The Machines Catch Up

Richardson's forecast factory arrived, in a much smaller and more practical form, in 1950, when a team including Jule Charney, Ragnar Fjørtoft, and John von Neumann used one of the first electronic computers, ENIAC, to calculate a forecast using a simplified version of the equations Richardson had worked with by hand. Charney's key insight was to filter out exactly the fast-moving waves that had ruined Richardson's earlier attempt, keeping only the slower, large-scale motion that actually corresponds to the weather patterns forecasters care about. That first computed forecast, run on a machine that filled a room and was slower than a modern pocket calculator, took roughly a day to compute a day's forecast, a pace barely useful in practice but proof that the underlying method worked. It was, in every sense that mattered, Richardson's vision made real.

## Ensembles and the Modern Forecast

Modern forecasting has grown so far beyond that first computer run that the comparison can feel almost unfair, though the lineage is direct and unbroken. Today's models divide the atmosphere into millions of grid points rather than dozens, run on machines that complete in minutes what once took entire computing centers hours, and incorporate observations from satellites, weather balloons, ships, and aircraft that Richardson could not have dreamed of having access to. But forecasters have also absorbed a lesson that Richardson's era did not yet appreciate: that the atmosphere is chaotic, in the technical sense that tiny uncertainties in the starting conditions can grow into large differences in the forecast given enough time, a discovery credited to the meteorologist Edward Lorenz working with early computer models. Rather than run one forecast from one best guess at the current state of the atmosphere, modern centers run dozens of forecasts, each starting from a slightly different, equally plausible version of today's conditions, and look at how much those forecasts agree or diverge.

Where the ensemble members agree closely, forecasters can speak with real confidence, often extending that confidence a week or more into the future for the broad shape of a pattern even if not for every local detail. Where they scatter widely, the honest answer is that the atmosphere's future, at that range, genuinely is uncertain, an answer built into the method itself rather than papered over. This willingness to express a forecast as a spread of possibilities rather than a single confident number is arguably as important an advance as the raw increase in computing power, and it is a piece of intellectual honesty that would not exist without a Quaker mathematician's failed six-hour forecast a century earlier.
