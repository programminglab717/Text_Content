# Deserts in the Shadow of Mountains

Drive east out of California's Central Valley and up into the Sierra Nevada, and the landscape does something dramatic within the space of a single day's travel. Pine forests climb the western slopes, fed by weather rolling in off the Pacific. Cross the crest, though, and descend the eastern side, and the pines give way within a remarkably short distance to sagebrush, then to the open, dry basins of the Great Basin, a landscape that receives only a fraction of the precipitation falling just a few dozen kilometers to the west. The mountains have not moved the Pacific Ocean any further away on their eastern side. What they have done is wring the moisture out of the air before it ever gets there, a process behind some of the driest places on every continent that has mountains anywhere near a source of moisture.

## Air That Climbs, Cools, and Wrings Itself Out

When prevailing winds carry moist air toward a mountain range, that air has nowhere to go but up and over. As it rises, it expands, because atmospheric pressure drops with altitude, and expanding air cools, at a fairly predictable rate for as long as it remains unsaturated. Eventually, if the mountain is tall enough and the air moist enough, that cooling brings the rising air to its dew point, water vapor begins condensing into cloud, and if the process continues long enough, the cloud produces rain or snow that falls on the mountain's windward slope. This is why windward slopes facing a reliable source of moist wind, the western slopes of coastal mountain ranges facing the Pacific, for instance, or the southern slopes of the Himalaya facing the moisture-laden monsoon, tend to be some of the wettest places in their respective regions, capable of supporting dense forest on comparatively little else besides the mountain's own geography.

## What's Left When It Comes Down the Other Side

The air that finally crosses the mountain crest has given up much of its original moisture as precipitation on the way up, and what happens next makes the situation worse for anything downwind. As that now-drier air descends the far side of the range, it is compressed by increasing atmospheric pressure and warms as a result, and this warming happens at a faster rate than the cooling did on the way up, because the descending air, no longer condensing anything, warms at the full dry rate rather than the slower rate that applied while cloud was forming. The combination of having lost much of its moisture and then warming quickly on the way down leaves the air on the leeward side both drier and warmer than it would otherwise be, a double effect that can turn a stretch of land only a modest distance from a wet, forested mountain slope into true desert. Meteorologists call the resulting dry zone a rain shadow, an apt name for a dryness genuinely cast by the mountain blocking the moisture from ever quite reaching that side.

## Real Places Carved by This Effect

This mechanism has left its signature across the map in some of the planet's most distinctive dry landscapes. The Great Basin of the western United States sits in the rain shadow of the Sierra Nevada and, further west, the Cascade Range, catching only what moisture survives two consecutive mountain crossings. Patagonia, in southern Argentina, is famously dry despite sitting at a latitude that in most other parts of the world receives ample rainfall, a dryness attributable in large part to the towering wall of the Andes just to its west intercepting nearly all the moisture arriving off the Pacific. The Atacama Desert, commonly cited as one of the driest places on the planet, owes part of its extreme aridity to the same Andean rain shadow, compounded by a cold offshore current that further suppresses rainfall along the coast. Even the dry interior of Asia, including much of the Gobi and the Tarim Basin, sits in a rain shadow cast collectively by the Himalaya and the Tibetan Plateau, blocking monsoon moisture that would otherwise have a chance of reaching much further inland.

## Not Every Desert Needs a Mountain

Rain shadows are not the only way deserts form, and some of the largest deserts on Earth owe comparatively little to any nearby mountain range. The Sahara, the largest hot desert on the planet, sits mostly beneath a broad band of sinking air associated with the descending branch of the Hadley circulation, the great overturning loop of tropical atmosphere that rises near the equator and sinks again around thirty degrees latitude in each hemisphere. Sinking air, whatever the reason it is sinking, warms and dries for essentially the same reason descending air does on the leeward side of a mountain, so this subtropical belt of sinking air produces desert conditions across a wide swath of latitude with no orographic assistance required at all, which is why a band of major deserts lines up so neatly along roughly the same latitude across multiple continents.

## Life on the Dry Side

Deserts shaped by rain shadows are rarely as lifeless as their reputation suggests, and the Atacama offers one of the more striking examples of life finding an unlikely workaround. Along stretches of its coast, a fog known locally as the camanchaca rolls in regularly off the cold Pacific current, and although it produces almost no rain in the conventional sense, it deposits enough moisture directly onto rocks, cacti, and specially adapted lichens to sustain small but genuine ecosystems in a place that otherwise gets essentially no rainfall in an ordinary year. Similar fog-dependent life shows up in other coastal deserts shaped by the same combination of a rain-shadowing landform and a nearby cold current, a reminder that the same descending, drying air responsible for making a place a desert in the first place can, at the coastal margin where it meets cold water, deliver just enough moisture through an entirely different channel to keep something alive.

## Reading a Rain Shadow on a Map

Once you know what to look for, rain shadows become almost easy to spot on an ordinary physical map, long before checking any climate data. Find a mountain range, note the direction the prevailing wind arrives from at that latitude, whether the steady westerlies of the middle latitudes or the trade winds of the tropics, and the dry side reveals itself as the side facing away from that wind. This is why the interior of a continent downwind of a major range so often carries a name suggesting dryness or emptiness long before any scientist arrives to measure rainfall there, a naming pattern repeated by different cultures on different continents who noticed the same basic fact about the land they lived on: mountains make rain on one side and take it away on the other, a rule so reliable that it holds on nearly every continent where a substantial mountain range interrupts a steady, moisture-bearing wind.
