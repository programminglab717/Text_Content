# How a Snowflake Takes Shape

Catch one on a dark glove before it melts and, if it has fallen slowly through calm, cold air, you can sometimes see the whole intricate structure before it collapses into a shapeless speck of ice: six arms, each one branching in a pattern so close to its neighbors that the whole thing looks less like an accident than a design. It is, in a sense, both. The six-fold symmetry is guaranteed by the physics of how water freezes. Everything else about a given snowflake's shape, the length of its arms, how heavily they branch, whether it grew into a flat plate or a slender column instead of a star at all, is a record of the exact path that one small crystal took through a cloud, and no two crystals ever take quite the same path.

## Ice Needs Something to Grab Onto

A snowflake does not begin as a raindrop that happens to freeze. It begins as water vapor, invisible gas, depositing directly into solid ice without ever passing through a liquid stage, a process that requires both a temperature well below freezing and a microscopic particle to serve as a nucleus for the ice to form around. Without such a nucleus, water vapor can remain unfrozen at temperatures many degrees below zero, a state called supercooling, but once a suitable nucleus is present, usually dust, and sometimes certain kinds of airborne bacteria that carry proteins particularly effective at organizing water molecules into ice, a crystal begins to grow, adding molecules from the surrounding vapor layer by layer.

## Why Six Sides

The six-fold symmetry traces back to the shape of the water molecule itself and the way it bonds to its neighbors once locked into solid form. In ice, each water molecule bonds to four others through hydrogen bonds arranged in a way that, extended through a whole crystal, naturally produces a hexagonal lattice, the same underlying geometry whether the final crystal turns out to be a hexagonal plate, a hexagonal column, or a branching star. Every visible shape a snow crystal can take is a variation on this single hexagonal theme, which is why six-sided symmetry shows up in nearly every ice crystal you might examine, even ones that look, at first glance, nothing alike.

## Temperature and Humidity Sculpt the Shape

What actually determines whether a given crystal grows into a flat plate, a slender needle, a stubby column, or one of the elaborate branching stars most people picture when they hear the word snowflake is a combination of temperature and how much moisture is available in the air relative to what the air can hold at that temperature. The Japanese physicist Ukichiro Nakaya spent years in the early twentieth century growing artificial snow crystals under carefully controlled conditions in a laboratory, and from that work built a now-famous diagram mapping crystal shape against temperature and humidity. Broadly, crystals grown near a few degrees below freezing tend toward flat plates and simple stars. Crystals grown somewhat colder tend toward hollow columns and needles. Colder still, and plates and stars reappear, now often more elaborate, with the most extravagantly branched dendrites forming in a narrow temperature band where humidity is also high. A single snowflake falling through a real cloud does not experience just one of these zones; it drifts through a sequence of them, and each shift in conditions adds a new stage of growth onto whatever shape the crystal had already built, which is part of why the most elaborate flakes show a kind of layered, almost architectural structure when examined closely.

## No Two Alike, Roughly Speaking

The familiar claim that no two snowflakes are exactly alike holds up about as well as any such broad claim can, for reasons that follow directly from how sensitive crystal growth is to its immediate surroundings. Two crystals separated by even a short distance in a turbulent cloud will experience slightly different temperature and humidity histories as they fall, and because each new layer of growth builds directly on the irregularities left by the previous layer, small early differences compound rather than average out. This makes elaborate, heavily branched crystals essentially guaranteed to be unique, since so much of their structure depends on a long, particular sequence of conditions. Simple crystals are a different matter. A basic hexagonal plate or a plain short column, having had comparatively little opportunity to develop distinguishing branches, can end up looking very much like plenty of others, which is really just to say that uniqueness in snow crystals is a function of complexity: the more elaborate the flake, the more confidently you can say it is one of a kind.

## From Crystal to Snowpack

A single hexagonal crystal is rarely what actually reaches the ground. As crystals fall, particularly through the relatively warm, moist air near cloud base, their delicate branches can interlock with other falling crystals, clumping together into the larger, fluffier clusters that people usually mean when they picture a snowflake drifting down, sometimes visibly large enough to track individually against a dark background. Crystals falling through a cloud containing abundant supercooled liquid water droplets take on a different fate entirely, as those droplets freeze onto the crystal's surface on contact, a process called riming that can eventually obscure the original hexagonal shape completely and build a dense, roughly round pellet known as graupel, an entirely different kind of ice than the airy, branching crystal it started as.

Once snow settles on the ground, its transformation is far from over. Under its own weight, and under repeated small cycles of partial melting and refreezing, or of vapor moving from warmer, deeper layers of the snowpack toward the colder surface, individual crystals lose their original branching shape and round off, or in some conditions grow into large, weakly bonded, faceted grains known to skiers and avalanche forecasters as depth hoar. This ongoing rearrangement, called snow metamorphism, determines how a snowpack holds together or fails, which is why the freshly fallen, perfectly formed crystal that landed on your glove is really only the first chapter of a much longer story playing out underfoot.

## Why Cold Places Do Not Always Get the Prettiest Snow

There is a common assumption that the coldest places on Earth must produce the most spectacular snowflakes, but the opposite is often closer to the truth. The most elaborate, widely branched dendrites need a fairly narrow combination of moderate cold and high humidity to grow, conditions found more reliably in a maritime climate with a steady supply of moisture than in the extreme, bone-dry cold of a continental interior in the depths of winter. Air that cold generally cannot hold much moisture at all, and snow that does fall under those conditions tends to consist of small, simple plates or columns rather than the intricate stars associated with snow in the popular imagination. The showiest snowflakes, in other words, tend to fall in places cold enough to freeze water but still humid enough to feed a crystal's more ambitious growth, which is one more way a snowflake's shape ends up telling you something real about the exact atmospheric conditions it grew up in.
