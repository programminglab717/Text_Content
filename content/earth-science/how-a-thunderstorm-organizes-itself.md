# How a Thunderstorm Organizes Itself

A summer thunderstorm looks like chaos from underneath. Wind arrives from a
direction it had not been coming from a minute earlier, the light goes green,
and rain falls in sheets that seem to have no interest in any particular
trajectory. But the storm overhead is not disorganized at all. It is a heat
engine, and a fairly disciplined one, converting the latent energy stored in
humid air into vertical motion, and then into rain, hail, and the electrical
discharge that gives the whole thing its name.

The fuel is water vapor. When the sun heats a patch of ground more efficiently
than its surroundings — a bare field next to a woodlot, a parking lot next to a
lake — the air sitting on that patch warms, expands, and becomes less dense than
the air around it. It rises. This is unremarkable in itself; thermals of this
kind lift hawks and gliders on any clear afternoon without producing weather.
What turns a thermal into a storm is what happens to the water riding along
inside it.

## The Trigger and the Cap

Rising air cools as it expands, at a rate of roughly ten degrees Celsius per
kilometer of ascent as long as it stays unsaturated. At some altitude it cools
to its dew point, and the vapor begins condensing onto the dust, salt, and
biological debris that serve as cloud condensation nuclei. That altitude is the
flat base you see on a cumulus cloud on a fair-weather day, and it is flat
because every parcel rising out of the same air mass reaches saturation at
approximately the same height.

Condensation is where the storm gets its power. Turning vapor into liquid
releases the energy that was absorbed to evaporate that water in the first
place, usually from an ocean surface hundreds of miles away and days earlier.
That released heat warms the parcel from the inside, which slows its cooling to
something closer to six degrees per kilometer. If the surrounding atmosphere
cools with height faster than the parcel now does, the parcel stays warmer than
its environment no matter how high it goes, and it keeps accelerating upward.
Meteorologists call this condition instability, and they quantify the fuel
available as convective available potential energy.

Instability alone does not guarantee a storm, which is why muggy afternoons so
often pass without incident. Very often a layer of warm, dry air sits a kilometer
or two up — subsided from aloft, or blown in off a hot plateau — and a rising
parcel bumps into it, finds itself suddenly cooler than its surroundings, and
stops. This is the cap. It is genuinely useful: it lets the boundary layer
below continue accumulating heat and moisture through the afternoon without
bleeding it off in a dozen small showers. When something finally breaks the cap
— a front sliding through, a sea breeze converging, air forced up a ridge, or
simply enough surface heating to overwhelm it — the accumulated energy goes up
all at once. Storms that form after a strong cap breaks tend to be the violent
ones.

## The Updraft and the Anvil

Once a parcel breaches the cap it can rise unimpeded for ten kilometers or more,
reaching speeds that in strong storms exceed fifty meters per second. Air moving
that fast vertically does strange things to water. Droplets are carried upward
past the freezing level and remain liquid well below zero, supercooled, until
they strike something and freeze instantly. Ice crystals collide with those
droplets and accrete layers of rime. Hailstones grow in this way, cycling through
the updraft, accumulating alternating clear and opaque ice shells, and they fall
out only when they get too heavy for the updraft to hold — which is why the size
of hail is a decent proxy for the strength of the updraft that made it.

Eventually the rising column reaches the tropopause, where the atmosphere stops
cooling with height and starts warming. The parcel is no longer buoyant. It
spreads horizontally into the flat, fibrous ice cloud that gives a mature
thunderstorm its anvil profile, usually drifting downwind of the storm. A
particularly strong updraft will punch a hundred or several hundred meters above
the tropopause before falling back, producing the overshooting top that
satellite forecasters watch for as a sign of severity.

## Where the Lightning Comes From

Charge separation inside a storm is still not understood in complete detail, but
the dominant mechanism is well supported. In the mixed-phase region, where
supercooled droplets, small ice crystals, and larger soft-hail pellets called
graupel all coexist, collisions between graupel and ice crystals transfer charge
between them. Which way the charge goes depends on temperature and liquid water
content. In the colder upper part of that zone, graupel tends to take on
negative charge and the lighter crystals positive. The updraft then sorts them
by weight: light crystals are carried to the top of the cloud, heavy graupel
lags or falls. The result is a cloud with a positive upper region, a strong
negative layer in the middle, and often a smaller positive pocket near the base.

Air is an excellent insulator, so this arrangement can hold potentials of
hundreds of millions of volts before anything happens. When it finally breaks
down, it does so in steps — a faint, branching stepped leader groping downward
in fifty-meter increments, connecting with an upward streamer from the ground,
and then the return stroke racing back up the established channel at a
significant fraction of the speed of light. That return stroke is the flash you
see. The channel heats to tens of thousands of degrees in microseconds, the air
around it expands explosively, and the shock wave decays into the sound you hear
as thunder. Nearby strikes crack because you receive the shock from a short,
close segment of channel. Distant ones rumble because you are hearing sound
arrive from different parts of a kilometers-long channel at different times.

## The Downdraft, and How Storms Kill Themselves

The same precipitation that the updraft works so hard to loft eventually becomes
the storm's undoing. Falling rain drags air down with it through friction. More
importantly, as rain falls into drier air below the cloud, some of it evaporates,
and evaporation absorbs heat. The air cools, becomes denser than its
surroundings, and accelerates downward. It hits the ground and spreads out in
all directions as a cold pool, its leading edge marked by the gust front — the
sudden wind shift and temperature drop that arrives before the rain.

In an ordinary single-cell storm, the updraft and downdraft occupy the same
space, and the cold pool undercuts the inflow that was feeding the storm. Starved
of warm moist air, the updraft collapses. The whole life cycle takes perhaps
forty-five minutes, which is why isolated summer storms are brief.

Wind shear changes this outcome entirely. If winds increase and turn with
height, the updraft tilts, and precipitation falls out of the side of the storm
rather than back down its own throat. Updraft and downdraft then coexist
indefinitely, separated in space. Storms organized this way persist for hours.
In the most extreme case, horizontal vorticity generated by the shear is tilted
into the vertical by the updraft, and the entire rising column begins to rotate.
That rotating updraft — a mesocyclone — defines the supercell, the storm type
responsible for most large hail and nearly all strong tornadoes.

Cold pools also explain squall lines. When many cells merge, their individual
cold pools combine into a single outflow boundary hundreds of kilometers long.
That boundary lifts the warm air ahead of it, continuously generating new cells
along its leading edge. The line becomes self-sustaining, propagating not by
drifting with the wind but by repeatedly rebuilding itself forward, and it can
cross several states before the warm sector ahead of it is exhausted.

None of this requires anything exotic. Warm moist air near the surface, colder
air aloft, something to give the first parcel a shove, and wind that changes with
height. The rest is thermodynamics working itself out at a scale large enough
to shake the windows.
