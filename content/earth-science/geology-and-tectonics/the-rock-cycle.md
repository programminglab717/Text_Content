# The Rock Cycle

Pick up almost any rock and you are holding something with a past life, or several. A pebble in a riverbed might once have been part of a mountain, and before that a seabed, and before that the cooled crust of a long-vanished lava flow. None of that history shows on the surface. It just sits there, gray or brown, unremarkable, until you know what to look for. Geology's oldest and most useful organizing idea is that rock is not a fixed category of object but a stage in an ongoing process, one that recycles the same material through three broad transformations, endlessly, on a timescale that dwarfs anything in human experience.

## Three Kinds of Rock, One Ongoing Process

Geologists sort rock into three families based on how it formed. Igneous rock crystallizes directly from molten material, either underground, where it cools slowly into coarse-grained rock like granite, or at the surface, where lava chills quickly into fine-grained rock like basalt. Sedimentary rock forms when loose material — grains of sand, fragments of shell, dissolved minerals, the settled remains of dead organisms — accumulates in layers and is gradually compacted and cemented into solid rock, as sandstone forms from sand or limestone from accumulated shell and coral debris. Metamorphic rock is neither of these directly; it is older rock, of any origin, that has been transformed by heat and pressure without fully melting, its minerals reorganizing into new crystal structures while the rock stays solid throughout, the way marble forms from limestone or slate from mudstone.

What makes this a cycle rather than just a classification scheme is that each type can become either of the other two, and the paths between them loop back on themselves in more than one direction. Igneous rock, exposed at the surface and broken down by weathering, supplies the sediment that becomes sedimentary rock. Sedimentary rock buried deep enough and squeezed hard enough becomes metamorphic rock. Metamorphic rock carried deep enough eventually melts and becomes the source material for new igneous rock. But rock can also skip steps or double back: igneous rock can be metamorphosed without ever passing through a sedimentary stage, and metamorphic rock can be weathered at the surface into sediment just as easily as igneous rock can. There is no fixed starting point and no required order, only a set of possible transformations, each driven by a different combination of heat, pressure, and exposure to the surface environment.

## From Melt to Solid Ground

Start with magma, since it is as good a starting point as any. Molten rock generated deep in the mantle or lower crust rises because it is less dense than the solid rock around it. If it stalls before reaching the surface, it cools slowly, over thousands to millions of years, inside a chamber underground. Slow cooling gives mineral crystals time to grow large, which is why intrusive igneous rocks like granite have a coarse, visibly crystalline texture — you can pick out individual grains of quartz, feldspar, and mica with the naked eye. If magma instead reaches the surface and erupts as lava, it cools within hours or days, and the minerals inside barely have time to nucleate before the whole mass locks solid, producing the fine-grained or even glassy texture of extrusive rocks like basalt and obsidian. The same chemical ingredients, cooled at different rates, produce visibly different rock.

Either way, once igneous rock is exposed at Earth's surface, it starts falling apart. Weathering does this in two complementary ways. Mechanical weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its chemistry — water freezing in cracks and expanding, plant roots wedging into fractures, the sheer abrasion of wind-blown sand or grinding ice. Chemical weathering actually alters the minerals themselves, as when feldspar reacts with slightly acidic rainwater and slowly transforms into clay, releasing dissolved ions into solution along the way. Between the two, even the hardest granite eventually reduces to a mixture of loose mineral grains and dissolved chemical remnants.

## Sediment on the Move

That loosened material does not usually stay where it formed. Gravity, running water, wind, and ice all move sediment, sorting it by size and density as they go — a process called erosion when it strips material away and transport when it carries that material somewhere else. Coarse gravel drops out of a fast mountain stream almost immediately, while fine clay stays suspended long enough to be carried all the way to a lake bed or the deep ocean floor, which is why sedimentary layers so often grade neatly from coarse to fine as you move away from their source.

Wherever moving water, ice, or wind finally slows down enough to drop its load, sediment accumulates in layers, with the oldest material buried under everything deposited afterward. Left alone, loose sediment would just sit there indefinitely. Turned into rock, it goes through lithification, which usually happens in two overlapping stages: compaction, as the weight of overlying layers squeezes water and air out of the pore spaces between grains, and cementation, as minerals dissolved in groundwater — commonly silica or calcium carbonate — precipitate into those same pore spaces and glue the grains together. A beach's worth of loose sand, buried deeply enough and left long enough, becomes sandstone, still bearing the ripple marks and cross-bedding it had as sand, just locked permanently in place.

Not all sedimentary rock is made of eroded fragments, either. Some forms chemically, when dissolved minerals precipitate directly out of water, as when evaporating seawater leaves behind layers of salt or gypsum. Some forms biologically, built almost entirely from the remains of once-living things, as when coral reefs and shell debris accumulate and lithify into limestone, or ancient swamp vegetation compresses into coal. Sedimentary rock, more than the other two types, tends to preserve direct evidence of the environment it formed in and often the fossilized remains of what was alive at the time — which is why nearly all of the fossil record comes from sedimentary rock rather than igneous or metamorphic.

## Squeezed, Heated, and Sent Back Down

Bury any of these rocks deeply enough, or catch them at the collision zone between two plates, and conditions change enough to alter the rock without melting it outright. Rising temperature and pressure cause minerals to recrystallize into new forms that are more stable under those conditions, and in rocks with a directional stress applied — the kind of squeezing common in mountain-building — flat or elongated minerals often align themselves perpendicular to the pressure, giving many metamorphic rocks a banded or foliated appearance distinct from their unmetamorphosed parent. Shale, a fine sedimentary rock made of compacted clay, becomes slate under modest heat and pressure, then phyllite, then schist, then gneiss as conditions intensify further, each step recrystallizing the minerals into larger and more distinctly banded arrangements without ever fully melting the rock.

Push the conditions further still, past the point where the rock can remain fully solid, and metamorphism gives way to melting. The resulting magma, chemically related to but not identical to its metamorphic parent, rises back toward the surface and, eventually, cools into new igneous rock — completing a loop that, depending on the path taken, might have spanned all three rock types or looped through just two.

## A Cycle With No Real Beginning

Because any rock type can transform into either of the others, and because the paths between them can run in more than one direction, the rock cycle has no true starting point, despite how it is usually drawn as a circular diagram with arrows pointing from one box to the next. A single mineral grain of quartz, weathered out of a granite boulder today, might end up as sand on a beach, then sandstone a few million years from now, then — pulled into a subduction zone and buried deep enough — schist, and eventually melt into magma that erupts as a new volcano built from material that started as a very different kind of rock entirely. The timescales involved are long enough that no human has ever watched a single grain complete the full circuit, but the evidence is written into rock everywhere: granite exposed at the surface, weathering visibly into soil; sandstone with the fossil ripple marks of a beach preserved inside it; gneiss that shows, in its banding, the ghost of sedimentary layers it started as. The rock beneath your feet, wherever you happen to be standing, is simply wherever the cycle currently has it paused.
