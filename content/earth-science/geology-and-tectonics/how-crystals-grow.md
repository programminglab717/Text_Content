# How Crystals Grow

A geode looks like a magic trick: a plain, unremarkable rock that, cracked open, reveals a hollow interior lined with pointed crystals catching the light. There is no trick to it, only chemistry and time. Every crystal, whether it is a hexagonal quartz point large enough to hold in your hand or a grain of salt too small to see without a microscope, forms by the same underlying rule: atoms or ions, given the freedom to move and a reason to stop, arrange themselves into a repeating, orderly lattice, because that ordered arrangement is a lower-energy, more stable configuration than a random jumble of the same atoms. Left with enough time and the right conditions, matter organizes itself. Crystal growth is that principle playing out at a scale you can hold in your hand.

## Starting From Nothing: Nucleation

Before a crystal can grow, it has to start, and starting is the hardest part. Nucleation is the moment a handful of atoms or ions, drifting in a melt or a solution, happen to bump into each other in the right orientation and stick, forming a tiny cluster with the beginnings of the mineral's characteristic lattice structure. Most such clusters are unstable and fall back apart almost immediately, because the energy cost of building a new surface exceeds the energy saved by the ordered arrangement inside. Only a cluster that happens to reach a certain minimum size — vanishingly small, but not zero — becomes stable enough to keep growing rather than dissolve back into the surrounding fluid.

This is why crystals so often need either a strong push, in the form of a solution or melt that is significantly oversaturated or overcooled relative to equilibrium, or a helping hand, in the form of an existing surface to grow on rather than needing to nucleate from scratch. Dust, existing mineral grains, even the microscopic scratches on a container wall can all serve as a template that lowers the energy barrier to nucleation, which is why crystals so often grow in clusters starting from a shared surface, rather than as isolated points scattered randomly through open space. In a magma chamber cooling underground, the first minerals to nucleate become tiny seed crystals suspended in the remaining melt, and it is on these seeds, more than through fresh nucleation, that most of the chamber's eventual crystal mass accumulates.

## The Lattice Does the Choosing

Once nucleation succeeds, growth proceeds by atoms or ions from the surrounding fluid attaching themselves to the crystal's outer surface, one layer at a time, in positions dictated by the lattice already established. This is the reason crystals of a given mineral always show the same family of angles between their faces no matter their overall size or how distorted their growth was by crowding — the angles are a direct geometric consequence of how the atoms are packed internally, and growth can only add material in positions the existing lattice allows. Quartz crystals, however stubby or however needle-thin, meet at the same roughly 120-degree angles between adjacent prism faces, because silicon and oxygen atoms inside quartz are locked into a specific spiral arrangement that permits no other geometry at the surface.

This also explains why so many crystals look geometric and faceted rather than round. A sphere is the shape you get when material is added with no directional preference at all, the way a raindrop forms. A crystal facet is flat because atoms attach more readily, and more slowly detach, at certain orientations relative to the underlying lattice than at others; growth proceeds fastest in directions where new material bonds easily and slowest where it does not, and the slowest-growing faces are the ones that persist and become visible, since fast-growing faces tend to grow themselves out of existence. A crystal's outward shape is really a visible record of which of its internal faces grew reluctantly.

## Why Some Crystals Are Huge and Others Are Microscopic

The single biggest factor determining crystal size is how much time growth had, and specifically how slowly the surrounding material cooled or how slowly the solution concentration changed. Rapid cooling forces a large number of nucleation sites to form nearly simultaneously, and each resulting crystal is starved of raw material by its many close neighbors all growing and competing for the same finite pool of atoms, so all of them stay small — this is why lava chilled quickly at the surface produces basalt too fine-grained to see individual crystals in at all, sometimes cooling fast enough to skip crystallization altogether and freeze as glass. Slow cooling, by contrast, allows relatively few nuclei to form, spaced well apart, each with an enormous surrounding reservoir of melt to draw from and abundant time to draw from it — this is how pegmatites form, a rare type of igneous rock that cools so slowly, often from water-rich residual magma that stays molten longer than normal, that individual crystals can grow to be measured in meters rather than millimeters, producing some of the largest single crystals known.

The same principle governs crystals that grow from solution rather than melt. A geode's interior crystals grew slowly, over long stretches of time, as mineral-rich groundwater seeped through a cavity and deposited material in fits and starts, which is why geode crystals are often large, well-formed, and visibly transparent, compared to the fine-grained rock of the geode's outer shell that solidified quickly by comparison. Stalactites in caves grow this way too, one nearly imperceptible mineral film at a time, which is part of why they grow so slowly that a single formation can represent tens of thousands of years of accumulated groundwater seepage.

## Impurities, Twins, and Imperfection

Real crystals are rarely as clean as the geometric ideal suggests. Impurity atoms of a similar size to the crystal's main constituents can substitute into the lattice in small quantities without disrupting the overall structure, and these substitutions are often responsible for a mineral's color — pure corundum is colorless, but trace chromium turns it into ruby, while trace iron and titanium turn the same mineral into blue sapphire. The base mineral scarcely changes; a handful of foreign atoms scattered through an otherwise identical lattice is enough to change the color dramatically.

Crystals can also grow with internal flaws called twins, where two intergrown crystal segments share part of their lattice but are oriented as mirror images or rotations of each other, producing distinctive V-shaped or star-shaped forms rather than a single simple geometric solid. Growth is rarely perfectly undisturbed, either: a crystal growing in a crowded vein or cavity may be forced up against a neighbor before it finishes forming, leaving one face flattened or absent altogether, or it may be interrupted and resume later under slightly different conditions, leaving a visible growth band or zone inside the finished crystal, not unlike a tree ring, that records a change in the fluid it was growing from partway through its history.

## Same Ingredients, Different Lattice

One of the stranger consequences of how crystals grow is that identical chemistry can produce entirely different minerals, depending purely on the temperature and pressure present during growth rather than on any difference in the atoms involved. Carbon crystallizing under the crushing pressure and heat found deep in the mantle locks into the tightly packed, three-dimensional lattice of diamond, the hardest naturally occurring substance known. Carbon crystallizing under ordinary surface pressure instead forms graphite, in which atoms bond into loose, weakly stacked sheets that slide past each other so easily that graphite works as a lubricant and a pencil lead. Nothing about the chemistry differs between them; the sole distinction is which lattice geometry was favorable under the specific conditions each one crystallized in. Chemists call this relationship polymorphism, and it shows up throughout mineralogy — calcium carbonate forms both calcite and aragonite depending on conditions, and the aluminosilicate mineral with the formula behind kyanite, andalusite, and sillimanite is, again, one identical chemical composition crystallizing into three distinct structures depending on the temperature and pressure it experienced.

Polymorphism matters to geologists for a practical reason beyond curiosity: because each polymorph is stable only within a specific range of temperature and pressure, finding a particular polymorph in a rock sample can itself serve as a rough gauge of the conditions that rock experienced, functioning almost like a built-in thermometer and pressure gauge. A metamorphic rock containing kyanite formed under different conditions than one containing andalusite, even though both minerals share the exact same chemical formula, and mapping which polymorph appears where across a region of metamorphic rock is a standard way geologists reconstruct the pressure and temperature history of rock that has since been uplifted and exposed at the surface, far from the depth at which it actually crystallized.

## Reading a Crystal's History

None of this randomness undermines the basic order of crystal growth; if anything, the imperfections make a crystal more informative rather than less. A geologist looking at a crystal is not just seeing a finished geometric object but reading a short biography: how fast it grew, whether conditions changed partway through, what impurities happened to be present in the fluid it grew from, whether it was crowded by neighbors or given open space. Every faceted crystal on a rock shop shelf, however ordinary looking, is the visible outcome of atoms finding the single most efficient way to pack themselves together, patiently, one layer at a time, for as long as conditions allowed it to continue.
