# The Layered Interior of the Earth

Nobody has ever drilled deeper than a small fraction of the way to the center of the Earth. The deepest borehole ever sunk, a scientific project on the Kola Peninsula that took decades to bore just over twelve kilometers down, did not even fully penetrate the crust, the thinnest of Earth's major layers. And yet geologists will describe the composition, temperature, and physical state of material six thousand kilometers below your feet with genuine confidence, in detail that can sound almost suspicious given that no one has ever gone there or ever will. The confidence is earned. It comes from a method that does not require physically reaching a place to know quite a lot about it: listening to how earthquakes sound when they travel through it.

## Learning About the Inside From the Outside

Earthquakes release energy in the form of seismic waves, and those waves travel outward from the rupture point through the solid body of the planet, eventually reaching seismometers scattered across the globe. The key insight that makes this useful for mapping Earth's interior is that seismic waves do not travel at a constant speed through all material. Their speed depends on the density, composition, temperature, and physical state of whatever rock or fluid they happen to be passing through, and whenever a wave crosses a boundary between two materials with different properties, it bends, speeds up, slows down, or partially reflects, in ways precisely analogous to how light bends when passing from air into water.

By recording exactly when and where seismic waves from a given earthquake arrive at seismometers around the world, and comparing those arrival times and paths against what would be expected from a uniform, unlayered Earth, seismologists can work backward to infer where the boundaries between different internal layers must lie, and something about the physical properties on each side of them. This technique, refined over more than a century, is how essentially all of what is known about Earth's deep interior was learned, layer by layer, boundary by boundary, without a single direct sample from anywhere near the deepest layers involved.

## The Crust: Thin, Variable, and Familiar

The outermost layer, the crust, is also by far the thinnest, and it comes in two distinctly different varieties. Oceanic crust, dense and basaltic, runs only about five to ten kilometers thick and is geologically young everywhere, constantly being created at mid-ocean ridges and eventually recycled back into the mantle at subduction zones, so that no oceanic crust anywhere on Earth is older than roughly two hundred million years — young compared to the planet's overall age. Continental crust is thicker, typically thirty to forty kilometers and considerably more under mountain ranges, less dense than oceanic crust, and in places extremely old, with some continental rock dating back well over three billion years, since continental crust is too buoyant to subduct and so tends to persist at the surface indefinitely once formed, accumulating a much longer history than the ocean floor ever gets the chance to.

Beneath both types of crust lies the boundary that first revealed the existence of any deeper layering at all: the Mohorovičić discontinuity, usually just called the Moho, identified in the early twentieth century by a seismologist studying earthquake wave speeds and named for him. Below the Moho, seismic waves abruptly speed up, indicating a change to denser rock, and this boundary marks the transition from crust into the layer beneath it, the mantle.

## The Mantle: Solid Rock That Flows

The mantle makes up by far the largest share of Earth's volume, extending from the base of the crust down to roughly half the planet's radius, and it is, despite popular imagination sometimes picturing it as an ocean of liquid magma underfoot, solid rock throughout almost its entire extent. What makes the mantle behave in ways that can seem liquid-like over long timescales is not that it is molten, but that solid rock under enough sustained heat and pressure can slowly deform and flow without ever melting, the same principle that lets glacial ice flow as a solid. Over millions of years, mantle rock circulates in slow convective currents, hot material rising in some regions and cooler, denser material sinking in others, and this circulation is understood to be the ultimate engine behind plate tectonics, dragging and pushing the rigid plates of the lithosphere above it.

The mantle itself is not uniform throughout its depth. Around four hundred to seven hundred kilometers down lies the transition zone, where increasing pressure forces the same minerals present in the shallower mantle to reorganize into denser crystal structures without any change in overall chemical composition, producing distinct seismic boundaries at fairly consistent depths worldwide. Below that lies the lower mantle, extending most of the way to the core, generally understood to be more uniform and sluggish in its convection than the upper mantle, though still capable of large-scale flow over long enough timescales.

## The Core: Where Iron Takes Over

At a depth of roughly 2,900 kilometers, seismic waves encounter the most dramatic boundary in the entire planet, marked by an abrupt and substantial change in wave behavior that reveals a shift from silicate rock to a core composed predominantly of iron and nickel. This boundary also reveals something about the state of matter on each side of it: certain types of seismic wave, ones that can only travel through solids and not liquids, simply fail to pass through the outer portion of the core at all, which is one of the principal pieces of evidence establishing that the outer core is liquid, a shell of molten iron alloy roughly two and a quarter thousand kilometers thick.

It is the convective churning of this liquid metal outer core, combined with Earth's rotation, that generates the planet's magnetic field through the dynamo process, making the outer core not just a structural layer but the direct source of the field that shields the surface from much of the sun's particle radiation and has done so for most of the planet's history.

Deeper still, at a radius of about 1,220 kilometers, lies the inner core, and here the pattern reverses again: despite being hotter than the liquid outer core surrounding it, the inner core is solid, because pressure at that depth is high enough to force iron into a solid state even at temperatures that would liquefy it at any pressure found nearer the surface. The inner core is thought to be slowly growing over geological time, as the whole planet gradually cools and the boundary where liquid iron freezes solid creeps very slowly outward, releasing latent heat and lighter elements into the liquid outer core as it does, which itself helps power the very convection that drives the magnetic field. There is even evidence that the inner core rotates at a very slightly different rate than the rest of the planet, decoupled from the mantle and crust above it by the liquid layer surrounding it, drifting gradually relative to the surface over a timescale of decades.

## A Planet Sorted by Density

Standing back from the individual layers, the overall pattern is one of density sorting on an enormous scale: light silicate rock at the surface, denser silicate rock in the mantle beneath it, and the densest material of all, iron and nickel, concentrated at the very center. This is not a coincidence or an arbitrary arrangement. It is the leftover signature of Earth's earliest history, when the whole planet was hot enough throughout its volume to behave, in effect, as a very viscous fluid, and the heaviest available material sank toward the center under gravity while lighter material rose toward the surface, a process called planetary differentiation that appears to have happened remarkably early and quickly compared to the planet's overall age. Every layer discussed here, from the thin crust we stand on to the solid iron ball at the center, is essentially a fossil of that original sorting, still slowly cooling, still convecting, and still — through the earthquakes it periodically produces — willing to describe its own interior in detail to anyone patient enough to listen for it from the surface.
