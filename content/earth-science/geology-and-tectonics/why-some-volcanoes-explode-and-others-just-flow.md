# Why Some Volcanoes Explode and Others Just Flow

Stand at the edge of an eruption in Hawaii and, danger aside, you could get remarkably close. Lava creeps across the ground at walking pace or slower, glowing orange, occasionally spattering from a fountain a few meters high. Stand anywhere near an eruption of the kind that struck Mount St. Helens or Pinatubo, by contrast, and there is no getting close, because the mountain does not so much erupt as detonate, throwing ash twenty kilometers into the stratosphere and sending clouds of superheated gas and rock racing down its flanks faster than a car on a highway. Both events are volcanic eruptions. Both involve magma reaching the surface. The difference between a lava flow you can outwalk and a blast that levels a forest for kilometers around comes down almost entirely to chemistry — specifically, to how much silica the magma contains, and how much gas is dissolved inside it.

## Silica Is the Key Variable

Magma is not a single substance but a family of molten rock compositions, and the single most useful number for predicting how a given magma will behave is its silica content — the proportion of silicon dioxide in the melt. Magmas low in silica, generally called basaltic or mafic, tend to run around 45 to 55 percent silica and are relatively fluid, similar in viscosity to thick syrup or, in slower-moving cases, wet cement. Magmas high in silica, called rhyolitic or felsic, can exceed 70 percent silica and behave more like cold molasses or even solid taffy, resistant to flowing at all.

The reason comes down to what silica does at the molecular level. Silicon and oxygen atoms in a melt link together into chains and networks, the same kind of bonding that makes ordinary glass rigid. The more silica a magma contains, the more extensively these networks form, and the more the melt resists flowing past itself, exactly the property chemists call viscosity. A basaltic magma has fewer of these networked chains and flows easily. A rhyolitic magma is laced with them and flows sluggishly, if it flows at all before solidifying.

Where a magma falls on this spectrum depends heavily on where it came from and what it has done on the way up. Magma generated by decompression melting in the mantle beneath a mid-ocean ridge, or by flux melting above a subduction zone, starts out relatively low in silica because the mantle rock it melted from is itself silica-poor. But magma that lingers in a crustal chamber for a long time before erupting has the opportunity to evolve. As it cools slowly, certain minerals crystallize out first — ones that are comparatively poor in silica — and settle out of the remaining liquid, which concentrates the silica left behind in the melt that has yet to solidify. Magma can also assimilate silica-rich continental crust as it rises through it, or mix with older, more evolved magma already sitting in a chamber. The result is that volcanoes built on continental crust, where magma has both crust to interact with and time to sit before erupting, tend to erupt more silica-rich, higher-viscosity magma than volcanoes built directly on thin oceanic crust over a mantle hot spot.

## Gas Is the Trigger

Viscosity alone explains why some lava flows fast and some flows slowly, but it does not by itself explain explosions. For that, add dissolved gas — mostly water vapor, along with carbon dioxide and sulfur compounds — which is present in essentially all magma, held in solution the same way carbon dioxide is held in solution in a sealed bottle of soda, kept dissolved by pressure. As magma rises toward the surface, pressure drops, and the gas begins to come out of solution as bubbles, exactly as bubbles form when a bottle is opened.

In a low-viscosity, low-silica magma, those bubbles can move through the melt relatively freely and escape at the surface, or burst in small fountains and sprays, the incandescent lava fountains characteristic of Hawaiian-style eruptions. The gas leaks out about as fast as it forms, so pressure never builds to a dangerous level. The eruption is spectacular but comparatively survivable, and it is often effusive rather than explosive: lava simply flows.

In a high-viscosity, high-silica magma, the same bubbles cannot escape so easily. The melt around them is too stiff to let gas migrate and separate out. Pressure inside the rising magma column keeps building as more gas comes out of solution with no way to vent, like shaking a bottle of soda with the cap still on. Eventually the confined gas pressure exceeds what the surrounding rock and magma can contain, and the whole column fragments almost instantaneously into a spray of pulverized rock, ash, and gas expanding outward at enormous speed. This is what makes an eruption explosive rather than effusive: not the amount of magma involved, but whether its gas content had an easy way out.

## Reading the Signs in the Rock

Geologists can often tell, just from the texture of erupted rock, which regime it came from. Basalt from an effusive eruption tends to be dense, dark, and only lightly vesicular, meaning it has relatively few gas bubble cavities frozen into it because most of the gas escaped before the rock solidified. Pumice, by contrast — the pale, astonishingly lightweight rock that can float on water — is essentially frozen volcanic foam, so thoroughly riddled with bubble cavities from trapped, expanding gas that it is mostly empty space, a direct physical record of a magma that could not release its gas gradually and instead fragmented catastrophically. Obsidian, volcanic glass, forms from the same high-silica magmas when they cool so fast that no crystal structure has time to form at all, freezing the melt's internal disorder in place.

The type of volcanic edifice itself also reflects this chemistry. Shield volcanoes, broad and gently sloped like an inverted warrior's shield, form from repeated basaltic flows that spread thinly and travel far before solidifying — Hawaii's Mauna Loa is the textbook example. Stratovolcanoes, the steep, conical, classically volcano-shaped mountains like Fuji or Rainier, form from more viscous, silica-richer magma that piles up close to the vent rather than spreading, interbedded with layers of ash from the explosive episodes that same stiff magma tends to produce. The shape of a volcano is, in a real sense, a portrait of the chemistry of the magma that built it.

## Why This Matters for Risk

This chemistry has direct consequences for how dangerous a given volcano is likely to be, which is why volcanologists monitoring an active system pay close attention to the composition of the gas and ash it is already emitting, and to seismic signals that hint at whether magma rising toward the surface is becoming more silica-rich as it interacts with crust on the way up. A volcano that has historically produced fluid, gas-poor basalt is unlikely to suddenly produce a catastrophic explosive eruption, because that would require an entirely different magma chemistry arriving from depth. A volcano sitting on a body of stiff, gas-charged, silica-rich magma, on the other hand, is capable of an eruption whose main danger has little to do with lava at all — it comes from pyroclastic flows, fast-moving avalanches of superheated ash and gas, and from ash columns that can disrupt weather and air travel across an entire region. Two mountains, both built by the same underlying process of magma reaching the surface, can pose almost entirely different kinds of threat, and the reason traces back to a single number: how much silica happened to be locked into the melt before it ever broke through the ground.
