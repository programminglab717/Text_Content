# How Glaciers Carve the Land

A river valley and a glacial valley look nothing alike, and once you know what you are looking at, you can tell which tool did the carving from a considerable distance. A river valley is a narrow V in cross-section, cut by water working relentlessly along a single thin channel. A glacial valley is a broad U, with steep, straightened walls and a flat or gently curved floor, because ice does not carve like water. It does not stay in a channel. It fills the entire valley from wall to wall, and it carves and polishes everything it touches across the full width of that fill, planing off the walls as thoroughly as the floor. Ice is, among other things, a strangely effective sculptor, and understanding how it works means understanding two related processes: how a glacier picks material up, and how it puts that material back down somewhere else.

## Ice That Flows Like a Very Slow Liquid

The first thing to understand about a glacier is that it moves, and it moves not by sliding as a single rigid block but by flowing, in the way that solid materials under enough sustained pressure can deform gradually without ever melting. Glacial ice, buried under the weight of hundreds of meters of ice above it, deforms internally, its crystal structure shifting and slipping along internal planes, allowing the whole mass to creep downhill or outward from an accumulation zone at rates typically measured in centimeters to meters per day. Where a glacier sits on a bed of unfrozen ground, it can also slide as a mass over a thin film of meltwater at its base, adding a second mode of motion on top of internal deformation.

This flow is what makes a glacier fundamentally different from a static ice sheet just sitting in place. A glacier is a river, just an almost unimaginably slow and viscous one, with ice continuously supplied at its source — usually a high-altitude accumulation zone where snowfall each year exceeds summer melting — and continuously removed at its terminus, either by melting or, for glaciers that reach the sea, by calving icebergs directly into open water. As long as accumulation at the top roughly balances loss at the bottom, the glacier maintains a stable size while the ice within it is constantly moving through, a bit like a conveyor belt that keeps the same overall length while the material on it is continuously replaced.

## Plucking and Abrasion: The Two Tools of Glacial Erosion

Glaciers erode bedrock through two distinct mechanisms working together. The first, called abrasion, happens because the base of a glacier is studded with rock fragments of every size, frozen into the ice or dragged along beneath it, and as the glacier flows forward these fragments are dragged across the bedrock surface like the world's largest and slowest sheet of sandpaper. Abrasion polishes bedrock smooth and leaves fine scratches called striations, often running in perfectly parallel sets aligned with the direction of ice flow, that remain visible on exposed bedrock for thousands of years after the ice itself has retreated — striations are, in fact, one of the clearest pieces of physical evidence geologists use to reconstruct exactly which direction ancient ice sheets were moving, long after any ice is left to observe directly.

The second mechanism, plucking, is more violent and works on a larger scale. Meltwater at the base of a glacier seeps into cracks and joints already present in the underlying bedrock, and when that water refreezes, it expands and wedges the joint further apart, loosening blocks of rock from the surrounding bedrock mass. As the glacier continues flowing forward, it can then physically pluck these loosened blocks away entirely, incorporating them into the ice and carrying them off, often leaving behind a jagged, steplike bedrock surface on the downstream side of an obstacle, in sharp contrast to the smooth, gently sloped, abraded surface typically left on the upstream side facing the oncoming ice. A single glaciated bedrock knob, smooth on one side and jagged on the other, is basically a compass: the smooth upstream face and the plucked, ragged downstream face tell you unambiguously which way the ice was flowing when it passed over.

## The Shapes Ice Leaves Behind

Because a glacier fills and scours its entire valley rather than following a single channel, glacial valleys end up broad and U-shaped, often with strikingly steep walls the ice has planed flat over repeated glaciations. Where a fjord forms, this same U-shaped glacial valley has since been flooded by the sea after the ice retreated and global sea levels rose, producing the steep-walled, deep, narrow inlets characteristic of coastlines like Norway's or the west coast of South America — a fjord is, at its core, simply a glacial valley that the ocean has since moved into.

At higher elevations, where glaciers originate rather than merely pass through, a different and more angular set of landforms takes shape. A cirque is a bowl-shaped hollow carved into a mountainside at the head of a glacier, gouged out as ice forms, plucks at the surrounding rock, and slowly enlarges its own starting basin through repeated freeze-thaw action at its back wall. Where cirques form on multiple sides of the same peak and erode toward each other, they can leave behind a sharp, pyramidal summit called a horn — the Matterhorn is the textbook example, its dramatic four-sided peak sculpted entirely by cirques attacking the same original mountain mass from different directions simultaneously. Where two parallel glacial valleys are separated by a ridge that cirques have eaten into from both sides, the ridge itself can be reduced to a knife-edged crest called an arête, thin enough in some cases that it is only a few meters wide at its top.

## Where the Ice Drops What It Carried

Everything a glacier erodes eventually has to go somewhere, and glacial deposition is, in its own way, just as diagnostic as glacial erosion. Unlike a river, which sorts sediment carefully by size and weight as it deposits it, ice has no such discrimination — a glacier carries boulders, gravel, sand, and fine clay all together, frozen into the same mass, and when the ice melts it simply drops everything at once in a jumbled, unsorted mixture called till. A moraine is a ridge or mound built from this till, deposited wherever a glacier's edge remained relatively stationary for long enough to accumulate a substantial pile, or bulldozed forward like a plow blade in front of an advancing glacial snout. Terminal moraines, marking the farthest point a glacier ever reached before retreating, are often the clearest evidence left behind of how extensive an ice sheet once was, long after the ice itself is entirely gone.

Some of the most visually odd landscape features left by glaciation come from meltwater rather than ice directly. As a glacier melts, streams of water running through, under, and off the ice carry and sort sediment much the way an ordinary river would, depositing well-layered sand and gravel in features like eskers — long, sinuous ridges that mark the former course of a river that once ran in a tunnel underneath the ice, now standing above the surrounding landscape because the ice that once flanked it is gone and the channel sediment inside it did not erode away nearly as fast as the ice did. An erratic, a boulder of a rock type not native to wherever it currently sits, tells a similar story on an individual scale: a rock picked up by ice potentially hundreds of kilometers away and simply dropped, isolated and conspicuous, wherever the glacier carrying it eventually melted.

Put together, all of this means a glaciated landscape carries a legible record of ice long after the ice is gone — polished and striated bedrock, U-shaped valleys, horns and arêtes at the highest elevations, moraines marking old ice margins, eskers tracing old subglacial rivers, and erratic boulders scattered like abandoned cargo. Reading that record is largely a matter of recognizing that ice, unlike water, erodes broadly rather than narrowly, carries indiscriminately rather than sorting, and leaves nearly everything exactly where it stopped moving.
