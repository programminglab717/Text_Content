# A History of the Microprocessor

In 1969 a delegation of engineers from a Japanese calculator company called Busicom arrived at a three-year-old chip startup in Mountain View with a plan for a printing calculator. The plan called for something like a dozen custom integrated circuits, each hard-wired to perform one part of the job. Intel at the time had a few dozen employees and made memory chips; it did not have the design staff to produce a dozen bespoke logic devices for one customer. An engineer named Ted Hoff looked at the proposal and suggested inverting it. Instead of a dozen chips that each knew how to do one thing, build one chip that knew how to do general operations, and put the calculator's specific behaviour into a program stored in memory alongside it.

This was not a new idea in computing. It was how every mainframe and minicomputer already worked. What was new was the claim that it could be squeezed onto a single piece of silicon roughly the size of a fingernail, and that the result would be cheap enough to put inside a desk calculator. Federico Faggin, who joined Intel in 1970 and had come from Fairchild, where he had worked on the silicon-gate process that made dense MOS logic practical, did the actual design work with Busicom's Masatoshi Shima. The chip they produced, the 4004, went on sale in 1971. It handled four bits at a time and contained somewhere around 2,300 transistors.

## Who Was First Is a Messy Question

The 4004 is conventionally called the first microprocessor and the label is defensible, but it is not uncontested. Texas Instruments was working on a comparable single-chip processor at the same time for the same sort of customer. Lee Boysel's Four-Phase Systems had built processing logic onto very few chips somewhat earlier. Most striking of all, a team led by Ray Holt had designed a chipset for the flight computer of the F-14 Tomcat around 1970 that performed the essential function of a microprocessor; the work was classified and stayed unknown for decades, so it influenced nothing and was credited by no one at the time.

What makes the Intel line the one that mattered is not primacy but what came after. Intel sold the 4004 as a catalogue part to anyone who wanted one, published the documentation, and followed it quickly. The 8008, released in 1972, handled eight bits and came out of a contract with Computer Terminal Corporation, which had specified a processor for its Datapoint 2200 terminal and then declined to use the chip when it arrived. Two years later came the 8080, faster, easier to design around, and the first of these devices to be treated by hobbyists and engineers as a general-purpose computer rather than a component in an appliance.

## The Machines Nobody Planned For

The Altair 8800 of 1975 was an 8080 in a box with switches on the front and nothing resembling a user interface. It was also the first computer that a person could buy for a few hundred dollars and own outright, and the response to it caught everyone by surprise, including the company selling it. Within a couple of years a whole ecosystem had grown around the 8080 and its compatible successor, the Z80, designed at Zilog by Faggin after he left Intel, with Shima again doing much of the detailed work. The Z80 ran the CP/M operating system, and CP/M made the idea of software you could buy separately from hardware seem normal.

The other decisive chip of that era came from a different direction entirely. A group of engineers including Chuck Peddle left Motorola's 6800 project, joined MOS Technology, and produced the 6502 in 1975. Its architecture was spare, its instruction set small, and its price extraordinary: it was introduced at about twenty-five dollars when competing processors were selling for several times that. Cheapness did more for computing in that decade than cleverness. The 6502 and its variants ended up inside the Apple II, the Commodore PET and VIC-20 and 64, the Atari 2600, the BBC Micro, and the Nintendo Entertainment System. A generation of programmers learned what a computer was by writing assembly language for that chip.

## An Accident Becomes a Standard

Intel's 8086, from 1978, was a sixteen-bit design that kept a deliberate family resemblance to the 8080 so existing software could be mechanically translated to it. The 8088 that followed was the same processor with an eight-bit external bus, a compromise that made surrounding circuitry cheaper. When IBM assembled its personal computer in 1981 from mostly off-the-shelf parts, it chose the 8088, largely for that cost reason and because second sources existed.

The IBM PC was cloned, the clones ran the same software, and the software became the asset worth protecting. Every subsequent Intel processor — the 286, the 32-bit 386 of 1985, the 486, the Pentium — had to keep running programs written for the chip before it. Backward compatibility became the central constraint of the industry's most valuable product line, and the x86 instruction set, designed under deadline pressure as a bridge from an eight-bit chip, was locked in for the next forty years.

It did not have to go that way. Motorola's 68000, also from 1979, was by most engineering measures the nicer design: a clean, orthogonal, 32-bit-flavoured architecture with a large uniform register set. It powered the original Macintosh, the Amiga, the Atari ST, laser printers, arcade boards, and early Sun workstations. It lost the desktop anyway, for reasons that had more to do with IBM's purchasing decisions and the clone market than with anything inside the silicon.

## Doing Less, Faster

While the commercial fight was happening, a quieter argument was running through academic and research computing. John Cocke's group at IBM, David Patterson's at Berkeley, and John Hennessy's at Stanford converged in the late 1970s and early 1980s on a heretical-sounding conclusion: processors had been accumulating elaborate instructions that compilers rarely used, and the complexity was slowing down the instructions that actually mattered. Strip the instruction set down to simple, fixed-length operations, restrict memory access to dedicated load and store instructions, give the chip plenty of registers, and let the compiler do the sophisticated work. The transistors saved could go toward making the common case fast.

Reduced instruction set computing produced SPARC, MIPS, PA-RISC, POWER, and Alpha, and it dominated workstations and servers for a decade. It also changed the chips that nominally rejected it. From the mid-1990s, x86 processors began decoding their complicated legacy instructions into simple internal operations and executing those on a core that looked, structurally, much like a RISC machine.

The techniques that made processors faster during that period are still the ones in use. Pipelining splits instruction execution into stages so that several instructions are in flight simultaneously, like cars on an assembly line. Caches keep recently used data in small fast memory close to the core, because main memory had by then fallen catastrophically far behind the processor in speed. Superscalar execution issues more than one instruction per clock cycle. Out-of-order execution lets the chip run instructions as their inputs become available rather than in program order, and branch prediction guesses which way a conditional will go so the pipeline never has to stand idle waiting to find out. Each of these buys performance without asking the programmer to do anything differently, which is precisely why they were pursued so hard.

## The Wall, and What Came After It

For thirty years, shrinking transistors made them faster and less power-hungry at the same time, a relationship that let clock speeds climb decade after decade. That relationship broke down in the early 2000s. Leakage currents rose, power density stopped falling, and cooling a chip running at ten gigahertz became a problem nobody could solve at consumer prices. Around 2004 Intel cancelled the high-clock designs it had been planning and pivoted, as did everyone else, to putting multiple cores on a single die. Performance would continue to grow, but only for software willing to be rewritten to use it.

Two other shifts followed. One was mobile. ARM began as an in-house project at Acorn Computers in Cambridge, where Sophie Wilson designed the instruction set and Steve Furber led the chip work; it was a small team's RISC processor for a British home computer, and its low power consumption was as much a consequence of the team's modest ambitions as a design goal. That characteristic turned out to be the only thing that mattered when computers went into pockets. ARM's decision to license designs rather than manufacture chips let dozens of companies build processors around the same instruction set, and the phone in your hand almost certainly contains one.

The other shift was specialization. When general-purpose cores stopped getting dramatically faster, the way to gain performance was to stop being general-purpose: graphics processors for parallel arithmetic, dedicated blocks for video encoding and cryptography and signal processing, and whole accelerators built for one kind of mathematics. A modern processor is not really a processor any more. It is a system on a chip, a collection of specialized engines sharing memory with a handful of general cores, often assembled from separate silicon dies bonded into one package because a single large die would yield too poorly to be affordable.

Fifty-odd years after a calculator contract produced a chip with a couple of thousand transistors on it, the thing that has changed least is the basic bargain Ted Hoff proposed: build one flexible machine and let software specialize it. What keeps changing is where the bottleneck sits — instruction count, then memory latency, then clock frequency, then power, then the cost of moving data across a die — and each generation of design is mostly an answer to whichever constraint was binding at the time.
