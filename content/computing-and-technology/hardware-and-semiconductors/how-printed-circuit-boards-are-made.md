# How Printed Circuit Boards Are Made

Open the back of almost any dead appliance and you will find the same object: a flat green card with components soldered to it and a maze of fine lines running between them. The green is not the board. It is a lacquer sprayed over the top. Scrape it and you find a hard tan material, glassy and slightly fibrous at a cut edge, with copper bonded to its surface in a pattern that somebody drew. That laminate is the printed circuit board, and almost everything electronic made since the middle of the twentieth century sits on one.

The board does two jobs at once, and the second is easy to forget. It holds parts in position and connects them electrically, which is the obvious job. It also determines how signals behave on the way between parts — how fast a fast edge stays fast, how much a switching current couples into a neighbouring trace, whether a return path exists where it is needed. At kilohertz frequencies the copper is just wire. Above a few hundred megahertz it is a network of transmission lines with properties set by geometry, and the fabrication process is what fixes that geometry.

## Glass, Resin, and Foil

The standard substrate is FR-4, a name that describes a flammability grade rather than a recipe. Woven fiberglass cloth is soaked in epoxy resin and cured under heat and pressure into a rigid sheet. Copper foil, most commonly the thickness the industry still calls one ounce — about thirty-five micrometres, from the weight of copper per square foot — is bonded to one or both faces during that cure. The resulting double-sided sheet is a core.

The other raw material is prepreg: the same glass cloth impregnated with resin but only partially cured, so it is stiff, slightly tacky, and will flow and harden when heated again. Prepreg is the glue that holds a multilayer board together, and because it flows, it also fills the spaces between etched copper features on the layers it bonds.

A designer's contribution arrives at the factory as a set of files — historically Gerber, increasingly more structured formats — describing one image per copper layer, a list of hole positions and diameters, the solder mask openings, the printed legend, and the board outline. A separate stackup drawing specifies which cores and prepregs go in which order and how thick each dielectric must be. That drawing is not a formality; it is the document that decides whether the finished traces hit their target impedance.

## Printing the Copper

The copper pattern is made by subtraction. The panel starts fully covered and the unwanted copper is dissolved away.

A photosensitive dry film is laminated over the foil under heat and rollers. It is then exposed, either through a photoplotted film held in contact with the panel or, in modern lines, by a laser writing the image directly from the data with no intermediate artwork at all. Exposure cross-links the resist where light hits it. A mild alkaline developer washes away the unexposed regions, leaving bare copper wherever the design wants no metal and hardened polymer wherever it wants a trace.

The panel then goes into an etchant — typically cupric chloride or an ammoniacal solution — sprayed at it from both sides. The chemistry attacks copper and ignores the resist. It also, inconveniently, attacks sideways as well as down, so a trace comes out narrower at the top than at the bottom and slightly narrower overall than drawn. Fabricators compensate by drawing the artwork a little wide, and this undercut is one of the reasons fine features are difficult: the thicker the copper, the longer the etch, and the more sideways attack there is to correct for. Stripping the resist leaves a finished inner layer, which is then scanned by an optical inspection machine that compares the actual copper against the design file and flags anything that looks like a nick, a short, or a narrowed trace.

## Pressing, Drilling, and Making Holes Conduct

Inner layers are etched before the board exists as a solid object. Once they pass inspection, their copper is chemically treated to roughen and oxidize the surface so the resin will grip it, and the stack is built up: core, prepreg, core, prepreg, and outer sheets of plain copper foil, aligned on tooling pins. The whole assembly goes into a press under vacuum, where heat softens the prepreg, pressure squeezes it into every gap, and the resin cures into one monolithic panel. Layer-to-layer alignment is decided at this moment and cannot be fixed afterwards, and since the material shrinks slightly during cure, fabricators scale artwork to anticipate it.

Holes come next. A CNC drill with carbide bits spinning at tens of thousands of revolutions per minute works through stacked panels with a sacrificial entry sheet on top and backing board beneath. Bits are retired on a hit count rather than on inspection, because a dull bit smears more than it cuts. Drilling melts resin, which redeposits along the hole wall and can insulate the very inner-layer copper the hole is supposed to connect, so the panel is desmeared with permanganate chemistry or plasma before anything else happens.

Now comes the step that makes multilayer boards possible at all. A drilled hole is a tube of glass and cured epoxy — an insulator. To connect layers, that wall must be metallized. A thin film of copper is deposited chemically, by an electroless bath or a direct-metallization process, just conductive enough to serve as a seed. The panel then goes into an electroplating tank where copper is built up to working thickness on the hole walls and the surface alike. Through-hole plating is the quiet hinge on which the whole industry turns: without it, a board is limited to two layers and whatever wire links a designer is willing to solder by hand.

Outer layers are patterned after plating, and the usual sequence inverts the inner-layer logic. Resist is imaged so that the traces and pads are the exposed areas, copper is plated up in those regions, and a thin layer of tin is plated on top of it. The resist is stripped, the now-unprotected background copper is etched away while the tin protects the pattern, and finally the tin is chemically removed. The result is a board whose outer traces and plated holes were built up together in the same bath.

## Mask, Finish, Test, and Cut

Bare copper oxidizes within days and will not solder reliably, so two coatings follow. Solder mask — a liquid photoimageable epoxy, coated over the whole panel, exposed, developed, and thermally cured — covers everything except the pads. It prevents solder bridging between adjacent pins during assembly and protects the copper underneath for the life of the product. Green is conventional largely out of inertia and because it gives good contrast for inspection, but the chemistry does not care. The printed legend, the white outlines and reference designators, goes on afterwards as ink or inkjet.

The exposed pads still need a solderable surface. Hot air solder levelling dips the board in molten alloy and blows off the excess, which is cheap but leaves a slightly domed pad. Electroless nickel with immersion gold gives a flat, durable, gold-coloured finish suited to fine-pitch parts. Immersion silver, immersion tin, and organic solderability preservatives each trade cost against shelf life and flatness.

Every board is then tested electrically against its netlist, either by a bed-of-nails fixture built for that specific design or by flying probes that walk across the board making measurements point by point. The test looks for two failures: connections that should exist and do not, and isolation that should exist and does not. Finally the individual boards are cut from the panel, by routing around the outline with small tabs left to hold them in place or by V-scoring straight lines that snap cleanly later.

## Why the Stackup Is a Circuit

A trace on the outside of a board with a solid copper plane beneath it forms a transmission line whose characteristic impedance depends on the trace width, the distance to that plane, the copper thickness, and the dielectric constant of the material between. Fabricators adjust the first two to hit a target, commonly fifty ohms for a single-ended signal or around a hundred for a differential pair, and will often etch and measure a test coupon on the panel edge to prove it.

The part that catches people out is the return path. Current that goes out along a trace comes back, and at high frequency it comes back in the plane directly beneath that trace, hugging it as closely as the geometry allows because that is the route of lowest inductance. Cut a slot in the plane — for a connector, for a row of through-holes, for an isolation boundary — and the return current has to detour around it. The loop area grows, the signal degrades, and the board radiates. Plenty of boards that fail emissions testing fail for that reason and no other.

Assembly is a separate business from fabrication, usually a separate company. A laser-cut stainless steel stencil lays solder paste on the pads, a placement machine sets components down at thousands of parts per hour, and the board travels through a reflow oven whose temperature profile is tuned to activate the flux, bring the whole assembly up evenly, hold it briefly above the alloy's melting point, and cool it without shocking anything. Surface tension does something pleasing in that moment: parts that were placed slightly off-centre pull themselves into alignment as the solder wets their terminations. Ball grid arrays, whose joints are hidden under the package, are inspected afterwards with X-rays, because nobody can see them any other way.
