# How Graphics Chips Took Over General Computing

A single frame of a game at 1080p contains roughly two million pixels, and every one of them needs a color. At sixty frames per second that is on the order of a hundred and twenty million color decisions every second, and for the overwhelming majority of them the work is the same short sequence of arithmetic applied to different inputs: where this bit of surface sits in space, which way it faces, what the lights are doing, what the texture says. The decisive detail is that almost none of those two million answers depends on any of the others. The pixel at the top of the screen does not have to wait for the pixel below it to finish.

That property — independence — is why the graphics processor became a separate species of machine. It is also, three decades later, why the same silicon ended up running fluid simulations, seismic imaging, molecular dynamics, and enormous piles of matrix arithmetic that have nothing whatsoever to do with drawing a picture.

## Two Different Bets About Where the Transistors Go

A general-purpose CPU is built on the assumption that the work arriving at it is a single sequential stream of instructions with unpredictable branches and unpredictable memory access, and that the only way to go faster is to get through that one stream more quickly. So a CPU core spends an extraordinary share of its transistor budget on machinery that computes nothing: branch predictors guessing which way an `if` will go before the condition is known, out-of-order engines reordering instructions to keep the arithmetic units fed, register renaming to break false dependencies, speculative execution, and a deep hierarchy of caches whose only job is to hide the fact that main memory takes hundreds of cycles to answer. The arithmetic logic itself — the part that adds and multiplies — is a small island in the middle of all that control overhead.

A graphics chip makes the opposite bet. If you have millions of independent work items queued up, you do not need to guess which branch comes next, because you can simply run something else while you wait. You do not need a giant cache to hide memory latency, because you can hide it with sheer oversubscription: keep thousands of work items resident, and when one stalls on a memory fetch, switch to another in a single cycle. What you need instead is arithmetic units, lots of them, and enough memory bandwidth to keep them supplied. Strip out the speculation machinery, make each core simple and in-order, and spend the recovered area on more cores.

The consequence is a chip that is miserable at running one thing quickly and superb at running ten thousand things at once. Early 3D accelerators of the mid-1990s made that trade with fixed-function hardware — dedicated circuits for perspective-correct texture mapping and depth testing, with no programmability at all. The card did exactly one pipeline and did it fast. By the turn of the century the pipeline had grown a hardware transform-and-lighting stage, and marketing began calling the result a GPU rather than an accelerator.

## From Fixed Pipeline to Programmable Cores

The change that mattered came when the fixed stages became programmable. First vertex shaders, then pixel shaders: small programs, initially just a few dozen instructions with no loops and no branching, that the developer supplied and the hardware ran once per vertex or once per fragment. Artists got effects that fixed-function hardware could never have anticipated. More quietly, the chip acquired an instruction set.

Then came unification. Separate vertex and pixel units meant that a scene heavy on geometry left the pixel hardware idle, and a scene heavy on overdraw left the vertex hardware idle, and either way a large fraction of the die was doing nothing. The fix, arriving in hardware in the middle of the 2000s, was to build one pool of general arithmetic cores and schedule them onto whichever stage needed capacity at that instant. A unified shader architecture is, structurally, a many-core parallel processor with a rasterizer bolted to the side.

People noticed. Before there was any sanctioned way to do it, researchers were coaxing scientific computation out of graphics cards by lying to them — packing matrices into texture memory, expressing an operation as the act of drawing a rectangle that covered the screen, and reading the answer back out of the framebuffer. It worked, barely. Precision was poor, there was no way to write to an arbitrary memory address, and debugging meant looking at pictures of your data. But the throughput was so much better than a CPU's that people kept doing it.

The vendors eventually met them halfway with C-like languages and runtimes that exposed the hardware directly, dropping the pretense that everything was a triangle. Once a programmer could allocate memory, launch a grid of threads, and write to arbitrary addresses, the graphics heritage became mostly a historical detail.

## The Execution Model, and Where It Bites

Programming one of these chips means writing code from the point of view of one thread and then launching a few hundred thousand copies of it. But the threads are not independent down at the hardware level. They are bundled into fixed-size groups — thirty-two on one vendor's hardware, sixty-four on another's, historically — and every thread in a group shares a single instruction pointer. The whole group executes the same instruction in the same cycle on different data.

This is where the abstraction leaks, and it leaks in two specific places.

The first is branch divergence. If some threads in a group take the `if` and others take the `else`, the hardware cannot run both paths at once. It runs one path with the non-participating threads masked off and idle, then runs the other path with the mask inverted. A branchy algorithm where neighboring work items make different decisions can lose most of its throughput to threads sitting out. Restructuring the data so that threads which travel together make the same decisions is one of the main jobs of anyone tuning this kind of code.

The second is memory access. The memory system is built to service a whole group at once, fetching wide contiguous chunks. When the threads in a group read consecutive addresses, one transaction feeds all of them. When they read scattered addresses, the hardware issues a separate transaction per thread and effective bandwidth collapses by an order of magnitude. Pointer-chasing data structures — linked lists, trees, graphs with irregular connectivity — are exactly the shape this hardware is worst at, which is why plenty of workloads that look parallel on paper never actually get faster.

## Why the Whole Industry Bent This Direction

None of this would have escaped graphics if single-threaded CPU performance had kept doubling. It stopped, and for a physical reason. For decades, shrinking a transistor let you also reduce its operating voltage, so that power density stayed roughly constant as transistors got smaller and faster. Around the middle of the 2000s the voltage could no longer be scaled down alongside the dimensions — leakage current became unmanageable below a certain threshold — and power density started climbing. Clock speeds flattened near a few gigahertz and have stayed there.

After that, the only remaining lever was doing more per cycle, which means parallelism. CPUs grew multiple cores and wider vector units. GPUs, which had been organized around throughput per watt from the beginning, suddenly looked less like a peripheral and more like the general answer.

The pressure then moved to memory. Arithmetic throughput has grown far faster than the bandwidth available to feed it, so the useful question about any algorithm became its arithmetic intensity: how many operations it performs per byte moved from memory. Algorithms with low intensity — adding two large arrays, say — run at the speed of the memory bus no matter how many arithmetic units the chip has. Dense matrix multiplication is the opposite case and the reason it became the workload everything else gets reshaped into: it does work proportional to the cube of the dimension on data proportional to the square, so a tile loaded into fast on-chip memory can be reused many times before it is evicted. Hardware responded in kind, first with stacked memory sitting on a silicon interposer next to the die to widen the bus enormously, and then with dedicated matrix units that consume small blocks of numbers and emit a multiply-accumulate result in one instruction.

What has not gone away is the limit Gene Amdahl pointed out long before any of this hardware existed. If a tenth of a program is inherently sequential, then perfect infinite parallelization of the other nine-tenths still leaves you no better than ten times faster. Real speedups therefore tend to come not from porting an existing program but from choosing a different algorithm — one that may do more total arithmetic, and would look wasteful on a CPU, but does it in a shape the hardware can swallow whole.

The name never caught up with any of this. A rack of these chips in a datacenter, moving no pixels and attached to no display, is still called a graphics card, for the same reason a car's engine output is still measured against the pulling power of a horse.
