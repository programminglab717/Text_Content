# How Analog-to-Digital Conversion Works

A microphone's output is a line with no gaps in it. The diaphragm moves, the voltage follows, and between any two instants there are infinitely many more instants, each with a value of its own. A number is not like that. A number is a single value, held, with nothing between it and the next one along. Every difficulty in analog-to-digital conversion comes from that mismatch, and the converter's whole job is to throw away most of a continuous signal in a way that leaves the part you wanted intact.

The discarding happens along two axes, and it helps to keep them separate in your head. Sampling chops the signal in time: instead of a continuous curve, you keep the value at regular instants and nothing in between. Quantization chops it in amplitude: instead of a value that can be anything, you round to the nearest rung of a finite ladder. Each operation has its own failure mode, its own theory, and its own countermeasures, and confusing them is the source of a great deal of muddled thinking about digital audio and instrumentation alike.

## Everything Above Half the Sample Rate Becomes a Lie

Sampling theory has a hard boundary and a soft one. The hard boundary is the Nyquist rate: a signal containing no frequency at or above half the sampling rate can be reconstructed exactly from its samples. Not approximately — exactly. The soft part is that real signals never obey the premise without help.

What happens to a frequency above that limit is not that it gets lost. It gets disguised. A sine wave at slightly more than half the sample rate produces exactly the same sequence of samples as a lower-frequency sine wave, and once those samples exist there is no test that can tell the two apart. The high frequency has folded down into the band you care about, and it now sits there as a false signal indistinguishable from a real one. This is aliasing, and its visible cousin is the wagon wheel that appears to roll backwards in a film, sampled at twenty-four frames per second while its spokes move faster than that rate can follow.

The only cure is to remove the offending content before the sampler sees it, which means an analog filter, in hardware, ahead of the converter. Real filters do not have brick walls; they roll off over some span of frequency. If you want a clean signal up to twenty kilohertz and you sample at forty-four, the filter has to fall from full transmission to negligible transmission within a couple of kilohertz, which takes a steep, high-order design with unpleasant phase behaviour near the corner. Sample at a much higher rate instead and the filter only needs to be gentle, because there is a wide empty gap between the top of the signal band and the fold-over point. The steep filtering can then be done digitally, where it costs arithmetic rather than precision analog components. This is why oversampling became standard practice, and why the anti-alias filter on a modern converter chip is often nearly trivial.

## The Error You Can Hear

Quantization is the second cut. An N-bit converter has two-to-the-N available codes, and every input is assigned to the nearest one, which means every sample carries an error of up to half a step. The size of that step is the full-scale range divided by the number of codes, and it sets the noise floor of the whole system. The convenient rule is that each additional bit buys about six decibels of dynamic range, so twelve bits gets you somewhere in the seventies and sixteen bits approaches a hundred.

Treating quantization error as noise is an approximation, and where it breaks down is instructive. For a busy, rapidly varying signal the errors are effectively random and the approximation holds. For a very quiet or very slowly moving signal the error becomes correlated with the signal itself, and correlated error is not noise — it is distortion, which the ear detects far more readily than hiss. The standard fix is counterintuitive: add a small amount of deliberate random noise, roughly one step in amplitude, before quantizing. This dither decorrelates the error, converting a grating distortion into a smooth background hiss, and in doing so it lets information about signals smaller than a single step survive in the statistics of the samples. Deliberately degrading the signal-to-noise ratio makes the result sound better, which is one of the more satisfying results in signal processing.

There is also a timing error hiding inside the amplitude domain. A converter does not grab the input instantaneously; it closes a switch onto a capacitor, holds the value, and measures it. If the instant of that hold wobbles from sample to sample — aperture jitter — the captured voltage is wrong by however much the signal moved during the wobble. Jitter therefore matters more for fast-moving signals than slow ones, and a converter with excellent static accuracy can still perform poorly at high frequencies if its clock is dirty.

## Four Ways to Guess a Voltage

The architectures in common use are fewer than the catalogue suggests, and each is a different bargain between speed, resolution, power, and area.

A flash converter compares the input against every possible threshold at once, using a resistor ladder to generate the reference levels and a bank of comparators — one fewer than the number of codes — to decide where the input falls. One clock cycle produces a result, which makes it the fastest thing available. It is also the most expensive: each extra bit doubles the comparator count, so flash converters are almost always confined to six or eight bits, used where speed dwarfs every other consideration.

A successive approximation converter runs a binary search instead. It guesses the midpoint, compares, keeps or discards that bit, then halves the remaining range and repeats, taking one clock per bit. Inside, the guessing is usually done by a network of binary-weighted capacitors that both samples the input and generates the trial voltages. It is compact, remarkably power-efficient, and accurate enough at twelve to sixteen bits that it is the converter built into practically every microcontroller.

A pipelined converter splits the difference. Each stage resolves a few bits coarsely, subtracts its own estimate from the input, amplifies what is left, and passes that residue to the next stage. Stages work on different samples simultaneously, so a result emerges every clock cycle even though any individual sample takes several cycles to travel through. The output is delayed but the throughput is high, which suits video, radar, and radio receivers. Deliberate redundancy between stages lets later digital logic correct small errors made earlier.

A delta-sigma converter abandons the idea of measuring each sample precisely. It samples enormously faster than needed — often hundreds of times faster — with a very coarse quantizer, sometimes a single bit, wrapped in a feedback loop containing integrators. The loop cannot make the quantizer accurate, but it can push the quantization noise out of the frequency band of interest and up into the high frequencies, where a digital filter removes it along with the excess sample rate. The result is extraordinary resolution over a limited bandwidth, purchased with digital arithmetic rather than precision analog parts. This is what converts audio and what sits inside precision weighing and temperature instruments.

A fifth type survives in bench multimeters: the dual-slope integrator, which charges a capacitor from the unknown input for a fixed time, then discharges it with a known reference and counts how long that takes. It is slow and largely immune to component drift, since the same capacitor and clock appear on both sides of the ratio. Its other virtue is deliberate: integrating over an exact whole number of mains cycles causes hum picked up from the power line to average to zero, which is why a good multimeter reads steadily in an electrically noisy room.

## The Reference Is the Ruler

A converter does not measure volts. It measures a ratio between its input and its reference, and reports that ratio as a number. Everything about the accuracy of the result therefore rests on the reference. Feed a sixteen-bit converter from a switching regulator rail that wanders by a tenth of a percent with load and you have thrown away several bits before the first conversion, no matter how good the silicon is. The counter-trick is to exploit the ratio rather than fight it: power a resistive sensor from the same reference that the converter uses, and a drift in that reference moves the input and the ruler together, cancelling out.

The other habitual trap is source impedance. A successive approximation converter has to charge its internal sampling capacitor from your circuit in the brief acquisition window before conversion begins. If the source cannot deliver that charge quickly — a high-value potential divider, a sensor with significant output resistance — the capacitor never fully settles and every reading comes back low by an amount that depends on what was measured previously. The symptom is maddening, because the converter itself is behaving perfectly. The fixes are to buffer the input with an amplifier, lengthen the acquisition time, or lower the source impedance.

Datasheets quantify the remaining imperfections with a vocabulary worth knowing. Differential nonlinearity describes how uneven the steps are, and a large enough error produces a missing code that the converter can never output. Integral nonlinearity describes how far the overall transfer curve bends away from a straight line. Effective number of bits states, honestly, how many of the advertised bits are actually above the noise. That last figure is the one to read first, because a converter sold as eighteen-bit that delivers fourteen usable ones is common and entirely legal.

What all of this adds up to is that conversion is the irreversible step. Filtering, averaging, and every clever thing done downstream operate on the numbers, and the numbers are all that survives. An alias folded into the band cannot be unfolded, a transient missed between samples was never recorded, and a bit buried under reference noise does not come back. The care belongs at the front.
