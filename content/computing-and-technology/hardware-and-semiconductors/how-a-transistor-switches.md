# How a Transistor Switches

A mechanical light switch works by brute force. Flip it, and a metal contact physically moves through the air until it touches another metal contact, closing the circuit. You can hear it happen. There is friction, wear, and eventually failure, because anything with moving parts eventually breaks. A modern processor contains billions of switches doing something functionally identical — closing and opening circuits, millions of times a second, for years on end — and yet nothing inside them moves in the way a light switch moves. The device that makes this possible is the transistor, and understanding how it flips between on and off without any mechanical motion is close to understanding what a computer actually is underneath all its software.

## A Material That Can Be Persuaded

Silicon sits in an odd position on the periodic table. It is not a good conductor like copper, where electrons wander freely between atoms, and it is not a good insulator like glass, where electrons are locked firmly in place. In its pure crystalline form, each silicon atom shares its four outer electrons with four neighbors, forming a rigid lattice with no free charge carriers to speak of. Left alone, silicon barely conducts electricity at all.

What makes silicon useful is that this behavior can be deliberately broken. Chipmakers introduce trace amounts of other elements into the crystal, a process called doping, and the choice of dopant determines what kind of material results. Add phosphorus or arsenic, atoms with five outer electrons instead of four, and each dopant atom contributes one electron that has no bonding partner and is free to move. This is n-type silicon, named for the negative charge of its extra carriers. Add boron instead, with only three outer electrons, and each dopant atom leaves behind a vacancy, an absence of an electron that behaves for all practical purposes like a positive charge carrier, or hole, that can hop from atom to atom. This is p-type silicon.

Neither doped material is especially interesting by itself. The interesting part happens at the boundary between them.

## The Structure of a Switch

A transistor, in the form most common inside logic chips, is built around exactly this kind of boundary, arranged into three regions: a source, a drain, and a channel between them, with a gate perched above the channel and separated from it by an extremely thin layer of insulating oxide. In the classic arrangement, the source and drain are small islands of n-type silicon embedded in a larger body of p-type substrate. Under normal conditions, current cannot flow from source to drain, because doing so would require crossing the p-type region in between, and the junctions at each end block that path the way two batteries wired in opposition would.

The gate is where the switching happens, and it does its work without ever touching the channel directly. Apply a sufficient positive voltage to the gate, and its electric field reaches down through the thin oxide layer and begins to repel the positive holes in the p-type substrate just beneath it, while attracting the sparse free electrons that exist there. Push hard enough, past a threshold voltage, and enough electrons accumulate at the surface to invert it, turning a thin sliver of what was p-type material into something that behaves like n-type material. That inverted sliver forms a bridge, a channel, connecting source to drain. Current can now flow. Remove the voltage, and the channel dissolves, the bridge collapses, and the path closes again.

This is the entire trick: a voltage on one terminal, applied through an insulator so no current actually needs to flow into the gate itself, controls whether current can flow between two other terminals. No arm swings, no contact clicks shut. A field either exists or it doesn't, and the material underneath either conducts or it doesn't, purely as a matter of electron behavior in a crystal lattice.

## What the Switch Is Actually Doing

Calling the transistor a switch is useful shorthand but slightly incomplete, because the same device, biased differently, works as an amplifier, letting a small change in gate voltage produce a larger change in current between source and drain. That amplifying property is what made transistors revolutionary in radios and audio equipment decades before anyone was building computers out of them. Digital logic asks the transistor to do something narrower: to sit reliably in one of two states, hard on or hard off, with as little time as possible spent in the ambiguous region between. Engineers achieve this by driving the gate voltage well past the threshold in one direction or the other, so that manufacturing variation and electrical noise are unlikely to flip a transistor into the wrong state by accident.

Pair two transistors of complementary types — one built the way described above, called NMOS, and one built as its mirror image using p-type source and drain in an n-type body, called PMOS — and something convenient happens. Wire them so that a single input voltage drives both gates at once, with the NMOS transistor connecting the output to ground and the PMOS transistor connecting the output to the supply voltage, and exactly one of the pair conducts at any given time. This complementary arrangement, CMOS, is the basis of essentially all modern digital logic. It has a quietly important property: current flows through the pair only for the brief instant during a switch, while the circuit is transitioning between states. At rest, in either the on state or the off state, almost no current flows at all. A chip full of CMOS transistors doing nothing draws relatively little power; a chip full of them switching billions of times a second draws quite a lot, which is exactly what shows up as heat that needs to be carried away.

## From One Junction to Everything Else

Wire enough of these paired switches together in the right topology and you get a logic gate — an inverter, a NAND, a NOR — the small circuits that output a single bit of true or false based on their inputs. Wire enough logic gates together and you get an adder, a multiplexer, a register. Wire enough of those together, clocked in careful synchrony, and you get a processor capable of executing instructions. Every layer of abstraction that software developers work in, from machine code up through operating systems and applications, ultimately bottoms out in this specific physical fact: a voltage on a gate, separated from a channel by a sliver of oxide only a few atoms thick, deciding whether electrons can cross from one side of a silicon crystal to the other.

The relentless miniaturization of the semiconductor industry has mostly been a decades-long argument with the physical limits of this mechanism. Make the oxide layer thinner to keep the gate's electric field strong relative to the shrinking channel length, and eventually electrons begin tunneling straight through it regardless of voltage, leaking current even when the transistor is supposed to be off. Make the channel shorter to fit more transistors per chip, and the source and drain regions start interfering with each other's electric fields in ways that undermine the gate's control. The industry's responses — new insulating materials with better electrical properties than plain silicon dioxide, channels wrapped on multiple sides by the gate instead of just one, structures raised vertically off the wafer surface — are all, at bottom, attempts to keep this same one-hundred-year-old idea of a voltage-controlled valve working at dimensions where the old assumptions about how electrons behave start to break down. The valve itself has not changed conceptually since it was first built. What has changed is how cleverly its geometry has to be arranged to keep behaving like a clean, dependable, binary choice between on and off.
