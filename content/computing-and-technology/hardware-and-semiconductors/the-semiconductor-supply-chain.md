# The Semiconductor Supply Chain, Link by Link

Pry the heat spreader off a modern processor and you find a slab of silicon smaller than a thumbnail sitting on a green fiberglass substrate. Nothing about the object suggests its history. It is one of the most widely travelled manufactured items in existence: the design was written in one country, the silicon grown in another, patterned in a third, sliced and packaged in a fourth, and tested in a fifth, using machines built on three continents from subassemblies that themselves crossed borders several times. The finished chip may have flown around the world twice before it was ever soldered to a board.

The phrase "supply chain" suggests a line of suppliers, each of whom could be swapped for another if the price were right. Semiconductors are not like that. The chain is better understood as a sequence of chokepoints, most of them occupied by one to three firms globally, each sustained by decades of accumulated process knowledge that is not written down anywhere in transferable form.

## Sand Is the Easy Part

It begins, as every explanation of this industry insists, with sand — specifically quartzite, reduced in an electric arc furnace to metallurgical-grade silicon of about ninety-nine percent purity. That grade is fine for aluminum alloys and useless for electronics. To get to semiconductor grade, the silicon is converted into a gas, distilled the way a refinery distils crude, and deposited back out as a solid. What emerges is polysilicon pure to roughly one foreign atom in a billion, which is a level of purity with very few industrial analogues. A handful of plants worldwide, concentrated in the United States, Germany, Japan, and China, make the material that meets electronic specifications.

Polysilicon is then melted and turned into a single crystal. A seed crystal is dipped into the melt and drawn slowly upward while rotating, and the silicon freezes onto it atom by atom, continuing the seed's lattice orientation without interruption for a metre or more. The result is a cylindrical ingot weighing a couple of hundred kilograms in which, ideally, every atom sits on the same crystal grid. Getting the diameter uniform, the dopant concentration even along the length, and the defect count near zero is an exercise in extremely patient thermal control. The ingot is then ground round, sawn into wafers with a wire saw, lapped, etched, and polished to a flatness measured in nanometres across three hundred millimetres of diameter. Wafer manufacturing is dominated by two Japanese companies plus a small cluster of German, Korean, and Taiwanese producers. The industry tried and failed to move from 300 mm wafers to 450 mm; the tooling cost could not be justified, so the standard has now sat still for a long time.

## The Fab, and the Machine at Its Centre

A leading-edge fabrication plant costs tens of billions of dollars, takes years to build, and is obsolete in the sense that matters within about five. Inside, the wafer makes hundreds of trips — a thousand or more distinct process steps at the leading edge — between the same few classes of machine, building the chip upward in layers. Deposition tools lay down films a few atoms thick. Etch tools remove material in precisely shaped voids. Implanters fire dopant ions into the crystal at controlled depths. Chemical-mechanical polishing tools grind the surface flat again so the next layer has something level to build on. Metrology and inspection tools look for defects at every stage, because finding a problem forty steps after it happened means scrapping forty steps of work on every wafer in flight.

Each of those tool categories is an oligopoly. Deposition and etch are split largely among a few American and Japanese firms; inspection and metrology are dominated by one American company; and lithography — the step that defines the pattern — has one supplier for the leading edge and no alternative.

Extreme ultraviolet lithography deserves its reputation as the most improbable machine in routine industrial use. Ordinary light cannot print features this small, so the machine uses light at a wavelength of 13.5 nanometres, which is absorbed by essentially all matter, including air and glass. There are no lenses; the optics are mirrors coated with dozens of alternating molybdenum and silicon layers, figured to a precision such that if one were scaled to the size of a country, the largest bump would be a few millimetres high. The light itself is made by firing droplets of molten tin through a vacuum chamber at a rate of tens of thousands per second and hitting each one twice with a high-power carbon dioxide laser — once to flatten it into a pancake, once to blast it into a plasma hot enough to emit at the right wavelength. The whole apparatus weighs as much as a large truck, costs as much as a wide-body aircraft, ships in many crates, and is assembled on site by the vendor's engineers. One Dutch company makes them, using optics from one German supplier and a light source from a subsidiary acquired in the United States.

## Chemicals, Gases, and the Things Nobody Thinks About

Running underneath all of this is a layer of consumables where the concentration is, if anything, worse. Photoresist — the light-sensitive polymer that actually records the pattern — is made to formulations guarded as closely as anything in the industry, mostly by Japanese chemical companies. Ultra-high-purity hydrogen peroxide, specialty etchants, and the slurries used in polishing all come from similarly short lists. Rare gases matter more than their obscurity suggests: neon, used in the excimer lasers of older-generation lithography tools, was for years supplied largely as a byproduct of steelmaking in a small number of plants, and disruption there rippled straight through to fab operations.

Even the humble parts are constrained. Quartz crucibles for crystal growing, ceramic components for plasma chambers, particular grades of valve and fitting, the pods that carry wafers between tools — each has a qualified supplier list that took years to establish and cannot be expanded quickly, because in this industry a component is not qualified because it meets a specification but because it has been demonstrated not to change yield.

## Where the Design Comes From, and Where the Chip Goes After

Silicon is only half the chain. A chip begins as a design expressed in a hardware description language, and turning millions of lines of that into a physical layout requires electronic design automation software — simulation, synthesis, place-and-route, timing and power analysis, verification. Three companies supply nearly all of it. Most designs also license pre-built blocks rather than building everything from scratch: processor cores, memory controllers, interface protocols, all bought as intellectual property from vendors whose entire business is licensing designs they never manufacture.

That split is the defining structural fact of the modern industry. For decades a semiconductor company owned its fabs. Then the rising cost of each new generation made that untenable for all but a few, and the business separated into fabless design houses and pure-play foundries that manufacture to order and sell no products of their own. The arrangement made chip design accessible to companies that could never have financed a fab, and simultaneously concentrated leading-edge manufacturing capacity into a very small number of sites in a very small number of places.

When wafers leave the fab they are not finished. They go to assembly and test — historically the low-value end of the chain, subcontracted to specialist firms in Taiwan, Malaysia, the Philippines, and China. Wafers are thinned, diced into individual chips, mounted on substrates that are themselves sophisticated multilayer boards from another short supplier list, wired or bumped, encapsulated, and tested. That end of the chain has stopped being an afterthought. As transistor scaling has slowed, more performance is being extracted from packaging: stacking memory directly on logic, connecting multiple small dies side by side on a silicon interposer so they behave as one large chip, bonding wafers face to face. Advanced packaging capacity is now itself a bottleneck, and the ability to build it is not widely distributed.

## Why It Cannot Simply Be Rebuilt Elsewhere

The obvious response to all this concentration is to duplicate it somewhere safer, and several governments are spending heavily to try. The obstacle is not the equipment, which can be bought, or the buildings, which can be built. It is yield.

A new fab starts out throwing away most of what it makes. The path from there to profitability runs through thousands of small corrections — a tweak to a bake temperature, a change in how a tool is cleaned, a revised handling procedure that eliminates a source of particles — discovered empirically by engineers watching the same process day after day. That knowledge lives in the people and the procedures, not in the blueprints, and it does not transfer by document. It is why a second fab built to identical specifications in a different country typically takes years to match the original's output, and why the firms at the leading edge have stayed there: the learning compounds, and the people who did the learning stay in the building.
