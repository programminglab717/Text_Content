# How Electronics Shed Heat

Hold a phone against your palm after twenty minutes of turn-by-turn navigation and you are feeling the output of its processor in the most literal sense. Almost every joule of electrical energy that enters a piece of consumer electronics leaves it again as heat. A speaker converts a sliver into sound, a display throws some out as light, an antenna radiates a little away, but the accounting is otherwise brutal in its simplicity: watts go in, watts come out, and the exit is thermal. A chip does not consume energy so much as degrade it, taking clean electrical power at one end and producing warm air at the other.

That would be a curiosity rather than a problem if silicon did not care about its own temperature. It cares a great deal. Leakage current rises with heat, so a hot transistor wastes more power doing nothing, which makes it hotter still — a feedback loop with the wrong sign. Electromigration, the slow drift of metal atoms in the direction of current flow, accelerates with temperature and eventually opens a connection that was never meant to open. Electrolytic capacitors dry out; the old rule of thumb is that their working life roughly halves for every ten degrees Celsius of additional operating temperature. Solder joints crack from thermal cycling, because the board, the package, and the solder between them all expand at different rates every time the device warms and cools. Heat is not a side effect to be tolerated afterwards. It is usually the thing that eventually kills the product.

## Temperature Is a Voltage, Heat Is a Current

Thermal engineering borrows Ohm's law almost wholesale, and the loan works well enough that most of the field is conducted in its vocabulary. A temperature difference takes the role of voltage. Heat flow, in watts, takes the role of current. The obstruction between them is thermal resistance, measured in degrees Celsius per watt, and resistances along a path add in series exactly as electrical ones do.

The temperature of the silicon above ambient is then just the power dissipated multiplied by the total resistance from junction to air. A processor burning sixty-five watts through a path with a total resistance of half a degree per watt will sit around thirty-three degrees above whatever air the case is breathing. If that air is already at forty degrees because it has been preheated by a graphics card upstream, the junction is at seventy-three and the headroom is thinner than it looks. Every design decision — the size of the heatsink, the thickness of the grease, the direction of airflow — is an argument about one term in that sum.

The path is conventionally split into three pieces. Junction to case is fixed by the chip's packaging and is the part a system designer cannot touch: it covers the route from the active silicon, through the die, through whatever solder or grease sits beneath the integrated heat spreader, to the metal lid. Case to sink is the interface the assembler controls. Sink to ambient is the heatsink and the air moving over it, and it is almost always the largest of the three, which is why it absorbs almost all the engineering attention.

## The Problem of Touching

Two machined metal surfaces pressed together look like they are in contact. They are not. Even a lapped, flat heatsink base meets a processor lid at a scattering of high points, and the true contact area is a small fraction of the apparent one. Everything in between is air, which is among the worst thermal conductors available outside a vacuum. Bolting a beautiful copper heatsink directly onto a bare chip produces disappointing numbers for exactly this reason.

Thermal interface material exists to fill those voids. Silicone grease loaded with zinc oxide, alumina, or boron nitride; soft polymer pads that conform under clamping pressure; phase-change films that soften at operating temperature and flow into the gaps; and, at the aggressive end, liquid metal alloys that wet both surfaces outright. None of these conducts heat anything like as well as solid copper. All of them conduct far better than trapped air. The practical consequence is that the correct quantity of paste is the least that still covers the surface: the material is there to displace air, not to be a layer in its own right, and a thick application makes matters worse rather than better.

## Spreading, Then Dumping

Heat leaves a modern chip from an area smaller than a fingernail, and no fin array can breathe through an opening that size. Cooling therefore happens in two stages that are worth thinking about separately: spread the heat over a large area, then hand it to the air.

Spreading is a conduction problem, solved with copper, aluminium, graphite sheet, and increasingly with phase change. A heat pipe is a sealed copper tube holding a wick and a small charge of working fluid, usually water, at a pressure low enough that it boils near room temperature. Heat at one end evaporates the fluid; vapour rushes to the cooler end, condenses, and releases the latent heat it absorbed; capillary action in the wick draws the liquid back. Nothing pumps it, and nothing about it needs power. The effective conductivity of the assembly runs an order of magnitude or more beyond solid copper, which is why laptops and graphics cards are full of flattened copper tubes running from the die to a fin stack somewhere with room for it. A vapour chamber is the same idea made planar, a flat sealed cavity moving heat sideways across a whole package.

Dumping it into the air is a surface-area problem with a catch. Every fin adds area, but fins packed too closely choke the flow between them, and a stagnant boundary layer of warm air clinging to each surface insulates the metal from the bulk stream. Fin spacing is therefore a negotiation with whatever is pushing the air: a low-pressure case fan wants wide, open fins, while a blower that can develop real static pressure will drive air through a dense stack and pull far more out of the same volume. Fin efficiency falls off as well, since heat must conduct along the fin before it can convect away, and the tip of a tall, thin fin runs barely warmer than the air around it.

Fans themselves are described by a curve rather than a number: airflow plotted against static pressure, running from maximum flow with no obstruction down to maximum pressure with no flow at all. The heatsink presents an opposing curve. Where the two cross is the operating point, and it sits well away from either optimistic endpoint printed on the box. Noise scales viciously with blade tip speed, which is why a large slow fan is nearly always quieter than a small fast one moving the same volume of air, and why thin laptops sound the way they do under load.

## What the Rating on the Box Means

Thermal design power is one of the more widely misread numbers in computing. It is not a measurement of how much power a chip draws, and it is not a maximum. It is a specification handed to whoever designs the cooling: build a solution capable of removing this many watts continuously while holding the case below the specified temperature, and the part will behave. A processor can exceed its rated figure for short bursts by design, and can sit far below it when idle. Two chips with identical ratings can have quite different peak draws and quite different sustained behaviour.

Verifying any of this means measuring it. Thermocouples epoxied to package lids and board hotspots give point readings; an infrared camera shows the whole field at once, though it lies convincingly about shiny metal unless the surface is painted or taped to a known emissivity. Most of the real work now happens in simulation before hardware exists, with computational fluid dynamics models predicting where the air will actually go — which, in a crowded chassis, is frequently not where the vents suggest it should.

## When There Is No Room

Phones and tablets have none of this apparatus. There is no fan, no fin stack, and no path to ambient except the skin of the device, which cannot be allowed much past forty-five degrees because people hold it. The entire cooling system is a graphite sheet or a thin vapour chamber spreading heat away from the processor so that no single point on the glass becomes intolerable, plus the thermal mass of the chassis, which buys time.

Time is the real design variable in these products. A phone processor can run at full clock for a few tens of seconds while the aluminium and glass around it soak up the energy, then must fall back to whatever power the surface can shed in steady state. Benchmarks measure the burst. Long video calls and sustained navigation measure the steady state, and the gap between them is often a factor of two. Designing for it means deciding quite deliberately what the device should be good at for ten seconds and what it should be good at for an hour.

At the opposite extreme the same equations produce a different answer. Racks of accelerators now dissipate more per square metre than moving air can practically carry away, and the industry has gone back to liquid — cold plates bolted directly to the package with water or glycol running through them, and in some installations entire boards submerged in dielectric fluid. Liquid is not magic. It is a working fluid with a far higher heat capacity per unit volume than air, which means a modest flow rate can remove a load that would otherwise require a gale.

All of it comes back to the fact that a product's thermal path is usually settled before anyone writes a line of firmware. Vent placement, lid thickness, the decision to use a metal chassis instead of plastic, whether there is room for a fan at all — those choices fix the total resistance from junction to air, and everything the silicon is permitted to do afterwards has to fit inside the budget they left behind.
