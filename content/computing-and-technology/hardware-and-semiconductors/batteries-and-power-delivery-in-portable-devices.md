# Batteries, Chargers, and the Long Path to the Chip

A phone reading forty percent shuts off on a cold platform. Bring it inside, wait ten minutes, and it turns back on still claiming a third of a charge. Nothing was lost and nothing was broken. The battery's internal resistance rose in the cold, the voltage sagged under the current the radio demanded, and the device saw the supply dip below its cutoff and protected itself. That single failure contains most of what is worth knowing about portable power: the energy in the cell, the voltage the cell can hold under load, the guesswork involved in reporting a percentage, and the long chain of conversion between the chemistry and the silicon.

## Charging Moves Atoms, Not Just Charge

A lithium-ion cell is not a container that fills with electricity. It is two materials that trade lithium atoms back and forth, and the electricity is a side effect of that trade.

On the positive side sits a layered metal oxide — cobalt, nickel, manganese, and aluminium in various proportions, or iron phosphate in cells built for durability over density. On the negative side, graphite: sheets of carbon stacked with just enough room between them to host lithium ions. Charging drives lithium out of the oxide lattice, through a solvent-based electrolyte and a porous separator, and into the gaps between graphite sheets, a process called intercalation. Discharging lets them travel back. The electrons cannot follow through the electrolyte, so they go the long way around through the device, and that detour is the useful part.

Two things follow directly. First, capacity is set by how many lithium atoms the two hosts can hold, which is why energy density improves slowly and in increments: you are limited by what the materials will accept, not by engineering cleverness. Silicon is attractive as an anode material because it can take on roughly an order of magnitude more lithium per gram than graphite, and difficult because absorbing that much lithium swells it enormously, cracking the particles and tearing the electrode apart over repeated cycles. The small percentages of silicon blended into today's graphite anodes are a compromise between those facts.

Second, moving atoms takes time and takes place at a rate that depends on temperature. A cell forced to accept lithium faster than the graphite can absorb it will instead deposit metallic lithium on the anode surface — plating — which is irreversible, permanently removes that lithium from circulation, and can grow structures that eventually reach the other electrode. Cold makes it worse, because diffusion slows. This is why charging is throttled in the cold, and why fast charging is aggressive only in the lower part of the range, where the anode has plenty of empty space.

## The Percentage Is an Estimate, and a Fairly Crude One

There is no float in the tank. The only quantities a device can measure directly are terminal voltage, current in and out, and temperature. Everything else is inference.

The basic method is coulomb counting: integrate current over time to track how much charge has left or entered the cell. It works well in the short term and drifts badly over hours, because small errors in the current measurement accumulate without bound, and because self-discharge and parasitic loads are not perfectly captured. So the counter is corrected against open-circuit voltage, which has a fairly reliable relationship to state of charge — but only when the cell has been resting long enough for the internal chemistry to relax, and only where the voltage curve has enough slope to be informative. Across much of the middle of the range, lithium-ion cells sit on a plateau where a large change in charge produces a small change in voltage. That plateau is excellent for powering electronics, which prefer a stable supply, and terrible for estimating remaining capacity.

Hence the familiar behaviours: a percentage that hangs at fifty for an hour and then drops rapidly, a device that recalibrates its estimate after a full charge, and a reading that jumps when the load changes. The gauge is also tracking a moving target, because the cell's full capacity shrinks as it ages, and a gauge that has not recently seen a full charge and a deep discharge has little evidence about where the new endpoints are.

## How a Cell Wears Out

Degradation is not one process.

The dominant one begins immediately. The electrolyte is not stable against the charged graphite anode, and it reacts to form a thin passivating film on the surface — the solid electrolyte interphase. That film is necessary; without it the electrolyte would decompose continuously. But forming it consumes lithium, and it keeps thickening slowly over the cell's life, consuming a little more each time and raising internal resistance as it grows. This is calendar ageing, and it proceeds whether the cell is used or not.

What accelerates it is heat and voltage. A cell held near full charge sits at a higher potential, which drives those side reactions faster; a warm cell runs every reaction faster still. The two multiply. A battery kept at a high state of charge in a hot car is ageing on both axes at once, which is why manufacturers' advice on storage converges on something like a partial charge in a cool place, and why phones and laptops increasingly learn a user's routine and hold at eighty percent overnight, finishing the charge shortly before the alarm. The practical version of this advice is simple: shallow cycles in the middle of the range are gentle, long rests at full charge are not, and heat is worse than anything else you can do to a cell. Charging overnight is not itself the problem; sitting at maximum voltage for six hours afterwards is.

Cycling adds mechanical damage on top. Electrode particles expand and contract as lithium enters and leaves, and over hundreds of cycles they crack, exposing fresh surface that forms more film and eventually losing electrical contact with the rest of the electrode.

The failure mode everyone worries about is separate and much rarer. If a cell is punctured, crushed, overcharged, or internally shorted by a manufacturing defect, a local hot spot can start an exothermic reaction that heats the surrounding material enough to start further reactions — thermal runaway. The defences are layered: separators designed to melt and close their pores at a set temperature, interrupting ion flow; a current interrupt device that disconnects the cell if internal pressure rises; a protection circuit that cuts off on overvoltage, undervoltage, overcurrent, or temperature; and a charging controller that will not exceed the cell's limits. The swollen battery occasionally found in an old laptop is gas from electrolyte decomposition, a sign the cell has been abused or simply left too long at too high a charge in too warm a place, and a reason to stop using it.

## From Cell Voltage to Core Voltage

The cell delivers something in the neighbourhood of three to four and a bit volts, sliding downward as it discharges. Nothing in the device wants that. A processor core may want well under a volt, memory something different, radios, sensors, display driver, and storage each something else again, and all of them want it steady while the supply underneath them wanders.

The conversion is done by switching regulators, and the reason they are used rather than simpler linear regulators is efficiency. A linear regulator burns the difference between input and output voltage as heat; converting four volts to one at any serious current would waste three-quarters of the energy. A switching converter instead chops the input with a transistor at a high frequency and uses an inductor and capacitor to average the result, transferring energy rather than dissipating it, and reaching efficiencies in the nineties. A power management IC packs a dozen or more such converters, each feeding its own rail, along with the sequencing logic that brings rails up and down in the correct order.

The regulators are only half the strategy. The other half is that modern chips continuously adjust what they ask for: dropping clock frequency and supply voltage together when the work is light, because switching power scales with the square of the voltage, and cutting power entirely to blocks that are idle. That is why finishing a task quickly and returning to a deep sleep state usually beats running slowly for longer — the idle state costs so much less than the active one that getting there sooner wins.

A different problem is transient response. When a processor wakes from idle and starts executing in earnest, its current draw can jump by amperes in nanoseconds — far faster than any regulator's feedback loop can react. The gap is filled by decoupling capacitors placed as close to the load as the packaging allows, supplying the initial surge locally while the regulator catches up. Insufficient decoupling shows up as a momentary voltage droop, and a droop below the level the logic needs is a crash.

## Talking to the Charger

The last link is the one you plug in, and it is now a negotiation rather than a connection. A USB-C cable carries configuration channel pins that let the two ends identify each other and agree on terms: the charger advertises what voltage and current combinations it can supply, the device selects one, and only then does the supply move off the default five volts. Getting more power through a cable means raising the voltage rather than the current, since resistive losses go with current squared, which is why fast chargers run at nine, fifteen, or twenty volts and the device steps it back down internally.

The more recent refinement lets the device request a finely adjustable voltage instead of a fixed step, and set it just above what the cell itself needs. That allows the charger to act almost as the cell's own regulator, moving the heavy conversion out of the phone and into the brick, where the heat does not sit next to the battery. Wireless charging pushes in the opposite direction: an inductive link across an air gap loses a meaningful share of the energy, and it loses it as heat generated in the phone, directly against the cell. It is convenient and it is unkind to the battery, and both things are true at the same time for the same physical reason.
