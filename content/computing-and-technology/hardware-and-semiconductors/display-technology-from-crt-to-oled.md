# From the Electron Gun to the Emissive Pixel

Point a phone camera at an old television and a dark band rolls slowly up the picture, a band your eye cannot see when you look directly at the screen. The band is the truth about how a cathode ray tube works. At any given instant almost none of that screen is lit. A single point of light is racing across it, left to right and top to bottom, and what you perceive as a stable image is the combination of phosphor afterglow and your own visual persistence stitching together something that never actually exists all at once. The camera, with its short exposure and its own rolling shutter, catches the display in the act.

Every display technology since has been an argument with some consequence of that arrangement.

## One Beam, Three Phosphors, and a Great Deal of Glass

A colour CRT is a vacuum chamber with three electron guns at the narrow end and a phosphor-coated faceplate at the wide end. Each gun fires a stream of electrons; electromagnets in the deflection yoke around the neck steer the beams across the screen in a raster pattern; where the electrons land, the phosphor fluoresces. The gun's current sets the brightness of that spot, and because the beam current is a continuous analogue quantity, a CRT has no fixed number of brightness levels and no native resolution in the modern sense. Feed it a different scan rate and it simply draws a different number of lines.

Colour requires keeping the three beams honest. Just behind the faceplate sits a perforated metal sheet — a shadow mask with a grid of holes, or an aperture grille of fine vertical wires, the approach Sony used in Trinitron sets and which produced a brighter picture at the cost of one or two faint horizontal damper wires visible against light backgrounds. Either way, the geometry is arranged so that from the position of each gun, only the red, green, or blue phosphor is visible through each aperture. The mask absorbs most of the beam energy to achieve this, which is why CRTs ran hot and why the mask had to be made of alloys with low thermal expansion: heat it unevenly and the holes move, and the beams start landing on the wrong phosphor.

The whole design has awkward consequences. The tube must be deep, because the beam needs distance to sweep a wide angle. The glass must be thick, because it is holding back atmospheric pressure over a large area. The image needs constant correction — convergence, geometry, pincushion — because steering three beams magnetically across a curved surface is never exactly right. Stray magnetic fields, including the Earth's, will magnetise the mask and discolour a corner of the picture, hence the degaussing coil and the thump it made at power-on.

But CRTs did two things that took flat panels twenty years to match. Because each phosphor dot is briefly struck and then dark, motion is genuinely crisp: the eye is given a series of short impulses rather than a continuously held image. And because an unlit phosphor emits nothing at all, black is black.

## Twisting Light Instead of Making It

An LCD makes no light. It is a shutter, and everything about its behaviour follows from that.

The sandwich, from back to front, is a backlight, a polarising filter, a layer of liquid crystal, a second polarising filter rotated ninety degrees from the first, and colour filters. Two crossed polarisers alone would pass nothing: the first admits light of one orientation, the second blocks exactly that orientation. The liquid crystal in between is the trick. Its rod-shaped molecules align with grooves in the surfaces that contain them, and because those surfaces are rotated relative to each other, the molecules form a gentle helix that rotates the polarisation of light passing through it — twisting it just enough to slip through the second filter. Apply a voltage across the cell and the molecules stand up and align with the field, the helix unwinds, the rotation stops, and the light is blocked.

Making that work at scale required a second invention: an active matrix. Every subpixel gets its own thin-film transistor and a small storage capacitor, deposited directly onto the glass. The display is addressed one row at a time; when a row is selected, each column line drives its subpixel's capacitor to the right voltage, and that charge holds the cell in place until the row is refreshed. Fabricating millions of working transistors across a sheet of glass a metre wide is a manufacturing problem of the same family as chipmaking, and it is what separates a usable flat panel from the sluggish, ghosting passive matrices of early laptops.

The three main liquid crystal arrangements trade against one another in ways anyone who has shopped for a monitor has encountered without necessarily knowing why. Twisted nematic panels switch fastest and cost least, but the optical path changes with viewing angle so drastically that colours shift and invert off-axis. In-plane switching rotates the molecules within the plane of the panel rather than tipping them toward the viewer, which holds colour steady across wide angles at some cost in contrast and switching speed. Vertical alignment starts with the molecules perpendicular to the glass, blocking light very effectively, and delivers the deepest blacks of the three — but tips them at an angle when driven, which produces its own off-axis behaviour and slower transitions in dark tones.

What none of them can fix is leakage. The shutter is imperfect, so some backlight always reaches the viewer through a nominally black pixel, and the black of an LCD is a dark grey lit from behind.

## Making the Backlight Do Some of the Work

The response has been to stop treating the backlight as a constant. Cold-cathode fluorescent tubes gave way to white LEDs, first arranged around the edge of the panel with a light guide spreading the illumination, later placed in an array directly behind it. Once the LEDs are behind the pixels they can be grouped into independently dimmable zones: dark regions of the image get less light, so their leakage is proportionally smaller. Shrink the LEDs and you can fit thousands of zones rather than dozens, which is what mini-LED backlighting is.

The limit is that the backlight's resolution is far lower than the image's. A bright object against a dark field — white text on black, a star field, subtitles — illuminates its whole zone, producing a visible halo, and the dimming algorithm has to choose continuously between crushing shadow detail and showing the bloom.

Colour got a separate upgrade. A white LED is usually a blue emitter with a yellowish phosphor on top, and its broad, lumpy spectrum passes messily through the red and green colour filters, limiting how saturated those primaries can be. Quantum dots fixed that: a film of semiconductor nanocrystals, sized so that each absorbs blue light and re-emits it in a very narrow band, with the emitted colour set by the crystal's diameter. Put that film over a blue backlight and you get clean, narrow red and green peaks, and a much wider colour gamut out of the same liquid crystal panel.

## Removing the Backlight Entirely

An OLED pixel makes its own light. Between two electrodes sit organic layers a few tens of nanometres thick; current injects electrons from one side and holes from the other; they meet in the emissive layer, form a bound excited state, and drop back down by emitting a photon whose colour is set by the molecule. No shutter, no backlight, no leakage. A pixel told to be black is simply switched off, and it is as black as the glass in front of it. Contrast ceases to be a specification and becomes a property.

The consequences run further than black level. Each pixel's brightness is set by the current through it, and that current can change within microseconds, so the transition from one frame's value to the next is effectively instantaneous rather than the millisecond-scale reorientation of a liquid crystal. The panel can also be built on plastic instead of glass, which is why phones have curved edges and foldable screens.

The difficulties are chemical. The three emitter colours do not age at the same rate, and blue is the problem child: the higher-energy emission degrades its host molecules fastest, so a display that has spent thousands of hours showing the same station logo or the same navigation bar will eventually show it faintly forever, as differential wear rather than any kind of stuck pixel. Manufacturers compensate by oversizing the blue subpixel, shifting the image by a pixel or two periodically, and running compensation cycles that measure and re-level the panel.

Manufacturing colour is its own puzzle. Small panels deposit red, green, and blue emitters through a fine metal mask, which works beautifully at phone size and becomes unwieldy on a television-sized sheet. The large-panel answer has been to build a white-emitting stack across the whole surface and cut colours out of it with filters — simple and reliable, at the price of throwing away light — or, more recently, to use a blue emitter layer with quantum dots converting part of it to red and green, which keeps the efficiency of conversion rather than subtraction. Either way, total panel brightness is limited by how much current the organic layers can take without accelerating their own decay, which is why a full white screen on an OLED dims noticeably compared with a small bright highlight.

Which leaves one CRT virtue still unrecovered. Flat panels hold each frame steady for its entire duration, and when your eye tracks a moving object across a held frame, the image smears across your retina. The blur is in the viewer, not the display, and no improvement in pixel response speed touches it. The fixes are all versions of going back to impulses — inserting black frames, strobing the backlight, or simply refreshing often enough that each held frame is too brief to smear. The last of those is why high refresh rates look the way they do, and why a thirty-year-old tube still holds up when something moves quickly across it.
