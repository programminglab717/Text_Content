# Embeddings and the Geometry of Meaning

Consider the words "king" and "queen." Any person immediately senses that these two words are related, and related in roughly the same way that "man" and "woman" are related: same underlying relationship, different starting point. A computer, left to its own devices, has no such sense. To a machine that only understands numbers, "king" and "queen" are just two arbitrary symbols, no more related to each other than "king" and "umbrella" unless something is done to give them a meaningful numerical relationship in the first place. Embeddings are the technique that does this, and the fact that they work as well as they do is one of the quietly strange achievements of the field.

## From Symbols to Points in Space

An embedding takes some discrete thing, a word, a product, a user, a chemical compound, and assigns it a location in a continuous, multi-dimensional space: a long list of numbers, functioning much like coordinates, except in far more than the two or three dimensions a person can picture. The entire purpose of choosing those coordinates carefully is so that geometric relationships in that space, how close two points sit to each other, and in which direction one lies relative to another, end up correlating with meaningful relationships in the real world that the points represent. Words that tend to mean similar things end up landing near each other. Words that play similar grammatical or relational roles end up separated from other pairs by similar directions and similar distances, even when the specific words involved are entirely different.

This last property produces one of the more startling demonstrations to come out of early work on word embeddings. Take the numerical representation of "king," subtract the representation of "man," and add the representation of "woman," all as simple arithmetic performed directly on the coordinate lists, and the resulting point lands close to the representation of "queen," despite nobody ever telling the system anything explicit about royalty, gender, or the relationship between the two. The system had no dictionary definition of any of these words. It had only learned, from patterns of usage, where to place each one, and the geometry of that placement turned out to capture something real about how the words relate.

## How the Placement Gets Learned

Embeddings are almost never designed by hand; they are learned automatically, typically as a side effect of training a model to perform some other, more mechanical task on a very large body of text or other sequential data. A common version of this asks a model to predict a word from the words surrounding it, or to predict the surrounding words from a given word, over and over across a huge number of real sentences. The model is never told directly that "cat" and "dog" are related concepts. But because these words tend to appear surrounded by similar neighboring words, appearing in similar sentences about pets, veterinarians, and leashes, the training process gradually pushes their representations toward each other purely as an incidental consequence of getting better at the actual prediction task it was assigned. This general approach echoes an old observation from linguistics, sometimes summarized as the idea that you can know a word by the company it tends to keep.

An important and slightly humbling detail is that individual dimensions in a learned embedding space almost never correspond to any single concept a person would recognize, the way latitude and longitude cleanly separate north-south position from east-west position. Meaning in these spaces tends to be smeared across many dimensions at once, and it is usually only specific directions or combinations of dimensions, discovered after training rather than designed beforehand, that turn out to align with something interpretable, like gender, formality, or sentiment. Nobody sat down and decided which dimension would represent royalty. It emerged from the geometry as an accident of what the training data contained and what the prediction task rewarded.

## Beyond Words

The same underlying trick generalizes well past vocabulary. Images can be embedded into a similar space, so that photographs of visually or conceptually similar objects land near each other even if their pixel values look nothing alike. Individual users of a service can be embedded based on their behavior, so that people with similar tastes end up close together, which is much of what powers a recommendation feature that surfaces items liked by people who resemble you in this learned sense. Entire sentences, paragraphs, or documents can be compressed down into a single point in such a space, enabling a search system to retrieve text that is similar in meaning to a query even when it shares barely any words with it directly, since the comparison happens in the geometry of meaning rather than in the literal spelling of words.

Comparing two points in one of these spaces is usually done not by measuring straight-line distance between them but by comparing the angle between the two coordinate lists, treating each one as a direction rather than strictly as a location. Two representations that point in nearly the same direction are treated as expressing similar meaning, regardless of exactly how far out along that direction each one happens to sit, a subtlety that turns out to matter because the overall scale of an embedding can vary for reasons that have little to do with meaning itself.

## The Trouble With Learning Geometry From the World

Because embeddings are learned entirely from patterns of real usage rather than from any explicit definition of correctness, they tend to absorb whatever associations happen to exist in the data they were trained on, including associations that are better described as bias than as meaning. A space trained on ordinary text will often end up placing certain occupations closer to one gendered term than another, not because of any inherent property of the occupation, but because that is simply how the association appeared, statistically, across the enormous body of writing the model learned from. The geometry has no built-in sense of which of the patterns it absorbed are fair, accurate, or worth keeping; it reflects co-occurrence, indiscriminately, unless something further is done to adjust it afterward.

This is not a flaw that better arithmetic can fix on its own, since the arithmetic is doing exactly what it was built to do: reflecting the statistical structure of its training data as faithfully as possible. Any correction has to come from somewhere outside the geometry itself, whether that means curating the training data more carefully beforehand or explicitly identifying and adjusting the directions in the space known to encode an unwanted association after the fact. Either way, the underlying lesson is that a technique for measuring meaning is not automatically a technique for judging which meanings are worth preserving.

There is something genuinely strange, even a little elegant, about the underlying move here: meaning, which philosophy and linguistics spent centuries treating as resistant to precise measurement, turned out to be usefully approximable with a ruler, provided the ruler is allowed several hundred dimensions and nobody insists on being able to point to what any single one of them represents.
