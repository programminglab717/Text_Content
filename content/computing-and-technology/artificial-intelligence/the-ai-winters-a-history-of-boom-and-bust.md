# The AI Winters: A History of Boom and Bust

Researchers in the field have a dark joke about their own history, borrowed from the language of nuclear war: the AI winter. It describes what happens after a period of extravagant promises fails to deliver, when funding agencies grow embarrassed, journalists move on, and a technology that had recently been described as imminent quietly becomes unfundable for the better part of a decade. The striking thing about the history of artificial intelligence is not that this happened once, but that it happened at least twice, in remarkably similar shapes, separated by about twenty years.

## The First Optimism

The field received its name in 1956, at a summer workshop held at Dartmouth College where a small group of mathematicians and engineers gathered around the proposal that essentially any feature of human intelligence could, in principle, be described precisely enough to be simulated by a machine. The mood in the years that followed was strikingly confident. Early programs could prove simple mathematical theorems, play a passable game of checkers, and carry on rudimentary conversations within tightly limited topics, and to researchers watching these results arrive in rapid succession, it seemed entirely plausible that broad machine intelligence was a matter of years, not generations. Several of the field's founding figures went on record suggesting that machines might rival human intellectual performance across a wide range of tasks within a generation, a timeline that, in hindsight, badly underestimated how much of what a person does effortlessly, seeing, understanding a sentence in context, forming a plan, would resist the techniques available at the time.

Government interest, particularly in the United States, followed the optimism, with substantial sums directed toward machine translation in particular, driven by Cold War interest in automatically translating scientific and military text out of Russian. By the mid-1960s, though, an official government review concluded that machine translation had not lived up to its promises, that it remained slower and less accurate than human translators, and that no breakthrough seemed close. Funding for that line of research was cut sharply. Around the same time, a widely discussed book laid out mathematical limits on what the simplest kind of neural network could ever learn, dampening enthusiasm for that entire approach for years. By the early 1970s, a critical report commissioned in Britain concluded that AI research had failed to achieve its grandiose stated goals and recommended steep funding cuts there as well. The first winter had arrived.

## Spring, of a Particular Kind

Interest did not stay frozen. Through the later 1970s, a different style of AI project began attracting attention: rather than chasing general-purpose reasoning, researchers built narrow systems that captured a human specialist's rules of thumb for one specific task, like identifying chemical compounds from instrument readings or recommending treatment for a bacterial infection. These expert systems, as they came to be called, produced results that were genuinely useful within their narrow domains, and that practicality reignited enormous enthusiasm. By the early 1980s, corporations were funding internal AI divisions, specialized computer hardware companies formed to sell machines optimized for the symbolic programming languages these systems were written in, and a major national research initiative in Japan committed substantial resources toward a next generation of computing built around these ideas. Money and optimism flowed back into the field on a scale that dwarfed the first boom.

## The Second Freeze

The trouble, once again, was distance between demonstration and reality. Expert systems turned out to be extraordinarily expensive to build and, worse, to maintain. Capturing an expert's judgment as a set of explicit rules was slower and harder than anticipated, since much of real expertise resists being written down as tidy if-then statements in the first place. Systems that performed well on the narrow cases their designers anticipated frequently broke down, with unhelpful confidence, the moment a real case fell slightly outside that anticipated range. Meanwhile the specialized hardware built to run these systems was rapidly overtaken by cheaper, faster, general-purpose machines, and the companies built around that hardware mostly collapsed within a few years. The ambitious national computing initiative in Japan wound down without having delivered on its early promises. By the late 1980s and into the early 1990s, investment contracted again, sharply enough that many researchers reportedly avoided the phrase "artificial intelligence" in grant applications altogether, describing closely related work instead as machine learning, pattern recognition, or applied statistics. The underlying failure came to be summarized as a knowledge acquisition bottleneck: every new rule had to be extracted from a human expert by hand, one interview at a time, and no amount of additional funding could make that bottleneck open much wider, since the limiting resource was expert attention and patience rather than computing power or capital.

## Quiet Progress Underground

What is easy to miss, looking only at funding headlines, is that research did not actually stop during either winter. It continued, often under a different name and at a smaller scale, frequently migrating into statistics and engineering departments where the practical, narrower framing fit better with what funders were still willing to support. Programs that played games well enough to be broadly newsworthy kept appearing periodically, including a chess-playing system that defeated the reigning world champion in a widely covered match in 1997, a result that briefly rekindled public fascination even while broader research funding remained modest. Techniques for recognizing handwriting, filtering spam, and recommending products quietly matured throughout the 1990s and early 2000s, largely outside the spotlight that had burned the field twice already.

## A Field Prone to This Pattern

It is worth asking why this particular corner of engineering seems especially susceptible to booms and busts, when other technical fields advance in a comparatively steadier line. Part of the answer is semantic: the word intelligence carries the full weight of everything a human mind can do, so a program that performs well on one narrow slice of that territory, translating a sentence, recognizing a digit, winning a board game, invites an inference toward the whole territory that the actual result never justified. A faster aircraft is simply a faster aircraft; nobody assumes it implies an imminent leap toward spacecraft. A program that translates Russian competently, by contrast, was routinely read as evidence that fluent, general translation, and perhaps general reasoning alongside it, was close behind.

Part of the answer is also structural. A demonstration can be rehearsed, curated, and shown under favorable conditions, while the harder question, how the same system behaves across the enormous variety of cases it will meet once deployed broadly, only reveals itself slowly, after the funding decision has already been made and the optimistic press coverage has already run. By the time the gap between demonstration and robust general capability becomes undeniable, the money has often moved on to the next promising demonstration, and the correction, when it finally arrives, tends to land all at once rather than gradually.

## The Third Boom, and the Shape of the Pattern

The current era of enthusiasm, which took hold around the early 2010s, was triggered by a specific, visible result: networks with many layers, trained on far larger collections of data than had previously been practical and run on repurposed graphics hardware, began sharply outperforming older techniques on tasks like recognizing objects in photographs. Investment, both corporate and governmental, returned at a scale that made the two earlier booms look modest by comparison, and with it returned a familiar confidence about how quickly further progress would arrive.

Looking across all three cycles, a shape repeats. A genuine technical advance produces results impressive enough to justify real optimism. That optimism, retold enough times through funding proposals, conference keynotes, and press coverage, drifts away from the narrower claim the original result actually supported and toward a much broader one about what is now within reach. Investment follows the broader claim rather than the narrower result. When the broader claim fails to materialize on the promised schedule, as broad claims about hard, poorly understood problems generally do, the disappointment lands not on the original narrow result, which may have been perfectly real, but on the field's credibility as a whole, and the money recedes until the next genuine advance restarts the cycle. Whether the current period of enthusiasm will eventually be remembered as a third variation on this same pattern, or as the point where the pattern finally broke, is not a question history has answered yet.
