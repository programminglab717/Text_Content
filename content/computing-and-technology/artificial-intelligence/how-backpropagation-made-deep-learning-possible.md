# How Backpropagation Made Deep Learning Possible

Imagine a network with a dozen layers has just looked at a photograph and confidently announced the wrong answer. Somewhere inside that stack of layers, a change needs to happen so that the same mistake is less likely next time. But which of the network's weights, out of possibly millions buried at every depth, deserves the blame? And by how much should each one be nudged, and in which direction? This is the credit assignment problem, and for a long stretch of the twentieth century it was the single largest obstacle standing between simple learning machines and anything resembling deep, layered intelligence.

## A Promising Start, and a Wall

The earliest trainable neural models, developed in the late 1950s, were single layers of artificial neurons called perceptrons. A perceptron could be shown examples, told whether its answer was right or wrong, and adjust its own weights according to a fairly simple rule, gradually improving at tasks like separating two visually distinct groups of patterns. That simplicity was also its ceiling. A single layer of this kind can only draw a straight-line boundary between categories, and by the end of the 1960s it had been shown clearly that many useful problems are not solvable with a straight line at all, no matter how the weights are tuned. Interest in neural approaches to learning cooled sharply for years afterward.

The frustrating part was that the fix seemed almost obvious in principle. Stack several layers of neurons on top of each other, let the earlier layers reshape the input into something a later layer could draw a straight line through, and the straight-line limitation disappears. Multi-layer networks of exactly this kind had been proposed. The trouble was training them. The learning rule that worked for a single layer had no obvious way of reaching backward through several layers to figure out how an early-layer weight, several transformations removed from the final answer, should be adjusted.

## Passing the Blame Backward

The solution, when it finally took hold, turned out to rest on a piece of ordinary calculus called the chain rule, which describes how a change deep inside a sequence of nested functions ripples forward to change the final output. Run that relationship in reverse, and it also tells you how a change in the final output's error should be distributed backward across every step that produced it. Applied to a neural network, this means starting at the output layer, where the size of the mistake is easy to measure directly, and then walking backward through the network one layer at a time, at each step computing how much each weight in that layer contributed to the error and passing an appropriately adjusted error signal further back to the layer before it. By the time this backward pass reaches the first layer, every single weight in the network has been assigned its own small share of responsibility for the mistake. This procedure is what came to be called backpropagation: propagating the error backward through the same structure the input had just traveled forward through.

Pieces of this idea had been worked out in various forms since at least the early 1970s, including in doctoral research that applied the same chain-rule logic to networks without much notice at the time. The idea did not become a central part of the field's toolkit until the mid-1980s, when a widely read paper demonstrated, in a clear and persuasive way, that multi-layer networks trained with this backward error-propagation method could learn internal representations that were genuinely useful, discovering intermediate features nobody had explicitly designed, rather than simply memorizing input-output pairs. That demonstration is often treated as the moment backpropagation moved from a mathematical curiosity to the default method for training layered networks.

## Descending the Slope

Knowing how much each weight contributed to an error is only half the story. The other half, called gradient descent, is a very old and much simpler idea: once you know, for a given weight, which direction of change would make the error a little smaller, take a small step in that direction. Then look at the next batch of examples, recompute the error, work out the new direction, and take another small step. Repeat this an enormous number of times, and the collection of weights gradually settles into values that produce fewer mistakes overall.

It helps to picture the total error the network makes across many examples as a landscape, an undulating surface with hills and valleys, except the surface exists in a space with as many dimensions as the network has weights, which for any serious network is a great many more than three. Training is the process of standing somewhere on that surface and repeatedly stepping downhill. The size of each step is a setting chosen ahead of time, often called the learning rate. Too large a step and the process can overshoot a valley entirely, bouncing around without ever settling. Too small a step and training crawls along so slowly that it might never finish. And the landscape itself is not simple: it is riddled with shallow dips that look like the bottom but are not, ridges, and long flat stretches where the slope barely points anywhere useful at all.

## Why It Took So Long to Work Well

Even after backpropagation was described and gradient descent was well understood, training networks with many layers remained stubbornly difficult for years. One recurring problem was that the error signal, as it propagated backward through layer after layer, tended to shrink at each step, until by the time it reached the earliest layers there was almost nothing left to learn from. Those early layers, ironically often the ones responsible for detecting the most basic and reusable patterns, ended up barely adjusting at all, no matter how long training continued. This difficulty became especially visible through the 1990s as researchers experimented with deeper architectures and kept running into the same wall.

Progress came from several directions arriving together rather than any single fix. New ways of initializing a network's weights before training began kept the early signal from shrinking or exploding as it passed through many layers. New nonlinear functions replaced older ones that were especially prone to crushing the backward-flowing error signal toward zero. Far larger collections of labeled examples became available to train on. And, in a genuinely unlikely turn, specialized graphics processors originally designed to render video game scenes turned out to be extraordinarily well suited to the exact kind of repeated matrix arithmetic that both the forward and backward passes require, making it possible to train much larger networks in a practical amount of time. By the early 2010s, these threads had come together enough that networks with many more layers than earlier researchers had ever successfully trained began outperforming older approaches on hard, real-world tasks, often by a wide margin.

## The Same Dance, at Scale

What is easy to lose sight of, given how sophisticated modern networks have become, is that the training procedure underneath them has not fundamentally changed since it was first popularized. Compute how wrong the network was. Walk backward through the network distributing blame for that error to every weight along the way. Nudge every weight a small step in the direction that would have reduced the error. Repeat, on a new batch of examples, an almost unimaginable number of times. Networks today are far deeper, trained on far more data, and run on hardware that would have seemed like fantasy to the researchers who first wrote the backward pass down on paper. But the basic two-step dance of assigning blame and then descending the slope is still, underneath everything else, exactly what is happening.
