# What a Neural Network Actually Computes

Feed a computer a photograph of a handwritten seven, and by the time it prints out a label, the image has already been reduced to nothing but numbers. A grid of pixels, each one a brightness value, gets flattened into a long list, and that list is the only thing the network ever receives. It does not see a loop and a diagonal stroke the way a person does. It sees a few hundred or a few thousand numbers between zero and one, and its entire job is to turn that list into a much shorter list, one that says how strongly the input resembles each of the categories it has been built to recognize. Understanding what happens in between turns out to be an exercise in arithmetic, not magic, even though the end result can feel uncanny.

## The Single Neuron

Start with the smallest working part: one artificial neuron. It receives some number of inputs, each an ordinary number. Each input carries a weight, another number, and the neuron's first task is to multiply every input by its matching weight and add up the results. It then adds one more number, called a bias, which shifts the whole sum up or down regardless of what the inputs happen to be. At this point the neuron holds a single weighted total, and if the story stopped there, it would just be computing a weighted average with extra steps.

The final step is what makes it interesting. That sum gets passed through a nonlinear function, often one that squashes very negative numbers toward zero while letting larger ones through along a gentle curve, or one that simply zeroes out anything negative and passes positive numbers through unchanged. Whichever function gets chosen, its purpose is to introduce a bend into what would otherwise be a straight line. That bend is arguably the single most consequential design decision in the whole field, because a network built entirely of neurons without such nonlinearities, no matter how many layers deep, collapses mathematically into one plain linear equation. You cannot draw a curved boundary around a cluster of points using nothing but straight lines stacked on straight lines. Nonlinearity is what lets the network bend space to fit the shape of a problem.

## Stacking Neurons Into Layers

A single neuron is not very expressive on its own. The power shows up once thousands of them are arranged in layers. The first layer of neurons all look at the same input list, the raw pixel values, and each one computes its own weighted sum and its own nonlinear output. That produces a new list of numbers, not necessarily the same length as the input, which becomes the input to the next layer. Every neuron in that second layer looks at every output from the first layer, weights them all over again with its own separate set of weights, and produces one more number. This repeats for as many layers as the designer has included, and it is this repetition, layer after layer, that gives the field its name. Depth just means several of these transformations are chained together rather than one or two.

Picture the earliest hidden layers as noticing something extremely local: a short edge in one corner of the image, a patch of contrast between two neighboring regions. Layers further along combine those simple signals into more complicated ones, an edge plus another edge at the right angle beginning to resemble a corner. By the time information reaches the final hidden layers, it has been recombined so many times that it can represent something as abstract as the loop at the top of an eight, without any human ever writing a rule that says so. Nobody programs that rule directly. It falls out of the arithmetic as a consequence of how the weights end up being set, which is a separate story from what the network computes at any given instant.

## From Numbers to Answers

Eventually the chain of layers reaches an output layer, and here the shape of the problem determines what happens next. For a network built to identify a handwritten digit, the output layer typically has ten neurons, one for each possible digit, and each produces a raw number that can be any size, positive or negative. Those raw numbers on their own are awkward to interpret, so a final conversion step turns them into something that behaves like a set of probabilities: every value becomes positive, and the whole set sums to one. The digit whose corresponding output ends up largest is read as the network's answer, and the size of that number is often treated as a rough measure of how confident the network is.

It is worth sitting with how modest each individual step in this chain really is. Multiply, add, bend, repeat. There is no branching logic anywhere in a plain feedforward network, no if-this-then-that reasoning of the kind older rule-based programs relied on. Every neuron performs the same category of calculation no matter what it has learned to detect, and the only thing that differs from neuron to neuron, and from network to network, is the specific numerical values of the weights and biases. A network that recognizes handwriting and a network that estimates the value of a house are, at the level of raw computation, doing the same kind of arithmetic. What differs is which numbers ended up in the weights during training and what shape the layers were built in to begin with.

## Where the Architecture Comes From

The number of layers, how many neurons sit in each one, and which neurons connect to which others: none of that is discovered by the training process itself in a typical network. Those choices, collectively called the architecture, are decided ahead of time by whoever is building the system, usually through some mixture of theory, precedent, and trial and error. Training then searches only within the space that architecture allows, adjusting weights and biases until the network's outputs match the desired answers as closely as possible across many examples. A network with too few neurons in its hidden layers may simply lack the capacity to represent the pattern being asked of it, the way a two-piece jigsaw cannot depict a detailed landscape no matter how the pieces are arranged. A network with far more capacity than the problem needs runs a different risk: memorizing the specific examples it was shown rather than learning the broader pattern behind them.

This is why architectural choices matter so much even before any training begins. Connecting every neuron in one layer to every neuron in the next, often called a fully connected arrangement, is the simplest possible design and works reasonably well for modest problems, but it spends enormous numbers of weights on structure that a more targeted design could exploit instead. An image, for instance, has spatial structure: nearby pixels tend to be related in ways that distant pixels are not. Architectures built around that observation restrict which neurons see which inputs, trading generality for efficiency, and often perform far better as a result on the specific kinds of data they were shaped around.

## A Strange Kind of Function

Mathematically, the whole network, however many layers and however many millions of weights it contains, is simply one large function. It accepts a fixed-size list of numbers as input and produces a fixed-size list of numbers as output, and every intermediate layer is a stepping stone between the two. Functions of that general kind have been studied for a long time, but what makes this particular family remarkable is how flexible it becomes simply by adding more layers and more neurons. Given enough capacity, this style of network can approximate an enormous range of relationships between inputs and outputs: curved, discontinuous, riddled with special cases, far more varied than the smooth and well-behaved functions found in an introductory calculus course.

That flexibility is also the entire reason training such a network is difficult, but the computation itself, the thing that happens the instant an image is pushed through an already trained network, remains almost embarrassingly simple to describe. It is multiplication, addition, and a bend, repeated over and over, at a scale large enough that the pattern it produces stops resembling arithmetic at all and starts looking, from a distance, like recognition.
