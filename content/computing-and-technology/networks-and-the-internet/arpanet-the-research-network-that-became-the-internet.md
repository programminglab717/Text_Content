# ARPANET: The Research Network That Became the Internet

In the autumn of 1969, a graduate student at UCLA sat down to send the first message across a new experimental network to a computer at Stanford Research Institute, several hundred kilometers up the California coast. The plan was to type the word LOGIN. The network, functioning about as well as any brand-new piece of research equipment does on its first real trial, transmitted the L, transmitted the O, and then crashed before the G arrived. The full word went through a short while later, once the fault was found and fixed. It's a fitting origin story for a system that would spend its early years being patched, debugged, and gradually made trustworthy by the people building it in real time, long before anyone imagined what it would eventually grow into.

That network was ARPANET, funded by the Advanced Research Projects Agency, an arm of the United States Department of Defense tasked with funding ambitious, speculative research. It's tempting, and commonly repeated, to describe ARPANET's origin as a scheme to build a communications network able to survive a nuclear attack. The more accurate story is less dramatic and, in its own way, more interesting: ARPA's Information Processing Techniques Office, under a succession of directors who shared a vision of computers as tools for collaboration rather than isolated calculating machines, wanted to let researchers at different universities actually use each other's computers — expensive, scarce machines, each running different software, each essentially an island. Robert Taylor, who ran the office through the middle years of the 1960s, is often credited with pushing the idea from vision toward funded project, building on thinking that had circulated for several years among a small community of computer scientists about linking machines together for shared use. Some of that thinking traced back to Taylor's own predecessor at the office, J.C.R. Licklider, who had circulated memos years earlier sketching an early, largely conceptual vision of a broad network connecting computers and the people using them, a vision Licklider himself only half-jokingly referred to using language suggesting something on a galactic scale. Licklider never built ARPANET and had moved on from the office by the time serious funding and engineering work began, but the intellectual groundwork he left behind shaped how the people who came after him thought about what a computer network might actually be for. The resilience properties that made packet switching so attractive — a separate line of research, developed earlier and independently by Paul Baran, motivated explicitly by the nuclear survivability concern people tend to associate with ARPANET itself — turned out to be a valuable side effect of a network built for an entirely different, far less dramatic reason.

## Building the First Nodes

Turning the concept into working hardware fell to Bolt, Beranek and Newman, a Cambridge, Massachusetts engineering firm that won the contract to build the network's switching computers, known as Interface Message Processors. Each site on the fledgling network would connect its own research computer to one of these IMPs, and the IMPs would handle the actual business of routing packets to each other, insulating the research computers themselves from having to understand the network's inner workings. The first IMP arrived at UCLA, chosen partly because Leonard Kleinrock, whose earlier work had helped establish the theoretical basis for how packet-switched networks behave under load, ran a lab there well suited to testing and monitoring the new network's performance.

Three more nodes followed within weeks and months: Stanford Research Institute, which happened also to host a large project building tools for augmenting human intellect, and thus had plenty of interesting software worth sharing across a network; the University of California, Santa Barbara; and the University of Utah, known for its strength in computer graphics research. Four nodes doesn't sound like much of a network, but each connection proved a different piece of the concept, and growth from there was steady rather than explosive — new sites added every few months as the technology matured and more research institutions wanted in. The links joining these early IMPs ran over leased telephone lines, the same kind of circuits the phone company rented out to any ordinary business, carrying data at speeds that would seem impossibly modest by any later standard, though at the time they represented a considerable improvement over mailing magnetic tapes between institutions, which had been a genuinely common way to move large amounts of data between distant computers before any of this existed.

## The Application Nobody Planned For

ARPANET's designers built the network primarily to let researchers log into distant computers and run programs remotely, and to shuttle files between machines. Neither of those was what came to dominate actual usage within a few years. In the early 1970s, an engineer at Bolt, Beranek and Newman named Ray Tomlinson adapted an existing program for leaving messages for other users of the same computer so that it could address a message to a user on a different machine entirely, connected somewhere else on the network. He needed a character to separate the username from the name of the remote machine, and he picked the at symbol sitting mostly unused on his keyboard, reasoning it wouldn't plausibly appear in anyone's actual username.

Network email, as this quickly became known, was not a planned feature of ARPANET, and its designers hadn't anticipated it would become the network's most heavily used application within just a few years. But it did, remarkably quickly, consuming a large share of the network's total traffic almost as soon as word of it spread from site to site. The lesson it offered — that the most consequential use of a new communications technology is often not the one its builders had in mind — would repeat itself many more times as the network that grew from ARPANET kept expanding into uses nobody involved in its early design had specifically planned for.

## Outgrowing Its Own Protocol

ARPANET's original internal protocol, the Network Control Program, worked well enough for a single, centrally administered network of the kind ARPANET was in its first decade. But by the late 1970s, other networks had sprung up using different technologies entirely — radio-based networks, satellite links, and various local networks at research institutions — and connecting all of them into a single, larger internetwork required something the original protocol had never been designed for: a way to link fundamentally different kinds of networks together as equals, rather than assuming everyone spoke the same protocol underneath.

Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn's design for what became TCP and IP addressed exactly this, treating the network of networks as a genuinely new layer sitting above whatever technology any individual network happened to use internally. Adopting it across the whole of ARPANET required an enormous, coordinated effort: every connected computer had to switch over essentially simultaneously, since a network that spoke two incompatible protocols at once would simply fragment. The switchover, planned meticulously in advance and carried out on a single day at the start of 1983, is remembered in networking circles as flag day, the moment ARPANET's individual research network fully became the substrate of what people would soon simply start calling the internet.

## A Culture as Much as a Technology

What made ARPANET's growth possible was not purely technical. The community of researchers building and using it developed a set of working habits that shaped the network as much as any piece of hardware did: technical decisions documented and circulated openly in a simple series of numbered notes anyone could contribute to, disagreements settled by rough consensus and working code rather than by formal authority, and a general willingness to let the network's actual users discover and propose new uses for it rather than restricting it to whatever its original designers had specified. That culture of open documentation and informal consensus outlived ARPANET itself and shaped how the wider internet's technical standards continued to be developed for decades afterward, long after the original small community of researchers had been joined by a much larger and more diverse set of institutions and companies.

## What Outlasted the Original Network

ARPANET itself was formally decommissioned in 1990, its physical hardware retired, its individual sites folded into the larger internet that had grown up around and beyond it. Almost nothing of the original physical network survives in any literal sense. What survived instead was a set of design decisions — packet switching, decentralized control, a deliberate indifference to what kind of network sat underneath the common protocol layer, and a tolerance for letting unplanned uses reshape the network far beyond its designers' original intentions. Those decisions, made by a comparatively small group of researchers solving a comparatively modest problem about sharing expensive computers, turned out to describe, essentially unchanged, the architecture of a network that would eventually connect most of humanity.
