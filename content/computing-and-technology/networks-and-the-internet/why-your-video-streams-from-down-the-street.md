# Why Your Video Streams From Down the Street

Light is fast, but not infinitely fast, and a signal traveling through fiber optic cable moves at only a bit more than half the speed of light in a vacuum. That sounds like a technicality until you do the arithmetic on real distances. A round trip between, say, a city on one continent and a city on another, even along the shortest practical cable routes, takes a meaningful fraction of a second — not enough to notice waiting for a single web page, perhaps, but very much enough to notice in a video call, an online game, or a page that has to fetch dozens of separate resources one after another before it finishes loading. Distance, on the internet, is not free, and it never stops mattering no matter how much the rest of the technology improves.

For a long time this simply meant that anyone far from a website's server had a slower experience than someone nearby. The fix that emerged, now such a standard part of internet infrastructure that most people encounter it dozens of times a day without knowing it, was to stop making everyone reach all the way back to one origin server and instead put copies of the content much closer to the people requesting it. This is the basic idea behind a content delivery network, or CDN: a distributed system of servers, positioned in data centers and internet exchange points scattered across the globe, each holding cached copies of content that would otherwise have to travel from a single, distant origin every single time it was requested.

## Copies, Not Trips

A CDN's core unit of work is caching — keeping a local copy of something so a later request for the same thing can be answered without re-fetching it from wherever it originally came from. This works best, and was first widely applied to, content that doesn't change from one request to the next: images, videos, style sheets, and scripts that make up the bulk of a typical web page's total size and that are identical whether the millionth person or the first person requests them. A CDN server sitting in a data center near a cluster of users can hold a cached copy of a popular video and serve it to every nearby viewer directly, without any of those requests needing to travel back to wherever the video was originally uploaded and stored.

Modern CDNs have pushed this idea considerably further, into caching content that's technically dynamic but changes slowly enough, or can be computed close enough to the user, to still benefit from proximity — parts of a web page assembled at the edge rather than shipped whole from a distant origin, or security and filtering logic that runs on the CDN's own servers before a request ever reaches the origin at all. The line between a cache and a distributed computing platform has blurred considerably as these networks have grown more capable over time.

## Finding the Nearest Copy

None of this matters if a user's request doesn't actually end up at a nearby CDN server. Solving that routing problem is arguably the more technically interesting half of what a CDN does. One common approach uses the domain name system itself: when a device looks up the address for a piece of CDN-hosted content, the CDN's own DNS infrastructure can examine where the request appears to be coming from and answer with the address of a server judged to be nearby, rather than handing out the same address to everyone everywhere. A different, increasingly common approach uses a routing technique called anycast, in which the very same numeric address is announced from many physical locations simultaneously, and the ordinary mechanics of internet routing, without any special awareness of geography, end up delivering each user's traffic to whichever announcing location happens to be topologically closest, which usually correlates well with physical closeness too.

Either approach depends on the CDN operating enough server locations, spread widely enough, that nearby is a meaningful concept for the overwhelming majority of the world's internet users, which is precisely why the major CDN operators have spent years and considerable capital building out points of presence in as many cities and countries as they reasonably can.

## What a Cache Cannot Fix

Caching works beautifully for content that's the same for everyone, and considerably less beautifully for content that isn't. A page showing a shopper their own cart, a dashboard showing someone their own private data, a search result tailored to an individual query — none of this can simply be cached once and handed out to the next visitor, since the next visitor needs something entirely different. CDN operators have developed increasingly elaborate workarounds for this boundary: splitting a page into cacheable and non-cacheable pieces and assembling them at the edge, caching the shell of a page while fetching only the personalized fragment from the origin, or pushing small amounts of logic to edge servers so they can make simple personalization decisions locally without a round trip back to the origin for every single request.

Even so, there remains an irreducible category of traffic that has to reach the actual origin server no matter how sophisticated the caching layer in front of it becomes — the first time any particular piece of content is requested, or any interaction that genuinely needs to read or write data specific to one individual. A CDN reduces how much traffic makes that long trip; it has never been able to eliminate the trip entirely, and reasoning correctly about which requests fall into which category is a large part of what makes running one well a genuinely difficult engineering discipline rather than a simple matter of caching everything by default.

## More Than Just Speed

Latency reduction is the most intuitive benefit a CDN provides, but it's not the only one, and for many of the largest sites, it may not even be the most important. By absorbing the vast majority of requests at the edge, a CDN dramatically reduces the load that ever reaches a site's own origin servers, which means those origin servers can be sized for a much smaller fraction of total traffic than they'd otherwise need to handle. This matters enormously during unexpected spikes — a piece of breaking news, a product launch, a viral moment — when traffic can multiply many times over in a short span, traffic a CDN's much larger, already-distributed capacity can typically absorb far more gracefully than a single origin ever could on its own.

CDNs have also become a first line of defense against a particular category of attack, in which an adversary tries to overwhelm a target with an enormous flood of illegitimate requests. Because a CDN's edge servers already sit between users and the origin, and because the CDN operates enormous aggregate capacity spread across many locations, absorbing and filtering a flood of malicious traffic before it ever reaches the actual origin server is something these networks are, in practice, unusually well positioned to do, and it has become a significant part of the business for the larger operators in this space.

## An Industry Born From an Academic Problem

The commercial CDN industry traces back to research done at a university in the late 1990s, when a small team working on the mathematics of efficiently distributing content across a large, uneven network built a company around the idea, essentially betting that the growing web would need exactly this kind of infrastructure long before that became obvious to most of the industry. That bet paid off substantially as the volume and size of web content, and eventually of video specifically, grew far beyond what any single, centrally located set of servers could reasonably serve to a global audience.

Today the idea has become thoroughly ordinary, offered by numerous companies at every scale from small specialized providers to the enormous infrastructure operated internally by the largest technology companies for their own services. Nearly every popular website or app relies on one, whether its operators think about it often or not, quietly ensuring that the video someone watches, or the page someone loads, is very likely arriving not from wherever it was originally created, but from a server considerably closer to home.
