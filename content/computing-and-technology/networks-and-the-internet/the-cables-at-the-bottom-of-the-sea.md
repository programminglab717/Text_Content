# The Cables at the Bottom of the Sea

Picture the internet and satellites are probably part of the image — a mesh of signals bouncing overhead, connecting continents through space. It's a reasonable picture and almost entirely wrong. The overwhelming majority of intercontinental internet traffic, the data behind a video call from Tokyo to Toronto or an email from London to Lagos, travels instead through cables lying on the floor of the ocean, thin enough in places to coil in your hand, carrying light down strands of glass barely wider than a human hair.

There are, by now, a few hundred of these systems crossing the world's oceans, ranging from short hops between neighboring countries to single unbroken runs stretching most of the way around the globe. Each is, at its core, a bundle of optical fibers wrapped in layers of steel wire, copper, and insulation, the whole assembly no thicker than a garden hose in the deep ocean sections, thickening considerably near shore where it needs armor against fishing trawlers, anchors, and the general violence of shallow water.

## Laying a Line Across an Ocean

Installing one of these cables is a slow, deliberate, almost surgical process. Before any cable touches water, engineers spend months surveying the proposed route with sonar, mapping the seabed's contours and avoiding known hazards: fault lines prone to underwater landslides, existing cables that shouldn't be crossed carelessly, shipwrecks, coral formations worth protecting. The route that finally gets chosen is rarely the shortest possible path; it's the shortest path that also keeps the cable off unstable seafloor and clear of the routes ships are known to anchor along.

Specialized cable ships, of which only a modest number exist worldwide, carry enormous coiled stores of cable in tanks belowdecks and pay it out over the stern at a controlled rate as they steam along the surveyed route, typically at little more than walking pace. In shallow coastal waters, a plow towed behind the ship buries the cable a meter or more beneath the seabed for protection; in the deep ocean, burial isn't practical or necessary, and the cable simply lies on the bottom, resting wherever gravity and the terrain put it. The entire process, for a long transoceanic system, can take the better part of a year, and that's before the cable is even carrying traffic — each one needs to be tested extensively before it goes live.

## The Amplifiers Nobody Sees

Light traveling through glass fiber degrades gradually with distance, growing dimmer as it goes, and after some tens of kilometers it needs to be boosted or the signal simply fades into noise. On land, that's a straightforward matter of installing repeater equipment somewhere convenient. In the middle of the ocean, at depths that can exceed several kilometers, it's a genuine engineering feat: repeater units, sealed in pressure-resistant housings and spliced directly into the cable at regular intervals along its length, sit on the seafloor for the cable's entire operational life, powered by electrical current sent down a conductor running the whole length of the cable from stations on land.

These repeaters have no on-site maintenance, ever. A unit that fails partway through a multi-decade cable lifespan generally can't be repaired without hauling up that entire section of cable, an enormous undertaking, so they are engineered with an emphasis on sheer longevity that has few parallels in ordinary consumer or even enterprise electronics. A repeater is expected, essentially, to work without attention for the cable's entire service life, sitting under enormous pressure in near-freezing water, drawing power continuously, amplifying an optical signal it never once stops passing along.

## Chokepoints and Sharp Objects

For a system this critical, undersea cable infrastructure is more fragile than most people assume, and the threats to it are almost never dramatic. The leading cause of cable damage worldwide, year after year, isn't sabotage or natural disaster — it's fishing trawlers dragging nets across the seabed and ships dropping anchor in the wrong place. Certain narrow, shallow waterways where large numbers of these cables converge and where ship traffic is dense — the approaches to major shipping hubs, stretches of narrow seas, parts of busy channels — see a disproportionate share of the world's cable faults for exactly this reason. Sharks have, on rare recorded occasions, bitten cables near the surface, apparently drawn to the faint electromagnetic field generated by the current running through them, prompting some manufacturers to wrap vulnerable sections in extra layers of protective material as a precaution.

When a cable does break, repair follows a well-established routine: a cable ship steams to the location, uses a grapnel to hook the cable and haul the broken ends up from the seafloor, splices in a new section, tests it, and lowers the repaired cable back down. It's a process measured in days to weeks rather than hours, which is precisely why every major cable route is built with redundancy in mind — traffic that would have crossed a damaged cable is meant to reroute automatically across others, assuming enough spare capacity exists elsewhere on the route, which is not always a safe assumption when several cables happen to share the same narrow chokepoint.

## Who Owns the Wires Now

For most of the history of transoceanic cables, they were built and owned by consortiums of national telecommunications carriers, each contributing funding in exchange for a share of the finished capacity, a model suited to an era when cables mostly carried telephone calls and international data traffic was comparatively modest. That picture has shifted substantially over the last couple of decades. The largest consumers of international bandwidth today are not telephone companies but a handful of technology companies whose services generate enormous volumes of video, cloud storage traffic, and data replication between their own facilities on different continents.

Those companies have increasingly moved from being customers of cable systems to being owners of them outright, funding entire new cables built primarily or exclusively to move their own traffic, sometimes in partnership with a traditional carrier and sometimes alone. It's a quiet but significant shift in who controls a piece of infrastructure that nearly the entire world now depends on without ever thinking about it.

## An Old Idea in New Materials

There is something almost poetic in how little the basic concept has changed since the first telegraph cables were laid across ocean floors in the nineteenth century, connecting continents with a copper wire that could carry, at first, only a slow trickle of Morse code. The materials are entirely different now — glass instead of copper, light instead of electrical pulses, capacity that would have been unimaginable to the engineers of that earlier era — but the fundamental challenge those first cable-layers faced is exactly the challenge faced today: getting a fragile, valuable line across thousands of kilometers of open water, keeping it working for decades once it's down there, and accepting that a certain amount of damage from ships, fishing gear, and the ordinary hazards of the seafloor is simply the cost of doing business this way. A layer of glass, steel, and seawater-resistant polymer, lying largely forgotten on the ocean floor, still does more to connect the modern world than anything orbiting above it.
