# Border Gateway Protocol and the Internet's Web of Trust

Nobody runs the internet. There is no central switchboard, no master control room, no single company or government whose routers everyone else ultimately answers to. What exists instead is a confederation of tens of thousands of independently operated networks — internet service providers, universities, cloud companies, content platforms, government agencies — each responsible for its own slice of address space, each making its own decisions about who it will exchange traffic with and on what terms. That every one of these independent fiefdoms can nonetheless deliver a packet to almost any address on the planet, usually in well under a tenth of a second, is the work of a single protocol most people have never heard of: the Border Gateway Protocol, or BGP.

Each of these independently operated networks is called an autonomous system, and each is identified by a number, an AS number, assigned by one of the regional registries that parcel out internet numbering resources. A large residential internet provider is an autonomous system. So is a major university, a national research network, or a big technology company, which often runs several of its own. What BGP does is let these autonomous systems tell each other, constantly and in real time, which blocks of address space they can reach and by which path.

## Announcing a Path, Not Just a Destination

Interior routing protocols, the kind that operate inside a single company's own network, generally care about finding the shortest or fastest path to a destination, measured in something like hop count or link speed. BGP works differently, and the difference matters. When an autonomous system announces a route — "I can reach this block of addresses" — it doesn't just claim the destination. It lists the entire chain of autonomous systems the announcement has passed through to get there, called the AS path. A network receiving that announcement can then pass it along to its own neighbors, tacking its own number onto the front of the path, so that everyone downstream can see not just that a destination is reachable, but exactly which sequence of networks a packet would cross to get there.

This path information serves two purposes at once. It lets a network avoid loops — if you see your own AS number already present somewhere in an announced path, you know accepting that route would send packets in a circle, so you discard it. And it gives each network something to make policy decisions on. A network might prefer a shorter path, but it might just as easily prefer a longer one that avoids a competitor's network, or one that routes through a partner it has a favorable financial arrangement with. BGP doesn't dictate these choices; it simply gives each autonomous system the information and lets it decide, locally and unilaterally, which of the paths it hears about it actually wants to use and pass on to others.

## Peering, Transit, and Money

Underneath the protocol's mechanics sits a layer of business relationships that shapes how routes actually propagate. Two networks of roughly comparable size, each with plenty of its own customers, often agree to exchange traffic directly and without payment — a relationship called peering, justified because it benefits both roughly equally and saves both the cost of routing that traffic through a third party. A smaller network, on the other hand, typically has to pay a larger one for transit: the right to have its traffic carried onward to the rest of the internet, reaching destinations the smaller network has no direct relationship with of its own.

These relationships are invisible in the raw mechanics of route announcement but they govern it completely. An autonomous system generally will not announce, to a peer, routes it learned from a different peer, because doing so would mean carrying that peer's traffic for free across its own infrastructure to benefit a third party who isn't paying for the privilege. It will, however, announce essentially everything it knows to a paying customer, since that's precisely the service being sold. The tangled, seemingly organic structure of who routes through whom on the modern internet is, underneath, a fairly legible map of who has commercial agreements with whom, rendered into thousands of individual routing decisions made independently by each network's own operators.

## Where Peering Actually Happens

Much of this peering doesn't happen over long private lines strung between distant company headquarters. It happens at internet exchange points: physical facilities, often occupying a floor or a room within a much larger data center, where dozens or hundreds of different networks bring their own equipment and connect to a shared switching fabric maintained by the exchange operator. Once connected to that fabric, a network can establish peering relationships with many other participants without needing to run a separate physical link to each one individually, turning what would otherwise be an enormous tangle of point-to-point connections into something closer to a single shared meeting room.

Some of these exchange points have grown into significant pieces of infrastructure in their own right, handling a substantial share of a country's or region's internet traffic through a single building. The decision about where to place a new one, or where to lease space within an existing one, is itself a quiet piece of business strategy governed by much the same calculations that shape route announcements: how many networks already gather there, how much it costs to connect, and how good the resulting paths will be to the destinations that network's own customers care about reaching. A well-placed exchange point can shave a meaningful amount of latency off traffic that would otherwise have to travel out to a distant peering location and back, simply by giving two networks a way to hand traffic to each other in the same city, or sometimes literally the same room, where both of their customers already happen to be.

## Taking Everyone's Word for It

BGP was designed in the late 1980s, in an internet small enough that the networks participating in it mostly knew each other, at least by reputation, and had little incentive to lie. The protocol reflects that era: when a network announces a route, its neighbors generally accept the announcement without any built-in mechanism to verify that the announcing network actually owns, or has been legitimately authorized to route, the address space it claims. This omission has aged into one of the most persistent structural weaknesses of the modern internet.

The failure mode this permits is usually accidental rather than malicious, though it can be either. A network operator makes a configuration error and announces a block of addresses it doesn't actually control — a typo in a router configuration, perhaps, or a filter that was supposed to be narrower than it turned out to be — and that erroneous announcement propagates outward through peering relationships before anyone catches it. Because BGP has no built-in way to distinguish a legitimate announcement from an illegitimate one, other networks, seeing a path to a destination, may simply start sending traffic that way, sometimes in preference to the correct path, if the bogus announcement happens to look shorter or more specific. Traffic bound for the real destination gets pulled instead toward whichever network mistakenly, or occasionally maliciously, claimed it. Incidents like this have, on more than one well-documented occasion, made a major website briefly unreachable across large stretches of the internet, simply because one distant, unrelated network's router configuration went wrong in exactly the wrong way, and other networks had no basis for distrusting what it announced.

## Patching a Foundation While Standing On It

Fixing this properly has proven to be one of those problems that is conceptually straightforward and practically glacial. The general approach, called Resource Public Key Infrastructure, lets the organizations that legitimately hold blocks of address space cryptographically sign statements about which autonomous systems are authorized to originate routes for those blocks, and lets other networks check incoming announcements against those signed statements before accepting them. The idea has existed for years and adoption has grown steadily, but it requires cooperation across an enormous number of independently operated, commercially competitive networks, none of which is under any real obligation to move faster than its own priorities dictate.

Part of the difficulty is that the benefit of validating routes accrues mostly to everyone else on the internet rather than to the network doing the validating work. A network that carefully signs its own announcements protects other networks from being fooled by someone falsely claiming its addresses, but it does relatively little to protect that same network from being fooled by someone else's unsigned, unverified announcement. This is a fairly classic problem of incentives spread unevenly across a system with no central authority able to simply mandate a fix, and it explains a good deal about why a known, well-understood weakness has persisted for decades after the technology to substantially address it first became available.

## Trust as Infrastructure

In the meantime, the internet's routing system keeps functioning largely on the same basis it always has: not cryptographic proof, but the accumulated, informal trust of tens of thousands of network operators who have learned, mostly by making mistakes and fixing them quickly, to watch each other's behavior and raise an alarm when something looks wrong. It is a strange thing to realize about a piece of infrastructure this consequential — that its correct operation, day to day, still rests substantially on good faith and rapid human correction rather than on any mechanism that makes bad behavior impossible in the first place.
