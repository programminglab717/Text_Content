# How TCP Turns an Unreliable Network Into a Reliable One

Send a letter through the postal system and you accept, tacitly, that it might not arrive. It might get lost in a sorting facility, delivered to the wrong address, or arrive weeks late and out of sequence with the letter you sent the day before. The Internet Protocol, the addressing and routing layer underlying practically everything online, makes exactly the same non-promise. IP is often described as best effort: routers will try to get each packet where it's going, but nothing guarantees delivery, order, or timing. A packet can be dropped by a congested router, corrupted by a bad link, or duplicated by a routing hiccup, and IP itself does nothing about any of it.

This would be a serious problem if applications had to deal with it directly, and in the earliest designs, to some extent, they did. The fix that stuck, formalized in the mid-1970s by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn and refined over the years since, was to add a layer above IP whose entire job is manufacturing the illusion of a reliable, ordered, continuous connection out of a substrate that offers none of those things. That layer is the Transmission Control Protocol, and the illusion it builds is good enough that most programmers writing network code never think about the mess underneath at all.

## Saying Hello First

Before a single byte of actual data moves, two computers speaking TCP perform a small ritual known as the three-way handshake. The computer initiating the connection sends a packet with a flag called SYN set, along with a randomly chosen starting sequence number it intends to use for its own byte stream. The receiving computer replies with a packet that does two things at once: it acknowledges the first computer's SYN, and it sends its own SYN with its own randomly chosen starting sequence number, since the connection is symmetric and both sides will be sending data. The first computer then sends a final acknowledgment of that second SYN, and the handshake is complete.

Three messages just to say hello seems wasteful, but each one does real work. The random starting sequence numbers guard against old, delayed packets from a previous, unrelated connection between the same two machines being mistaken for part of a new one. And the round trip lets each side confirm not just that it can send to the other, but that the other can send back — a distinction that matters on real networks, where a path can be open in one direction and broken in the other.

## Numbering Every Byte

Once the handshake finishes, TCP treats the data flowing in each direction as a single, continuous stream of bytes, and it numbers them. Not packets — bytes. If an application hands TCP four thousand bytes to send, TCP knows precisely which sequence numbers those bytes occupy, and it can split them across as many packets as it likes, since the receiving TCP will use the sequence numbers to reassemble everything in the correct order regardless of what order the packets actually arrive in, or how they were divided along the way.

This numbering is what makes reliability possible. Every time the receiver gets a chunk of data, it sends back an acknowledgment naming the sequence number it now expects next. If the sender doesn't see that acknowledgment within an interval it has been estimating from recent round-trip times, it assumes the data, or the acknowledgment itself, was lost, and it sends the data again. This is the entire trick, really: never assume the network delivered anything until the other side confirms it, and time out and retry when confirmation doesn't come. The receiver, for its part, uses the sequence numbers to detect gaps — a chunk numbered five thousand arriving right after one numbered three thousand tells it something in between is either still in transit or gone for good, and it can hold the later data in a buffer rather than delivering it out of order to the application above.

## Not Overwhelming Either End

Reliability alone would be simple if speed didn't matter: send one chunk, wait for acknowledgment, send the next. But that wastes almost all of a fast link's capacity waiting on round-trip delay. TCP instead uses a sliding window, letting the sender have multiple chunks in flight unacknowledged at once, up to a limit the receiver advertises based on how much buffer space it currently has free. This solves flow control — keeping a fast sender from drowning a slow receiver — but it doesn't address a subtler problem: keeping a fast sender from drowning the network itself.

That second problem surfaced painfully during the mid-to-late 1980s, when the growing early internet suffered a string of what became known as congestion collapses: links would fill up, routers would drop packets, senders would interpret the drops as ordinary loss and retransmit, and the retransmissions would add yet more load to an already overloaded link, driving effective throughput toward zero even as everyone sent as fast as they possibly could. Van Jacobson's response, still the conceptual basis of TCP's congestion control today, was to make the sender infer the network's condition from the pattern of acknowledgments and losses it observes, and throttle itself accordingly — starting cautiously, ramping up while things go well, and backing off sharply the moment a loss suggests a queue somewhere has overflowed. No router tells a TCP sender to slow down; the sender has to read the tea leaves of delayed or missing acknowledgments and infer it on its own.

## Ending Things Properly

A TCP connection doesn't just stop; it closes with almost as much ceremony as it opened. Either side can initiate teardown by sending a packet with a FIN flag, meaning roughly "I have no more data to send, though I might still receive some." The other side acknowledges it, and once it too has finished sending, it sends its own FIN, which is acknowledged in turn. Because the connection is symmetric, this handshake lets each direction of the conversation close independently, which matters for protocols where one side finishes talking well before the other does.

After the final acknowledgment, the side that initiated the close typically lingers in a state called TIME_WAIT for a period before fully discarding the connection's records. This isn't laziness; it exists so that if the final acknowledgment itself gets lost and the other end retransmits its FIN, there's still a live record of the connection able to respond, rather than a new, unrelated connection accidentally receiving a stray packet left over from an old one.

## What Reliability Costs

None of this comes free, and TCP's design reflects a series of deliberate tradeoffs rather than a single obviously correct answer. The handshake before data flows, the acknowledgments after it, the buffering to hold out-of-order arrivals, the timers ticking on every unacknowledged chunk — all of it adds delay and overhead that a simpler, connectionless protocol doesn't pay. For an exchange as short as a single request and reply, the handshake alone can represent a meaningful fraction of the total time the conversation takes, which is one reason some newer application protocols have looked for ways to shave a round trip off the setup process, or have abandoned TCP's guarantees altogether for traffic where a lost or late chunk matters less than raw speed, such as live audio or video, where retransmitting a fraction-of-a-second-old frame is often less useful than simply moving on to the next one. TCP's reliability is a general-purpose tool, extremely well suited to file transfers, web pages, and anything else where correctness matters more than absolute minimum latency, but it was never meant to be the only way of moving data across the network, only the default one.

## An Illusion Worth Keeping

None of this machinery is visible to the application using it. A web browser or an email client hands TCP a stream of bytes to send and reads a stream of bytes back, and as far as either side of the application is concerned, it might as well be a direct wire. Underneath, packets are being numbered, timed, resent, reordered, and occasionally discarded and rebuilt, all so that the layer above never has to know the network was unreliable in the first place. It's a peculiar kind of honesty in reverse: the protocol's whole purpose is to lie convincingly, manufacturing order and reliability its foundation was never designed to provide.
