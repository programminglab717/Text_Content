# The Rewrite Trap

Every few years, in a meeting nobody scheduled for this purpose, somebody says it out loud: we should just rewrite it. There is usually a pause, and then a kind of collective exhale, because everyone in the room has been thinking it for months. The old system is slow to change. Its tests are flaky. Three people understand the billing module and two of them have left. The proposal that follows is almost always the same shape — a clean build, modern stack, the lessons of the last five years applied from the first commit — and the estimate that accompanies it is almost always between six and nine months. It is not a coincidence that six to nine months is roughly the longest horizon a team can picture in detail. It is the length of the imagination, not the length of the work.

What happens next is well documented enough to be a genre. The rewrite starts fast, because greenfield code always does. It reaches something that looks like feature parity surprisingly early, maybe a third of the way through the schedule, and everyone relaxes. Then it spends the remaining two-thirds of its budget, and then another budget after that, on the parts nobody put on the plan: the seventeen customers with grandfathered pricing, the batch job that has to run before the batch job that has to run before month-end close, the integration with a partner whose API returns a 200 with an error body. Meanwhile the old system is still in production, still receiving bug fixes and small features, because the business did not stop needing things. Now there are two systems, and the new one is chasing a moving target that has a whole team pushing it forward.

## Why the Old Code Looks Worse Than It Is

The single most reliable driver of the rewrite instinct is that reading code is harder than writing it, and most of what an engineer does with a legacy system is read. Writing is a generative act where the mental model comes first and the text follows. Reading is reverse engineering — reconstructing someone else's model from its residue, without the context they had. Code you wrote last year feels comprehensible because you can recall the constraints. Code someone else wrote under constraints you never saw feels like nonsense, and the natural explanation for nonsense is incompetence.

Joel Spolsky made the durable version of this argument around the turn of the millennium, and his key observation was about the weird parts. A mature codebase is full of lines that look arbitrary: a check for a null that shouldn't be possible, a retry with a strange backoff, a branch for a browser version that no longer exists. Each of those lines is usually a bug report. Somebody hit something in production, at some cost, and that line is the scar. Throw the file away and you throw away the accumulated knowledge of every edge case the system ever encountered, most of which was never written down anywhere except in the code itself. The rewrite doesn't start from zero. It starts from negative — with all the old behavior to reproduce and none of the old field experience to draw on.

This is the software version of Chesterton's fence: before you remove something whose purpose is unclear, find out why it's there. The trouble is that a rewrite removes several thousand fences at once, and the cost of each removal arrives individually, months later, as an incident.

There's a second, subtler force pulling in the same direction. Fred Brooks called it the second-system effect: the most dangerous system an architect ever designs is the second one, because it collects every feature deferred from the first, every generalization that seemed elegant in hindsight, every abstraction the designer promised themselves they'd get right next time. First systems are lean because their builders are cautious and ignorant. Second systems are bloated because their builders are confident and experienced, which turns out to be a worse combination than it sounds.

## The Specification Is the Code

Teams planning a rewrite usually assume they know what the system does. They know what it's supposed to do. Those diverge more than anyone expects, and the divergence is where the schedule goes.

Hyrum Wright's observation about interfaces captures the mechanism: with enough users, every observable behavior of a system will be depended on by somebody, regardless of what the contract says. A response field that happens to be sorted, a timestamp that happens to have millisecond precision, an error that happens to arrive before rather than after a side effect — none of these are promises, and all of them have become promises by the time a system is a decade old. A rewrite honors the documented contract and breaks the undocumented one, and the undocumented one is the one customers actually built against.

Data makes this worse. A long-lived production database is a geological record: rows written by four generations of application code, each with different validation rules, including a stratum from the year the email field was optional and another from the migration that didn't quite finish. The new schema is designed for data as it should be. The migration has to handle data as it is. Teams routinely budget weeks for cutover and spend quarters, because the hard part was never building the new thing — it was the dual-write period, the backfill, the reconciliation job that keeps finding discrepancies of a few hundred rows that nobody can explain.

Netscape's rewrite is the case everyone cites, and it deserves its status. Rather than continue evolving a browser that was messy but shipping, the company committed to a ground-up rebuild of its layout engine. Years passed without a competitive release. By the time the new engine matured into something genuinely good — and it did, eventually, become the foundation of Firefox — the market share it was meant to defend had gone to Internet Explorer and was not coming back. The rewrite succeeded technically and lost the war that prompted it, which is the characteristic failure mode: not a crash, but an absence during the period that mattered.

## When a Rewrite Is Actually the Right Call

The argument against rewrites gets overstated by people who have been burned, and there are situations where incremental change is genuinely the more expensive path.

The clearest is when the ground beneath the system is gone: a runtime nobody patches anymore, a vendor database that's end-of-life, a language version with unfixed security holes and no upgrade path. If the platform is dead, the code's quality is irrelevant. Another is a genuine change in the problem, not just the implementation — a system built to process a few thousand records nightly that now needs to handle a continuous stream is not badly written, it's answering a question nobody is asking anymore. Small scope helps enormously: rewriting one service with a well-defined interface and a week of work behind it is an ordinary refactor wearing a dramatic name. And it matters a great deal whether the people who built the original are still around to say what the strange lines were for.

The reliable warning sign is the opposite set of conditions: a large system, an unclear boundary, no one left from the original team, and a justification that lives mostly in the register of feeling — it's a mess, it's unmaintainable, nobody wants to touch it. Those statements may be true and still not be a plan.

## Strangling Instead of Replacing

The incremental alternative has a name, borrowed by Martin Fowler from a tree that germinates in the canopy, sends roots down around its host, and is eventually left standing as a hollow cylinder after the original trunk rots away. You put a routing layer in front of the old system. You pick one endpoint, one job, one bounded piece of behavior, and you implement it in the new system. You route a small fraction of traffic there, compare results against the old path, and when you believe it, move the rest. Then you delete the old code for that piece — actually delete it, because a strangler that never removes anything is just a second system with extra steps. Repeat until the old trunk is hollow.

This is slower in the way that compound interest is slower. It is also the only approach where every week of work produces something in production, where the old system's undocumented behaviors get discovered one at a time instead of all at once during cutover, and where the project can be stopped halfway and still have delivered value. A rewrite is a bet that pays out only at the end, and organizations are terrible at sustaining a bet with no interim payouts through a leadership change, a reorg, or a bad quarter.

What makes this hard is rarely technical. It's that a rewrite is often a proxy for a conversation the organization doesn't want to have directly. Sometimes the real issue is that no one owns the system, and a rewrite creates an owner. Sometimes a strong engineer wants a green field and will leave without one, and the rewrite is a retention strategy nobody will admit to. Sometimes the team is demoralized and a fresh start is the only morale lever anyone can find. These are real problems with real costs, and they deserve real solutions — but they don't get solved by a rewrite, they just get postponed behind one, and they come back with the same mess to clean up plus a year of lost ground.

There's a question worth asking in the meeting where someone proposes the rewrite, and it separates the serious proposals from the rest. Name three behaviors of the current system that you don't understand. Not three things that are ugly — three things that are there and whose purpose is a mystery to you. If the answer is that there aren't any, the system hasn't been examined closely enough to be replaced. If the answer comes easily, and there are more than three, that's the actual size of the job.
