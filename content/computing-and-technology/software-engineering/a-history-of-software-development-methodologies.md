# A History of Software Development Methodologies

In October 1968, a few dozen people gathered in Garmisch, in the Bavarian Alps, at a conference sponsored by NATO's science committee. The organizers had chosen a deliberately provocative title. Calling the subject "software engineering" was meant as an argument rather than a description — the claim was that building large programs ought to be a discipline with methods and standards, and manifestly was not one yet. The discussions that week circled around a shared embarrassment. Projects were running years late. Budgets were being exceeded by multiples. Delivered systems sometimes did not work at all, and the people involved could not explain why this kept happening to intelligent, well-funded teams.

What they were describing was real and structural. Programs had grown from artifacts one person could hold in their head to systems requiring hundreds of people, and nothing about the craft scaled with that growth. Fred Brooks, writing a few years later out of his experience managing IBM's OS/360, would put the central asymmetry memorably: adding people to a late software project makes it later, because the communication paths between them multiply faster than the work gets divided. Nearly everything that followed over the next half century can be read as successive attempts to answer the problem that conference named.

## The Waterfall Nobody Actually Prescribed

The first systematic answer borrowed from disciplines that already knew how to coordinate large efforts. Civil engineering and hardware manufacturing worked in phases, with documented handoffs between specialists, and the analogy seemed obvious: gather requirements, write a design, implement it, test it, deploy it, maintain it, each phase completed and signed off before the next begins.

There is a persistent irony in how this model became canonical. The paper most often credited with it, Winston Royce's 1970 description of managing large software developments, presents the simple sequential diagram early on and then says plainly that implementing it this way is risky and invites failure. Royce's actual recommendations were iterative — build a pilot version first, involve the customer throughout, expect the design to change once implementation reveals what the design got wrong. Later readers kept the diagram and dropped the argument. The name "waterfall" was attached afterward by others, and the model hardened into procurement policy, most consequentially in United States defense standards of the 1980s that required contractors to produce a specified sequence of documents and reviews. When a methodology becomes a contracting requirement, it stops being a hypothesis about how work goes best and becomes a structure for assigning blame, which is a different thing entirely.

The sequential model was not stupid. It solved a genuine problem — coordination at scale among people who could not all talk to each other — and in domains where requirements truly are fixed and the cost of a late defect is catastrophic, something close to it remains appropriate. Its weakness was an assumption it inherited from the physical trades: that you can know what you want before you build it. For most software, the act of building is how the requirements get discovered, and a process that forbids learning after the design review forces everyone to either lie or file change requests.

## Structure, Modules, and the Long Object-Oriented Detour

Alongside process, the same decades produced ideas about how code itself should be organized, and these mattered as much. Dijkstra's short 1968 letter arguing against unrestricted goto statements launched the structured programming movement, which established that a program's text should correspond legibly to its execution. David Parnas made a subtler and more durable argument a few years later about decomposing systems into modules: the right boundaries are not the steps of the process, but the decisions likely to change, each hidden behind an interface so that changing it does not ripple outward. That single idea — information hiding — underlies essentially every architectural fashion since.

The 1980s brought two waves that promised to industrialize development. CASE tools offered to generate code from diagrams, on the theory that the bottleneck was typing rather than thinking; they mostly produced expensive diagrams. Object orientation was more substantial, and by the early 1990s had become the assumed default, carrying with it a whole apparatus of modeling notation that converged into UML and a heavyweight process, the Rational Unified Process, that wrapped iteration in a large formal structure of phases, disciplines, and artifacts. Barry Boehm's spiral model belongs to this period too, and was the most intellectually honest of the bunch: it organized development around risk, with each loop identifying the biggest remaining unknown and building something specifically to resolve it. Other strands pushed differently — Harlan Mills's cleanroom approach at IBM emphasized statistical quality control and formal verification, while James Martin's rapid application development argued for timeboxes and prototypes against the document-heavy norm.

## The Lightweight Revolt

By the mid-1990s a number of practitioners, working independently, arrived at similar conclusions from the opposite direction. Rather than adding rigor to the sequential model, they proposed removing most of it.

Jeff Sutherland and Ken Schwaber formalized Scrum, taking the name from a Harvard Business Review article by Takeuchi and Nonaka that had used the rugby metaphor to describe product teams moving down the field together rather than passing work between specialist stages. Scrum kept a deliberately thin skeleton: a fixed-length iteration, a prioritized list of work, a daily coordination meeting, a review with stakeholders, and a retrospective on the process itself. Kent Beck's Extreme Programming, developed on a payroll project at Chrysler, went further and made specific engineering practices part of the method rather than leaving them to taste — pair programming, test-first development, continuous integration, collective ownership of all code, and a refusal to design far ahead of demonstrated need. Others were working in parallel: Alistair Cockburn's Crystal family, feature-driven development, the British DSDM consortium's timeboxing.

In February 2001, seventeen of these people met at a ski lodge in Snowbird, Utah, and produced a short document. The Agile Manifesto asserted four preferences — individuals and interactions over processes and tools, working software over comprehensive documentation, customer collaboration over contract negotiation, responding to change over following a plan — each phrased to acknowledge value on both sides while stating which the signatories weighted more heavily. It is a remarkably modest text for the industry it reorganized, and the modesty is routinely stripped out when it is quoted. The signatories agreed on very little beyond those preferences; some of them disliked each other's methods considerably.

## Agile Becomes an Industry

What happened next is the familiar fate of a successful idea. A movement built around reducing process overhead was absorbed by an economy of certifications, two-day training courses, consultancies, and scaling frameworks designed to apply iteration to organizations with thousands of developers. Enterprises adopted the ceremonies and skipped the engineering practices, because standing meetings and two-week iterations can be mandated from above while test-driven development and refactoring cannot. The predictable result was the thing practitioners started calling agile in name only: sequential development with all its handoffs intact, chopped into fortnights, with a daily status meeting bolted on and a burndown chart pointed at a fixed deadline that nobody was permitted to move.

The critique cuts in more than one direction, though. Some of the manifesto's original commitments genuinely do not survive contact with large organizations, regulated industries, or hardware dependencies. A team can respond to change quickly only if the surrounding organization can absorb a changed answer, and most cannot. Meanwhile the lightweight methods' own blind spots became visible with time — an aversion to upfront design that sometimes produced systems needing architectural surgery later, and a focus on the single co-located team that offered little guidance for coordinating dozens of them.

## Continuous Everything

The most consequential shift after 2001 came from a direction the manifesto had not emphasized much: operations. Agile teams had compressed development cycles to weeks while release remained a quarterly event involving change advisory boards, manual deployment runbooks, and a separate department whose incentives rewarded stability and therefore rewarded saying no. The bottleneck had moved, and the response was a set of practices for removing it — automated build and test pipelines, infrastructure defined as code and provisioned on demand, deployment automated to the point of being unremarkable, and shared responsibility for production between the people who wrote the code and the people who ran it.

The word DevOps came out of a small conference Patrick Debois organized in Ghent in 2009, and the argument spread rapidly on the strength of demonstrations that it worked at scale — a widely discussed talk that year on performing many deploys a day at Flickr showed people that release frequency was a choice rather than a physical constraint. Continuous delivery formalized the discipline: keep the software in a releasable state at all times, so the decision to ship becomes a business decision rather than an engineering event. Feature flags decoupled deployment from release, letting code reach production before it reaches users. Cloud infrastructure made the whole apparatus affordable for teams that could not have bought a staging environment a decade earlier.

Strip away the branding and a single trajectory runs through all of it. Every method since Garmisch has been an attempt to shorten the interval between making a decision and discovering whether it was correct. The phased model set that interval at the length of the project. Iteration cut it to weeks. Continuous delivery cuts it to hours, and automated testing cuts it to seconds for the narrow questions a test can answer. The arguments about ceremonies and certifications are mostly noise around that measurement, which is the one worth watching when a new methodology arrives claiming to be different.
