# Open Source Governance and Maintainer Burnout

The issue has been open for two years. It has forty-one comments, of which thirty are variations on "any update on this?" and one is a patch that would work if it didn't break the other thing. The maintainer reads it on a Sunday afternoon at a kitchen table, decides again not to answer, and closes the tab. Somewhere downstream, a Fortune 500 company has this library in production, generating revenue that would comfortably fund a small team, and has never contributed a line, a dollar, or a thank-you note. The maintainer has never met anyone from that company and never will.

This arrangement holds up a remarkable portion of the world's software, and it holds up mostly by accident. A well-known xkcd strip drew the whole edifice of modern digital infrastructure as a precarious stack of blocks resting on one thin piece that some anonymous person in Nebraska has been thanklessly maintaining since 2003. It landed because everyone who works in the field recognized a specific dependency in their own stack. Usually several.

## Writing It Is the Fun Part

The asymmetry at the center of this is that creating a library and maintaining one are completely different jobs, and only the first one is the job people signed up for. Writing the thing is creative work with a clear arc and a satisfying end. Maintaining it is a service position: triaging bug reports of wildly varying quality, explaining for the ninetieth time that the error means the config file is in the wrong place, shepherding pull requests from contributors who will never return, keeping continuous integration green against six runtime versions, cutting releases, handling security reports from researchers who want a response within twenty-four hours, and absorbing the occasional stranger who is furious at you for something you gave them for free.

The demand side scales and the supply side does not. A project that gets adopted by a popular framework can go from a few thousand installs a week to millions without a single line changing, and the author's circumstances — one person, evenings, a day job, a family — remain exactly as they were. Issue volume roughly tracks users. Capacity tracks nothing.

Gratitude and criticism also arrive at wildly different rates. People who install a library and have it simply work say nothing, because there is nothing to say. People who hit an edge case at 4pm on a deadline open an issue, and the tone of that issue reflects the deadline rather than any considered opinion of the maintainer. The feedback a maintainer receives is therefore a heavily biased sample, skewed toward frustration, which is a reliable recipe for concluding that the world resents you when in fact the world mostly doesn't think about you at all.

## Governance Is Load-Bearing, Not Bureaucratic

It's tempting to treat governance — charters, steering committees, voting procedures — as ceremony that large projects put on once they stop being fun. The projects that have lived through a governance crisis describe it differently. Governance is what determines who absorbs conflict, and conflict absorption is a substantial fraction of what exhausts maintainers.

The benevolent dictator model is the oldest and, for small projects, the most efficient. One person has final say, decisions are quick, the project has a coherent design sensibility. It also means one person is personally the target of every contested decision, permanently. Python's long-running experiment with this ended in 2018, when Guido van Rossum stepped back from the role after a bruising public argument over assignment expressions — a syntax change that, by any reasonable measure, was not worth what the debate cost. The community replaced him with an elected steering council, which is slower and less elegant and spreads the weight of a controversial decision across five people rather than concentrating it on one. That trade has proven durable.

The foundation model goes further. The Apache Software Foundation's structure — committers who earn commit rights through contribution, project management committees that hold authority collectively, and a foundation that owns the trademark and provides legal shelter — was designed so that no individual and no employer can capture a project. Its slogan about valuing community over code isn't sentiment; it encodes the observation that code can be rewritten and a functioning community cannot. Similar reasoning drives the Linux Foundation and CNCF, where the real product is vendor neutrality: a place where competing companies can contribute engineers to shared infrastructure without any one of them being able to close the door later.

The corporate-steward model is now extremely common and has the obvious advantage that maintainers get paid, full-time, with benefits. It also has a failure mode that the last several years have made unmissable. A company that owns a project can change its license, and several have — moving from permissive terms to source-available ones once cloud providers began reselling the software as a service. Each time, the community's recourse has been the same one it has always had: fork. Elasticsearch produced OpenSearch. Terraform produced OpenTofu. These forks are expensive and disruptive, and they work, which is precisely why the right to fork is the foundational governance mechanism of open source rather than an edge case. It was also the mechanism behind the io.js split from Node.js in 2014, where the dispute was about release cadence and control rather than licensing; that one ended in reconciliation, with the reunified project adopting the open technical steering committee the forkers had wanted.

The point running through all of these is that a project without written-down governance still has governance — it's just implicit, contested, and relitigated in every heated thread. Writing it down doesn't make the conflicts disappear. It makes them resolvable without anyone's personal exhaustion being the deciding factor.

## The Security Bill Comes Due

For a long time the burnout problem could be framed as a labor issue or a fairness issue, which meant it could be deferred. A series of incidents converted it into a security issue, which is harder to ignore.

The left-pad episode in 2016 was the comic version: a developer in a dispute over a package name unpublished his modules from npm, one of which was a few lines long and depended upon, transitively, by an enormous amount of the JavaScript ecosystem. Builds broke worldwide within minutes. The registry changed its unpublishing rules afterward, but the underlying lesson was about the shape of dependency graphs — enormous structures resting on tiny, individually-owned pieces.

Log4Shell in late 2021 was the serious version. A critical vulnerability in a logging library embedded in a staggering number of enterprise Java applications turned into a global emergency, and the people expected to fix it over the holidays were a small group of volunteers who had been maintaining it in their spare time for years while the companies now demanding an immediate patch had contributed nothing.

And the xz utils backdoor in 2024 was the version that should have ended the argument. A compression utility present on essentially every Linux system had one overloaded maintainer, who had said publicly that his capacity was limited. Over roughly two years, an attacker operating under a pseudonym became a helpful, productive contributor, took on maintenance work, and gradually acquired release authority — a process assisted by other accounts appearing in the project's mailing list to complain about slow progress and push for the new contributor to be given more control. Having earned that trust, the attacker inserted an obfuscated backdoor into the release tarballs, targeting authentication on remote servers. It was caught almost by chance, by a Microsoft engineer investigating why SSH logins were a fraction of a second slower than expected. The attack's actual attack surface was not a buffer overflow. It was a tired volunteer and a community norm of being grateful for help.

## What Helps, and What Only Looks Like Help

Money is necessary and insufficient. Sponsorship platforms and foundation grants have made a real difference for some projects, but a maintainer with a full-time job and modest donation income has not gained time — they've gained income and a heightened sense of obligation, which can accelerate burnout rather than relieve it. The arrangements that work tend to be ones that buy time explicitly: an employer assigning a portion of someone's paid week to upstream work, or a foundation hiring maintainers outright.

The structural fixes are less glamorous. More than one person with release keys, so that illness or a bad month doesn't stall the project or create the vacuum an attacker can fill. Written scope — a statement of what the project deliberately does not do, so that refusing a feature is a policy rather than a personal rejection to be argued with. Permission to say no, and a culture that treats it as maintenance work rather than rudeness, since every accepted feature is a permanent obligation. Honest archiving: marking a project unmaintained is a service to its users, and far better than the slow rot of a repository that looks alive because the README is still cheerful.

From the consuming side, the change that matters is treating dependencies as relationships rather than free goods. A company that would not hesitate to pay six figures for a vendor support contract will build a critical path through a library maintained by one person on weekends, and then file an urgent issue when it breaks. The license that library ships under says the software is provided as is, without warranty of any kind. Everyone reads that clause as a legal formality. It is in fact an accurate description of the arrangement, and the only party who ever actually agreed to it is the person who wrote the code.
