# Estimating Software Work

Someone catches you in a hallway and asks how long the export feature will take. You think about it for maybe four seconds — there's a table, there's a CSV writer somewhere, the hard part is the date formatting — and you say two days. Nine days later it ships, and in between there was a discovery that the report needs to include archived records, a half-day on a timezone bug, a rewrite of the query when it turned out to lock a table, two rounds of review, a flaky integration test, and a Tuesday that disappeared into someone else's incident. Nothing went catastrophically wrong. This is what two days looks like.

The interesting question is not why you were wrong. It's why, knowing that this happens every time, the next hallway estimate will also be two days. People do not learn from their own estimation history nearly as much as you'd expect, and the reason is well studied outside software entirely.

## The Work Is, by Definition, Work Nobody Has Done

Software has a property that makes it unusually hostile to estimation: anything done twice gets turned into a function, a library, a template, or a script. The repetitive parts are continuously eliminated by the nature of the medium. What remains on an engineer's plate is, almost by construction, the part that hasn't been done before in this exact configuration. A bricklayer estimating a wall is drawing on thousands of nearly identical bricks. A developer estimating a feature is estimating a piece of novel design work with an unknown number of surprises in it, then reporting a number as though it were bricks.

This is compounded by the planning fallacy, which Kahneman and Tversky identified as a general property of human forecasting rather than an engineering vice. People asked how long a task will take produce something close to a best-case scenario — the path where nothing goes wrong — even when they have direct personal experience that things go wrong. The bias survives being told about it. It survives keeping records. What partially defeats it is switching from the inside view, in which you imagine the steps of this particular task, to the outside view, in which you ask how long tasks of this general kind have actually taken. That second question feels less intelligent, since it ignores everything specific about the task at hand, and it produces substantially better answers.

There's a reason the outside view wins. When you imagine the steps, you can only enumerate the steps you can think of, and the schedule is blown by the ones you can't. Decomposition — breaking work into pieces and adding them up — is genuinely useful and carries this bias baked in: the sum of the parts you remembered omits the parts you forgot, and the forgotten parts are invisible by definition. Historical data includes them, without needing to name them.

## Estimates, Targets, and Commitments Are Three Different Things

Steve McConnell drew a distinction that resolves a startling amount of ordinary workplace friction. An estimate is a prediction: an honest assessment of how long something is likely to take. A target is a business desire: we want this before the conference. A commitment is a promise: we will deliver this by that date.

These are routinely collapsed into one number, usually in the direction of the target. An engineer offers an estimate, hears it repeated back in a planning document as a date, and discovers a month later that they made a promise. Once that's happened a few times, engineers start padding, and once managers notice the padding they start discounting, and both sides are now negotiating rather than forecasting. The estimate stops carrying information about the work and starts carrying information about the relationship.

Anchoring makes this worse and is trivially easy to do accidentally. A manager who opens with "we were hoping to have this by the end of the month" has just set the number, and every subsequent estimate in the room will cluster near it regardless of the work involved. Teams that care about the quality of their forecasts elicit them before stating any target, and often collect them independently before discussion, for the same reason that juries deliberate after individual verdicts rather than before.

The single-number habit is its own problem. "Two days" destroys everything the estimator actually knows. "Two days if the payment library behaves the way the docs describe, two weeks if it doesn't, and I can find out which by Thursday afternoon" communicates the shape of the uncertainty and points directly at the most valuable next action. Ranges feel evasive to people who want a date, but a range with a stated reason is more useful for planning than a point estimate that quietly buries a coin flip.

## Why Points Were Invented, and How They Die

Story points came out of an attempt to dodge these dynamics. The theory: engineers are bad at estimating absolute duration but decent at relative sizing — this is about twice the work of that — and if you size in abstract units, measure how many units the team completes per iteration, and divide, you get a forecast grounded in that team's actual throughput rather than anyone's optimism. The abstraction was meant to be a shield. A point is not a day, so a point cannot be turned into a promise.

In practice the shield leaks, and it leaks in a predictable sequence. Someone works out that the team does roughly twenty points a fortnight, so a point is roughly half a day. The mapping is now fixed. Then velocity gets reported upward and compared between teams, at which point it has become a productivity metric, and any measure used for evaluation will be optimized directly. Estimates inflate. The same work costs more points each quarter and the chart shows improvement. Nothing has changed except the units, and the team has lost the honest planning conversation the practice was supposed to protect.

The empirical alternative skips estimation for most work. Count finished items and measure how long each one took from start to finish. That distribution — not the average, the whole spread — is the forecast. If the last fifty tasks finished within nine days at the eighty-fifth percentile, then a new task of no special character will probably finish within nine days, and you didn't have to hold a meeting to find out. For multi-item projects, running a simple simulation over the historical distribution produces a probabilistic delivery range that outperforms most estimation ceremonies, largely because it silently includes every overhead the team habitually forgets: review latency, interruptions, holidays, the incident that ate Tuesday.

This works because task sizes within a given team tend to be less variable than people assume, and because the variation that matters is mostly wait time rather than work time. A task that takes nine days usually contains about six hours of typing. The rest is queueing — waiting for review, for a decision, for a dependent team, for an environment. That observation reframes the improvement question entirely: cutting work in progress shortens delivery time more reliably than estimating it better, because items sitting in a queue are the bulk of the elapsed time.

## Estimating Is a Way of Finding Out What You Don't Know

The most valuable output of an estimation session is frequently not the number. It's the moment when two engineers discover they've been picturing different features, or when someone asks whether this needs to work for the enterprise tenants and the room goes quiet. Widely divergent estimates are a signal worth more than the average of those estimates — they mean the group does not share a model of the work, and averaging them papers over exactly the disagreement that would have been useful.

When the uncertainty is concentrated in one unknown, the right move is to buy information rather than guess. Timebox a spike: two days to determine whether the third-party API supports partial refunds, with a written answer at the end and no expectation of shippable code. This converts an estimate that would have been a wild range into two known days plus a much tighter range afterward. Teams under schedule pressure resist this because it feels like two days of not building, which is precisely backwards — those two days are the cheapest part of the project.

There is a strand of argument that concludes most estimation should be abandoned entirely, and it has a real point: for a team doing continuous small-batch delivery, estimating individual items costs meaningful time and adds little over just counting. But the position overreaches when it forgets why anyone asks. Sometimes the question is idle. Often it isn't — there's a contract with a date in it, a hardware launch, a regulatory deadline, a marketing campaign someone has to book, a decision about whether this project is worth doing at all compared to the other one. "We'll know when it's done" is not an answer a business can plan around, and treating the request as illegitimate tends to get engineers excluded from the conversation where the date is actually set.

What the estimating team owes the business is not accuracy, which is unattainable, but honesty about uncertainty. A wide range offered early and narrowed publicly as work proceeds is more useful than a confident number that turns out to be fiction, even though it is much less satisfying to hear. The confident number buys three weeks of calm and then detonates. The range is uncomfortable immediately and stays approximately true, which is the only thing an estimate can be asked to do.
