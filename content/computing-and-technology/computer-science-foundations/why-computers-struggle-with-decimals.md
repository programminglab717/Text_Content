# Why Computers Struggle With Decimals

Type 0.1 plus 0.2 into almost any programming language and, instead of the tidy 0.3 a calculator or a schoolchild would give you, you will often get something like 0.30000000000000004. Nothing is broken. No bug has crept into the software. The machine is doing exactly what it was built to do, precisely and consistently, and the strange extra digits are not an error so much as an honest confession about a limitation baked into how computers represent numbers that are not whole.

## Binary Doesn't Do Tenths

The trouble starts with the choice of base. Computers store everything in binary, using only the digits zero and one, and while every whole number has an exact binary representation, most ordinary decimal fractions do not. This is not a flaw specific to binary; it is the same phenomenon that shows up in familiar decimal notation when you try to write one-third as a decimal. One-third is a perfectly simple, exact fraction, and yet writing it in decimal produces an endless string of threes that never resolves cleanly no matter how many digits you're willing to write. Decimal notation simply cannot represent one-third exactly, because three does not divide evenly into any power of ten. Binary has exactly the same problem with numbers like one-tenth, because five does not divide evenly into any power of two. A number that looks perfectly clean and finite in decimal, like 0.1, turns out to be an endlessly repeating fraction once translated into binary, and a computer, forced to stop somewhere, stores a very close but not perfectly exact approximation instead.

## A Standard for Approximation

Computers represent these approximate values using a scheme conceptually similar to scientific notation, but in binary: a sign to say whether the number is positive or negative, a set of digits representing the significant value, and an exponent saying how far to shift the decimal — or rather, binary — point. This format, standardized industry-wide under a specification usually just called IEEE 754, was itself a genuine achievement, because before it, different manufacturers' machines could compute the same arithmetic and quietly produce slightly different answers, making numerical software unreliable to move between different computers. The standard fixed the exact number of bits given to the significant digits and the exponent, and specified precisely how rounding should happen when a result doesn't fit exactly, so that today the same floating-point calculation, run on essentially any modern hardware, produces the identical bit pattern as its answer — predictable, just not perfectly exact.

## Where the Error Hides and Grows

A single rounding error at the seventeenth decimal digit is usually harmless, invisible in any result a person will actually look at. The trouble is that these tiny errors do not always stay tiny, and certain patterns of calculation let them grow. Subtracting two numbers that are very close to each other in value is a particularly sharp trap, known as catastrophic cancellation: the large, meaningful digits the two numbers shared cancel out, leaving behind mostly the tiny rounding error that had been hiding, invisible, in each of the original numbers, now suddenly making up a much larger share of what remains. A long chain of calculations can also accumulate rounding error step by step, and — because floating-point addition, despite appearances, is not always strictly associative in the way ordinary arithmetic is — adding the same set of numbers in a different order can occasionally produce a very slightly different final answer, a fact that surprises most people the first time they encounter it, since associativity feels like it should be one of the safest assumptions in all of arithmetic.

## Living With Imperfect Numbers

None of this means floating-point numbers are unreliable in any everyday sense; it means they demand a specific kind of caution that whole numbers never required. Comparing two floating-point results for exact equality is a common beginner's mistake, since two values that are mathematically supposed to be identical may differ by a sliver in their least significant digits purely because of the order in which they were computed; the standard fix is to check whether two values are within some acceptably small tolerance of each other rather than demanding they match exactly. Financial software in particular tends to avoid floating-point representation altogether for exactly this reason, preferring to count money in whole cents, or to use a specialized decimal format designed to represent decimal fractions exactly, precisely because a rounding error of a fraction of a cent, invisible in a physics simulation, is entirely unacceptable when it's somebody's actual money and the numbers need to add up exactly, every time, to the penny.

## The Trade-off Nobody Wants to Give Up

Given all of this, it is fair to ask why floating-point representation remains the default nearly everywhere rather than being replaced by something more exact. The honest answer is speed and range. Modern processors have dedicated hardware built specifically to perform floating-point arithmetic extremely quickly, an investment made over decades precisely because so much scientific, graphical, and engineering computation depends on it. The format also comfortably spans an enormous range of scales within the same representation, from the mass of a single particle to the distance between galaxies, without needing a different numeric type for each situation. Exact arbitrary-precision arithmetic, which avoids rounding error entirely by representing numbers as exactly as needed, does exist and is used where it matters, but it is markedly slower and hungrier for memory, because the amount of space and effort needed to store and manipulate a number grows with its precision rather than staying fixed.

Floating point's odd little surprises, in other words, are not an oversight anyone forgot to fix. They are the visible edge of a deliberate trade, made decades ago and remade in essentially every processor since, between exactness and the sheer practical speed that most computing has always needed more.
