# A Short History of Programming Languages

The earliest programmers did not write in any language at all, in the sense anyone would recognize today. To make an early electronic computer perform a different calculation, operators physically rewired it, moving cables between sockets on a large plugboard, encoding a procedure directly into the geometry of the connections rather than into any text a person could read back later. Changing the program meant changing the wiring, by hand, sometimes taking longer than actually running the calculation once the machine was finally reconfigured. Everything that followed in the history of programming languages can be read as a long series of attempts to put more and more distance between the person describing what they want a computer to do and the raw physical or numeric detail of how the machine actually does it.

## Naming the Numbers

The first real abstraction was modest but transformative: assembly language, which gave short, readable names to the numeric codes a processor's instructions were built from. Instead of writing a specific number that meant "add," a programmer could write a short mnemonic instead, and a simple translating program, an assembler, would convert it back into the exact numeric instruction the machine required. This was still extremely close to the hardware — one line of assembly corresponded to one machine instruction, and a program written for one machine's instruction set generally would not run at all on a different one — but it replaced pure numeric bookkeeping with something a human could read, write, and correct without holding an entire numeric instruction table in memory.

## Teaching Machines to Read Formulas

The next leap aimed at a different audience entirely: scientists and engineers who wanted to compute mathematical formulas without becoming experts in any particular computer's instruction set. Fortran, developed at IBM in the 1950s, let a programmer write something close to ordinary algebraic notation and have a compiler translate that formula into the many machine instructions actually required to evaluate it, handling the tedious translation automatically and, remarkably for the time, doing it efficiently enough that scientists were willing to give up the total control assembly language offered in exchange for the enormous gain in clarity and speed of writing. Around the same period, Grace Hopper's work on automatic programming pushed a similar idea toward business computing, arguing, against real skepticism at the time, that people should be able to write instructions in something closer to English and let a translating program handle the rest — an argument that fed into the eventual development of COBOL, aimed squarely at the recordkeeping and accounting tasks that made up so much of early computing's actual workload.

## A Language Built on an Idea

Not every important early language was aimed at making arithmetic convenient. Lisp, designed by John McCarthy in the late 1950s, took a strikingly different approach, built around the idea that a program's own instructions could be represented and manipulated using the very same data structures the program used for everything else. This made it natural to write programs that generated or transformed other programs, an idea that seemed exotic at the time and became foundational decades later as techniques for treating code as manipulable data spread into far more mainstream languages. Lisp's influence shows up less in its exact syntax, which many programmers still find unusual at first glance, than in the ideas it normalized: functions treated as ordinary values that can be passed around, recursion as a natural default rather than an exotic technique, and a willingness to blur the line between a program and the data it operates on.

A different, equally influential idea from roughly the same period concerned not formulas or symbols but the shape of programs themselves: organizing a program around bundles of data and the behavior attached to them, rather than around a plain sequence of instructions acting on separate data sitting elsewhere. That idea, later called object-oriented programming, was explored earlier and more thoroughly than most people realize in Smalltalk, a research language whose influence on how later mainstream languages structured programs, and even on how graphical, window-based interfaces came to look, considerably outran its own popularity as a language people actually wrote in day to day.

## Systems Languages and the Question of Portability

By the early 1970s a different kind of problem had become urgent: operating systems and the tools around them were typically written in assembly language specific to one machine, meaning the entire operating system had to be rewritten from scratch for every new kind of hardware. C, developed at Bell Labs alongside the Unix operating system, offered a language low-level enough to manipulate memory and hardware directly, the way assembly could, while still being written in a form that a compiler could retarget to a new processor without a human rewriting the whole program by hand. This combination proved enormously influential; Unix itself was rewritten in C specifically to make it portable across different manufacturers' machines, an almost unheard-of ambition at the time, and the language's directness and relative simplicity made it the base that an entire following generation of languages either extended or explicitly reacted against. C++ layered object-oriented features on top of C directly. Java, arriving in the 1990s, kept much of C's surface familiarity while compiling to a portable intermediate form designed to run unmodified on any machine with the right supporting software installed, chasing the promise of portability C had first made real a generation earlier, but from a different angle entirely.

## The Scripting Era and an Unplanned Empire

As computing spread beyond specialists, a family of languages emerged that prioritized getting small tasks done quickly over raw execution speed or rigorous structure: Perl for text processing and system administration, Python for something closer to general-purpose clarity and readability, both flourishing through the 1990s as the tasks people needed computers for diversified far beyond scientific calculation and business recordkeeping. JavaScript's origin belongs in its own category entirely: designed at Netscape in the mid-1990s under enormous time pressure to give web pages some ability to react to a user without reloading the entire page, it was sketched out in a matter of days rather than the years most languages take to mature, and it shows in some of its more famously eccentric behaviors. And yet, because it shipped inside every web browser at the exact moment the web became the default way people used computers at all, it ended up running on more machines, more often, than almost any language designed with far more care.

Every generation in this history solved real, pressing problems of the one before it — readability, portability, safety, convenience — while quietly introducing new headaches of its own, which is as good an explanation as any for why the history has never stopped, and why newer languages focused on eliminating entire categories of memory-related bugs continue to appear, chasing the same old goal from yet another direction. Each new language arrives promising to have finally learned the lesson of everything that came before it, and the honest student of the field has learned to admire the ambition while waiting to see what the next generation of headaches turns out to be.
