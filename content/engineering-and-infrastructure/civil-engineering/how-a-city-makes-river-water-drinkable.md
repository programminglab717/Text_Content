# How a City Makes River Water Drinkable

The raw water arriving at a treatment plant in late March is usually the worst
of the year. Snowmelt and rain have scoured the catchment, the river is carrying
clay washed off ploughed fields, and a sample held up to the light is the colour
of weak tea with something suspended in it that will not settle no matter how
long you leave the glass on the bench. By the time that same water leaves the
plant a few hours later it will be clear enough to read through a metre of it,
free of anything that can make a person ill, and carrying a faint measured dose
of disinfectant that has to survive a journey of possibly several days through
buried pipe before anyone drinks it.

The sequence that accomplishes this has been more or less stable for a century.
The individual steps are each fairly simple. What makes municipal treatment
interesting is how they compensate for one another, and how much of the design
is about failure rather than performance.

## The Trick with Charge

The problem with river turbidity is not the size of the particles but their
stubbornness. Sand settles out in the river itself. What reaches the intake is
colloidal — clay platelets and organic fragments a micron or less across, light
enough that Brownian motion keeps them in suspension indefinitely. Worse, they
almost all carry a negative surface charge, so they repel one another and refuse
to clump.

Coagulation neutralises that charge. A metal salt — typically aluminium sulphate,
which everyone calls alum, or a ferric salt — is dosed into the water and mixed
violently for a few seconds in a chamber with a high-speed impeller. In water the
metal ion hydrolyses, producing positively charged hydroxide species that
collapse the repulsive barrier between particles, and also producing a gelatinous
hydroxide precipitate that physically sweeps particles out of suspension as it
forms. The mixing has to be fast because the useful chemistry happens in under a
second; dose the coagulant into a slow-moving stream and the floc forms in
streaks rather than uniformly.

Then the water moves into flocculation basins, where the logic inverts. Now the
goal is gentle, prolonged contact so the destabilised particles collide and stick
and grow. Paddles turn slowly — often in stages, faster at the inlet and slower
toward the outlet — for twenty or thirty minutes. Too little agitation and the
particles never meet. Too much and the shear tears the floc apart faster than it
assembles. Operators watch the basin the way a baker watches dough: at the far
end the water should be carrying visible, ragged, slowly tumbling flakes a
few millimetres across.

Getting the coagulant dose right is the part that resists automation. It depends
on turbidity, on temperature — cold water is more viscous and floc forms
sluggishly — on pH, and on how much dissolved organic carbon the river is
carrying. Plants run jar tests, literally a row of beakers on a gang stirrer with
graduated doses, to find the day's answer.

## Settling and Filtering

The flocculated water drifts into a sedimentation basin and slows down almost to
a stop. Detention time is a couple of hours. Floc settles to the floor, where
mechanical scrapers drag it to a hopper, and the sludge is pumped off for
thickening and disposal. Many plants accelerate this with plate or tube settlers
— stacks of inclined surfaces in the upper part of the basin that shorten the
distance a particle has to fall before it lands on something, effectively
multiplying the basin's settling area several times over within the same
footprint.

Sedimentation removes most of the load but never all of it, and the filters take
what is left. A rapid gravity filter is a bed of granular media, commonly a layer
of coarse anthracite over finer silica sand, with water percolating down through
it at a metre or two per hour of face velocity. It is not a sieve. The pores
between grains are far larger than the particles being captured, and removal
happens through depth filtration — particles are carried into the bed by the flow
and adhere to the grains by the same surface chemistry the coagulant set up
upstream. This is why coagulation matters even when a plant's raw water is
already fairly clear: without it, the filters have nothing to grab with.

As a filter runs, captured solids clog the pores, head loss climbs, and
eventually the bed either blinds or begins passing particles through. So it is
taken offline and backwashed — clean water forced upward through the bed fast
enough to fluidise it, often with an air scour first, flushing the accumulated
solids to waste. The coarse-over-fine media arrangement exists because of this:
after fluidisation the grains re-sort by size and density, and the lighter, larger
anthracite settles on top, restoring the gradation every time.

Filtered water turbidity is the plant's most closely watched number, and not
mainly because cloudy water looks bad. Cryptosporidium, a protozoan parasite that
is remarkably resistant to chlorine, is removed physically or not at all. A
filter performing well enough to hold turbidity to a small fraction of a
nephelometric unit is a filter that is very likely also removing oocysts. A
filter that spikes during the first minutes after a backwash — a known,
well-documented vulnerability — is a filter briefly letting things through.

## Killing What Is Left

Disinfection is the step that transformed urban mortality, and it is governed by
a simple idea: the product of disinfectant concentration and contact time. A high
dose for a short while and a low dose for a long while can be equivalent, and
regulators specify required values of that product for different pathogens at
different temperatures and pH values. Which is why plants have contact tanks —
long serpentine basins, baffled so that water cannot short-circuit from inlet to
outlet, forcing every parcel to spend its required minutes in the presence of
chlorine.

Chlorine is cheap, effective against bacteria and viruses, and leaves a residual
that keeps working out in the distribution system. Its liability is that it
reacts with natural organic matter to form trihalomethanes and haloacetic acids,
byproducts that are themselves regulated. That tension drives much of modern
plant design: remove more organic carbon upstream, through better coagulation or
granular activated carbon, so that less chlorine is needed and less byproduct
forms. Many utilities convert their residual to chloramine — chlorine combined
with ammonia — which is a weaker disinfectant but far more stable and forms fewer
byproducts over a long residence in the mains.

Ultraviolet light has become common as a second barrier. It does not kill in the
ordinary sense; it damages nucleic acid so that organisms cannot reproduce, and
it is devastatingly effective against exactly the chlorine-resistant protozoa
that worry filter operators. It leaves no residual at all, so it never replaces
chlorine, only supplements it. Ozone does both jobs — powerful oxidation plus
destruction of the taste-and-odour compounds that cyanobacteria release into
reservoirs in late summer, the earthy geosmin that customers notice immediately
and complain about loudly.

## Chemistry for the Pipes

The last thing a plant does is arguably the least intuitive. It deliberately
adjusts the finished water so that the water is mildly scale-forming rather than
mildly corrosive.

Distribution mains are iron, sometimes lead in old service connections, sometimes
copper with lead solder inside houses. Water that is even slightly aggressive
will dissolve those metals slowly and carry them to the tap. So lime or soda ash
is added to raise pH and alkalinity, and many utilities dose orthophosphate,
which forms a protective mineral film on the pipe wall. That film is a genuine
piece of infrastructure — decades in the making, invisible, and destroyable in
weeks if the water chemistry changes. The lead crisis in Flint, Michigan followed
a switch of source water without maintaining corrosion control, which stripped
the protective scale off the inside of the city's service lines.

Fluoride is added at this stage too, in the small quantities associated with
reduced tooth decay, and the finished water goes into a clearwell where the last
of the disinfection contact time is accumulated before high-lift pumps push it
into the mains.

## After the Plant

What happens next is not treatment but it determines whether the treatment
mattered. Water enters a network of pipe that is pressurised at all times,
precisely so that any crack leaks outward rather than drawing groundwater in.
Elevated tanks and reservoirs float on that pressure, filling at night and
drawing down during the morning peak, which both levels out pumping and provides
a buffer for fire flow. Residual chlorine is sampled at points across the system,
and where it has decayed too far — in a dead-end main serving a few houses, in an
oversized pipe laid for growth that never came — the utility flushes, or
re-doses at a booster station.

The plant's operators will tell you the hardest days are not the muddy ones. A
spring flood is visible, predictable, and answerable with more coagulant. The
difficult events are the invisible ones: a solvent spill upstream, a warm still
week that sets the reservoir stratifying and releases manganese from the
sediment, a main break that drops pressure across a neighbourhood and puts the
whole distribution system under a boil-water notice regardless of how clean the
water leaving the plant was.
