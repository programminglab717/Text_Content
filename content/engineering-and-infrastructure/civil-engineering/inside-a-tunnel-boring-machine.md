# Inside a Tunnel Boring Machine

Calling it a machine undersells it. A large tunnel boring machine is a factory
laid out in a line, and the line can be a hundred metres long or more. Only the
first ten metres or so actually dig. Behind that come the gantries: the
hydraulic power packs, the transformers, the control cabin, the grout plant, the
ventilation ducting, racks of concrete segments waiting their turn, a conveyor
running spoil back toward daylight, a refuge chamber, and usually a kettle. The
crew arrive at the face on a narrow-gauge train, and as the drive lengthens the
commute from the shaft can take longer than the walk from the car park.

The ancestry of all of it is a shield. Marc Brunel's, patented in the 1810s and
used to drive the Thames Tunnel through the treacherous ground under the river
at Rotherhithe, was a rectangular iron frame divided into cells, one miner in
each, working behind removable boards. He would take out a board, dig a few
inches of clay, replace the board a few inches forward, and move to the next.
When every cell had advanced, screw jacks pushed the whole frame ahead and
bricklayers built lining in the space it vacated. Brunel is supposed to have
taken the idea from a shipworm boring through timber and lining its burrow
behind itself with a secreted tube, which is exactly what the machines still do.
The tunnel took the better part of two decades and flooded repeatedly. Later in
the century James Henry Greathead made the shield circular, added compressed air
to hold back water, and used cast-iron segments instead of brickwork, and the
form of the thing has been recognisably fixed ever since: a shield that supports
the ground while you dig, and a permanent lining built inside its tail.

## From the Face Backwards

At the front is the cutterhead, a steel disc that rotates slowly — a couple of
revolutions a minute — and is armoured with tools appropriate to what it is
eating. In rock these are disc cutters: hardened steel rings on bearings, pressed
into the rock face with enormous force so that they roll rather than scrape,
cracking chips off between adjacent tracks. In soft ground they are drag picks
and scrapers, plus rippers and a set of openings through which the spoil passes
into the chamber behind.

Directly behind the cutterhead sits the main bearing and the drive motors, and
behind that the bulkhead separating the excavation chamber from the working part
of the machine. The shield — a steel can as long as it is wide, sometimes
articulated in the middle so it can bend around curves — encloses everything.
Around its rear circumference is a ring of hydraulic thrust rams. They do not
push against the ground. They push against the last ring of concrete lining
already built, which is why a tunnel machine can only advance as fast as the
lining goes up behind it, and why the two operations alternate: bore a ring
length, stop, build a ring, bore again.

The erector is a hydraulic arm inside the tail of the shield that picks up
precast concrete segments one at a time and places them against the ground. Six
or seven of them plus a tapered key make a ring. They are bolted or dowelled
together, and every segment carries a groove holding a rubber gasket that is
compressed as the ring is squeezed home, so the finished lining seals against
water. The rings are slightly tapered rather than square-ended, and by rotating
the taper from ring to ring the crew steer the tunnel: stack the thick sides
consistently to one side and the tunnel curves that way. Steering is also done
with the thrust rams, running the ones on one side harder than the others.

Immediately behind the tail is the one gap in the system. The shield is
necessarily wider than the outside of the concrete ring it leaves behind, so
each ring emerges into an annular void a few centimetres thick. If that void is
left open the ground closes onto the lining, and the ground surface above comes
down to match. So grout is pumped continuously through ports in the tail skin or
through the segments themselves, filling the annulus as fast as it forms, and
brush seals packed with grease keep the grout and the groundwater from tracking
forward into the machine.

## Holding the Face Up

The genuinely hard problem is not cutting. It is that you are removing material
from the front of a hole whose roof is currently supporting a river, a rail line
or somebody's house. How a machine solves that is what defines its type.

In competent rock the face stands by itself, and the machine can be open: a
gripper TBM braces itself against the tunnel walls with hydraulic pads, shoves
the cutterhead forward, then resets. Support behind is rock bolts, mesh and
sprayed concrete, applied as needed.

In soft, wet ground the face must be actively pressurised, and there are two
ways of doing it. An earth pressure balance machine uses the excavated ground
itself as the supporting medium. The chamber behind the cutterhead is kept
packed full of spoil, and material is removed from it not by a conveyor but by
an Archimedes screw, a long augur whose rotation speed meters the outflow. Run
the screw slower than the cutterhead is excavating and the chamber pressure
rises; run it faster and pressure falls. Operators hold that pressure to match
the earth and water pressure outside, and the whole drive is a continuous
balancing act read off pressure cells in the bulkhead. Because a bagged, sticky
or too-granular spoil will not hold pressure or will not flow, foam, polymers
and sometimes bentonite are injected through the cutterhead to condition the
ground into a plastic, low-permeability paste. Clays and silts suit this method
well.

Where the ground is clean sand and gravel, too permeable for spoil to hold
pressure, the answer is a slurry machine. The chamber is flooded with pressurised
bentonite suspension, which presses on the face and penetrates a short way into
it, depositing a low-permeability filter cake that lets the pressure act on the
ground rather than leaking through it. Spoil leaves as part of the slurry,
pumped back down the tunnel to a separation plant of screens, hydrocyclones,
centrifuges and filter presses that recovers the bentonite and ships out the
sand. It is more plant, more surface footprint and more energy, and it is the
method of choice under high water heads.

The number that tells you whether it is working is volume loss: the spoil
actually removed compared with the theoretical volume of the bore. Take out more
than you should and the surplus comes from somewhere, and where it comes from is
the ground above. On the surface that shows up as a settlement trough running
along the tunnel line, deepest over the crown and tapering away to each side in
a shape close to a normal curve. A well-controlled urban drive keeps that trough
to a few millimetres. When a structure cannot tolerate even that, engineers
drill an array of tubes horizontally from shafts either side, between the tunnel
and the building, and inject grout in small measured doses to jack the ground
back up as it moves — compensation grouting, used memorably beneath the Palace
of Westminster during the Jubilee Line Extension, with the clock tower
instrumented and grout crews responding to movements of a millimetre or two.

## When the Ground Does Not Cooperate

Everything above assumes the machine is in the material it was bought for. The
recurring nightmare of tunnelling is a mixed face, with hard rock across the
bottom of the cutterhead and soft wet ground across the top. The tools wear
unevenly, the machine wants to steer upward out of the hard band, and the soft
half can run into the chamber while the hard half refuses to break.

Cutters wear out, and changing them means getting to the front of the cutterhead
— which in a pressurised drive means entering a chamber held at the same
pressure as the ground outside. Crews lock through an airlock, work in
compressed air, and decompress afterwards on schedules borrowed from diving.
Where the pressure is too high for air, saturation techniques or mixed-gas
breathing are used, and where even that is impractical the ground ahead is
treated first — frozen, grouted, or dewatered — so it can be entered safely at
atmospheric pressure. The alternative is what happened to the machine under
downtown Seattle, whose main bearing seal system failed early in the drive and
which ultimately had to be reached by sinking a rescue shaft in front of it and
lifting the cutterhead out for rebuilding.

Deep rock tunnels have their own catalogue. Squeezing ground, where high
overburden stress causes weak rock to close in on the bore over hours or days,
can trap a shield entirely. Faulted and water-bearing zones can flood a heading
in minutes. Karst produces voids the machine falls into. Alpine drives such as
the Gotthard Base Tunnel met rock temperatures high enough to require active
cooling of the working air. The standard defence against all of it is probe
drilling: sending small-diameter holes ahead of the face to find out what is
coming before the cutterhead does.

Guidance, oddly, is the easy part. A laser in the tunnel shoots at a target on
the shield, a gyrocompass carries the bearing where sightlines cannot, and
surveyors work a traverse forward as the drive advances. Two headings driven
from opposite shores of the Channel met in the middle of a chalk seam beneath
the sea and lined up to within a few centimetres.

What happens to the machine afterwards is the part nobody expects. The back-up
gantries come out, and the drive motors and anything else worth salvaging are
stripped, but on many urban projects the shield and cutterhead cannot be
retrieved economically. They are driven into a prepared chamber off the alignment
and abandoned in place, or the hull is simply filled with concrete and becomes
part of the ground. The tunnel is the product. The factory that made it gets
left behind in the dark.
