# Why Tall Buildings Move in the Wind

The complaints that come out of a very tall building on a windy day are rarely
about safety. Nobody on the fifty-eighth floor thinks the tower is going to fall
over. What they report is that the blinds are tapping against the glass, that
the water in the toilet bowl is sloshing in a slow rhythm, that a partition
somewhere is creaking, and that they feel faintly seasick. Those are the
symptoms of a structure that is strong enough and not stiff enough, and managing
them is the central problem of tall-building design.

There is an inversion that happens as a building gets taller. A six-storey
office block is governed by gravity: the columns are sized to carry the floors
above them, wind is a modest side issue, and the structure that carries the
weight happens to be more than enough to resist the breeze. Somewhere in the
middle of the height range the two demands cross over. Beyond that point the
building's weight is almost incidental, and wind decides the size of the
columns, the thickness of the core walls, the shape of the floor plate, and
often the shape of the whole tower. Past a certain slenderness the governing
criterion is not even strength. It is how fast the top of the building
accelerates when a gust arrives, because that is what human beings can feel.

## Along the Wind and Across It

Wind pressure on a building rises with the square of wind speed, and wind speed
rises with height as you climb out of the rough, slow layer of air near the
ground. Over open water the profile is steep and the wind at roof level is
fierce; over a dense city the surrounding buildings chew up the flow and the
profile is blunter but far more turbulent. So the same tower placed on a
lakefront and in a downtown core sees quite different loads, and the design
begins with characterising the wind climate of the actual site — direction by
direction, because a city is not symmetrical and neither is its weather.

The intuitive load is the along-wind one: the wind pushes on the windward face,
sucks on the leeward face, and the building leans downwind. That component is
real and is roughly what the simple code formulas handle. It is usually not the
one that matters most.

As air flows past a bluff body it separates at the corners and rolls up into
vortices, and those vortices peel off the two sides alternately rather than
together. Each departing vortex produces a sideways pulse of force. The result
is a periodic push across the wind direction, at a frequency that scales with
wind speed divided by the building's width, and for a tall slender tower this
across-wind excitation typically produces larger motion than the direct push
does. If the shedding frequency wanders close to one of the building's natural
frequencies, the building's own motion begins to organise the vortex shedding to
match it — a lock-in effect — and the response climbs steeply.

Two properties make this worse in supertall buildings specifically. Their
natural frequencies are very low, on the order of a tenth of a hertz or less,
meaning the whole tower sways with a period of several seconds; those slow
frequencies coincide with wind speeds that actually occur. And their damping is
tiny. A welded steel tower may dissipate only a percent or two of critical
damping, so once energy is fed into the sway there is almost nothing to take it
back out, and the motion builds over many cycles.

What occupants perceive is acceleration, not displacement. The top of a tall
tower can travel a good fraction of a metre without anyone noticing, provided it
gets there slowly. Comfort criteria are therefore written in milli-g, and — this
is the part that surprises people outside the field — they are checked against a
fairly common wind, something with a return period of a decade or so, rather
than the rare extreme event used for strength. A building that only makes people
queasy in a once-in-a-century storm is fine. One that does it every March is a
commercial failure.

## Three Levers

Against that problem a designer has three things to pull on: stiffness, damping,
and shape.

Stiffness is the structural-systems story, and it is largely a story about
getting the material out to the perimeter, where it has the most leverage
against overturning. The earliest tall steel frames relied on the bending
stiffness of beam-to-column joints throughout the floor plate, which is
inefficient and stops working at height. Bracing the core helps but concentrates
everything into a narrow cross-section in the middle of the building. Fazlur
Khan's insight at Skidmore, Owings and Merrill in the 1960s was to treat the
exterior as the structure — a framed tube of closely spaced perimeter columns
tied by deep spandrel beams, so the tower behaves like a hollow cantilever box
rather than a bundle of separate frames. He then pushed the idea in two
directions: onto the John Hancock Center, where enormous X-braces on the facade
carry shear directly and make the tube nearly perfect; and onto the Sears Tower,
where nine tubes bundled together are terminated at different heights, so the
building both stiffens itself and changes its width as it rises.

The dominant modern form is a variation on the theme — a stiff reinforced
concrete core, a ring of large perimeter columns, and outrigger trusses or walls
at one or several mechanical floors that tie the two together. The outrigger is
what makes it work. Without it, core and columns sway more or less
independently; with it, the core's tendency to rotate is resisted by pushing
down on the columns on one side and lifting on the other, and the full width of
the building is mobilised.

Shape is the cheapest lever and often the most effective. The point is to stop
the vortex shedding from getting organised. A building whose width changes as it
rises — by tapering, by stepped setbacks — sheds vortices at a different
frequency at every level, and they never add up into one coherent across-wind
force. Softening the corners does something similar: rounding, chamfering or
notching a sharp corner, even by a small fraction of the building's width, moves
the separation point around and can cut the across-wind response dramatically
for very little money. Cutting a hole clean through the upper part of a tower,
as at the Shanghai World Financial Center, lets pressure equalise across the
building and disrupts the shedding. Twisting the whole tower through a slow
rotation, as at Shanghai Tower, means no two floors present the same profile to
the same wind. These moves are usually read as architectural gestures. Most of
them started as wind-tunnel results.

## What Happens in the Tunnel

None of this is settled by formulas. A serious tall building is tested in a
boundary-layer wind tunnel, a long low chamber with spires and floor roughness
at the upwind end to grow a turbulent profile resembling the real atmosphere.
The model of the tower sits on a turntable surrounded by a scale model of every
building within a substantial radius, and is rotated through the full circle so
that every wind direction is measured with the real shelter and the real
interference effects of its neighbours.

Several kinds of model get used. A rigid model on a high-frequency force balance
gives the overall base moments, from which response is computed. A model dotted
with hundreds of pressure taps gives the local peaks that size the glass and its
fixings — and those local suctions near corners and parapets are much higher
than the average pressure on the face, which is why cladding failures cluster at
edges. For the most slender projects an aeroelastic model, scaled in mass and
stiffness as well as geometry, is flown in the tunnel so it can actually deflect
and feed back into the flow. Tunnels also answer the question nobody asks until
it is too late: what the wind does at street level, where a smooth tall facade
can drive high-altitude wind down its windward face and dump it into the plaza
at the base.

Where stiffness and shape are not enough, damping is bought and installed. A
tuned mass damper is a large mass — a pendulum, a slab on bearings — hung near
the top of the tower and tuned so that it lags the building's sway by a quarter
cycle, pushing back against the motion and bleeding energy into hydraulic
dashpots. Taipei 101's is a polished steel sphere weighing several hundred
tonnes, deliberately left visible to visitors on an observation level, swinging
in a stairwell-sized void. Others are tanks of water whose sloshing period is
tuned to the building, or viscous dampers built into the outrigger connections
so that the relative movement between core and columns does the dissipating.

There is a cautionary story attached to all this, and it belongs to the Citicorp
Center in Manhattan. After the building was finished, its engineer, William
LeMessurier, worked through a question raised by a student about how the tower
behaved in winds striking its corners rather than its faces — a case that had
been analysed, but for which the chevron braces had, during construction, been
bolted rather than welded as a cost saving. The combination was worse than
either alone. Through the autumn of 1978, with a hurricane season running,
welders worked at night to plate the joints while the building was occupied. It
was, in the end, a competent response to a bad discovery. It is also the clearest
illustration of how the discipline works: the loads that govern a tall building
are the ones nobody drew on the first sketch, and finding them is most of the
job.
