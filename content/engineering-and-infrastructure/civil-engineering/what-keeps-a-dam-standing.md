# What Keeps a Dam Standing

Walk to the downstream toe of a large concrete dam and look up. The wall above
you is holding back a lake, and the pressure that lake exerts is not spread
evenly across the face. It grows with depth — roughly ten kilopascals for every
metre of water — so that at the base of a hundred-metre dam the water is pushing
with about a megapascal, ten atmospheres, on every square metre of concrete. The
total horizontal thrust on a dam of that size, per metre of its length, is
measured in thousands of tonnes. Nothing is bolting the structure to the valley.
It is standing there because of an argument about statics that someone made on
paper decades before the first truck arrived.

There are only a handful of ways to win that argument, and most dams in the
world use one of two.

## Weight Against Water, and the Middle Third

The simplest answer is mass. A gravity dam is a triangular wedge of concrete or
masonry, thin at the crest and thick at the base, and it resists the push of the
reservoir by being too heavy to slide and too heavy to tip. The horizontal thrust
tries to rotate the whole wedge about its downstream toe; the weight of the
concrete, acting down through the section's centre of gravity, resists that
rotation. Slide is resisted by friction along the foundation contact, which is
why the base is roughened, keyed into sound rock, and cleaned of every scrap of
loose material before the first lift is placed.

Designers of gravity dams live by a rule about where the resultant force lands.
Add the water thrust and the dam's own weight as vectors, and the combined force
passes through the base somewhere. If it passes through the middle third of the
base width, every point along that base stays in compression. If it wanders
outside the middle third, the upstream heel goes into tension — and concrete in
tension, particularly concrete at a joint between construction lifts, is not
something a designer wants to rely on. So the section is shaped, and reshaped,
until the resultant stays inside that band for the reservoir full, the reservoir
empty, and the reservoir full while the ground is shaking.

The alternative is to stop fighting the thrust and redirect it. An arch dam is
curved in plan, convex upstream, and it converts the reservoir's push into
compression running around the arch and out into the canyon walls. Concrete is
excellent in compression, so an arch dam can be startlingly thin — a shell tens
of metres thick at the base where a gravity dam of the same height would need
hundreds. The catch is that the entire load now goes into the abutments. An arch
dam is only as good as the rock it leans on.

The Malpasset dam in southern France made that lesson permanent. It was a thin
arch, elegantly designed, and in 1959 it failed almost instantly when the rock
of one abutment moved under the thrust. The gneiss there was foliated, with
planes of weakness oriented unhelpfully, and water pressure in those planes
reduced the friction holding them shut. The structure did not crack in any way
that a strength calculation would have predicted. The mountain simply stopped
pushing back.

## The Water You Cannot See

The thrust on the upstream face is the obvious load. The dangerous one is the
water that gets underneath.

No rock foundation is watertight. Reservoir pressure drives seepage into joints
and pores beneath the dam, and that pressurised water pushes upward on the base
of the structure. Engineers call it uplift, and it does two nasty things at once:
it reduces the effective weight of the dam, which is the whole basis of a gravity
section's stability, and it shifts the resultant force further downstream, toward
and past that middle third. A dam that is comfortably stable on paper with no
uplift can be marginal with full reservoir pressure acting along its entire base.

The standard countermeasures are a grout curtain and a drainage curtain, in that
order. Grout — cement slurry, sometimes chemical grout — is injected under
pressure into a line of holes drilled deep into the foundation near the heel,
filling the open joints and reducing how much water can get in. Then a second,
shallower line of open drain holes is drilled just downstream of the curtain,
connected to a gallery running through the body of the dam, so that whatever
seepage does get past has somewhere to go at atmospheric pressure. Water collects
in that gallery, runs to a sump, and is pumped or drained out. The flow is
measured. A slow increase over years is normal as joints open and close; a sudden
increase, or a change in the water's clarity, is an emergency.

The St. Francis Dam in California failed in 1928, at night, and the flood killed
hundreds of people in the valley below. Part of the explanation was the
foundation on one side: a conglomerate that looked competent when dry and lost
much of its strength when saturated. Another part was a fault contact and an
ancient landslide the design had not accounted for. The engineer in charge,
William Mulholland, had inspected the dam that same day and judged the seepage
acceptable.

## Embankments, Filters, and the Slow Failure

Most dams in the world are not concrete at all. They are embankments — compacted
earth, rockfill, or both — because those materials are usually sitting in the
valley already and can be placed by earthmoving equipment rather than formwork.
An embankment dam does not resist water by being rigid. It resists by being
enormous and by being, in its core, nearly impermeable: a zone of well-compacted
clay, or a concrete face slab, or an asphaltic membrane, flanked by coarser
shells that provide the bulk and the slope stability.

The failure mode that haunts embankments is internal erosion, also called piping.
Water seeping through or beneath the dam picks up individual soil particles and
carries them downstream. The path it travels widens. Higher velocity carries
larger particles, which widens it further, and the process runs away. From the
outside, the first sign is often a small spring on the downstream slope that runs
cloudy. By the time the muddy flow is obvious, there may be hours left.

The defence is the filter — a carefully graded zone of sand and gravel placed
downstream of the core, sized so that its pores are small enough to catch the
particles migrating out of the core but large enough to let water pass without
building pressure. The gradation rules governing this are decades old and
unglamorous, and they are probably the single most important thing in embankment
dam engineering. The Teton Dam in Idaho failed in 1976, on first filling, when
water found its way through fissured windblown silt in the foundation key trench
and into the core, with no adequate filter to stop the particles from washing
away. The reservoir emptied through the breach in a few hours.

## The Part That Usually Fails First

A dam does not have to break to kill people. It only has to be overtopped, or to
lose control of the water it is passing.

Every dam needs a spillway, a route for flood inflow that exceeds what the
reservoir can store. On a concrete dam this is usually a section of the crest,
gated or ungated, with the flow shooting down the face and into a stilling basin
at the bottom where a deliberately induced hydraulic jump dissipates the energy
before the water reaches the riverbed. That energy is substantial. Water falling
a hundred metres arrives at something like forty-five metres per second, and if
it is allowed to land on unprotected rock it will excavate a plunge pool
upstream toward the dam's own foundation.

Spillway chutes fail in ways that look small at first. A slab joint opens
slightly. Water at high velocity gets beneath the slab, where it acts as uplift,
and the slab lifts. Once one slab is gone, the flow attacks the foundation
directly, and the chute unzips upstream. The near-failure at Oroville in
California in 2017 followed roughly that script, and the more frightening part of
that episode was what happened when operators reduced flow down the damaged
chute: the reservoir rose to the emergency spillway, an unlined hillside, which
immediately began eroding headward toward the structure retaining the lake.

The largest dam disaster in Europe involved a dam that never broke at all. At
Vajont in Italy in 1963, a mountainside slid into the reservoir — an enormous
mass, moving fast — and displaced a wave that went over the crest of the arch dam
and down the gorge into the town of Longarone. Around two thousand people died.
The arch survived, structurally intact, and is still there. The slope movement
had been measured for years beforehand.

## Reading a Dam

Which is why a modern large dam is an instrumented object as much as a
structural one. Piezometers in the foundation and the embankment report pore
water pressure. Weirs measure seepage flow to the litre. Plumb lines and
inverted pendulums track the crest's deflection through the annual cycle as the
reservoir fills and drops and the concrete expands and contracts. Survey
monuments are resurveyed. Extensometers watch joints in the abutment rock.

None of this tells you that a dam is safe. What it tells you is whether the dam
is behaving the way it behaved last year, and the year before, under the same
reservoir level and the same temperature. A dam that is doing something new is
the one worth losing sleep over — not because the new behaviour is necessarily
dangerous, but because the models that justified the structure in the first place
have just been shown to be incomplete, and nobody yet knows in which direction.
