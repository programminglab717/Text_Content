# Combined Sewers and the Trouble with Rain

In the summer of 1858 the Thames in central London became unbearable. The river
was tidal past Westminster, so the city's waste did not leave; it sloshed
upstream and back twice a day, ripening. Curtains at the Houses of Parliament
were soaked in chloride of lime in an attempt to make the chamber usable, and
members debated adjourning to somewhere upwind. The episode was named the Great
Stink almost immediately, and within about eighteen days Parliament had passed
legislation funding a sewer system for the metropolis — a speed that says more
about the smell than about Victorian administrative efficiency.

The engineer who got the job, Joseph Bazalgette, built something that is still
carrying London's sewage. His design was not a network of pipes running to the
river; the river was already the problem. It was a set of enormous interceptor
sewers running roughly west to east, cutting across the existing drains that
sloped down to the Thames, catching their flow and carrying it by gravity far
downstream of the built-up city before releasing it. Building the northern
interceptors required reclaiming land from the river itself, which is why the
Victoria Embankment exists and why there is a District Line tunnel and a trunk
sewer stacked beneath a road that used to be foreshore.

The anecdote everyone repeats about Bazalgette is that he calculated the pipe
diameters needed for the population of London, then roughly doubled them,
reasoning that the job would only be done once. It is essentially true, and it is
the reason the system survived a century and a half of growth it was never sized
for. But his design also carried forward an assumption that would become one of
the more expensive inheritances in urban infrastructure: that sewage and rainfall
should travel in the same pipe.

## Why the Old Ones Are Egg-Shaped

Go down into a Victorian brick sewer and the cross-section is not circular. It is
an oval, narrow end down — an egg standing on its point. The shape is a piece of
hydraulic reasoning that has nothing to do with structural strength.

A gravity sewer flows partly full almost all the time, and it depends on velocity
to stay clean. Below roughly half a metre per second, grit and solids drop out of
suspension and start building a deposit on the invert, which narrows the channel,
slows the flow further, and eventually blocks it. Designers therefore size pipes
and slopes to guarantee a self-cleansing velocity at the low flows of three in
the morning, not just at the design peak.

That is hard in a circular pipe. A large circular sewer running at a tenth of its
depth spreads a thin film of sewage across a wide, flat arc of invert, with a
great deal of wetted surface and a great deal of friction for very little cross
section. The velocity collapses. Narrow the bottom of the section into a deep,
rounded channel and the same small flow becomes deeper and narrower, with less
wall contact per unit of area, and it keeps moving. The egg gives you a
small-flow channel at the bottom and full capacity at the top, in one piece of
brickwork. Modern practice achieves the same thing with a small circular pipe and
a larger one, but the Victorians did not have the luxury of a second excavation.

Everything about a sewer network follows from that same concern. Manholes exist
at every change of direction, grade, or diameter, because a sewer that cannot be
rodded or jetted will eventually need to be dug up. Pipes are laid on continuous,
carefully surveyed falls. Where topography refuses to cooperate, a pump station
lifts the flow and a pressurised force main carries it over the hill, which
introduces its own problems — sewage in a force main sits without oxygen, goes
septic, and produces hydrogen sulphide. Where that gas comes out of solution at
the discharge, bacteria on the damp crown of the downstream pipe oxidise it to
sulphuric acid, and concrete sewers dissolve from the top down. Crown corrosion
has destroyed more sewer pipe than structural overload ever has.

## The Bill for Sharing a Pipe

A combined sewer works beautifully in dry weather. It collects household waste
and street runoff in one conduit and delivers everything to a treatment plant.
The trouble arrives with a thunderstorm, when the flow in that conduit can rise
by a factor of fifty or a hundred within minutes.

No treatment plant can be built to handle that. So combined systems have always
included relief points — overflow weirs, set at a level such that when flow
exceeds a few times the dry-weather rate, the excess spills over and discharges
directly to the nearest watercourse, untreated. The combined sewer overflow is
not a malfunction. It is the design working as intended, and it was intended at a
time when diluting sewage into a river was considered an adequate answer.

It is no longer considered adequate, and unpicking it is extraordinarily
expensive. Separating a combined system means laying a second pipe network under
every street in the old city and reconnecting every property — a cost that,
across a large city, runs into figures nobody wants to put to voters. The
alternative that most large cities have chosen is storage: capture the overflow
volume, hold it until the storm passes, and pump it back for treatment
afterwards. Chicago spent decades boring a system of deep rock tunnels and
excavating reservoirs to do exactly that. London's answer was a storage-and-
transfer tunnel running for miles beneath the tidal Thames, intercepting the
overflow points that Bazalgette's interceptors had been discharging from for
generations, and carrying their contents east to treatment. The Victorian system
did not fail. It was simply asked to meet a standard that did not exist when it
was built.

## Storm Drains Are Not Sewers

Cities built or expanded in the twentieth century mostly used separate systems: a
sanitary sewer for waste, and a storm sewer that collects street runoff through
gutter inlets and discharges it to a creek or river with no treatment at all.
This solves the overflow problem and creates a different one.

Runoff from an urban surface is not clean. It carries road grit, tyre and brake
wear, petroleum residue, nutrients from fertilised lawns, animal waste, and in
winter an enormous load of chloride from de-icing salt. The concentration is
highest in the early part of a storm, when the accumulated grime of the previous
dry period washes off in the first few millimetres of rain — the first flush.
After heavy rain, an urban stream's chemistry can shift so sharply that the
biological difference is visible for days.

The hydrology changes too, and more permanently. A forested hillside absorbs most
of a moderate rainfall, releases it slowly through the soil, and produces a
gentle rise in the stream over hours. Cover that hillside with roofs, roads, and
parking, and almost all the rain becomes runoff within minutes, funnelled by pipe
directly to the channel. The stream's response to a storm becomes flashy: a sharp
peak, several times higher than before, arriving far sooner and falling away just
as fast. Channels respond by cutting down and widening, banks collapse, and the
bed coarsens as fine sediment is scoured away. Ecologists call the whole syndrome
by the name of its cause. It is not a pollution problem in the conventional
sense; it is a problem of too much water arriving too quickly.

## Slowing It Down

The engineering response for most of the late twentieth century was the detention
basin — a grassy depression at the bottom of a development, with a small outlet,
holding back the peak so that the post-development discharge rate matched the
pre-development one. It is a reasonable idea with a known limitation: matching
peak flow rate does not match total volume, and a hundred detention basins in a
catchment can each release their stored water on a schedule that combines
downstream into a longer, flatter, and still damaging flood.

The shift over the last few decades has been toward putting water back into the
ground rather than routing it. Bioretention cells and rain gardens take runoff
from a roof or a parking bay into an engineered soil mix planted with things that
tolerate both drought and inundation, where it infiltrates and where the soil and
roots strip out metals and nutrients. Permeable paving replaces the impervious
surface itself with an open-graded structure over a stone reservoir. Swales carry
water along grassy channels instead of pipes, losing some of it on the way. Green
roofs hold the first several millimetres of any storm and evaporate most of it.

None of these replace the pipe network. A severe storm still needs a conveyance
route, and infiltration is limited by soil, by groundwater depth, and by the
practical difficulty of maintaining ten thousand small facilities across a city
rather than one large one. What they do is shave the small, frequent storms — the
ones that deliver most of the annual pollutant load and do most of the cumulative
channel damage — off the top of the system. The heavy rain still gets a pipe. It
is the ordinary rain, the sort that falls on a Tuesday and nobody remembers, that
the last generation of design was quietly getting wrong.
