# What Is Under the Asphalt

The best place to understand a road is at the edge of a utility trench. Somebody
has sawn a rectangle out of the carriageway to reach a gas main, and the cut face
shows the whole thing in section: forty or fifty millimetres of dark, fine-
textured surfacing on top, a coarser and thicker layer beneath it, then a pale
band of angular crushed stone, then another, browner and dirtier, and finally the
native ground — clay or silt or whatever the glacier left. The black part that
everyone calls "the road" is the thinnest thing in the picture. Most of the
structure is rock.

That arrangement is not decorative. A truck tyre presses on the surface at
roughly the pressure inside the tyre, which is to say several hundred
kilopascals, over a contact patch about the size of a dinner plate. The soil
underneath cannot take that. Soft clay might fail at a tenth of it. Every layer
in the section exists to spread that concentrated load outward as it travels
down, so that by the time the stress reaches the subgrade it is diffuse enough
for ordinary ground to carry without deforming.

## McAdam's Two Ideas

Road building before the nineteenth century mostly meant building a foundation.
The Romans dug down and constructed a substantial layered raft; Thomas Telford,
working in Britain in the early 1800s, laid a pitched course of large stones set
on edge by hand beneath his surfacing. Both approaches worked and both were
expensive.

John Loudon McAdam argued that the foundation was unnecessary, and that two other
things mattered far more. The first was the stone itself. He insisted on
fragments that were angular, clean, and small — his surveyors were said to gauge
them against the mouth — because angular pieces of uniform size, once compacted,
interlock and distribute load among themselves as a mass rather than as loose
gravel. Rounded stone rolls. Mixed sizes and dirt let the assembly shift. The
second idea was drainage. McAdam held that native soil could carry substantial
traffic provided it stayed dry, so his roads were raised above the surrounding
ground, cambered so water ran off the surface rather than through it, and ditched
on both sides.

It worked well enough to reshape road construction across Europe and North
America, and it survived the arrival of the motor car only after an addition. A
macadam surface is held together by mechanical interlock and by the dust of its
own wear, which rain washes out and pneumatic tyres suck out. Spraying tar over
the stone to bind it produced tar-bound macadam, shortened almost immediately to
tarmac, and the modern asphalt road is a direct descendant: graded stone with the
voids filled by a bituminous binder.

## Reading the Layers

The modern section has names for each band in that trench face.

At the bottom is the subgrade, which is not constructed so much as prepared. It
is the in-situ soil, stripped of topsoil and organics, shaped to grade, and
compacted to a specified density at a specified moisture content. Getting that
moisture right matters enormously — soil compacted too dry cannot be densified,
and soil compacted too wet pumps and shears under the roller. Its load-carrying
ability is characterised by tests that are almost a century old and still in use,
such as the California Bearing Ratio, which measures resistance to a standard
plunger pushed into a prepared sample and expresses it as a percentage of the
resistance of a reference crushed stone. A firm gravel subgrade might come in at
twenty or thirty percent. Wet plastic clay can be down at two or three, and the
pavement above it has to be very much thicker as a result.

Above the subgrade sits the subbase — often a cheaper, coarser granular material,
sometimes a stabilised layer where lime or cement has been mixed into the
existing soil to give it strength and reduce its sensitivity to water. Its jobs
are structural and practical at once: it spreads load, it provides a working
platform for the plant that has to drive over it, and it separates the clean
upper layers from the fines below. That last function is why a geotextile is so
often rolled out at the interface. Without a separation layer, repeated loading
pumps fine subgrade material up into the voids of the stone above, and a clean
free-draining base slowly turns into contaminated, water-holding mud.

Above the subbase is the base course, the principal structural layer — dense,
well-graded crushed aggregate compacted in lifts, or in heavier pavements an
asphalt-bound or cement-bound material. And above that come the bituminous
layers, usually at least two of them. The binder course is coarser and carries
most of the structural duty of the bound section. The surface course on top is
finer, designed for skid resistance, tyre noise, drainage of surface water, and
durability against weather rather than for strength.

Concrete pavements do the same job with a different strategy. Instead of
spreading the load progressively through a thick flexible stack, a concrete slab
acts in bending and distributes the wheel load over a very wide area in one step,
so the layers beneath can be thinner. The trade is that concrete must be jointed,
because it shrinks as it cures and expands and contracts with temperature, and
every joint is a discontinuity that has to transfer load across itself. That is
what the steel dowel bars in a transverse joint are for — smooth, greased at one
end so the slabs can move apart, but stiff enough that a wheel crossing the joint
does not deflect one slab relative to its neighbour. When dowels corrode or were
never installed, slabs start to move independently, water works down the joint,
fines are pumped out from underneath, and one slab settles below the next. That
step is called faulting, and it is the reason old concrete highways develop the
rhythmic thump that makes a passenger drowsy.

## The Fourth Power

Ask a highway engineer how much damage cars do to a road and the honest answer is
approximately none.

Full-scale road testing in the United States in the late 1950s, where
instrumented pavement loops were run for years under controlled truck traffic,
produced a relationship that has governed pavement design ever since: the damage
caused by an axle rises roughly with the fourth power of its load. Double the
axle weight and you do about sixteen times the damage. The practical arithmetic
is startling. A loaded heavy truck axle can do the damage of several thousand
passenger car axles, which means that on a typical road the entire structural
design is dictated by the small fraction of traffic that is commercial. Designers
do not count vehicles; they convert the traffic spectrum into an equivalent
number of standard axle loads and design for that.

It also explains why overloading enforcement exists, why some pavements fail on
routes with a single quarry or distribution centre generating traffic nobody
forecast, and why a bus route through a residential street tears up a road built
for cars.

## Water, Again

Almost everything that destroys a pavement involves water.

Free water in the granular layers reduces their stiffness and their shear
strength. Water trapped beneath an asphalt layer is forced under pressure through
cracks by passing wheels, stripping binder from aggregate and washing fines out
of the base. Water in the subgrade softens it and increases the deflection under
every load, accelerating fatigue in the layers above.

In cold climates it does something more dramatic. Frost-susceptible soils — silts
are the worst offenders, with just the right pore size — draw water upward by
capillary action toward a freezing front and build discrete lenses of ice within
the soil. The lenses grow, the ground above them lifts, and the road surface
heaves, often unevenly. Then in spring the surface thaws first while the ground
below remains frozen and impermeable, so the meltwater has nowhere to drain and
the upper subgrade becomes saturated and extremely weak. Rural roads in northern
regions are routinely posted with reduced load limits during the thaw for exactly
this reason: for a few weeks each year the pavement is sitting on soup.

The defences are cross slope of a couple of percent to shed surface water, sealed
cracks and joints, edge drains and outlets to take water out of the granular
layers, ditches kept below the level of the subbase, and enough non-frost-
susceptible material above the frost line that the lenses never form under the
travelled way.

## How It Ends

Pavements fail in a small number of recognisable ways, and the pattern tells you
where the problem is.

Wheelpath ruts — parallel depressions with slight shoulders — mean permanent
deformation, either in an asphalt mix too soft for its climate and traffic or in
the unbound layers below. Interconnected cracks in a polygonal pattern, the thing
everyone calls alligator cracking, mean fatigue: repeated tensile strain at the
bottom of the bound layer has cracked it from underneath, and those cracks have
propagated up to the surface. It is a structural verdict, and patching the
surface will not fix it. Long transverse cracks at regular spacing in a cold
climate are thermal, the asphalt having shrunk faster than it could relax.
Ravelling — a surface that gets progressively rougher and sheds stone — is a
binder that has oxidised and hardened with age.

Potholes are the end of the chain rather than a distinct failure. A crack lets
water into the base, traffic pumps that water in and out, the material around the
crack loses support, a piece of the surface breaks free, and from then on every
tyre enlarges the hole.

The unglamorous conclusion of a century of this is that timing dominates cost. A
pavement in good condition can be kept there almost indefinitely with cheap
surface treatments — crack sealing, a chip seal, a thin overlay — applied while
the structure below is still sound. Let the same road deteriorate past the point
where cracking reaches the base and water gets in, and the only remedy left is to
remove and rebuild, at many times the price. The road that looks fine is the one
worth spending money on. The one that obviously needs work has usually been
ignored for about a decade too long.
