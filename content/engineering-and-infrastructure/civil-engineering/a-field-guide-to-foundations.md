# A Field Guide to Foundations

The first real information anyone gets about a building site usually arrives on
the back of a truck. A small rig sets up on a patch of mud, drills a hole, and
at intervals drives a hollow sampling tube into the bottom of it with a standard
weight falling a standard distance while somebody counts the blows. The tube
comes up full of whatever is down there — brown clay, wet sand, a slug of
crushed brick and ash from whatever stood on the plot a century ago — and the
count of blows becomes a number in a log. Everything above ground will be
designed from calculations. Everything below it is designed from a few dozen
holes and a great deal of judgement about what lies between them.

Foundations fail in exactly two ways, and it is worth keeping them separate,
because they call for different remedies. In a bearing failure the soil shears:
a wedge of ground beneath the footing slides sideways and up, and the structure
punches in, usually tipping as it goes. These are rare, sudden, and spectacular.
A grain elevator at Transcona in Manitoba, filled with grain for the first time
just before the First World War, went over sideways in a matter of hours when
the soft clay beneath it gave way as a whole — the structure itself, well built
in reinforced concrete, stayed essentially intact while lying at an absurd
angle.

The other failure is settlement, and it is the one that actually occupies
engineers. The ground does not shear; it simply compresses under the load, and
the building comes down with it. Settlement in itself is surprisingly harmless.
A building that sinks a hundred millimetres uniformly will have trouble at its
drain connections and its entrance steps and nowhere else. What cracks brickwork
and jams doors and shears services is differential settlement — one column going
down more than its neighbour — and the measure that matters is the angular
distortion between adjacent supports rather than the absolute movement of any
one of them.

## Why Soil Behaves the Way It Does

Soil is not a material in the way steel is a material. It is an assembly of
mineral grains with water and air in the spaces between them, and most of its
mechanical personality comes from that arrangement rather than from the mineral
itself.

The idea that organises the whole subject is effective stress, set out by Karl
Terzaghi in the 1920s. A soil's strength and stiffness depend not on the total
pressure applied to it but on the contact pressure between grains — the total
stress minus the water pressure in the pores. Raise the pore pressure and the
grains are held apart and the soil weakens, even though nothing has been
unloaded. Drain the water away and the grains press together and it strengthens.
Almost every strange thing soil does follows from this.

It explains, in particular, the deep difference between sand and clay, which in
practice behave like two different subjects.

Sand drains almost instantly. Load a footing on sand and the water leaves the
loaded zone as fast as you can apply the load, the grains take the stress
immediately, and essentially all the settlement happens during construction.
Strength comes from friction and interlock between grains, which means density
matters enormously — loose sand can be compacted into something several times
better. The characteristic hazards are scour, where flowing water removes sand
from beneath a foundation, and liquefaction, where shaking during an earthquake
raises pore pressures until effective stress falls near zero and a saturated
loose sand behaves briefly like a heavy liquid. Buildings do not so much collapse
in liquefaction as tip over intact or float up, and buried tanks have been known
to rise out of the ground.

Clay is the slow opposite. Its permeability can be smaller than sand's by many
orders of magnitude, so when load arrives the pore water cannot escape and takes
the stress itself. Only as that water squeezes out over months and years does the
stress transfer to the grains and the soil compress. This is consolidation, and
it means a building on clay is still settling long after the contractor has
left. It also means clay has two different strengths depending on how fast you
load it, and the relevant one for a rapid excavation is not the relevant one for
the finished building. On top of that, many clays shrink and swell with moisture
content. A row of houses with mature trees close by is a routine subsidence
claim, because the trees draw water out of the clay in dry summers and the ground
under the footings shrinks away.

## Choosing the Foundation

The site investigation is what turns all this from theory into a decision, and
it is the cheapest money on the project. It should start at a desk rather than
in the field: old maps and photographs, which reveal filled ponds, quarries,
mine workings, wartime bomb damage, and the made ground that accumulates on any
long-occupied site. Then boreholes and cone penetration tests, groundwater
standpipes left in to see where the water table really sits and how it moves
with the seasons, and laboratory work on the samples — oedometer tests for
compressibility, triaxial tests for strength, plasticity limits to classify the
clays. The single most common regret on a difficult site is that too few holes
were sunk, and the second is that they were all sunk under the building and none
under the car park that later had to carry a crane.

With that in hand the choice is mostly between spreading the load and reaching
past the problem.

Spreading means shallow foundations. Pad footings under individual columns,
strip footings under walls, sized so that the pressure they impose is a
comfortable fraction of what the soil can take and so that expected settlements
are tolerable. When footings grow until they nearly touch, it becomes cheaper
and better to join them into a single raft, which has the additional virtue of
bridging over local soft spots and averaging out variation the boreholes never
found. Rafts also allow a trick worth knowing: if you excavate a basement, you
remove a weight of soil, and if that weight equals the weight of the building
above, the net change in stress at depth is zero and settlement should in
principle be nil. Fully and partially compensated rafts of this kind are a
standard approach on very soft ground.

Reaching past means piles. They come in two broad families. Driven piles —
precast concrete, steel tubes, H-sections, timber — are hammered or vibrated in,
displacing the soil sideways and densifying it, which is efficient but noisy and
hard on neighbours. Bored piles, also called drilled shafts, are made by boring
a hole, sometimes under a supporting fluid, and filling it with reinforcement
and concrete; they are quiet, can be made very large, and can be taken to great
depth, but they remove soil rather than compact it.

Either kind carries load in two ways: end bearing on whatever it lands in, and
friction along its shaft. Short piles to rock are mostly end bearing. Long piles
in a deep clay basin may never reach anything hard at all and live entirely on
shaft friction, which is perfectly respectable. The awkward case is when the
ground around a pile settles more than the pile does — under new fill, say, or
where a compressible layer is consolidating. Then the friction reverses
direction and the soil hangs off the pile instead of holding it up. That
downdrag, or negative skin friction, is a load rather than a capacity, and it
has surprised people who assumed friction could only help.

## What the Water Is Doing

Groundwater causes more foundation trouble than soil does. A deep excavation
below the water table is holding back a head of water as well as a mass of soil,
and if the base is underlain by a permeable layer under pressure it can heave or
blow upward regardless of how good the walls are. Dewatering relieves that, but
dewatering lowers the water table over a radius that may extend well past the
site boundary, raising effective stress under the neighbours and settling their
buildings — the classic way to damage a structure you never touched.

The same buoyancy works in reverse on the finished building. A large basement is
a boat. Empty and light, with the water table high, it can float, and
underground tanks and empty swimming pools have been known to rise out of the
ground after heavy rain. Basements below the water table are therefore checked
for uplift with the building at its lightest, and are sometimes tied down with
anchors for that condition alone.

Two cities make the whole subject legible. Mexico City sits on the compressible
lakebed clays of a drained basin, and a century of pumping groundwater has
consolidated them so that the entire ground surface sinks. Buildings founded on
piles to a firm layer stay where they are while the street around them descends,
so their entrances gradually rise above the pavement and have to be re-stepped.
Buildings on rafts ride down with the city and are often in better shape than
their piled neighbours. And in Pisa the tower leans because a layer of soft clay
beneath it is weaker and more compressible on one side than the other — a
problem eventually stabilised not by strengthening the ground but by carefully
extracting small amounts of soil from beneath the high side and letting the
tower settle back toward vertical by a couple of degrees.

The thing that makes all of this nervous work is that a foundation is the only
part of a building nobody will ever look at again. A cracked beam can be
inspected, a corroded connection replaced, a failing facade panel swapped out.
The pile under column B7 will be seen once, by the man who concreted it, and
never again.
