# How a Suspension Bridge Holds Itself Up

Stand at the foot of a suspension bridge tower and look up along the main
cable. It leaves the saddle at the top of the tower and falls away in a long
shallow curve toward midspan, and then on the other side of the tower it drops
steeply toward the anchorage, where it disappears into a block of concrete the
size of a small apartment building. Almost everything about the bridge is
explained by that geometry. The cable is the structure. The towers exist to
hold it up in the air, the anchorages exist to keep it from being pulled in at
the ends, and the deck — the part everyone actually uses — hangs off it as an
afterthought, structurally speaking.

A cable cannot push. It has no bending stiffness worth speaking of and it
carries load in one way only, by pulling along its own length. This sounds like
a limitation and is in fact the whole advantage. Steel is enormously strong in
tension and its strength in tension does not depend on the shape or slenderness
of the member. A column of a given steel can be made to fail at a small
fraction of the material's strength simply by making it long and thin, because
it buckles. A wire of the same steel does not care how long it is. So if you
can arrange a structure in which the primary member is loaded purely in tension,
you can use the material at close to its real capacity, and you can build spans
that no arch or truss of the same material could reach.

## The Shape the Cable Chooses

Because a cable has no stiffness, it cannot choose its own shape. It takes
whatever shape puts it in equilibrium with the load hanging on it. A cable
carrying nothing but its own weight hangs in a catenary, the curve of a slack
chain between two posts. A cable carrying a load evenly distributed along the
horizontal — which is what a roadway deck of constant weight per metre is —
hangs in a parabola. On a real long-span bridge the deck and its hangers are
much heavier than the cable itself, so the main cable sits very close to a
parabola, and that is what designers work with.

The most important consequence of the shape is that the horizontal component of
the cable's tension is the same everywhere along the span. It has to be: there
is nothing pushing sideways on the cable between the towers. What varies is the
vertical component, which is zero at the low point at midspan and largest where
the cable meets the tower. Add the two together and you find the cable is least
stressed at midspan and most stressed at the tower, which is why cables are
sometimes built up from more strands near the towers, and why the cable band
spacing and hanger details get fussiest there.

That constant horizontal pull is set by the sag. A cable slung with a deep sag
pulls gently on its ends; a cable pulled nearly flat pulls on them ferociously.
Halve the sag and you roughly double the horizontal force. Designers typically
settle on a sag of something like a tenth of the main span, and the number is a
compromise rather than an optimum. Deeper sag means shorter cables, less steel,
smaller anchorages — but taller towers, which are expensive, and which have
their own wind and foundation problems. Shallower sag means squatter towers and
a great deal more force to dispose of at the ends.

That force is the part people underestimate. The anchorage of a major
suspension bridge is not a fitting; it is a geological intervention. Where
there is sound rock close to the surface, the cable strands can be splayed and
grouted into tunnels driven into the rock, and the mountain takes the load.
Where there is not, the bridge needs a gravity anchorage: a mass of concrete
heavy enough that friction against the soil beneath it resists the horizontal
pull, with the strand shoes embedded in it and splayed apart so the load spreads
through the block. On many famous bridges the anchorages cost more and took
longer than the towers.

## Why There Is a Stiffening Girder

If the cable does all the work, why is the deck so heavy and so deep?

Because a cable follows the load. Park a single very heavy truck at the quarter
point of a bare cable and the cable will deform into a kinked shape with a
corner under the wheel. That is fine for a cable and intolerable for a road.
The deck's job, beyond carrying traffic between hangers, is to be stiff enough
in bending that a concentrated load is spread out over a long stretch of
hangers before it reaches the cable — so the cable sees something closer to a
distributed load and barely changes shape at all.

This is why early suspension bridges had such a bad reputation. Several
nineteenth-century spans with light, flexible decks were destroyed by wind or
by marching troops or by cattle, and engineers of the period drew the reasonable
conclusion that suspension bridges were unsuitable for railways. John Roebling's
answer, used on the Niagara railway bridge and later on the Brooklyn Bridge, was
to stiffen the deck heavily and to add diagonal stays running straight from the
tower tops down to the deck, so that even if the main cable system misbehaved
the stays would hold. The Brooklyn Bridge is visibly over-built by modern
analysis, and it is still standing after more than a century of loads its
designer never imagined.

The theory that allowed engineers to stop over-building was worked out around
the turn of the twentieth century and is usually called deflection theory. Its
insight is that the cable's own change in geometry under load helps carry the
load — the system is not linear, and the stiffer the cable system, the less
stiffening the deck needs. Deflection theory let designers make decks dramatically
lighter and more slender. It was used with increasing confidence through the
1920s and 1930s, and it is essentially correct. What it left out was wind.

## What Happened at Tacoma, and What Changed

The first Tacoma Narrows bridge, opened in 1940, had a deck made of shallow
solid plate girders rather than an open truss, and it was exceptionally narrow
for its span. It moved constantly in ordinary wind, vertically, and the
movement was locally famous before the bridge was four months old. What
destroyed it that November was not the vertical motion but a twisting one, the
deck rolling about its own centreline with one kerb rising as the other fell.

The mechanism was aeroelastic. A deep bluff plate girder sheds vortices off its
edges, and once the deck begins to twist, the airflow around it changes in a way
that feeds energy into the next twist rather than damping it. The structure is
not being shaken by a matching external frequency; it is extracting energy from
a steady wind through its own motion. Engineers call this flutter, and it is the
same family of problem that aircraft designers had already met in wings and
control surfaces. Once started, it grows until something breaks.

Every long-span suspension bridge built since has been shaped with that in mind.
The traditional answer was the deep open stiffening truss, which lets wind pass
through and has very high torsional stiffness. The more elegant answer arrived
in Britain in the 1960s with the Severn Bridge, whose deck is a shallow welded
steel box shaped like a flattened aerofoil, with fairings on the edges and
gratings in the median to bleed air through. A closed box is torsionally stiff
in a way an open section can never be, and an aerodynamic profile sheds far less
energetic vortices than a blunt one. Most modern long spans are built this way,
and every one of them is tested as a sectional model and often as a full
aeroelastic model in a boundary-layer wind tunnel before anything is fabricated.

## Building the Cable in Place

You cannot lift a finished main cable into position — there is no crane in the
world for it. The cable is built where it will live, out of tens of thousands
of individual galvanized wires each about the thickness of a pencil lead.

The method Roebling developed and which is still used is aerial spinning. Temporary
catwalks are strung beneath the cable line, a hauling rope runs continuously over
the towers, and a spinning wheel carries a loop of wire across from anchorage to
anchorage, again and again, laying two wires per pass. Workers on the catwalks
adjust each wire's sag so that all of them will share load equally when the
bridge is finished, which is finicky, temperature-sensitive work and must be done
at night when the steel is at a uniform temperature. The wires are gathered into
hexagonal strands, the strands are squeezed together by a travelling compacting
machine into a single round cable, and the whole thing is wrapped in soft wire
and painted, or on newer bridges sealed and fed with dry air.

The alternative, used increasingly since the middle of the last century, is to
prefabricate parallel-wire strands in a factory, ship them on reels, and pull
them across as units. It is faster and less weather-dependent. It also gives up
some of the tuning that aerial spinning allows.

Either way the result is the one part of the bridge that cannot be replaced.
Decks get resurfaced, hangers get swapped out, towers get repainted, but the
main cable is the bridge. Corrosion inside it, where water has wicked in past
the wrapping and sits against the wires, is the problem that keeps suspension
bridge owners awake, and it is why modern practice pumps dehumidified air
through the cable and monitors what comes out the other end. A rope across a
gorge, scaled up until it can carry a city's traffic, still fails the way ropes
have always failed: quietly, from the inside, where nobody can see it.
