# Geothermal Power, from Steam Fields to Hot Rock

The Tuscan hills south of Volterra smell faintly of sulphur, and they have for
as long as anyone has written about them. The ground vents steam through
fissures the locals called *soffioni*, and by the early nineteenth century there
was an industry there boiling that steam's condensate down for boric acid. In
1904, Piero Ginori Conti, who ran the works, coupled a small reciprocating
engine to natural steam and used it to light five bulbs. Within a decade
Larderello had a commercial generating station, and a century later it is still
producing. Geothermal power is the only form of electricity generation that
began as a by-product of a chemical plant.

The field has never grown the way its physics suggests it should. The heat
underneath the continents is effectively unlimited — the planet's interior sheds
heat continuously from radioactive decay and primordial warmth, and the average
temperature gradient is something like twenty-five degrees Celsius per kilometre
of depth, everywhere. The problem has always been that heat alone is useless.
What a power plant needs is heat, water, and rock permeable enough to let the
water move. Get two of the three and you have an expensive dry hole.

## Where the Three Conditions Meet

Conventional geothermal fields sit where the crust is thin, young, or broken:
volcanic arcs, rift valleys, and the margins where plates converge. Iceland sits
directly on a spreading ridge. New Zealand's Taupō Volcanic Zone, Kenya's
stretch of the East African Rift, the Philippines, Indonesia, Japan, the
Salton Trough in California — the map of geothermal generation is largely the map
of active volcanism, with a few basin-and-range exceptions in Nevada where
faulting does the same job of cracking rock and letting water circulate deep.

In a productive field, meteoric water — rain, ultimately — percolates down
through fractures, heats against rock warmed by a magma body kilometres below,
and convects back up, trapped under a caprock of clay or self-sealed mineral
deposits. Drillers target the plume. A good well might be a couple of kilometres
deep and intersect a fracture set that delivers steam or brine at well over two
hundred degrees, pressurised by its own depth.

Exploration is geology rather than seismic imaging, because the fluid-filled
fractures that matter are narrow and hard to see. Crews map surface alteration,
sample fumaroles and hot springs for chemistry that fingerprints the reservoir
temperature, run resistivity surveys to find the conductive clay cap, and then
drill and hope. The failure rate on exploration wells is high enough that
financing geothermal has always been harder than financing the plant it would
feed — the money goes into the ground before anyone knows whether there is a
project.

## Three Ways to Turn Heat Into Torque

What comes up the well determines the plant.

Dry steam fields, the rarest kind, produce steam with essentially no liquid
water. Larderello is one. The Geysers, a sprawling field in the coastal ranges
north of San Francisco, is the largest in the world and still the largest single
geothermal installation anywhere. The plant is almost embarrassingly simple:
steam from the wells goes through separators to drop out rock particles, then
straight into a turbine, then to a condenser and a cooling tower. There is no
boiler, no fuel handling, no combustion.

Most fields instead produce hot pressurised brine. Bring it to the surface,
drop the pressure, and a fraction of it flashes instantly to steam — usually
ten or twenty percent, depending on temperature. The steam drives the turbine
and the remaining liquid goes to a second, lower-pressure flash vessel to
extract more, then to reinjection. Wairakei in New Zealand proved this at scale
in the late 1950s, and flash plants are now the industry standard.

The third design opens up resources too cool to flash usefully. In a binary
plant the geothermal fluid never touches the turbine; it passes through a heat
exchanger and gives its heat to a working fluid with a much lower boiling point
— isobutane, isopentane, sometimes a refrigerant — which vaporises, spins its
own turbine, condenses, and cycles again. An organic Rankine cycle can generate
from water in the low hundreds of degrees, and a well-known installation in
interior Alaska runs on a hot spring in the seventies Celsius. The thermal
efficiency is poor, by the standards of anything that burns fuel: at those
temperatures the second law permits only ten or fifteen percent of the heat to
become work. But the fuel is free and the plant runs at high capacity factor
regardless of weather or time of day, which is the quality that makes geothermal
valuable in a grid crowded with solar.

## The Chemistry Is the Hard Part

Geothermal brine is not water. It has spent thousands of years dissolving the
rock it sits in, and it arrives at the surface saturated with silica, calcium
carbonate, chlorides, metals, and dissolved gases. Cool it or depressurise it
and everything it is carrying starts coming out of solution — inside pipes,
inside heat exchangers, inside injection wells.

Silica is the persistent enemy. Dissolved quartz stays happily in solution at
reservoir temperature; as the brine cools past its saturation point it
polymerises and plates out as a glassy scale that narrows pipe and blinds heat
exchanger surfaces. Operators fight it by keeping brine above a threshold
temperature all the way to reinjection, by dosing acid to slow polymerisation, or
by deliberately precipitating the silica in a settling stage before it can pick
its own spot. Calcite scale forms the opposite way, when dropping pressure
releases carbon dioxide and raises pH in the wellbore. Hydrogen sulphide comes
up with the steam, smells like the whole business, and has to be scrubbed or
converted before it reaches the cooling tower plume. The Salton Sea field
produces brine so aggressively saline that handling it is the dominant
engineering problem — and, more recently, the reason anyone talks about
extracting lithium from it.

Then there is the reservoir itself, which is a finite thing even when the heat is
not. Producing steam faster than nature recharges the water drops the reservoir
pressure, and the wells go dry while the rock remains hot. The Geysers ran into
this hard in the late 1980s: output fell steeply, not because the field was
cooling but because it was drying out. The fix was to give it water back —
treated municipal wastewater from Santa Rosa and the Lake County communities,
piped dozens of kilometres uphill and injected into the field. A steam field is
now partly a sewage disposal system, and its output has stabilised. Every
modern geothermal project reinjects essentially all the fluid it produces, both
to sustain pressure and because the brine is not something you can put in a
creek.

## Making a Reservoir Where There Isn't One

If the limiting ingredient is permeability rather than heat, the obvious thought
is to manufacture it. Drill into hot crystalline basement, pump water in hard
enough to shear existing fractures and prop them open, drill a second well into
the stimulated volume, and circulate. Los Alamos scientists tried this at Fenton
Hill in New Mexico in the 1970s and demonstrated the principle while never quite
making it economic. European work at Soultz-sous-Forêts in Alsace pushed it
further.

The unavoidable complication is that shearing fractures at depth is, definitionally,
making small earthquakes. Most are imperceptible. Some are not. A deep
project beneath Basel was shut down in 2006 after stimulation produced a shock
strong enough to rattle the city and generate insurance claims, and a larger
event near Pohang in South Korea in 2017 was linked to a geothermal site with
enough confidence to end that programme too. Induced seismicity is now the
governing constraint on enhanced geothermal anywhere near people, managed with
traffic-light protocols that throttle or halt injection when monitored magnitudes
climb.

What has changed the outlook lately is borrowed technology. The shale industry
spent two decades learning to drill long horizontal wells and stimulate them in
precisely spaced stages, and those techniques transfer to hot granite with
surprisingly little modification. Recent projects in Nevada and Utah have drilled
horizontal pairs into basement rock and circulated water between them at
commercial flow rates, which is the thing that never quite worked at Fenton Hill.
If that approach generalises, the geographic constraint that has confined
geothermal to volcanic provinces mostly dissolves, because hot rock at four or
five kilometres exists under a great deal of the continental crust.

## The Larger, Duller Half

Electricity is not where most geothermal energy goes, and never has been. Heat
used as heat skips the thermodynamic penalty entirely. Reykjavík has been heated
by hot water pumped from nearby fields since the middle of the last century, and
nearly every building in Iceland is on a district loop; the same water runs
under pavements to melt snow and fills the swimming pools that the country
treats as civic infrastructure. Beneath Paris, the Dogger limestone aquifer
supplies sixty- to eighty-degree water to district heating networks serving whole
suburbs, drilled in doublets so that cooled water returns to the aquifer a safe
distance from the producer.

Shallower still, and not really the same phenomenon, ground-source heat pumps
exploit the fact that a few metres down the soil holds roughly the annual average
air temperature all year. That is not the Earth's internal heat; it is stored
sunshine and thermal inertia. A heat pump using the ground as its reservoir
moves several units of heat for every unit of electricity it consumes, because it
is working against a much gentler temperature difference than an air-source unit
does in February. It is the least glamorous thing in this article and by a wide
margin the most widely deployed.
