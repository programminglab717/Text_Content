# The Steam Turbine and the Thermal Power Plant

In the summer of 1897 the Royal Navy assembled at Spithead for Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee review, lines of warships at anchor stretching for miles, and an uninvited little vessel ran between the columns at a speed nobody present could match. Turbinia was long and thin and almost entirely engine. Her owner, Charles Parsons, had been unable to persuade the Admiralty to take his machinery seriously, so he arranged a demonstration that could not be ignored: something over thirty knots, in front of the entire fleet and the world's press, with a picket boat sent to chase her failing to get close. Within a few years the Navy was ordering turbine-driven destroyers.

The machine that embarrassed the fleet had been invented thirteen years earlier for a completely different purpose. Parsons had been trying to spin a dynamo. Electric generators want to turn fast and smoothly, and the reciprocating steam engines of the day did neither — they turned slowly, shook, needed heavy flywheels, and did not scale well upward. His idea was to take the pressure drop from boiler to exhaust and divide it among many small stages in series, each extracting a modest share of the energy, so that no single row of blades had to cope with a violent expansion. The first one produced roughly the power of a good horse team and turned at eighteen thousand revolutions a minute. The principle turned out to have almost no upper limit. A century and a bit later, the direct descendants of that machine, in units of half a gigawatt and more, still generate most of the electricity in the world.

## Squeezing Work Out of a Temperature Difference

Every thermal power station is a Rankine cycle wrapped in infrastructure. Water is pumped up to high pressure while still liquid, which is cheap because liquids barely compress. It is boiled and then superheated in the furnace. The resulting steam expands through the turbine, giving up energy to the blades. What comes out the far end is condensed back to water and returned to the pump. Four steps, and the whole engineering of the plant is an argument about how to make each of them less lossy.

The ceiling on the argument is thermodynamic. No heat engine can convert more of its heat into work than the fraction set by the difference between its hot and cold absolute temperatures, divided by the hot one. That is why plant designers are obsessed with two numbers at opposite ends of the machine: how hot the steam is going in, and how cold the condenser is.

The cold end is easier to explain and routinely misunderstood. A condenser is not there to save water. It is there to create a vacuum. Steam condensing at, say, thirty degrees Celsius occupies vastly less volume than it did as vapour, and the collapse holds the turbine exhaust at a pressure far below atmospheric — a few percent of a bar. That low back pressure is what allows the last stages of the turbine to keep expanding the steam and extracting work. A plant whose condenser vacuum degrades, because of air leaking in or because the river it cools with is warm in August, loses output immediately and measurably. This is also the source of the waste heat that thermal plants are notorious for. Roughly half to two thirds of the fuel energy leaves through the cooling water or the cooling towers, not because engineers have been careless but because the cycle requires a cold sink and the cold sink has to be somewhere.

The hot end is a materials problem. Higher steam temperature means better efficiency, and the limit is what the superheater tubes, the steam pipes, the valve chests and the first turbine stages can survive for decades of continuous service. Steel at six hundred degrees does not simply hold or fail; it creeps, deforming slowly under load, and the design life is a bet on the creep rate. Modern ultra-supercritical plants push past the critical point of water, above about two hundred and twenty bar, where liquid and vapour stop being distinguishable and the boiler no longer has a drum separating one from the other but is a single long once-through tube circuit. Such plants reach efficiencies in the mid-forties percent on coal, against perhaps thirty-five for an older subcritical unit — a difference that shows up directly in fuel bills and emissions.

Two further refinements are nearly universal. Reheat takes the steam after it has passed through the high-pressure cylinder, sends it back to the boiler to be brought up to temperature again, and then returns it to the intermediate-pressure cylinder. This raises efficiency and, just as importantly, keeps the steam drier through the later stages. Regenerative feedwater heating bleeds a fraction of the steam from selected turbine stages and uses it to warm the returning condensate in a series of heat exchangers, so that water entering the boiler is already hot. Both look like giving something up. Both pay, because they raise the average temperature at which heat enters the cycle.

## Inside the Cylinders

Open a large turbine during an overhaul and the change along its length is the most striking thing about it. At the high-pressure inlet the steam is dense and the blades are short, a few centimetres, set in rows so tight the rotor looks almost smooth. As the steam expands its volume multiplies enormously, and the blades must grow to pass it. By the last low-pressure stage they are extravagant objects — over a metre long in the largest machines, twisted from root to tip because the blade speed at the tip is several times that at the root and each radius needs its own angle of attack.

Those last blades live a brutal life. Spinning at three thousand or three thousand six hundred revolutions a minute, a single blade pulls on its root with a force of many tonnes, held by an interlocking fir-tree profile machined to a fit measured in hundredths of a millimetre. The steam reaching them has expanded so far that it is wet, and water droplets striking a leading edge at hundreds of metres per second erode metal, which is why the leading edges of last-stage blades are often faced with a hard cobalt alloy. Designers also spend enormous effort on resonance, because a blade that happens to have a natural frequency near a multiple of the rotation rate will fatigue and break, and a liberated blade at that speed does not leave quietly.

The staging comes in two flavours that textbooks separate more cleanly than reality does. In an impulse stage, the pressure drop happens entirely in fixed nozzles, which turn it into a fast jet; the moving blades simply change the jet's direction and absorb momentum at constant pressure. In a reaction stage, the moving blades themselves form converging passages, so the steam accelerates as it passes through them and pushes back. Parsons's machines were reaction machines; Gustaf de Laval, working at nearly the same time in Sweden, built single-stage impulse turbines and along the way had to invent a convergent-divergent nozzle to get supersonic steam and a deliberately flexible shaft to run above its own critical whirling speed. Real turbines mix the approaches, often impulse at the front where the pressure drop is steep and increasingly reaction further back.

## Running One

A turbine is not a device that is switched on. A cold rotor weighing tens of tonnes must be turned slowly on a barring gear for hours before and after operation, because a stationary hot rotor sags under its own weight and will not run true. Warming is done on a schedule dictated by thermal stress: the surface of a thick rotor or a valve chest heats faster than its interior, the two want to be different sizes, and forcing the issue cracks metal. A cold start on a large unit is a matter of hours, one of the practical reasons such plants resist being cycled daily to follow solar output.

The most dangerous moment is losing the electrical load. A generator connected to the grid is restrained by it; disconnect it while steam is still flowing and the turbine has nothing to push against. Acceleration is immediate and catastrophic, so every machine has overspeed protection, mechanical as well as electronic, which slams the stop valves shut within a fraction of a second. Even then, steam already inside the casing, and steam that flashes out of the reheat piping, has to be accounted for in the trip design.

## The Same Machine Under Different Names

What is remarkable about the steam turbine is how little the hot-end technology matters to it. Coal, gas, oil, uncontrolled municipal waste, wood pellets, geothermal brine, a field of mirrors focusing sunlight onto a tower of molten salt, or a nuclear reactor — all are ways of boiling water, and the machinery downstream is recognisably the same. Nuclear plants are the interesting variant, because water-cooled reactors deliver steam at a much lower temperature than a fossil furnace and often only just saturated. That forces lower cycle efficiency, much bigger turbines for the same output, moisture separators partway through the expansion, and frequently half-speed machines with four-pole generators to keep the enormous last-stage blades within their stress limits.

The other great variant runs the cycle second. A gas turbine burns fuel directly and exhausts at six hundred degrees or so, hot enough to be an excellent heat source for a Rankine cycle. Put a heat-recovery boiler behind it and pipe the steam to a steam turbine, and the combined arrangement converts around sixty percent of the fuel's energy into electricity, the highest of any heat engine in commercial service. It is a neat piece of opportunism: the gas turbine throws away heat at exactly the temperature at which the older machine does its best work.
