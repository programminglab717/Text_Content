# Hydropower and Pumped Storage

British grid controllers used to keep a television schedule on the desk. The reason was electric kettles. When a programme with a national audience reached its credits, or a match went to half time, several million people stood up and filled a kettle within the same ninety seconds, and demand on the network jumped by a couple of thousand megawatts — the output of a large power station appearing out of nowhere with no warning beyond the listings. The largest of those surges, after a penalty shootout in a World Cup semi-final, is still talked about in control rooms.

Nothing burning coal can answer a demand like that. What answered it sat inside a mountain in north Wales. Dinorwig was built in the caverns of an exhausted slate quarry above Llanberis in the late 1970s and early 1980s: six reversible machines, a little over seventeen hundred megawatts, fed by a lake near the summit of Elidir Fawr through shafts driven into the rock. Its units are often kept spinning in air — turning at full speed in dewatered casings, drawing a trickle of power, coupled to the grid and doing nothing at all. When the call comes, water is admitted and the plant reaches full output in something like sixteen seconds.

That combination of enormous power and near-instant availability is what makes moving water unlike every other way of generating electricity. It also comes with a constraint no other technology has quite so severely: you cannot put it where you want it. You put it where the landscape already has the two things it needs, which are height and water.

## Head and Flow

The power a hydro plant can produce is the product of just a few quantities: the density of water, the acceleration of gravity, the volume flowing per second, and the vertical distance the water falls. The last of these is called head, and it is the whole game. A given power output can be reached with a great deal of water falling a short way or a trickle falling a long way, and on paper those are equivalent. In practice they are utterly different machines, because flow is expensive — it demands enormous passages, huge runners and vast civil works — while head is, once you have found it, free.

This is why the classification of water turbines is really a classification of sites. Where head is measured in hundreds of metres, as in an alpine scheme drawing off a high lake, the water arrives through a long pipe at pressures that turn it into a weapon, and the machine of choice is a Pelton wheel. Nozzles convert the entire pressure into a fast jet; the jet strikes split, spoon-shaped buckets around the rim of a wheel; the bucket turns the water back on itself through almost a hundred and eighty degrees, extracting nearly all its momentum, and the water falls away with hardly any speed left. A Pelton runs in air at atmospheric pressure, which makes it robust and easy to regulate with a spear valve moving in the nozzle.

At moderate heads — tens to a few hundred metres, the range that covers most dams — the Francis turbine dominates, and it is the most-built prime mover of its kind in the world. Water enters all the way around the runner through a spiral casing, passes inward through adjustable guide vanes, is turned by curved blades and leaves downward along the axis. It is a reaction machine: pressure falls continuously through the runner, so the entire assembly is flooded and enclosed.

Where the head is small but the river is huge, the Kaplan turbine takes over, essentially a large ship's propeller in a tube, with blades that can be rotated in their hubs so that the machine stays efficient across a wide range of flows. Run-of-river plants on big lowland rivers use versions of it laid almost horizontally, the generator sealed inside a bulb in the middle of the flow.

Below any reaction turbine hangs a component that looks like plumbing and is not. The draft tube is a gradually widening passage carrying water from the runner down to the tailrace, and by slowing the water gently it converts leftover velocity back into pressure, creating suction under the runner. That suction is worth real energy — a turbine with a poorly shaped draft tube can lose several percent of its output — and it also lets the machine be installed above the level of the water it discharges into.

Getting the setting height right matters for another reason. If the pressure anywhere on a blade drops below the vapour pressure of water, the water boils cold: vapour bubbles form and are swept into a higher-pressure region where they collapse violently, and repeated collapse against a steel surface tears metal away microscopically. Cavitation damage looks like corrosion but is mechanical, and it is the reason machines are often set below tailwater level and why runners are inspected for pitting.

Done well, all this is astonishingly efficient. Large hydro turbines convert better than ninety percent of the energy in the falling water into shaft work, and the best exceed ninety-five, making them the most efficient prime movers humans build.

## The Weight of the Column

A hydro plant's other distinctive engineering problem is that the working fluid is heavy and nearly incompressible. Behind every turbine sits a column of water in a penstock that might be a kilometre long, all of it moving. Slam the gates shut and that mass has to stop, and the pressure spike produced — water hammer — can burst a pipe that comfortably handles its static head. The same effect reversed, when gates are opened too quickly, can pull a vacuum and collapse a penstock inward.

Schemes with long conduits therefore include a surge tank or surge shaft, a vertical stand of water open at the top, near the point where the pressure tunnel meets the penstock. When flow is cut, the moving column has somewhere to go: it rises in the shaft, oscillates for a few minutes, and settles. Without it, the governor would have to close over many seconds, which is far too slow for the machine to protect itself from overspeed.

That tension between the electrical need for a fast response and the hydraulic need for a gentle one runs through the design of every water turbine governor. Some plants resolve it with a pressure relief valve that opens as the wicket gates close, letting the water keep flowing while it stops doing work, and then shuts slowly once the column has been brought down.

## Storing Electricity by Moving Water Uphill

Pumped storage inverts the whole scheme. Two reservoirs at different elevations are connected through machinery that can run either way; cheap or surplus electricity drives water uphill, and it is released back through the turbines when power is worth more. Round-trip efficiency is usually somewhere in the seventies, meaning a quarter or so of the input is lost to friction, turbulence and electrical losses, which sounds poor until you compare it with the alternative of having no storage at all. The overwhelming majority of the world's grid-scale energy storage is still water in an upper reservoir; batteries have grown explosively but from a very small base, and they are measured in hours where a large pumped scheme is measured in days.

Most plants use a reversible pump-turbine, a Francis-type runner designed to work acceptably in both directions, driven by a motor-generator that changes roles. The compromise is real: a runner optimal as a pump is not optimal as a turbine, and reversing direction takes minutes. Starting the pump is its own puzzle, because a stationary runner cannot push against a full column of water. The usual method is to blow the water out of the casing with compressed air, run the machine up to speed in air, and then admit water once it is already turning.

Some schemes avoid the compromise with ternary sets, putting a separate pump and a separate turbine on a common shaft with the motor-generator, which allows a switch from generating to pumping in seconds and even a hydraulic short-circuit mode where the turbine runs while the pump does, letting the plant vary its net consumption continuously while charging. The more recent answer is variable-speed operation using a doubly-fed machine, which makes pumping power adjustable rather than all-or-nothing — a capability worth a great deal now that grids need something to absorb the surplus in the middle of a sunny day and regulate against the variability of wind.

Sites are the binding constraint, and the search has become creative: closed-loop schemes with no natural river at all, paired ponds on a disused mine, old open pits, and coastal cliffs with the sea as the lower reservoir, which one Japanese plant demonstrated for a number of years before it was retired.

## What the Reservoir Costs

The turbine hall is the easy part. A dam is a geological intervention, and its consequences run for hundreds of kilometres in both directions. Rivers carry sediment, and a reservoir is a place where the current stops and that sediment settles. Storage capacity is lost gradually and irreversibly unless the reservoir can be flushed; downstream, a river starved of its sediment load scours its own bed and stops rebuilding the delta and floodplain that depended on annual deposition. Egypt's coastline and the Mississippi's marshes both record what happens when the supply is cut.

Fish that migrate must be given a route past an obstacle they did not evolve for, and the ladders, lifts and bypasses built to provide one have a mixed record, particularly for juveniles heading downstream through turbine passages. Water released from deep in a stratified reservoir is cold and low in oxygen, which can change a warm river's ecology for tens of kilometres. Reservoirs in the tropics flood vegetation that decays without oxygen and emit methane for years afterwards. And large dams displace people, sometimes hundreds of thousands of them, which has ended more projects than engineering ever did.

Set against that, a reservoir is also the only form of stored energy on the grid that can sit for a season. A Norwegian or Canadian hydro system holds the runoff of a spring melt and spends it through a winter, following demand hour by hour and ramping from nothing to full output faster than any thermal plant can change its mind. That combination — bulk energy measured in months and response measured in seconds — is why the oldest way of making electricity remains one of the most useful things on the network.
