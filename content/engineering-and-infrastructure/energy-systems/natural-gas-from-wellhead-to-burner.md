# Natural Gas from Wellhead to Burner

The smell is fake. Methane is odourless, and so are the ethane and propane that
usually ride along with it, which means a leaking pipe used to give no warning
at all. The industry learned this in the most terrible way available: in 1937,
gas accumulating under a school in New London, Texas, found an ignition source
and killed close to three hundred students and teachers. Within weeks the state
required that gas be odorised, and the rest of the country followed. What you
smell at the stove is a few parts per million of a sulphur compound —
tert-butyl mercaptan, usually, sometimes blended with others — injected
deliberately at the last major facility the gas passes through before it reaches
a neighbourhood.

That injection point is worth thinking about, because it marks the end of a
process that has been going the other direction for the entire journey. Almost
everything else done to natural gas between the reservoir and the burner is
removal. Raw gas is a mixture, and most of what it contains when it comes out of
the ground is either worth more sold separately or actively destructive if left
in.

## What Actually Comes Up

A gas well produces methane, certainly, but rarely alone. Ethane, propane,
butanes, and heavier hydrocarbons come with it in proportions that depend on the
reservoir — a dry gas field might be ninety-five percent methane, while a wet
field or a well producing gas alongside oil can be a quarter or more heavier
components. Beyond hydrocarbons there is water vapour, always, because the gas
has been sitting in contact with formation brine for geological time. There is
carbon dioxide, frequently. There is nitrogen, which dilutes the heating value
and is a nuisance to remove. There may be hydrogen sulphide, which is corrosive,
and lethal at concentrations well below what your nose can register for long.
Some fields produce trace mercury. Some produce helium, in which case the
helium is quite possibly the most valuable thing in the stream.

The first separation happens within metres of the wellhead. A pressure vessel
lets the flow slow down so that liquid water and condensate — the gasoline-range
hydrocarbons that drop out as pressure falls — settle and are drawn off, while
gas leaves the top. From there the gas enters a gathering system: small-diameter
lines, often at modest pressure, converging from dozens or hundreds of wells on a
processing plant.

## Making It Fit the Spec

Pipeline companies do not accept whatever a field produces. They publish a
tariff specification, and it is unforgiving: hydrogen sulphide down to a few
parts per million, carbon dioxide to a couple of percent, water to a handful of
pounds per million cubic feet, and a heating value inside a narrow band so that
every appliance downstream burns correctly.

Acid gas comes out first. The workhorse is amine treating: the gas rises through
a tall absorber column against a falling stream of aqueous amine solution, which
chemically binds hydrogen sulphide and carbon dioxide. The loaded solution goes
to a regenerator where heat drives the acid gases back off, and the stripped
amine returns to the top of the absorber to do it again. Selective amines can be
chosen to grab the sulphide while leaving much of the carbon dioxide behind,
which matters when the CO2 is not worth the energy to remove.

What then happens to the concentrated hydrogen sulphide is one of the tidier
pieces of industrial chemistry. In a Claus unit, a third of it is burned to
sulphur dioxide, which then reacts with the remaining sulphide over a catalyst to
produce elemental sulphur and water. The sulphur runs off as a yellow liquid and
is sold. Between gas plants and refineries, this recovered stream is now where
most of the world's sulphur comes from — the sulphur mining industry was
essentially put out of business by pollution control.

Dehydration follows, and its purpose is not corrosion but blockage. Water and
light hydrocarbons under pressure form gas hydrates, ice-like crystalline solids
that grow at temperatures well above freezing and can plug a line solid. The
standard defence is a triethylene glycol contactor, another absorber column with
another regenerator, which takes the gas down to pipeline dryness. Plants that
will chill the gas cryogenically go further, polishing it through molecular sieve
beds to something near absolute dryness, because at minus a hundred degrees any
remaining water is a certainty of ice. Mercury, where present, is caught on
sulphur-impregnated carbon, for a very specific reason: mercury amalgamates with
aluminium, and the brazed aluminium heat exchangers used in cryogenic plants
fail catastrophically when it gets in.

## Splitting the Stream

The most elegant machinery in the plant does the separation of methane from
everything heavier, and it works by making the gas do the cooling itself.

Dried, treated gas at high pressure is expanded through a turboexpander — a
turbine that extracts work from the expansion rather than simply throttling it.
Taking work out drops the temperature far more than a valve would, down past
minus ninety degrees Celsius, and at that temperature ethane and everything
heavier condense while methane stays gaseous. The mixture drops into a
demethanizer, a distillation column operating in the cold, where methane leaves
the top as residue gas and the liquids leave the bottom. The shaft work
recovered from the expander is not wasted either; it typically drives a
compressor that partially repressurises the residue gas on its way to the
pipeline.

What comes off the bottom is a mixed stream of natural gas liquids, and it gets
shipped to a fractionation hub — in the United States, overwhelmingly to the
salt dome complex at Mont Belvieu on the Texas coast — where a train of
distillation columns separates it by boiling point in sequence. Ethane off the
first, propane off the next, then the butanes, with a natural gasoline stream
remaining. Ethane goes to steam crackers to become ethylene and eventually
polyethylene. Propane goes into tanks for heating and cooking, and increasingly
onto ships for export. The butanes go to refineries for blending or to
petrochemicals.

The decision of how much ethane to pull out is made fresh, sometimes daily.
Ethane left in the gas stream still sells as fuel on its heating value. Pulled
out, it sells as a petrochemical feedstock. When the feedstock price is weak, a
plant will reject ethane — deliberately leaving it in the residue gas up to the
limit the pipeline's heating value spec permits — and the columns idle.

## The Long Haul

Transmission pipelines are a different world from gathering lines: large
diameter, heavy wall, buried, and running at pressures of several dozen
atmospheres. Gas moving through steel loses pressure to friction, so every
hundred kilometres or so a compressor station restores it, using turbines that
burn a slipstream of the gas they are pushing, or increasingly electric motors.

Those stations are also the control points. A pipeline is not just a conduit but
a storage vessel: the mass of gas held inside a long transmission line at
pressure is substantial, and operators lean on that line pack deliberately,
packing the system overnight when demand is low and drawing it down through the
morning peak. The alternative and much larger storage is underground —
mostly depleted gas reservoirs near consuming regions, refilled all summer and
withdrawn all winter, plus salt caverns, which hold less but can be cycled in
and out within days and so are used for short, sharp swings.

The lines are inspected from inside. Tools called pigs are launched into the
flow and carried along by it, scraping accumulated liquids and debris ahead of
them; the sophisticated ones carry magnetic flux leakage sensors or ultrasonic
transducers and map wall thickness continuously, so that corrosion pits and dents
can be located to within a metre and excavated before they matter.

Where a transmission line meets a local utility, the gas passes through a city
gate: metering, filtration, a pressure reduction through regulators from
transmission pressure to distribution pressure, and odorisation. Because
expanding gas cools, city gates usually preheat before the regulators, or they
would frost themselves shut. Downstream of that, pressure falls again in stages
— distribution mains, then service lines, then a regulator at the meter that
delivers gas to a house at a pressure only slightly above atmospheric, low
enough that a burner orifice sized in the factory produces the right flame.

Much of the risk in the entire chain concentrates in that last, most mundane
segment. Old cities still carry cast iron mains laid a century ago, jointed with
lead and jute, which crack when the ground shifts or a nearby excavation
disturbs them. Bare steel lines from mid-century corrode where their coating
failed. Utilities run replacement programmes decades long, and in the meantime
send crews walking the streets with flame ionisation detectors, sampling the air
above the pavement.

The leaks matter for a second reason that has only recently reshaped the
industry's priorities. Methane is a far more potent greenhouse gas than carbon
dioxide over the decades immediately after release, so gas that escapes anywhere
along this chain — a venting compressor seal, a stuck pneumatic controller, an
unlit flare, a failed well casing like the one that emptied a storage reservoir
into the air above Los Angeles for four months in 2015 — erodes the advantage
that burning gas has over burning coal. The distribution of those emissions
turns out to be lopsided: aircraft and satellite surveys keep finding that a
small number of super-emitters account for a large share of the total. That is
discouraging as a description of how the system has been maintained and
encouraging as an engineering problem, because a lopsided distribution is one you
can actually go and fix.
