# How a Solar Cell Turns Light into Current

A silicon solar cell the size of a postage stamp and a silicon solar cell the size of a dinner plate produce the same voltage in sunlight: somewhere around six tenths of a volt. Make the cell bigger and you get more current, not more volts. That one fact, which surprises nearly everyone who measures it for the first time, is the doorway into how the whole device works, because it says that the voltage is a property of the material rather than of the object. It is set by what silicon atoms do to electrons, and no amount of engineering moves it very far.

What silicon does to electrons is hold most of them tightly and leave a gap. In an isolated atom, electrons occupy discrete levels. Pack atoms into a crystal and those levels smear into bands, and in a semiconductor the highest band that is full — the valence band, where the bonding electrons sit — is separated from the next available empty band by a forbidden zone about 1.1 electronvolts wide in silicon. Nothing can sit in the gap. An electron is either bound in a bond or free to roam the crystal, with nothing in between, and the energy required to make that jump is the material's fundamental currency.

Light arrives in quanta whose energy depends only on wavelength. Red photons carry less energy than blue ones. A photon with less energy than the band gap cannot promote an electron at all and passes straight through — silicon is, to infrared light beyond about eleven hundred nanometres, effectively transparent, which is why an infrared camera can see through a wafer. A photon with more than enough energy does the job and wastes the surplus: the freed electron is kicked high into the conduction band and then tumbles back down to the band edge within picoseconds, giving its extra energy to lattice vibrations, which is to say heat.

Those two losses are why no single-material solar cell can be extremely efficient. A wide band gap captures little of the spectrum but extracts good voltage from what it captures; a narrow one absorbs almost everything but throws away most of each photon's energy as heat. Balancing the two against the actual solar spectrum, together with the unavoidable radiative recombination that any good absorber must also be capable of, gives a theoretical ceiling for a single junction in the low thirties of a percent, with the optimum gap sitting not far from silicon's. This is the deep reason the industry converged on a material it chose originally for entirely practical reasons.

## Separating the Charges

Knocking an electron loose is only half the problem. A free electron in a uniform crystal wanders randomly and, within a millisecond or so, finds a hole — the vacancy it left behind, which behaves in every respect like a mobile positive charge — and drops back in. The energy comes out as heat or a faint glow, and nothing has been accomplished. To make a current, the electron and the hole must be persuaded to leave the cell through different terminals.

The classical way to do that is the p-n junction. One side of the wafer is doped with boron, an element with one fewer valence electron than silicon, leaving a population of mobile holes. The other is doped with phosphorus, which has one more, contributing mobile electrons. Put the two regions in contact and carriers diffuse across the boundary and annihilate, leaving behind the fixed, now-uncompensated dopant ions: negative on the p side, positive on the n side. That stranded charge sets up an electric field across a thin depletion region, and the field opposes further diffusion until equilibrium is reached. A built-in potential exists across the junction before any light falls on it.

Illuminate the cell and photogenerated carriers that reach that field are swept across it, each in its own direction — electrons toward the n side, holes toward the p side. The junction does not create the carriers. It sorts them. A more modern way to put it is that a solar cell needs two contacts with asymmetric selectivity, one that lets electrons out easily and blocks holes and one that does the reverse, and the doped junction is simply the oldest and cheapest way to build such a pair.

Whether a given carrier survives long enough to be collected is the practical question that separates a good cell from a poor one. The relevant quantity is diffusion length: how far, on average, a carrier travels before recombining. In a decent wafer it is a few hundred micrometres, comfortably more than the wafer thickness, which is exactly why cells can be made as thin as they are. Recombination happens at crystal defects, at metal impurities, and above all at surfaces, where the lattice abruptly ends and millions of dangling bonds offer easy places for an electron and hole to meet. Nearly every efficiency gain of the past three decades has been some form of surface passivation — capping those dangling bonds chemically, or repelling minority carriers from the surface with a fixed charge, or both.

## From Sand to Wafer

The silicon itself starts as quartzite reduced with carbon in an electric arc furnace to a metallurgical grade too impure by many orders of magnitude for electronics. Purification goes through a gas: the silicon is converted to trichlorosilane, distilled, and then decomposed onto hot rods, building up polysilicon of extraordinary purity. That polysilicon is melted in a quartz crucible with a measured pinch of dopant, a seed crystal is touched to the surface, and a cylindrical ingot is pulled slowly upward while rotating, growing as a single crystal — the Czochralski process, borrowed wholesale from the chip industry.

The ingot is squared off and sliced by a diamond wire into wafers on the order of a hundred and fifty micrometres thick. The wafer then gets etched in hot alkali, which attacks the crystal planes unevenly and leaves the surface covered in microscopic pyramids. Those pyramids matter more than they look: light that reflects off one face strikes another rather than escaping, and light entering at an angle travels a longer path through the silicon, which is important because silicon's indirect band gap makes it a weak absorber that needs all the optical path length it can get. A thin antireflection layer of silicon nitride goes on top, which both cuts reflection further and passivates the front surface, and which is responsible for the familiar dark blue colour. Metal fingers and busbars are screen-printed in silver paste and fired so that the paste eats through the nitride to contact the silicon beneath, a deliberately violent step that has to make good electrical contact over a minuscule fraction of the area while leaving the passivation intact everywhere else.

The rear surface used to be a full aluminium sheet, which was electrically convenient and optically terrible. The change that dominated the industry through the 2010s, usually called PERC, added a dielectric passivation layer to the back with the metal contacting only through small laser-opened points, so that most of the rear surface both reflects unabsorbed light back into the wafer and stops carriers from dying there. Its successors take the idea further, replacing the metal-silicon interface entirely with thin passivating films of oxide and doped polysilicon, or with layers of amorphous silicon in heterojunction cells, chasing the same goal of contacts that conduct without being recombination sinks.

## The Module and Everything After It

Individual cells are wired in series into a module, because six tenths of a volt is useless and sixty cells in a string give something a converter can work with. That series connection has a hazard built in: the current through a string is limited by its worst cell, and a cell that is shaded while its neighbours are illuminated is driven into reverse bias by the others, dissipating rather than producing power and developing a hot spot that can char the laminate. The fix is bypass diodes in the junction box, typically one across each third of the module, which turn on and route current around a troubled section. Halving cells and rewiring the module into parallel halves, now standard practice, cuts resistive losses and makes partial shading less punishing as well.

The stack is laminated: tempered glass, an encapsulant that must stay optically clear and electrically insulating through decades of ultraviolet and thermal cycling, the cell matrix, and a polymer backsheet or a second sheet of glass. Glass on both sides allows bifacial operation, picking up light scattered off the ground behind the array, which pays surprisingly well over sand, gravel or snow.

Heat is the module's quiet enemy. Efficiency falls by roughly a third of a percent for every degree the cell rises above the standard test temperature of twenty-five degrees, mostly through a drop in voltage, and cells in full sun run well above the air temperature. A bright cold day in March often yields more power than a hazy hot one in July, which trips up anyone extrapolating from datasheet ratings.

What comes out of the array is direct current at a voltage that shifts with light and temperature, and the inverter's first job is to find the point on the current-voltage curve where the product of the two is largest. That maximum power point sits at the knee of the curve and moves constantly, so the inverter perturbs its operating voltage every fraction of a second, watches which way the power goes, and hunts. Only after that does the more familiar work of synthesising an alternating waveform happen — along with the safety function of detecting that the grid has gone dead and refusing to keep energising a line that a repair crew may be holding.

None of this was inevitable. The photovoltaic effect was noticed in an electrolytic cell in the 1830s and selenium devices existed by the end of that century, but they converted well under one percent and stayed laboratory curiosities. The modern cell dates to Bell Labs in the mid-1950s, and the reason it survived its first two decades is that satellites needed power and did not care what it cost. Terrestrial economics arrived only through a long, grinding accumulation of manufacturing scale, each doubling of cumulative production shaving a roughly constant fraction off the price, until a technology once reserved for spacecraft became the cheapest way to generate electricity across much of the world.
