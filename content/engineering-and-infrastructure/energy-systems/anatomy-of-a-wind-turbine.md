# Anatomy of a Wind Turbine

Stand at the base of a large wind turbine and the first surprise is how slowly it turns. Ten or twelve revolutions a minute, five seconds between blades — a lazy, almost agricultural pace. The second surprise arrives a moment later, when you work out that the tip of a blade eighty metres long is covering more than half a kilometre every ten seconds, which is highway speed multiplied by three, and that the sound overhead is the trailing edge of an aerofoil cutting air at close to ninety metres per second. The machine is not slow at all. It is simply very large, and largeness disguises speed.

Almost everything about the modern turbine follows from a single unforgiving equation. The power available in moving air is proportional to the area swept by the rotor and to the cube of the wind speed. Cubed. Wind at twelve metres per second carries eight times the power of wind at six. This is why siting matters more than almost any other decision, why a ridge line or a coast or an offshore shoal is worth so much more than a sheltered valley, and why developers spend a year or more measuring a site before committing to it. It also explains the relentless growth in rotor diameter: swept area goes as the square of the diameter, so every metre added to the blade buys disproportionate energy, and the limit is structural and logistical rather than aerodynamic.

## Lift, Not Push

A common mental picture has the wind shoving the blades around like a child's pinwheel. That is not what happens, and machines built on that principle — drag devices like the cup anemometer or the Persian vertical-axis mills — are feeble by comparison. A turbine blade is a wing. It generates lift perpendicular to the airflow it experiences, and it is the airflow it experiences, not the wind you feel standing underneath, that governs its behaviour.

Because the blade is moving sideways through the air at high speed while the wind comes at it from the front, the airflow the blade actually meets is the vector sum of the two. Near the hub, where the blade's own motion is slow, that combined flow arrives almost head-on from the wind direction. Out at the tip, where the blade is moving many times faster than the wind, the combined flow arrives from almost straight ahead along the plane of rotation. The angle changes continuously along the span, which is why a blade is twisted, sometimes by twenty degrees or more from root to tip — each section is set at the pitch its own local airflow demands. The lift produced by that flow is tilted very slightly forward of the rotor plane, and that small forward component, integrated along the blade, is the torque that turns the shaft. Most of it comes from the outer third, where the speeds are high and the chord is still meaningful.

No rotor can take all of the kinetic energy out of the air passing through it, for the plainest of reasons: air that has given up all its energy has stopped, and stopped air cannot get out of the way of the air behind it. The flow simply diverts around the rotor instead. Working through the momentum balance gives a maximum extractable fraction of sixteen twenty-sevenths — a little over fifty-nine percent — which is known as the Betz limit, and which corresponds to slowing the wind to about two thirds of its upstream speed at the rotor. Good modern rotors get into the high forties or low fifties percent of the passing energy, which is remarkably close to a theoretical ceiling derived from nothing but conservation laws.

To stay near that ceiling, the rotor must turn at a speed proportional to the wind, keeping the ratio of tip speed to wind speed near its optimum, typically somewhere around seven. That requirement is the reason nearly every large turbine has three blades and runs at variable speed. Fewer blades need higher rotational speed for the same ratio, which means more noise and more erosion; more blades add cost and interfere with each other's flow without adding much. Three is also the arrangement whose rotating inertia stays constant as the rotor turns, which spares the tower a twice-per-revolution wobble that two-bladed machines have to absorb with a teetering hub.

## The Four Wind Regimes

Every turbine's behaviour is summarised by its power curve, and the curve has four distinct zones.

Below the cut-in speed, around three or four metres per second, the available power does not cover the machine's own losses and the rotor idles or parks. Between cut-in and rated wind, the controller is trying to harvest everything on offer: blades sit at their most efficient pitch, and the generator's counter-torque is varied to let the rotor speed up and slow down with the wind, holding that optimal tip-speed ratio. Torque control, not pitch, does the work here, and the rotor is deliberately allowed to behave like a flywheel, buffering gusts.

At rated wind — often eleven to thirteen metres per second — the generator reaches its design output and the strategy inverts. Now the goal is to throw energy away. Pitch actuators in the hub rotate each blade about its own axis, reducing the angle of attack, feathering the blade so that it produces exactly enough lift to hold rated power and no more. From here up, output is flat; the pitch system is continuously shedding a surplus that grows with the cube of the wind.

Above cut-out, traditionally around twenty-five metres per second, the loads become unreasonable and the machine shuts down, feathering the blades fully so they knife edge-on into the gale, applying the brake and letting the rotor drift. Modern controllers increasingly soften this cliff, de-rating gradually through the storm rather than tripping off all at once, because the sudden loss of a whole wind farm is unwelcome to a system operator and the last few hours of a storm are worth having.

One consequence of the power curve confuses people who compare turbines by nameplate rating. A machine reaches full output only in strong wind, which is uncommon; most of its annual energy comes from moderate wind in the sloping part of the curve. Fitting a larger rotor to the same generator therefore raises annual production substantially even though the nameplate does not change, because it moves the rated point down to a wind speed that occurs more often. Capacity factors that once sat near a quarter now commonly reach forty percent onshore and better than half offshore, and a good deal of that improvement is simply bigger blades in front of unchanged generators.

## What Is in the Box

The nacelle is a shed on a stick, and it contains a drivetrain arranged around a problem of mismatched speeds. The rotor wants to turn at twelve revolutions a minute; a conventional generator wants something closer to fifteen hundred. The classical answer is a gearbox with a planetary first stage and helical stages behind it, multiplying speed roughly a hundredfold. Gearboxes have been the industry's most stubborn reliability headache, since they carry enormous torque under loads that reverse and fluctuate constantly, and a gearbox replacement offshore requires a vessel with a very large crane. The alternative is to eliminate it: direct-drive machines use a permanent-magnet generator of much larger diameter, turning at rotor speed, with hundreds of poles. It is heavier and demands rare-earth magnets, but there is much less to wear out.

Either way, the electrical output passes through a power converter, which rectifies whatever frequency the generator happens to produce and synthesises a clean grid-frequency output. That is what permits variable-speed operation in the first place, and it also decouples the machine mechanically from the grid.

Behind the drivetrain sit the yaw drives, a ring of motors that rotate the whole nacelle on its bearing to keep the rotor facing the wind, guided by a vane and anemometer mounted on the roof — sensors which are themselves sitting in the rotor's wake, so their readings must be corrected. Because the power and control cables run down the inside of the tower, continuous yawing in one direction eventually twists them, and every turbine keeps a cable-twist count and periodically unwinds itself.

The blades themselves are hollow shells of glass fibre in epoxy, with a load-bearing spar running the length of the blade, shear webs tying the two shells together, and carbon fibre in the spar caps of the longest designs where stiffness is critical to stop the tip from striking the tower. Each blade carries lightning receptors near the tip and a conductor down to the hub, because an isolated composite object two hundred metres up is going to be struck.

Fatigue, rather than any single peak load, sets the design. Once per revolution, every blade rotates from the twelve o'clock position to six o'clock, and its own weight bends it first one way and then the other. Once per revolution it also passes through wind that is faster at the top of the rotor disc than the bottom, and through the pressure disturbance in front of the tower. Over twenty years that is a few hundred million fully reversing load cycles, superimposed on the random buffeting of atmospheric turbulence. Designing for it is less about strength than about the slow accumulation of microscopic damage in a laminate.

Turbines also interfere with each other. A working rotor leaves behind a slower, more turbulent column of air that recovers only gradually as the surrounding flow mixes back in, so a machine planted in a neighbour's wake produces less and suffers more fatigue. Farm layouts trade land area against those losses, with spacing measured in rotor diameters and long axes oriented across the prevailing wind. Some operators now yaw upwind turbines deliberately off the wind, giving up a little of their own output to steer a wake sideways past the machine behind. On cold mornings offshore, when the air is humid and the sea is warm, the mixing shows up as cloud, and satellite images reveal the wakes trailing forty or fifty kilometres downwind of the array like the wash behind a fleet of ships.
