# Balancing the Grid in Real Time

On a wall in every transmission control room there is a number displayed to two or three decimal places, and it is almost always wrong. In North America it should read 60.000 hertz. In Europe, 50.000. What it actually reads is 59.987, or 50.014, drifting up and down in a slow wander that never quite settles. That wandering number is the most important diagnostic the grid has, because it is not really a measurement of frequency at all. It is a measurement of whether the continent is generating more power than it is using, or less, at this instant.

The link is mechanical. Almost every large generator on a synchronous grid is a spinning mass — a turbine shaft and rotor weighing tens or hundreds of tonnes — turning in lockstep with every other such machine on the network, electromagnetically coupled through the transmission system as rigidly as if they were bolted to a common shaft. Add load without adding fuel and those rotors give up kinetic energy to supply it, which means they slow down. Remove load and they speed up. Frequency is the tachometer reading of a machine the size of a subcontinent.

This is why the phrase "the grid cannot store electricity" matters so much in practice. Some storage exists in the system — the rotational energy in those rotors, a little in the magnetic fields of transformers and lines — but it amounts to seconds of consumption, not minutes. Generation must equal demand continuously, including the losses in getting from one to the other. Nobody dispatches a grid to a daily total. They dispatch it to right now, and then to right now again.

## The First Thirty Seconds

When a large generating unit trips offline, the deficit does not wait for anyone to notice. It is made up instantly by every spinning machine still connected, each contributing kinetic energy in proportion to its inertia, all of them decelerating together. The initial rate of that deceleration — the rate of change of frequency, which operators call RoCoF — depends on the size of the loss divided by the total inertia on the system. A big interconnection with many heavy thermal and hydro units has enormous inertia and slides downward gently. A small island grid, or a large one running mostly on inverter-connected wind and solar with the thermal fleet shut down, has far less, and the same megawatt loss drops the frequency faster and further.

Inertia buys time; it does not fix anything. The fix begins a second or two in, when turbine governors respond. A governor is a speed controller: it senses shaft speed and opens the steam or water or fuel valve when speed falls. Crucially, it is deliberately not a perfect controller. Governors are set with what is called droop, typically around four or five percent, meaning that a five percent fall in frequency calls for the unit to go from no output to full output, proportionally. Droop is what lets hundreds of independent machines share a disturbance without fighting each other. If every governor tried to restore exactly 60.000 hertz, they would hunt and oscillate. With droop, they settle cooperatively at a new, slightly lower frequency, each having picked up a share of the deficit set by its own capacity.

So primary response arrests the fall but leaves the frequency low. Restoring it is the job of the next layer, automatic generation control, which runs as software in the control centre rather than as hardware on the turbine. Every few seconds, AGC computes a quantity called area control error: the deviation of power flowing on the tie lines to neighbouring areas, adjusted by a term proportional to the frequency deviation. The arithmetic is designed so that the error points at whoever caused the imbalance. If a generator in your area tripped, your tie-line flows and the frequency both move in a way that assigns the error to you, and your area's AGC nudges set-points on the units enrolled in regulation until it returns to zero. Your neighbours' governors, which helped in the first seconds, gradually back off.

Behind that sits a slower tier still: reserves that a human dispatcher calls on over minutes, to replace the capacity the fast-responding units just used up so that the system is ready for the next contingency. The planning rule, used almost everywhere in some form, is N-1 — the network must survive the loss of any single element, generator, line or transformer, without violating limits or shedding customers. That principle sizes the reserve requirement: at minimum, enough to cover the largest single unit or import that could vanish without warning.

## Dispatching Before the Fact

Real-time balancing is only the last step of a process that started the day before. Somewhere around noon, a system operator or market runs unit commitment: a large optimisation deciding which generators will be online tomorrow, hour by hour, given forecast demand, forecast wind and solar, planned outages, and the physical constraints that make thermal plants awkward. A coal unit may need eight hours to come up from cold and cannot be cycled freely. It has a minimum stable output below which its boiler becomes unstable, and a minimum run time once started. Those constraints, not fuel cost, often decide what runs overnight.

Within the committed fleet, economic dispatch decides how much each unit produces, re-solved every five minutes in most markets, stacking offers from cheapest to most expensive until supply meets forecast demand. The cost of the last unit needed sets the price. The solution must also respect the network: send too much power down a particular corridor and a line overloads, so the optimisation is security-constrained, which is why the price of electricity is not one number but hundreds or thousands of them, one per node, diverging wherever the wires are the binding constraint.

Forecasting has become the discipline that holds all this together, because wind and solar are not dispatched so much as accommodated. Operators work with net load — demand minus whatever the weather is providing — and net load has a shape that conventional plants were never designed for. In places with heavy solar penetration, midday net load sags almost to nothing and then climbs steeply for three hours as the sun sets into the evening peak. The problem is not the energy; it is the ramp. A fleet must be kept ready to increase output very quickly for a few hours a day, which favours gas turbines, hydro, imports and batteries, and penalises anything that takes an hour to turn around.

## Voltage Is a Different Problem

Frequency is a global property. Every point on a synchronous grid has the same frequency, which is what makes it such a clean control signal. Voltage is not like that. It is stubbornly local, governed by the flow of reactive power — the component of alternating current that sloshes back and forth without doing useful work but is necessary to magnetise motors and transformers and to overcome the inductance of the lines themselves.

Reactive power does not travel well. Pushing it any distance consumes more of it, so voltage has to be supported near where it is needed, using generator excitation, capacitor banks switched in and out, static compensators, and tap-changing transformers. A heavily loaded network with too little local reactive support can enter voltage collapse, in which falling voltage causes motors to draw more current, which drops voltage further. It is a genuinely dangerous mode, and it moves faster than a dispatcher can react. Several major blackouts began as voltage problems that nobody on the frequency display could see going wrong.

## When It Goes

Cascading failures follow a recognisable script. Something ordinary happens — a line sags into a tree on a hot afternoon, a generator trips — and its load redistributes onto neighbouring lines, which are now closer to their thermal limits. If an operator notices and redispatches, the event ends there. If they do not, perhaps because the alarm system is silently failing, the next line trips on overload, and the redistribution gets worse. The intervals shorten. Within minutes, the sequence runs faster than human response, then faster than the communications between control areas, and protection relays acting on purely local information begin islanding the system into fragments. Each fragment has its own generation-to-load balance, almost none of them balanced, and most of them collapse.

Defences against this are mostly automatic and mostly blunt. Under-frequency load shedding relays disconnect blocks of customers at pre-set thresholds a fraction of a hertz apart, on the logic that dropping a tenth of demand is better than losing everything. Out-of-step protection deliberately splits the network along planned seams. Both amount to sacrificing part of the system to keep the rest synchronised.

Recovery is slower than most people expect, because a dead grid cannot be energised from a dead grid. Most power stations need substantial electricity to start: to run feed pumps, fans, control systems, lubrication. A handful of units are designated black-start capable, typically hydro plants, which need little more than a battery to open a gate, or gas turbines with dedicated diesel generators. From those seeds, operators energise a transmission path, pick up a small block of load to stabilise the island, bring another station up on that supply, and grow the island outward, matching frequency and phase angle at each interconnection before closing the breaker. Restoring a large grid is less like flipping a switch than like relighting a pilot flame and carefully feeding it.

What is changing now is the first thirty seconds. Wind, solar and batteries connect through power electronics, which have no rotating mass and, in their conventional grid-following form, wait to see a stable voltage waveform before injecting current into it. A grid built mostly from such machines has little inertia and, in the limit, nothing establishing the waveform at all. The answers being deployed are partly to synthesise the missing response — batteries detecting a frequency excursion and answering within a fraction of a second, faster than any governor could — and partly to change the control philosophy, to grid-forming inverters that impose a voltage and frequency of their own and let current follow, behaving in the crucial early moments rather like the spinning machines they are replacing.
