# How Paper Is Made

Tear a sheet of printer paper down its length, then tear a second sheet across the short way. One tear runs nearly straight and comes away clean. The other wanders, and the edge it leaves is furred with loose fibre. The difference is grain: in machine-made paper the fibres lie mostly along the direction the sheet was travelling as it formed, so a tear running with them slips between fibres, while a tear across them has to snap each one. That small asymmetry is a decent introduction to what paper actually is — a thin mat of plant fibres filtered out of water, squeezed, dried, and given a direction by the machine that produced it.

Nothing glues the mat together. Cellulose, the long chain sugar that makes up most of a plant cell wall, is studded with hydroxyl groups, and when two wet fibres are pressed into contact and the water between them is driven off, surface tension pulls them close enough for those groups to hydrogen-bond directly to one another. Paper is held together by the same weak attraction that makes water wet. That explains why a sheet goes limp the instant it is soaked and recovers when it dries, and why the entire industry is really in the business of controlling how much fibre surface is available to bond.

## An Invention That Travelled Slowly

Chinese tradition credits a Han court official named Cai Lun with presenting paper to the emperor early in the second century, though fragments older than that have been excavated, so he is better understood as the man who systematized and publicized a craft already underway. The early recipes used what was cheap and fibrous: hemp rags, worn fishing nets, the inner bark of the mulberry. Boiling with wood ash softened the material, pounding separated the fibres, and a screen dipped into the resulting slurry caught a layer thin enough to dry into a sheet.

The technique moved west slowly and in stages, reaching Central Asia and then Baghdad and Damascus during the eighth and ninth centuries. A well-worn story has Chinese papermakers captured at a battle near the Talas River and made to teach the craft to their captors; the story is probably too tidy to be literal, but the chronology it compresses is real enough. Paper mills followed the spread of Islam across North Africa and into Iberia, and by the twelfth century sheets were being made at Xàtiva in Spain. Italian papermakers at Fabriano took the craft over during the thirteenth century and changed it substantially, driving the pounding hammers with water power, forming sheets on moulds of drawn brass wire rather than woven grass, and sizing the finished sheet with animal gelatin so that iron gall ink would sit on the surface instead of feathering into it. The watermark, a shape in wire sewn onto the mould so that a slightly thinner layer of fibre formed over it, was their invention too, and it has been used ever since as a maker's signature and a check against forgery.

The consequence was economic. Parchment required a herd; a large Bible could consume the skins of a couple of hundred animals. Paper required rags and water, both of which a town already had. Printing with movable type in the fifteenth century made sense partly because a cheap, uniform, abundant substrate was already waiting for it.

## The Hand Sheet

Before machinery, a sheet was made by a crew of three working in rhythm around a vat of warm fibre slurry called stuff. The vatman dipped a wooden frame strung with wire — the mould — with a removable rim called the deckle sitting on top of it, lifted it out level, and shook it fore and aft and side to side as the water drained. That shake mattered enormously: it tumbled the fibres into a random orientation, which is why a good hand sheet has no grain direction and tears equally badly in every direction. The deckle held the slurry in and left the soft, thinned edge that is still called a deckle edge.

The mould was handed to the coucher, who rolled the still-sodden sheet face down onto a damp woollen felt in one motion and handed the mould back. Sheets and felts were stacked alternately into a pile, and the pile was squeezed in a heavy screw press until most of the water ran out. The sheets were then separated, hung over ropes in a drying loft with slatted walls, dipped in a gelatin tub once dry, pressed again, and sometimes burnished with a smooth stone. An experienced crew could turn out several sheets a minute, hour after hour, and the pace of that team set the pace of every printing shop in Europe.

## The Rag Famine and the Machine

Rags were the only feedstock, and demand for paper eventually outran the supply of worn-out linen. Governments restricted rag exports, printers complained, and naturalists cast around for alternatives. The observation that wasps build nests of a genuine paper made from chewed wood fibre was noted early in the eighteenth century and then sat unused for well over a century, because no one had a practical way to reduce wood to fibre.

Two mechanical inventions arrived first. The Hollander beater, developed in the Netherlands in the seventeenth century, replaced banks of stamping hammers with a roll carrying metal bars that swept the stuff past a fixed bedplate, cutting and bruising the fibres far faster. Then, at the very end of the eighteenth century, a French clerk named Nicolas-Louis Robert built a machine in which an endless belt of woven wire ran continuously through the vat, carrying off an unbroken ribbon of wet fibre instead of discrete sheets. The design was developed and financed in England by the stationers Henry and Sealy Fourdrinier and engineered into something reliable by Bryan Donkin. The Fourdriniers went bankrupt; the machine still carries their name.

Wood arrived in the 1840s, when grinding logs endwise against a wet revolving stone produced a cheap pulp suitable for newsprint. Mechanical pulp keeps nearly everything the tree contained, including lignin, the stiff aromatic polymer that cements fibres together in living wood — which is why newsprint yellows and grows brittle in sunlight. Chemical pulping, cooking wood chips under pressure to dissolve the lignin away, gives up perhaps half the mass of the log but yields long, clean, strong fibres. The sulfate or kraft process, worked out in Germany in the 1880s, cooks chips in sodium hydroxide and sodium sulfide; its great advantage is that the spent cooking liquor can be evaporated and burned in a recovery boiler, regenerating the chemicals and raising steam for the mill in the same operation. Kraft, named for the German and Swedish word for strength, is why a grocery sack can hold what it holds.

## Inside the Machine

A modern paper machine is essentially the hand process stretched out along a few hundred metres of floor. Pulp is diluted to a slurry that is overwhelmingly water, then passed through refiners, which are the Hollander's descendants: rotating discs with raised bars that fray the fibre walls into fine whiskers, a process called fibrillation, which multiplies the bonding surface and makes the finished paper denser, stronger, and less absorbent. Refine too little and the sheet is bulky and weak. Refine too much and it becomes hard, translucent, and slow to drain — which is exactly what is wanted for greaseproof paper and a disaster for blotting paper, which is barely refined and not sized at all.

The stock leaves a pressurized headbox through a long adjustable slot and lands on a moving plastic forming fabric. The relationship between the speed of that jet and the speed of the fabric is what sets grain direction: run the fabric faster than the jet and the fibres are drawn out along the machine. Water drains down through the fabric, helped by foils and suction boxes, and the web leaves the forming section holding perhaps four parts water to one part fibre. The press section squeezes it between felts and hard rolls to roughly half water, which is about the limit of what pressure can do. Everything after that is heat: two tiers of steam-heated cast iron cylinders, sometimes more than fifty of them, with the sheet threaded over and under in a serpentine, drying it to a few percent moisture. A size press applies surface starch, a calender stack of polished rolls irons the surface smooth, and the finished web is wound onto a reel at a speed that, on a fast tissue or newsprint machine, would be a respectable pace on a motorway.

## What Else Is in the Sheet

Paper is not only fibre. Mineral fillers — kaolin clay, or calcium carbonate precipitated on site — are cheap, brighten the sheet, and improve opacity and printability, but they also lodge between fibres and interrupt bonding, so every point of filler costs strength. Internal sizing controls how water behaves in the finished sheet. For most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries this was done with rosin fixed by alum, which left the paper mildly acidic; over decades that acidity hydrolyzes the cellulose chains and the paper turns tan and snaps at a fold. Entire library collections printed in that era are now too fragile to shelve, while incunabula printed on rag paper four centuries earlier remain supple. Mills shifted to alkaline sizing chemistries and carbonate fillers in the later twentieth century, and books printed since then should outlive their readers by a comfortable margin.

Recycled fibre complicates the picture in a different way. Every trip through a pulper, a screen, and a set of refiners shortens fibres and reduces their ability to bond, and printed stock has to be de-inked, which removes fines along with the pigment. A cellulose fibre can make the round trip perhaps five to seven times before it is too short to hold on to its neighbours, at which point it washes out as sludge. The system therefore cannot close: somewhere upstream of every recycled carton there has to be a tree, and the fibre in a fresh sheet of paper is always on its way toward becoming too short to matter.
