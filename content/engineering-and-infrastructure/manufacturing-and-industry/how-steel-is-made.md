# How Steel Is Made

Drop a cast iron skillet on a concrete floor and there is a fair chance it breaks into pieces. Drop a steel frying pan and it dents. Bend a paperclip back and forth and it eventually fatigues and snaps, but it bends a long way first. All three objects are essentially the same element, and the difference between them comes down to how much carbon is dissolved in the iron and what form that carbon has taken — a range that runs, for everything of practical interest, from almost nothing up to about four percent by weight. Almost no other material is so sensitive to so small an ingredient.

Pure iron is soft and unremarkable. Its atoms sit in a regular lattice, and deformation happens when line defects called dislocations slide through that lattice, which in clean iron they do very easily. Carbon atoms are small enough to squeeze into the gaps between iron atoms rather than replacing them, and they distort the lattice locally, which makes dislocation movement harder. Add a little and the metal gets stronger while remaining ductile: that is steel. Add a lot and the excess carbon comes out of solution as hard, brittle iron carbide or as flakes of graphite, and the metal becomes rigid, excellent in compression, good at damping vibration, and prone to cracking under a sharp blow: that is cast iron. Take nearly all of it out and you have wrought iron, tough and fibrous and easy to forge, the structural metal of the early industrial age.

## Getting Iron Out of Rock

Iron in the ground is an oxide, usually hematite or magnetite, and making metal from it means persuading the oxygen to leave. The modern blast furnace does this with carbon monoxide in a tall countercurrent shaft. Iron ore, coke, and limestone are charged in at the top; preheated air, often enriched with oxygen, is blown in near the bottom through nozzles called tuyeres. The coke burns to carbon monoxide, which rises through the descending charge and strips oxygen from the ore at progressively higher temperatures, until near the hearth the iron is molten and trickling down through a bed of glowing coke.

Coke does two jobs, and the second one is why coal itself will not serve. Raw coal softens and collapses under the weight of the charge, choking the furnace; coke, made by baking coal in the absence of air to drive off its volatiles, is a hard porous skeleton strong enough to hold the burden up and open so that gas can pass through it. Abraham Darby's success in smelting with coke at Coalbrookdale early in the eighteenth century mattered because it uncoupled iron production from the supply of charcoal, and therefore from the rate at which forests grow.

The limestone is there to take care of the rest of the rock. It decomposes to lime, which combines with silica and alumina into a molten slag that floats on the iron and can be tapped off separately. What comes out of the tap hole is pig iron, saturated with something like four percent carbon, along with silicon, manganese, sulfur, and phosphorus picked up from ore and coke. It is brittle, unforgeable, and only halfway to being useful. A blast furnace, once lit, runs continuously for years — the lining slowly wears, and relining it is a major campaign — because cooling and restarting one is far worse than keeping it fed.

## Taking the Carbon Back Out

For most of history, converting pig iron into something workable was the hardest, slowest step in the whole chain. Puddling, perfected in the 1780s, had a workman stirring molten pig iron in a reverberatory furnace with a long bar while the carbon burned out and the metal, its melting point rising as it purified, gathered into a pasty ball that had to be dragged out and hammered. It was brutal work and it produced wrought iron in lumps. Steel proper — the intermediate carbon range — was rarer still, made in Sheffield from the 1740s by sealing blister iron in clay crucibles and melting it, a batch at a time, in pots weighing a few tens of kilograms.

Henry Bessemer's converter, announced in the mid-1850s, changed the scale of the problem by a factor of hundreds. His insight was that pig iron contains its own fuel: blow air through several tonnes of the molten metal and the silicon and carbon oxidize so fiercely that the charge heats up rather than freezing, and in about twenty minutes the carbon is gone. The early difficulty was overshooting — the process took everything out, leaving a metal full of dissolved oxygen that cracked when forged. Robert Mushet's solution was to blow it clean and then add back a measured quantity of spiegeleisen, a manganese-rich iron that scavenged the oxygen and restored carbon to the level wanted.

The second difficulty was phosphorus, which makes steel brittle when cold and which the acid silica lining of Bessemer's converter could not remove. Since most European ore is phosphoric, this confined the process to a narrow range of raw materials until Sidney Gilchrist Thomas and his cousin Percy Gilchrist worked out in the late 1870s that a basic lining of burnt dolomite, with lime added to the charge, would hold phosphorus in the slag. Whole orefields in Lorraine and elsewhere became valuable overnight, and the phosphate-rich slag was ground up and sold as fertilizer.

Meanwhile the Siemens-Martin open hearth took the opposite approach: a shallow bath heated by a flame above it, with regenerative brick chambers recovering heat from the exhaust to preheat the incoming gas and air. It took hours instead of minutes, which sounds like a defect and was in fact the main attraction — a slow heat can be sampled, adjusted, and hit precisely, and it can digest large quantities of cold scrap. Open hearth steel dominated the first half of the twentieth century.

Both were displaced by the basic oxygen process, developed in Austria in the early 1950s once tonnage oxygen became cheap. A water-cooled lance descends into a vessel of molten pig iron and blows pure oxygen onto the surface at supersonic velocity. Without the nitrogen ballast of air, the reaction is faster and cleaner and the heat surplus is large enough to melt a useful fraction of scrap alongside the hot metal. A few hundred tonnes are refined in well under an hour.

## Arc Furnaces, Ladles, and the Continuous Strand

The other half of modern steelmaking skips the blast furnace entirely. An electric arc furnace is a refractory-lined tub with graphite electrodes lowered through the roof; the arc struck between them and the charge melts cold scrap directly. Arc furnaces made the minimill possible — a compact plant sited near a scrap supply and a market rather than near coal and ore — and they now account for the majority of steel produced in the United States. Their limitation is chemical: copper and tin from motors, wiring, and mixed scrap cannot be oxidized out of the bath, and they accumulate as tramp elements that cause cracking during hot rolling. The usual remedy is dilution with virgin iron units, either pig iron or direct reduced iron made by stripping oxygen from ore with natural gas without ever melting it.

Whatever the furnace, the steel it produces is only roughly right. The real chemistry happens afterward in the ladle: stirring with argon, adding alloys and deoxidizers, holding the metal under vacuum to pull hydrogen and nitrogen out of solution, and reheating it electrically so that composition and temperature can both be hit within tight bounds.

The finished heat used to be poured into ingot moulds, stripped, reheated in soaking pits, and rolled down through a chain of mills, with the top of each ingot cropped off and recycled because that is where the shrinkage cavity and the segregated impurities collected. Continuous casting removed most of that waste. Steel is poured through a water-cooled copper mould that solidifies a skin, and the strand is withdrawn continuously, bent through a curved path of rollers while its interior is still liquid, straightened, and cut to length with a travelling torch. Slabs, blooms, and billets emerge without anyone ever making an ingot.

## Making It Hard, Making It Soft

Composition is only half of what determines how steel behaves; the thermal history is the other half. Above roughly seven hundred and thirty degrees, iron rearranges into a face-centred lattice called austenite, which dissolves far more carbon than the room-temperature form. Cool it slowly and the carbon has time to precipitate out as carbide in fine layers, leaving a soft, machinable structure. Cool it violently — quench it into water or oil — and the lattice transforms without letting the carbon go anywhere. The trapped carbon strains the structure into a distorted, needle-like form called martensite, which is extremely hard and, on its own, uselessly brittle. Reheating it gently afterward, tempering, lets a little carbon precipitate and trades some hardness for toughness. Essentially every knife, spring, gear, and bearing has been through that quench-and-temper cycle, and the whole art of heat treatment is choosing where on the trade-off to stop.

Other elements shift the terms. Chromium above about a tenth of the alloy forms a self-repairing oxide film a few atoms thick that keeps oxygen from reaching the iron beneath — stainless steel, worked out in several laboratories around the same years before the First World War. Nickel stabilizes austenite so that it survives at room temperature, giving the non-magnetic stainless of kitchen sinks. Molybdenum and vanadium form fine carbides that hold hardness at red heat, which is what lets a cutting tool cut. Small additions of niobium and titanium, in the tens of thousandths, pin grain boundaries and produce the high-strength low-alloy steels used in pipelines and car bodies.

What has not changed since the blast furnace is the underlying reaction. Carbon takes the oxygen off the iron, and the carbon leaves as carbon dioxide — roughly two tonnes of it for every tonne of steel made by the ore-and-coke route, which is why steelmaking accounts for a striking share of global emissions for an industry most people never see. The alternatives on the table all involve finding another reductant: hydrogen, in plants already operating at demonstration scale in northern Sweden, or direct electrolysis of iron oxide, which would make steelmaking a matter of electricity rather than combustion. Both would end a relationship between iron and carbon that began the first time someone put ore in a charcoal fire.
