# The Logic of Statistical Process Control

An operator on a grinding machine measures five parts every half hour, averages them, and marks a dot on a chart taped to the machine's side panel. The dot lands well inside the two lines drawn across the paper, so by the rules he has been given he does nothing. But it is the eighth dot in a row above the middle line, and that pattern means something even though no single point looks unusual. He shuts the machine down and calls the setter, who finds that the coolant temperature has drifted up through the morning and the wheel is loading. Nothing had gone wrong yet. Something was about to.

That is the entire discipline in one scene: a method for telling the difference between the ordinary jitter of a process and a signal that something has actually changed, so that people intervene when intervention helps and — much harder — leave things alone when it does not.

## Two Kinds of Variation

The founding insight belongs to Walter Shewhart, a physicist working at Bell Telephone Laboratories in the 1920s, whose problem was the reliability of telephone equipment buried in the ground where repairs were expensive. Manufactured things vary. Shewhart's contribution was to argue that the variation comes in two fundamentally different kinds, which call for opposite responses.

Some variation is inherent in the process as it is currently constituted: a thousand small influences — slight differences in material hardness, vibration from the building, the operator's grip, humidity, the resolution of the gauge — none individually identifiable, none individually worth chasing, combining into a stable pattern of scatter around a mean. Shewhart called these chance causes; the usual term now is common cause. A process that displays only this kind of variation is predictable, not in the sense that you know what the next part will measure, but in the sense that you know the range within which it will fall and roughly how often.

The other kind is a real, findable disturbance: a tool that has chipped, a new lot of material with different properties, a fixture that has worked loose, an operator who was never shown the setup procedure. This is assignable cause, or special cause, variation, and it produces changes that stand out from the background pattern — a sudden shift, a trend, a burst of scatter wider than anything the process normally produces.

Confusing them is enormously costly, and it is costly in both directions. Treating a common-cause fluctuation as if it were a special cause leads to tampering: adjusting a machine because the last part came out a little high, which offsets the process in the other direction just as the natural variation swings back, so that the corrections and the noise add up instead of canceling. W. Edwards Deming used to demonstrate this with a funnel, a marble, and a target, showing that a perfectly reasonable-sounding adjustment rule produces a scatter pattern noticeably worse than leaving the funnel bolted in place. Meanwhile, treating a genuine special cause as ordinary noise means a drifting process runs on until it makes scrap.

## The Chart

The control chart is the instrument for making that distinction operationally, and its design is cleverer than it looks. Samples are taken in small subgroups — typically four or five consecutive parts — because parts made back to back are exposed to nearly the same conditions. The variation within a subgroup is therefore a clean estimate of the process's inherent noise, uncontaminated by whatever drifts between subgroups.

The chart then plots two things over time: the average of each subgroup, and the range within each subgroup. Control limits are calculated from the within-subgroup variation and placed at roughly three standard deviations on either side of the process's own centerline. A point outside the limits is treated as evidence that the process has changed.

The point that trips people up, and it trips up whole companies, is that control limits are not specification limits. Specifications come from the designer and express what the customer needs. Control limits come from the process and express what it actually does. They have nothing to do with one another, and plotting the spec limits on a control chart — a very common mistake — destroys the chart's purpose, because it invites the operator to adjust whenever a part drifts toward the tolerance boundary, which is exactly the tampering the chart exists to prevent.

The choice of three standard deviations is not a statement about probability so much as an economic compromise, and Shewhart said as much. Wider limits mean fewer false alarms but slower detection of real shifts. Narrower limits mean the reverse. Three sigma sits at a point where false alarms are rare enough that operators keep trusting the chart, which matters more than any theoretical optimum, because a chart that cries wolf gets ignored and then covered with a rag.

Supplementary rules extend the chart's sensitivity to patterns that single points would miss: a run of eight or more consecutive points on one side of the centerline, two of three points beyond two sigma, a steady trend of several points climbing or falling, an unnaturally tight hugging of the centerline that usually means someone is editing the data. These rules detect small persistent shifts that would take a long time to throw a single point past three sigma.

## Control Is Not Capability

A process can be in perfect statistical control and still produce garbage. Control means predictable; it says nothing about whether the predictable output fits the drawing. Conversely, a wildly unstable process can happen to fall inside a generous tolerance for a while.

The two questions therefore get separated. Stability is judged by the control chart. Capability is judged by comparing the process's natural spread against the width of the specification, which is what the capability indices express — one comparing the tolerance band to the process spread, another penalizing a process that is stable but centered off-target. A capable process has its natural variation comfortably inside the tolerance with room on both sides, so that ordinary drift does not immediately create defects.

The sequence matters. Calculating capability for an unstable process is meaningless, because there is no single process to characterize; you are averaging over several different processes that happened to run in sequence. Stabilize first, then measure capability, then improve. Improvement of a stable process is a different activity entirely from firefighting: it means changing the system — a stiffer fixture, a better material specification, a redesigned tool path — because by definition there is no single culprit to find.

## Why This Became a Management Argument

Shewhart's charts spread through American war production in the 1940s and then largely receded, surviving in pockets. Their second life came through Japan, where Deming and Joseph Juran lectured to engineers and executives in the years after the war and found an industry with no established practices to defend. Statistical methods were taken up not as a quality-department specialty but as something line supervisors and operators did, and the habit of charting became part of how Japanese manufacturers ran plants. The results reached American showrooms in the 1970s in the form of cars that did not rattle, and by the 1980s the same ideas were being reimported under new names, eventually including the Six Sigma program that Motorola built around them.

What had been imported, though, was not only a charting technique. Deming in particular drew a management conclusion from the two kinds of variation that many executives found uncomfortable. If most of the variation in output is common cause — built into the system of equipment, materials, training, and procedures that management owns — then exhorting workers to be more careful is not merely useless, it is a way of blaming people for results they do not control. He made the point with a demonstration in which volunteers drew beads from a container with a paddle, some proportion of the beads being red and therefore defective. Workers were praised, warned, ranked, and fired on the basis of their red-bead counts, which were entirely determined by the mixture in the container. The exercise was funny for about a minute and then uncomfortable, because everybody in the room had seen a performance review run on the same logic.

That is the connection between a chart on a machine and a theory of management. The chart is a device for asking, in a disciplined way, whether the thing you just saw is worth reacting to. Most of the time it is not, and the value of the chart lies in the permission it gives an organization to do nothing without anyone being accused of complacency. The rest of the time, when a point falls outside the limits or a run develops, it gives the opposite permission: to stop, right now, and go find out why.

Shops that keep charts and never act on them end up with decorated walls. Shops that act on every fluctuation end up chasing their own corrections. The narrow path between those two failures is the whole of it, and it is much more about the discipline of not intervening than anyone expects when they first see the arithmetic.
