# Casting and Forging: Two Ways to Shape Metal

Lift a cast iron manhole cover, if you ever get the chance, and look closely at its underside. It is rough, slightly grainy, with a texture like fine sandpaper baked into the metal, and if you look along its edge you may find a faint ridge running around the circumference — a seam where two halves of a mold met. Now picture a forged wrench, the kind sold at any hardware store, and imagine cutting it in half and polishing the cross-section. Under a microscope, the metal's internal grain would not run in random directions. It would follow the outline of the wrench itself, curving around the head and streaming down the handle, because the metal was never melted at all. It was squeezed into shape while still solid, and its structure was carried along for the ride.

These two objects represent the two oldest strategies humans have for turning raw metal into a useful shape without cutting anything away. Casting melts the metal and pours it, in liquid form, into a cavity shaped like the part. Forging leaves the metal solid, heats it only enough to make it plastic and workable, and deforms it under force — a hammer blow, a press, a die closing — until it holds the desired form. Nearly every metal object with a complex or load-bearing shape started life as one or the other, or occasionally both in sequence.

## Sand Casting, the Oldest Trick

Sand casting has barely changed in principle for thousands of years, which is a testament to how well the basic idea works. A pattern — a wood, plastic, or metal replica of the part, slightly oversized to account for the metal's shrinkage as it cools — is pressed into a fine, damp, slightly clay-bonded sand packed into a two-part frame called a flask. The pattern is withdrawn, leaving a cavity in its exact negative shape, and the two halves of the flask are closed back together. Molten metal, often iron, aluminum, or bronze, is poured through a channel into that cavity, fills it completely, and is left to solidify. Once it has cooled enough to handle, the sand is broken away and discarded or reconditioned for reuse, a process evocatively called shakeout, and the rough casting is cut free from the channels that fed it.

Sand molds are cheap and endlessly flexible in what shapes they can produce, since a new pattern is far less expensive than a new die of hardened steel, but they are also single-use — each mold is destroyed to free the part inside it — and the resulting surface finish is coarse, textured by the sand grains it solidified against. Internal cavities and passages, like the water jacket inside an engine block, are formed with cores: separate sand shapes, often baked hard with a binder, that are placed inside the mold cavity before it closes and dissolve or get shaken out afterward, leaving a hollow behind.

## Investment Casting and the Lost Wax Process

For finer detail and a smoother finish, foundries turn to investment casting, better known by its older name, lost-wax casting, a technique documented in various forms for thousands of years and still used today for everything from jewelry to jet engine turbine blades. A wax replica of the part is produced, often by injecting wax into an aluminum mold, and that wax pattern is dipped repeatedly into a ceramic slurry and coated with fine sand until a hard ceramic shell builds up around it. The whole assembly is then heated. The wax melts and drains away — the "lost wax" that gives the process its name — leaving a hollow ceramic mold with the part's exact shape, including fine surface detail the wax was able to capture that sand simply cannot.

Molten metal is poured into this ceramic shell, which is then broken away once the metal solidifies, sacrificing the mold just as sand casting does but capturing far more precision along the way. Because complex internal geometry can be built directly into the wax pattern, investment casting can produce shapes — the intricate internal cooling channels of a turbine blade, for instance — that would be difficult or impossible to machine from solid stock afterward.

## Die Casting for Volume

Where sand and investment casting sacrifice a mold with every part, die casting flips the economics entirely by using a mold, or die, made of hardened steel that can be reused for tens or hundreds of thousands of cycles. Molten metal — typically aluminum, zinc, or magnesium, chosen partly because their melting points are low enough not to destroy a steel die on contact — is forced into the die cavity under high pressure, filling even thin, complex sections in a fraction of a second before it solidifies. The die then opens, the part is ejected, and the cycle repeats, often several times a minute, on a fully automated machine that looks and behaves a good deal like an injection molding machine, because the underlying logic is nearly identical.

This is the process behind the metal housings of power tools, the structural brackets inside cars, and countless small components where geometry needs to be intricate, walls need to be thin, and volumes need to be enormous. What die casting cannot easily do is match the strength of a forged part in the same alloy, because the rapid solidification and turbulent filling of the cavity tend to trap microscopic gas bubbles and porosity within the casting, weaknesses a forging simply does not have.

## What Forging Does Differently

Forging takes an entirely different approach to the same basic problem. Rather than pouring liquid metal into a shape, forging starts with a solid piece — a billet or ingot, heated in a furnace until it glows and becomes plastic enough to deform without cracking — and reshapes it through compressive force. A blacksmith's hammer striking hot iron on an anvil is forging in its most ancient and literal form, and the underlying physics has not changed even though the hammers involved now weigh many tons and are dropped or driven by hydraulic and mechanical power rather than a human arm.

The metallurgical payoff is what makes forging worth the extra effort and cost. Metal has an internal grain structure, visible under magnification as elongated crystals that form when the metal originally solidified from its own molten state, back when it was first cast into a billet at the mill. Machining a part from a bar of that stock cuts straight through the grain wherever the part's contours diverge from the original bar shape, exposing grain ends at the surface the way cutting across a plank of wood exposes end grain. Forging, by contrast, pushes the existing grain structure to flow along with the part's new shape, so the grain lines curve around a corner rather than getting severed at one. The result, other factors held equal, is a part markedly more resistant to fatigue cracking and better able to handle the same stress a machined or cast part of identical geometry would fail under sooner. This is why crankshafts, connecting rods, wrenches, and other components that face repeated, jarring loads are so often forged rather than cast or cut from bar stock.

## Open-Die and Closed-Die Forging

Forging itself splits into a few distinct approaches. Open-die forging is the closest descendant of the blacksmith's anvil: a hammer or press squeezes the workpiece between two flat or simply shaped dies that never fully enclose it, and a skilled operator repositions and rotates the piece between blows to gradually work it toward the desired shape. This method suits very large, relatively simple parts — ship propeller shafts, forged rings for wind turbine bearings — where a fully enclosing die would be impractically large and expensive to make for what might be a production run of a handful of pieces.

Closed-die, or impression-die, forging instead uses two die halves, each cut with a mirror-image impression of part of the final shape, so that when they close on a heated billet the metal is forced to flow and fill the entire cavity between them, much as a sand or die casting fills its mold, except the metal never melts. Excess metal squeezed out at the parting line, called flash, is trimmed away afterward. This is the method behind mass-produced forgings like automotive connecting rods and hand tool heads, where the same die shapes thousands or millions of identical, high-strength parts.

## Choosing Between Them

The decision between casting and forging, where either is mechanically plausible, usually comes down to a trade among strength, geometric complexity, and cost. Casting wins when a part's geometry is intricate — full of internal passages, thin walls, or shapes that would be difficult to compress into with a die — or when the production volume does not justify a forging die's cost. Forging wins when the part will face serious cyclic stress in service and the extra fatigue resistance from continuous grain flow is worth paying for.

Many finished products use both in different components of the same assembly, and some use both in sequence on the same part: a rough shape forged for strength, then finish-machined on a lathe or mill to bring critical surfaces to their final dimension. Neither process has displaced the other in the centuries since both were understood in their modern industrial form, because neither one is actually trying to solve the same problem as the other, whatever their outward similarity as ways of pushing metal into shape.
