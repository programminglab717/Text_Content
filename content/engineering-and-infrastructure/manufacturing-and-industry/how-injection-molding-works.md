# How Injection Molding Works

Pick up almost any small plastic object within arm's reach — a phone case, a bottle cap, a toy figurine, the housing around a light switch — and turn it over. Somewhere on it, usually tucked into a corner or hidden on the underside, is a small circular blemish, or a faint hairline where two fronts of plastic met and fused. That mark is a signature. It tells you the object was injection molded: shot into shape in a few seconds inside a block of hardened steel, cooled just long enough to hold its form, and ejected to make room for the next one.

Injection molding is a large part of why so much of the manufactured world is plastic, and why so much of it is so cheap. It is not a fast process to set up. A production mold can take months to design and machine and can cost more than a house. But once it is running, it can turn out a finished part every few seconds, identical to the last one down to fractions of a millimeter, for years without complaint. Understanding how it works means understanding a trade: enormous upfront investment in tooling in exchange for extraordinarily low cost per part at volume.

## From Pellets to Melt

The raw material arrives at the molding machine as small pellets, usually about the size of a grain of rice, of a thermoplastic resin — polyethylene, polypropylene, ABS, nylon, polycarbonate, and hundreds of other formulations, each suited to different mechanical, thermal, and chemical demands. A phone case wants toughness and a bit of flex. A hot-drink lid wants heat resistance. A gear wants low friction and dimensional stability. The choice of resin happens long before anyone thinks about the mold, and it constrains everything that follows.

These pellets drop from a hopper into a long steel barrel housing a rotating screw, a mechanism related in principle to a meat grinder or a pasta extruder. As the screw turns, it drags the pellets forward while heating bands wrapped around the barrel bring the plastic up past its melting point. The screw's flights are cut with a channel that grows shallower toward the front, so the plastic is compressed as it travels, which drives out trapped air and works the melt into a uniform temperature and consistency. By the time the material reaches the front of the barrel it has gone from solid grains to a thick, viscous fluid, something like honey that has been left in a cold cupboard. The continued turning of the screw pushes this melt forward into a reservoir just behind the nozzle. At that point the screw stops rotating and instead behaves like a plunger, ready to drive the melt forward in a single stroke.

## The Shot

This is the injection in injection molding. The mold itself — two halves of hardened steel, machined with a hollow cavity in the exact negative shape of the part, mounted in a clamping unit that holds the halves together under enormous force — sits closed and waiting. The screw rams forward, forcing molten plastic through the nozzle, through a central channel called the sprue, out along smaller feeder channels called runners, and through a narrow constriction called a gate into the cavity proper. The whole cavity fills in a matter of seconds, sometimes a fraction of a second for small, thin-walled parts, because the plastic has to spread and reach every extremity of the mold before it cools enough to stop flowing.

Filling too slowly risks the plastic solidifying before it reaches the far corners of the cavity, leaving a part with a visible seam where two flow fronts met and failed to fuse properly — a weld line, which is both a cosmetic blemish and a structural weak point. Filling too fast can trap air pockets, scorch the resin as it shears against the mold surface, or blast a thin fin of plastic, called flash, out through any microscopic gap along the mold's parting line. Molders spend real effort tuning the injection speed and pressure profile for each part and material combination, often filling fast at first and slowing as the melt front nears the last corners of the cavity.

## Packing, Cooling, and Ejection

Once the cavity is full, the machine holds pressure for a short additional interval known as the pack and hold phase. Plastic shrinks measurably as it cools from a melt to a solid, and without continued pressure feeding a little more material into the cavity to compensate, the part would pull away from the mold walls unevenly and develop sink marks: shallow dimples, often over internal ribs or bosses, where the outer skin had already solidified while the thicker interior kept contracting underneath it.

Cooling is usually the longest single phase of the cycle, and it happens out of sight, through channels drilled through the steel of the mold that circulate water or oil at a carefully controlled temperature. The part has to cool enough to hold its shape rigidly before the mold opens, but the goal is never to cool it all the way to room temperature, since that would take far too long to be economical. Molders aim for the shortest cooling time that still lets the part survive ejection without warping or sticking to the tool. Uneven cooling — caused by thick and thin sections losing heat at different rates, or by cooling lines routed too close to one wall of the cavity and too far from another — is the single biggest cause of warped parts, which is why serious mold designers route their cooling channels to trace the contours of the part rather than drilling simple straight lines through the block.

When cooling finishes, the clamping unit opens the mold, and ejector pins — thin steel rods built into the moving half of the tool — push the part free of the core. In a well-built production mold this happens automatically and continuously, the finished piece dropping onto a conveyor or into a bin below while the screw has already begun plasticizing the next shot of material behind it.

## Designing the Mold

The mold is where most of the intellectual and financial investment in injection molding actually lives. A simple prototype mold might have a single cavity and open along one flat parting line. A production mold for a high-volume consumer item might have dozens of identical cavities firing at once, sliding side cores that retract to release features an ordinary mold could never eject, and a hot runner system that keeps the plastic in the feed channels molten between shots so that none of it solidifies into scrap that has to be reground and remelted.

Anyone designing the part itself has to think in terms the mold can actually deliver. Walls should stay a fairly uniform thickness throughout the part, because a thick section cools more slowly and shrinks more than its thinner neighbors, and that mismatch is exactly what produces warp and sink. Vertical walls need a slight draft angle — a degree or two off true vertical — so the part releases cleanly from the polished steel instead of scraping and galling as it is pushed out. Sharp internal corners are avoided in favor of small radiused fillets, both because stress concentrates dangerously at a sharp corner in a loaded plastic part and because a sharp inside corner is difficult to cut into steel and the first thing to wear out or crack under repeated thermal cycling. None of these are absolute laws, but ignoring them tends to announce itself quickly, in a mold that ejects defective parts or a tool that fails a fraction of the way through its expected production run of a million or more shots.

## Materials, Multi-Shot Molds, and the Limits of the Process

Not every job is a simple single-material shot. Overmolding lays a second material — often a soft rubber-like thermoplastic elastomer — over a rigid substrate already molded in a first shot, producing the soft-touch grips found on tool handles and toothbrushes. Insert molding places a pre-made component, commonly a metal fastener or electrical contact, into the mold cavity before the shot, so the plastic forms directly around it and the two become a single part without a separate assembly step. Thermosetting plastics, which cure through an irreversible chemical reaction rather than simply cooling, can also be injection molded, though the process runs in something close to reverse: the barrel keeps the material cool enough to stay uncured, and the heated mold is what triggers the reaction once the material arrives inside it.

The process has real limits. Wall sections that are too thick take a long time to cool and are prone to internal voids. Very tall, thin ribs can be difficult to fill completely. And the geometry has to be release-able from a mold that, in its simplest form, only opens in one direction, which is why complex internal passages usually call for a different manufacturing process entirely, or for cores that are melted or dissolved out of the finished part after molding.

## Why the Economics Work the Way They Do

A mold is, in effect, an extremely expensive and extremely precise stamp, and its cost is almost entirely independent of how many parts get made from it. This is why injection molding rewards volume so dramatically. Spread across ten thousand parts, a mold costing tens of thousands of dollars adds a noticeable sum to the price of each one. Spread across ten million parts, that same tooling cost becomes close to a rounding error, and the price of the part collapses to little more than the cost of the resin and a few seconds of machine time.

This is also why injection molding is a poor choice for a single prototype or a run of a few dozen parts — nobody commissions a steel mold to make five copies of something — and why the rise of 3D printing has not so much replaced injection molding as taken over the jobs it was always badly suited for. The two processes have settled into complementary niches: additive manufacturing for the one part needed tomorrow, injection molding for the ten million needed at a fraction of a cent of tooling cost apiece. Somewhere in an industrial park right now, a molding machine is running the same cycle it has run for years, day and night: melt, inject, pack, cool, eject, and begin again.
