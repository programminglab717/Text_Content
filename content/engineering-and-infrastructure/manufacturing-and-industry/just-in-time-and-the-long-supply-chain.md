# Just-in-Time and the Long Supply Chain

At a well-run car plant, the seats do not sit in a warehouse. A truck backs into a dock a few hundred meters from the line, carrying perhaps two hours' worth of seats, and they are stacked in the order the cars will need them — grey cloth, grey cloth, black leather, grey cloth — matching the sequence of bodies that are already painted and moving toward the trim area. The supplier built them that morning against a signal sent from the plant the day before. If that truck is late, the line stops within the hour, and there is no pile of spare seats anywhere to save it.

Stated that way, the arrangement sounds reckless. It was adopted deliberately, and the reasoning behind it is one of the more interesting inversions in industrial history: inventory was recast from an asset that protects you into a substance that conceals your problems.

## The Toyota Argument

Taiichi Ohno spent the postwar decades building a production system at Toyota under conditions that made American mass production impossible to copy. The domestic market was small and fragmented across many models, capital was scarce, and land in Japan was expensive enough that stockpiling parts carried a visible cost. Ohno's response was to attack batch size.

Conventional manufacturing logic says that changeovers are expensive, so you should run long batches to spread the setup cost over many parts. Ohno accepted the arithmetic and attacked the premise. If a press die change took most of a day, of course you ran thousands of panels between changes. So Toyota worked on the changeover itself — staging dies in advance, converting bolted adjustments into locating pins and clamps, doing everything possible while the press was still running — and drove die changes down from hours to single-digit minutes. Shigeo Shingo later codified this as a general method. Once a changeover is cheap, small batches become economical, and the entire justification for large in-process inventories evaporates.

Then came the signaling. Rather than a central schedule pushing parts forward according to a forecast, each station drew what it needed from the one upstream, which triggered replacement. Ohno said the idea came from American supermarkets, where the shelf is restocked according to what customers actually took rather than what a planner predicted they would take. The physical token of that signal was the kanban: a card traveling with a container that, when the container emptied, went back upstream as an authorization to make exactly one more container's worth. Nothing gets made without a card, which means work in process is capped by the number of cards in circulation, and the cap can be tightened deliberately.

That tightening is the part most often missed. Removing inventory from a system is not only a cost saving; it is a diagnostic. When there are four hours of buffer between two operations, a machine that breaks down for three hours causes no visible problem, and nobody fixes the machine. When the buffer is twenty minutes, the same breakdown stops the plant and becomes everyone's emergency. The often-quoted image is a rock-filled river: lower the water and you discover what you have been floating over. Just-in-time is a method for deliberately lowering the water, which only works in a plant that also has the capacity and the culture to deal with each rock as it surfaces — which is why the system arrived paired with the andon cord, the authority of any worker to halt the line, and a habit of chasing a stoppage back to its root cause rather than patching it.

## Why Everyone Else Copied It Badly

By the 1980s the results were impossible to ignore, and Western manufacturers went shopping for the technique. A great many of them bought the inventory reduction without the rest of it. The easiest way to show less inventory on your own balance sheet is to demand more frequent deliveries from suppliers and let them hold the stock, which converts the system's buffer into somebody else's working capital while doing nothing whatever about the underlying variability. Plants with unreliable equipment, long changeovers, and unstable schedules ran their stocks down anyway and discovered exactly what the theory predicted they would: constant shortages.

The version that worked required the harder companion changes — preventive maintenance so machines do not fail unpredictably, level scheduling so that the mix of models built each day stays roughly constant instead of lurching, quality good enough that a shipment can go straight to the line without inspection, and supplier relationships long-term enough that a supplier will invest in a dedicated line for you.

Level scheduling deserves a note, because it is the least intuitive piece. Making the same daily quantity in the same daily mix — rather than building all the wagons on Monday and all the sedans on Thursday — smooths demand not just at the plant but at every supplier behind it. Without it, a pull system faithfully transmits every lurch in the final schedule backward down the chain, amplified at each step.

## Amplification and the Long Chain

That amplification has a name. In a multi-stage chain where each participant reorders based on what the next stage downstream ordered, and each holds a little safety stock, a modest change in end demand grows into wild swings by the time it reaches raw materials. A ten percent bump at retail becomes a much larger order to the distributor, a larger one still to the factory, and a chemical plant several tiers back oscillating between full capacity and idle. Jay Forrester described the dynamic at MIT decades ago, and the classroom exercise built to demonstrate it reliably makes intelligent players produce enormous oscillations in a simulated beer supply chain while each of them behaves sensibly.

The bullwhip was tolerable when supply chains were short. They stopped being short. Containerization, which Malcom McLean set in motion in the mid-1950s by loading truck trailers onto a converted tanker, collapsed the cost of moving a box across an ocean to something close to trivial per unit. Ocean freight ceased to be a meaningful term in the sourcing decision, and the only remaining cost of distance was time. Standardized barcodes and electronic data interchange made it practical to coordinate with a supplier you would never meet. The result, over three decades, was a production geography in which a single consumer product routinely embodies parts from a dozen countries and subcontractors four or five tiers deep, most of them unknown by name to the company whose logo goes on the box.

Now combine that geography with thin buffers. Six weeks of transit between a component plant and an assembly plant is six weeks of pipeline that cannot be shortened in an emergency, and a decision to hold two days of stock at the receiving end does nothing about it. Meanwhile the drive for cost efficiency pushed toward single sourcing, because one supplier making all of a part gets the volume discount and the learning curve. Efficiency and fragility were being purchased with the same money.

## What the Disruptions Taught

The failures have been instructive precisely because they were rarely failures of the just-in-time concept as such. In the late 1990s a fire destroyed the plant of a Toyota supplier that made a small brake proportioning valve used in nearly every vehicle the company built. Toyota had days of stock. Production across the group halted almost immediately — and then recovered within about a week, because dozens of other firms in the supplier network, some of whom had never made such a valve, improvised tooling from drawings and started producing them. The vulnerability was single sourcing; the recovery came from a dense network of relationships that a purely transactional supply base would not have had.

The pattern recurred. An earthquake and tsunami in northeastern Japan in 2011 interrupted specialty materials that turned out to have one or two global sources. Floods in Thailand the same year took out a large share of the world's hard drive production. A container ship wedged sideways in the Suez Canal in 2021 stopped a significant fraction of Europe–Asia trade for a week. And the semiconductor shortage of the early 2020s left finished vehicles parked in fields waiting for chips that cost a few dollars each.

Notably, Toyota weathered the early part of that chip shortage better than most, having concluded after the 2011 earthquake that semiconductors were a category where long lead times justified holding months of stock. That is the actual lesson, and it is more precise than "just-in-time was a mistake." The right buffer is not zero and it is not uniform. It depends on lead time, on how many alternative sources exist, on whether the part can be substituted, and on what a shortage costs per hour. Holding a warehouse of seat frames is waste. Holding a year of a sole-sourced chip made in one fab on one island may be the cheapest insurance available.

What the last few decades really removed was slack of every kind — spare capacity, second suppliers, inventory, transit margin, even spare attention. Each removal looked locally rational and improved somebody's return on capital. The bill arrives all at once, in a quarter when everything is late, and it is paid by whoever happens to be holding the order book.
