# Industrial Robots on the Factory Floor

Stand outside the fence of an automotive body shop and the impression is of orchestrated violence. Six or eight orange arms swing around a partly assembled car body, each one carrying a welding gun the size of a bar stool, snapping from point to point with a sound like a heavy door slamming, throwing sparks in flat arcs against the mesh. Then a fault trips somewhere and the whole thing stops dead in whatever posture it was holding, arms frozen at odd angles, sparks still falling. Nothing about the scene suggests intelligence. It suggests a machine repeating a rehearsed sequence with perfect indifference to how well it is going — which is exactly what it is, and the source of both its usefulness and its limitations.

The first of these machines went to work in a New Jersey die casting plant at the beginning of the 1960s, unloading hot parts from a machine, a job that was dangerous and dull in equal measure. Six decades later the population runs into the millions, and the jobs are still recognizably in that family.

## Anatomy of an Arm

The standard industrial robot is an articulated arm with six rotary joints, and six is not arbitrary. Three joints — a waist rotating about the vertical, a shoulder, an elbow — are enough to put a point anywhere inside the working envelope. Three more, arranged as a wrist with intersecting axes, are needed to hold a tool at any orientation once it gets there. Fewer axes and some approach angles become impossible; a seventh axis adds redundancy, letting the arm reach the same point in different postures, which matters when reaching into a machine through a small door.

Each joint is an alternating-current servo motor coupled to a very high reduction gearbox, usually a strain wave gear or a cycloidal drive, both chosen because they produce large ratios in a compact housing with almost no backlash. Backlash is the enemy: a few arcminutes of play in a joint near the base multiplies into millimetres of slop at the end of a two-metre arm. Every joint carries a brake that engages when power is removed, which is why a robot dropped into an emergency stop stays where it was instead of collapsing. Every joint also carries an encoder reporting its angle back to a controller that is closing a position loop on all six axes simultaneously, thousands of times a second, so that the tool traces the path the program asked for rather than each joint independently racing to its own target.

Two numbers describe an arm on a datasheet, and both are routinely misread. Reach is measured to the wrist face, so a long tool extends the working envelope but does so at the cost of the second number, payload. Payload is not simply the weight the arm can lift; it is the weight it can lift at a stated distance from the wrist and a stated moment of inertia. A ten-kilogram gripper holding a two-kilogram part at arm's length can easily overload a robot rated for twenty, because what the wrist motors feel is torque, not mass.

## Repeatability Is Not Accuracy

An industrial arm will return to a taught position within a few hundredths of a millimetre, over and over, for years. That is repeatability, and it is the property the specification sheet advertises. Accuracy — going to a coordinate specified in space that it has never visited — is a different and much worse number, because it depends on the true lengths of the links, the deflection of the arm under its own load, and how warm the gearboxes have become since the shift started. A robot that has been running all night is not quite the same shape as it was when it was cold.

This is why the overwhelming majority of cells are still programmed by teaching. An operator takes a pendant, switches the robot into manual mode, and jogs it physically to each position, recording points as it goes. The controller does not need to know where anything really is in space; it only needs to go back to the same joint angles. Offline programming, where paths are generated from a model of the cell, is standard for large welding and painting jobs, but it requires a calibration step that measures the robot's real geometry and warps the ideal program onto it, and a touch-up pass by a human afterward is normal rather than exceptional.

Two geometric facts bite every new programmer. The first is the singularity: certain postures, most commonly when two wrist axes line up, leave the arm with no unique way to produce a requested motion, and the controller responds by trying to spin a joint at infinite speed and faulting out instead. Paths have to be planned around those regions. The second is that a given point in space can often be reached in several distinct joint configurations — elbow up or elbow down, wrist flipped or not — and a program that drifts from one configuration to another between two adjacent points will produce a sudden, startling whipping motion that has nothing to do with the short distance actually travelled.

## The Other Shapes

The six-axis arm is the general-purpose answer, not the only one. A SCARA has two parallel vertical-axis joints and a vertical slide, so it is stiff against downward force and fast in the horizontal plane; it dominates small parts assembly and board loading, where nearly everything is an insertion straight down. Delta robots hang three light linked arms from an overhead frame, keeping all the motors fixed on the frame so that the moving mass is tiny, and they pick items off a moving conveyor at rates the eye cannot follow. Cartesian gantries give up dexterity entirely for long travel and high load, which is what a machine spanning a whole bay of stock needs. Four-axis palletizers keep their wrists locked level because a box stacked on a pallet is always horizontal, and dropping the two unnecessary axes buys payload.

Most real installations add axes outside the arm. Positioners rotate a weldment to keep the joint being welded in the flat position; rails carry an arm along a row of machines. These are usually run as coordinated motion, with the controller solving for the robot and the external axes as one system, so that the tool traces a constant path relative to a part that is itself turning.

## Hands and the Presentation Problem

The arm is the commodity. What decides whether a cell works is the end-of-arm tooling and the way parts arrive. Grippers are pneumatic two-finger jaws, vacuum cups on foam pads, electromagnets, expanding bladders, or purpose-machined nests that cradle one specific casting and nothing else. A tool changer on the wrist lets one robot swap between several, at the cost of a little rigidity and a lot of compressed air fittings that eventually leak.

The harder problem is upstream. A taught path goes to a fixed location, so the part has to be in that location. Everything that makes a cell expensive — bowl feeders, escapements, precision fixtures, conveyor stops — exists to convert a bin of parts into a part that is always in the same place and the same orientation. Machine vision shifts some of that burden: a camera locates the part, computes an offset, and the controller shifts the taught frame by that offset before running the path. It works well on flat, well-lit, well-contrasted parts, and it requires a careful calibration tying the camera's coordinate frame to the robot's. It is not free, and a mechanical fixture that guarantees position is often still cheaper than a vision system that measures it.

For assembly where things must fit rather than merely be placed, a force-torque sensor at the wrist changes the strategy completely. Instead of driving to a coordinate, the robot pushes until it feels a specified force, then searches — a small spiral or a wiggle — until the resistance drops and the pin finds the hole. That is how a machine can do a task whose tolerances are tighter than its own accuracy.

## Keeping People Out, and Letting Them In

A conventional industrial arm has no idea a person is there. It will swing a hundred kilograms through its envelope at the speed it was told to, and the safety system is therefore entirely external: fencing, interlocked gates that cut motor power when opened, light curtains across loading apertures, laser area scanners watching the floor, and emergency stop circuits wired on two independent channels so that a single failure cannot disable them silently. Controllers add software-defined safe zones, verified by redundant processors, that will trip if the arm crosses a virtual plane regardless of what the program intended.

The exception is the teach pendant. Someone has to get inside the fence to jog the robot, so manual mode is governed by two interlocking rules: speed is limited to a slow walking pace, conventionally a quarter of a metre per second, and motion is enabled only while a three-position switch on the pendant is held at its middle detent. Release it and motion stops; clench it in alarm and motion also stops. It is a small, well-considered piece of design based on what a startled hand actually does.

Collaborative robots invert the model. Torque sensing in every joint lets the arm detect contact and stop, and the published guidance around these machines sets limits on the force and pressure a contact may produce against each part of the human body. That makes a fenceless cell possible, but the qualification matters: the certification covers the arm, not the application. An inherently safe arm holding a deburring bit, a hot glue nozzle, or a sheet of thin steel is not a safe cell, and the risk assessment has to be done on the whole installation. Collaborative arms are also, necessarily, slow. The energy a moving mass can deposit in a person goes up with the square of its speed, so safety is bought directly out of cycle time.

The competence on offer, then, is narrow and very deep. These machines are superb at holding a position, repeating a motion, and not getting tired, bored, distracted, or burned. They are poor at noticing that this particular casting has a flash line where the last thousand did not, and they have no way to care. The failure mode of almost every disappointing automation project is the same: a process that a person was quietly stabilizing with constant small corrections gets handed to a machine that makes none, and all the variation that was being absorbed invisibly turns into scrap at the end of the line.
