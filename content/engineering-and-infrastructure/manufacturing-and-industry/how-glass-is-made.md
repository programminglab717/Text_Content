# How Glass Is Made

Look at the edge of a broken bottle, not the face. The fracture surface is not flat and it does not follow any plane; it curves in shallow shell-like scoops, often with faint ripple lines radiating from where the break started. Mineralogists call that conchoidal fracture, and it is the signature of a material with no internal crystal structure to split along. A quartz crystal, chemically almost identical, breaks along its lattice. Glass has no lattice. It breaks wherever the crack happens to be going, which is why it can be flaked into a scalpel edge and why a chip in a windscreen spreads in a curve rather than a line.

That absence of structure is the whole story of the material. Molten silica is a tangle of silicon-oxygen tetrahedra linked corner to corner into a three-dimensional network. On cooling, the tangle should sort itself into a crystal, but the units are large, the linkages are stiff, and rearranging them takes time. Cool the melt faster than it can organize and the viscosity climbs until the network simply seizes up, frozen mid-shuffle. The result is rigid, transparent, and structurally still a liquid in the sense that its atoms are arranged the way a liquid's are.

This is where the old classroom claim comes from, that medieval window glass is thicker at the bottom because glass slowly flows. It does not. At room temperature the viscosity is so enormous that no measurable creep occurs over centuries — Roman glass in museum cases has not sagged. Old window panes are uneven because of how they were made. Crown glass was spun from a blown bubble into a flat disc by centrifugal force, which leaves it thinner at the rim and thicker toward the middle, and glaziers cutting panes from such a disc generally set the heavier edge downward.

## Sand, Ash, and Lime

Pure silica does make excellent glass, but it needs to be worked at temperatures approaching seventeen hundred degrees Celsius, which was out of reach for most of human history and is expensive even now. The practical solution, found empirically thousands of years ago, is to add a flux. Sodium carbonate — soda ash, originally obtained by burning coastal or desert plants — breaks some of the bridging oxygen links in the silica network, dropping the working temperature by several hundred degrees. The cost is that the resulting glass is water-soluble. Sodium silicate dissolves readily; it is sold today as water glass.

The fix is a stabilizer, and it is lime. Calcium oxide, from limestone in the batch, sits in the network and locks it against water attack without pushing the melting point back up. Soda, lime, and sand, in proportions that have not changed much in two millennia, make roughly nine tenths of all the glass in the world: windows, bottles, jars, drinking glasses, light bulbs.

The rest of the batch is corrective. Iron is the near-universal contaminant of sand, and even a fraction of a percent of it tints glass green — which is why the face of a window looks colourless while its cut edge looks distinctly green, the light having travelled the long way through. Manganese dioxide, known to old glassmakers as glassmaker's soap, counteracts the tint by adding a complementary purple. Refining agents are added to help dissolved gas coalesce into bubbles large enough to rise out of the melt rather than staying as the small trapped bubbles called seeds. And every furnace is charged with cullet, broken glass from rejects and recycling, which melts at a lower temperature than raw batch and cuts fuel consumption noticeably.

Change the recipe and you change the material entirely. Replace much of the soda and lime with boron oxide and you get borosilicate, developed in Germany in the late nineteenth century, whose low thermal expansion lets a laboratory flask go from a flame to a bench without cracking. Add lead oxide and you get a soft, heavy, high-refractive glass that sparkles when cut and rings when struck. Add a nucleating agent and deliberately grow crystals through the glass under controlled heat, and you get a glass-ceramic with almost no net expansion at all, which is why a ceramic cooktop can hold a red-hot ring next to cold air without shattering.

## Working the Gather

Glass has no melting point. It has a viscosity that falls continuously with temperature, and the entire craft depends on that fact. Somewhere in the middle of the curve is a working range where the material is stiff enough to hold a shape and soft enough to take a new one, and a glassblower's whole practice is about staying in that range, returning to the reheating chamber every half minute to buy another few seconds of plasticity. Glasses whose viscosity changes slowly with temperature are called long, and they are forgiving; a short glass stiffens fast and gives the worker almost no time.

Blowing itself appears in the archaeological record around the first century BCE, somewhere along the Syrian and Palestinian coast, and it changed glass from a luxury to a commodity within a couple of generations. A hollow iron pipe dipped into the melt picks up a gather; a puff of breath opens a bubble inside it; the bubble can then be swung, rolled on a flat steel marver, shaped with wet wooden blocks and the sprung iron tongs called jacks, and transferred to a solid rod, the punty, so that the opening can be finished. Every Roman glass vessel and every studio piece made today uses the same four or five tools.

Window glass was made from blown bubbles for centuries. In the crown method a bubble was opened and spun into a disc. In the cylinder method it was swung into a long tube, the ends cut off, the tube slit lengthwise, and the whole thing reheated in a flattening kiln until it slumped into a sheet. Both processes left surfaces with a slight waviness; plate glass good enough for mirrors and shop fronts had to be cast on a table and then ground and polished on both faces, a slow and expensive grind that consumed a great deal of the glass it started with.

## The Stress Inside

Hot glass that is allowed to cool at its own pace will destroy itself. The surface stiffens first while the interior is still contracting, and the mismatch locks permanent stress into the object — enough, often, to shatter it hours later on a shelf with no provocation at all. Every piece therefore goes into an annealing oven, called a lehr, which holds it just below the softening point long enough for the stress to relax and then cools it on a schedule slow enough that no new stress builds.

The counterpart is to put stress in on purpose. Heat a sheet of glass until it is near softening and then blast both faces with air, and the surfaces freeze while the core is still hot and shrinking. When the core finally contracts it pulls the surfaces into permanent compression, balanced by tension in the middle. Since cracks start at surface flaws and cannot propagate through compression, the pane becomes several times stronger. It also cannot be cut, drilled, or ground afterward — any breach releases the trapped tension, and the whole sheet dices instantly into blunt fragments. The natural version of the same phenomenon is the Prince Rupert's drop, a bead of glass quenched by dropping it into water, whose bulbous head will survive a hammer blow while a snip of its thin tail makes the entire drop explode.

Chemical strengthening does the same job by substitution rather than temperature. Soak the glass in a bath of molten potassium salt and potassium ions migrate into surface sites vacated by smaller sodium ions, wedging the surface layer into compression. It produces a thinner but more controllable compressed skin, and it is what makes a phone screen a couple of millimetres thick survive being sat on.

## Float, Bottles, and Fibre

The single biggest change in flat glass came in the 1950s, when Pilkington in England worked out how to pour a continuous ribbon of molten glass onto a shallow bath of molten tin. Tin is dense enough that glass floats on it, it stays liquid over exactly the right temperature range, and it does not wet or react with the glass. The ribbon spreads under its own weight until surface tension and gravity settle it at a uniform thickness with two dead-flat, fire-polished faces, then travels off the far end of the bath, through a long annealing lehr, and out to a line of cutters. Grinding and polishing disappeared from the industry within about a decade. Nearly every pane of window glass now in existence was made this way.

Containers are formed on individual section machines, which shear a measured gob of molten glass and drop it into a blank mould, form a rough parison either by blowing from below or by pressing with a plunger, then transfer it to a finish mould and blow it out to final shape. A line of such sections, each working on its own cycle but fed in rotation from a single gob stream, turns out bottles at a rate that would have astonished a nineteenth-century glasshouse.

Drawing glass rather than blowing or floating it gives fibres. A stream pulled from a bushing of tiny holes and attenuated by its own falling speed becomes glass wool or reinforcement roving. Pull a rod of ultrapure silica, made by depositing doped glass soot from a vapour and consolidating it into a solid preform, down a forty-metre tower, and its cross-section shrinks by a factor of hundreds while the arrangement of its layers is preserved exactly. The hair-thin fibre that emerges carries light for tens of kilometres before the signal needs refreshing, because the silica it is made from is more transparent than the air in the room.
