# CNC Machining Explained

A block of aluminum, solid and rectangular, sits clamped in a vise. A cutting tool spinning at several thousand revolutions per minute lowers into it, and a curl of bright metal peels away, followed by another, and another, each one flying off and landing in a small pile that grows through the afternoon. There is no operator standing at a hand crank, turning wheels to guide the tool. The machine is following a program, moving through hundreds of precise, pre-calculated positions in sequence, and by the end of the cycle, a rectangular block has become a bracket with pockets, holes, and curved fillets accurate to a couple of thousandths of an inch, without a person touching it after the start button.

This is CNC machining — computer numerical control — and it is one of the two dominant strategies for turning raw stock into a finished part, the other being processes like molding and casting that add or push material into shape. CNC does the opposite. It starts with more material than the final part needs and removes everything that is not the part, guided the whole time by a program rather than a hand.

## Subtraction, Not Addition

Manufacturing processes fall broadly into three families: forming, where material is pushed or squeezed into a new shape without leaving anywhere; additive, where material is built up layer by layer, as in 3D printing; and subtractive, where material is cut away from a larger block until what remains is the part. CNC machining belongs squarely to the third family, alongside older manual techniques like turning on a lathe or cutting on a manual mill, but with the crucial difference that a computer, not a human hand, controls the motion.

The appeal of subtraction is directness. A solid billet of aluminum, steel, titanium, or plastic already has the mechanical properties the final part needs, because it was cast or rolled or forged as bulk stock under conditions optimized for strength and consistency. Machining does not have to create those properties, only reveal the shape hiding inside the block. This is part of why machined parts are trusted in applications — aircraft structural components, surgical instruments, engine parts — where the material's internal grain structure and freedom from voids matters as much as its outer geometry.

## From Drawing to Toolpath

A CNC program does not spring directly from a designer's imagination. It begins as a three-dimensional model built in computer-aided design software, which defines the exact geometry of the finished part. That model then passes into computer-aided manufacturing software, where a programmer selects which cutting tools will be used, in what order, at what depths, and along what paths, simulating the entire machining sequence before any metal is touched. The output of this planning is a long list of coordinates and commands, traditionally called G-code, that tells the machine exactly where to move, how fast to feed the tool into the material, when to turn on coolant, and when to swap to a different tool.

Every machine tends to have its own dialect of this code, shaped by its particular controller, so the same toolpath plan is usually run through a post-processor that translates the generic instructions into the specific syntax a given mill or lathe expects. Get this translation wrong and the consequences range from a scrapped part to a genuinely dangerous collision between a tool moving at speed and a fixture it was never supposed to touch.

## The Machine Itself

The two archetypal CNC machines are the mill and the lathe, and they represent opposite ways of creating relative motion between tool and workpiece. On a mill, the workpiece is held stationary — bolted into a vise or a custom fixture on the table — while a rotating cutting tool moves through three or more axes to remove material from its surface. On a lathe, the relationship is reversed: the workpiece itself spins, chucked in a rotating head, while a stationary cutting tool advances into it, which is the natural way to produce round, symmetric shapes like shafts, bushings, and threaded fittings.

Modern machines often blur the line, combining milling and turning capability in a single unit, and adding axes beyond the basic three lets a tool approach a part from angles a simpler machine could never reach. A five-axis mill, for instance, can tilt and rotate the workpiece or the cutting head continuously during a cut, letting a single setup machine a complex, curved surface — a turbine blade, an impeller — that would otherwise require repositioning the part by hand multiple times, with all the accumulated error that repositioning invites. An automatic tool changer, a carousel or chain holding dozens of pre-set cutting tools, lets the machine swap between a roughing end mill, a finishing tool, and a drill bit in a few seconds, entirely without pausing for a human to do it.

## Cutting the Metal

Removing metal efficiently is its own discipline. The geometry of the cutting tool — the angle of its flutes, the sharpness of its edge, the coating applied to resist heat and wear — has to be matched to the material being cut and the type of cut being made. Feed rate, the speed at which the tool advances into the material, and spindle speed, how fast it spins, have to be tuned together: too aggressive and the tool overheats, dulls quickly, or breaks outright; too conservative and the job takes needlessly long and can actually wear tools out faster through rubbing rather than cutting cleanly.

Coolant, usually a water-based fluid flooded or misted directly at the cutting edge, serves two purposes at once: it carries heat away from the tool and workpiece, and it flushes chips out of the cut so they do not get re-cut or jam against the tool. Chip control matters more than it sounds. A long, stringy chip that should break into short segments can wrap around a spinning tool or workpiece and ruin a part or damage the machine, which is why tool designers cut specific geometries into cutting edges purely to control how the chip curls and breaks as it comes off.

## From Punched Tape to Computer Control

Numerical control predates the computer. In the years after the Second World War, aircraft manufacturers needed a way to machine complex, curved wing and fuselage components with a consistency that manual machining, dependent on a skilled operator's hands and eyes, struggled to guarantee. Researchers working with the U.S. Air Force and MIT's Servomechanisms Laboratory developed machines that read instructions from punched paper tape, translating a sequence of holes into precise motor movements. It worked, but generating that tape for a complex part was laborious, and every machine needed its own tape prepared and physically loaded.

The arrival of affordable minicomputers in the 1970s changed the economics entirely. A computer could store a program electronically, edit it without retyping a whole tape, and eventually calculate toolpaths itself from a geometric model rather than requiring a programmer to compute coordinates by hand. What had been numerical control became computer numerical control, and the falling cost of computing over the following decades is a large part of why CNC machines migrated from aerospace prime contractors down to small job shops running a handful of machines for local customers.

## Where It Fits Today

CNC machining occupies a particular niche in the manufacturing landscape: excellent for parts that need tight tolerances, complex geometry, or a material's full-strength properties, and for production runs too small to justify a dedicated mold or die. A single prototype, a run of a few hundred replacement parts, a one-off fixture for a factory floor — these are CNC's natural territory. At very high volumes, the calculation shifts, because machining removes material one cut at a time and a die-casting or injection-molding machine can form an equally complex shape in a single closed-and-open cycle, far faster per part once the tooling investment is made.

Many finished products still combine both worlds, arriving at a machined part after a rougher process has already gotten them close to shape: a forged crankshaft gets its precise bearing surfaces finish-machined; a casting gets critical mating faces milled flat and drilled. The subtractive step at the end is often what turns a rough, strong shape into one that will actually fit together with everything around it, which is a smaller, quieter kind of precision than the dramatic image of a shop full of spinning tools, but it is usually the reason the part works at all.
