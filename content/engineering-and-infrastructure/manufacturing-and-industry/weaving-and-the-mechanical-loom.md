# Weaving and the Mechanical Loom

Find a cut edge on a piece of woven cloth — the raw end of a bandage, the inside of a seam — and pull a single thread out of it. It comes away as one continuous strand running the full width of the fabric, leaving a gap behind it like a missing rung. Pull another and the cloth begins to unravel across, but not along, because the threads running the other way are not interlaced with each other at all. They are held apart and in order only by the ones you are removing. That is the whole trick of weaving: two sets of threads at right angles, neither of which is attached to anything, held together purely by the fact that they take turns going over and under.

The threads running the length of the fabric are the warp. They are put on the loom first, in their hundreds or thousands, under tension, parallel, and they have to survive the entire process. The thread that travels across is the weft, or the pick. Everything a loom does is in service of a single repeated operation: lift some warp threads, leave the rest down, throw a weft thread through the gap, and push it home.

## The Geometry of Cloth

Which warp threads get lifted is the entire design vocabulary of weaving. Lift every second one and alternate on each pass and you get plain weave — the densest possible interlacing, the most stable, the structure of a bedsheet or a canvas tent. Lift in a pattern that shifts by one thread each pass and the crossing points line up into diagonal ridges: that is twill, and the diagonal on the leg of a pair of jeans is the visible record of the warp lift sequence used to make the denim. Twill drapes better than plain weave because each thread passes under several others before it bends back, so there are fewer bends per centimetre and the cloth is more flexible. Push that logic further, scattering the binding points so that no two are adjacent, and the long uninterrupted floats of thread reflect light along their whole length. That is satin, and its softness and sheen come entirely from the interlacing pattern rather than from the fibre.

Cloth also has edges, and they are not an afterthought. Where the weft turns around and comes back, the fabric finishes itself in a firm band called the selvedge. That is a property of a shuttle carrying a continuous thread back and forth. Cut the weft on every pass, as most modern machines do, and you get loose ends that have to be tucked or fused instead, which is why the old closed selvedge has become a mark of cloth woven on shuttle looms.

## Five Motions

Strip away the cabinetry and any loom, from a backstrap loom in the Andes to a machine weaving airbag fabric, performs five motions. Shedding raises some warp threads and lowers others to open a triangular tunnel, the shed. Picking sends the weft through that tunnel. Beating-up drives the new pick against the edge of the finished cloth with a comb called the reed. Letting-off releases a controlled length of fresh warp from the beam at the back. Taking-up winds the completed cloth onto a roller at the front. The first three happen in strict sequence, dozens or hundreds of times a minute; the last two happen continuously and must be balanced against each other precisely, because the tension in the warp determines how tightly the cloth is packed and therefore how much thread ends up in a given length of fabric.

The selection mechanism is where looms differ most. Each warp thread passes through the eye of a heddle — a wire or string loop — and the heddles are hung on frames called shafts. Raise a shaft and every thread threaded onto it rises together. Four shafts are enough for most twills; a dobby mechanism can drive twenty or thirty and produce small repeating geometric figures. Beyond that you run out of combinations, because threads sharing a shaft can never be separated from one another.

Each thread also passes through a gap between two blades of the reed, which sets the spacing of the warp across the width and delivers the beat. The reed used to be made of split cane, and the name stuck long after the blades became steel.

## Pattern, and the Punched Card

Figured silk — brocade, damask, a woven portrait — needs individual control of every warp thread, and for centuries that was a matter of labour. The European drawloom carried an assistant, usually a boy, perched on or beside the loom, pulling groups of cords in a memorized sequence at every pass while the weaver worked below. It was slow, hot, exacting work, and a complicated pattern might take a day to advance a few centimetres.

Several French inventors attacked the problem across the eighteenth century, encoding the pattern in perforated paper or cylinders. Joseph Marie Jacquard, working in Lyon in the first years of the nineteenth century, assembled their ideas into a head that sat above the loom and worked reliably in production. Stiff cards, each one carrying the instruction for a single pick, were laced into an endless chain and pressed in turn against a bank of horizontal needles. Where a card was solid it pushed its needle back and disengaged a hook; where it was punched, the needle entered the hole, the hook stayed engaged, and a knife bar lifted that hook and the warp thread hanging from it. The pattern became a physical object, separable from the loom, copyable, sellable, and as long as you liked. The mechanism can fairly be described as reading a program, and the analogy was not lost on the people who later designed calculating machines.

## Power

Mechanizing the loom turned out to be harder than mechanizing spinning, and the sequence in which the bottlenecks moved is the whole industrial story of cotton in miniature. John Kay's flying shuttle, patented in the 1730s, let a single weaver drive the shuttle across a wide loom with a jerk of a cord instead of passing it hand to hand, which roughly doubled the rate at which a hand weaver consumed yarn. The spinners could not keep up. That pressure produced the spinning jenny, then the water frame, then the mule, and within a few decades the imbalance had reversed completely: mills could spin far more yarn than hand weavers could turn into cloth, and for a generation handloom weaving was unusually well paid work.

Edmund Cartwright patented a power loom in the 1780s, but his first machines were crude, and the real obstacle was not the mechanism. Warp threads under tension, rubbed by heddles and reed at machine speed, break constantly, and a loom that stops every few seconds while a human repairs a thread is worth nothing. The fix was chemical and preparatory: sizing the warp with starch paste to stiffen and lubricate it, applied on separate dressing machinery before the warp ever reached the loom, and washed out of the finished cloth afterward. With sized warps, cast iron frames, and steam, the power loom became genuinely superior sometime in the 1820s and 1830s, and the handloom weavers who had been prosperous a generation earlier were driven into destitution fast enough that the collapse became a standing example in every later argument about machinery and labour.

## The Shuttle's Last Century

The shuttle survived into the twentieth century, but it is a bad idea mechanically. It is a boat-shaped block of wood holding a bobbin of yarn, and it has to be flung across a metre or more of open shed, caught, stopped, and flung back — accelerating and reversing several times a second. Its mass limits speed, it makes a shattering noise, and if it ever escapes the shed it becomes a projectile.

The first big improvement left the shuttle in place and attacked the stoppages. The Northrop loom, developed in Massachusetts in the 1890s, could detect a nearly exhausted bobbin and punch a fresh one into the shuttle without stopping, along with warp stop motions that halted the loom the instant a thread broke. One weaver could then mind a dozen or two dozen looms instead of a handful, and the economics of weaving shifted again.

The shuttle was finally abandoned in the second half of the twentieth century in favour of machines that carry the weft across without carrying its supply with it. Projectile looms fire a small gripper that seizes the end of the yarn and is shot across in a guided channel. Rapier looms use one or two slender arms that reach in from the sides and hand the yarn over in the middle. Air-jet looms dispense with solid carriers entirely and blow the yarn through a tunnel formed by profiled reed blades, with relay nozzles firing in sequence along the width to keep it moving; water-jet machines do the same with a pulse of water for yarns that do not mind getting wet. The fastest of these insert well over a thousand picks a minute, which is beyond what any reciprocating shuttle could approach, and they draw weft from large stationary packages that need changing only occasionally.

What has not changed is the underlying sequence. A modern weaving shed, with electronically controlled shedding and jets instead of shuttles, is still opening a shed, inserting a pick, and beating it up, hundreds of times a second across a row of machines. The cloth coming off the front is made the way cloth has been made since before writing, only faster, and with nobody sitting on the loom pulling cords.
