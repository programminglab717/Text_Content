# The Assembly Line and What It Changed

In the spring of 1913, on the second floor of Ford's Highland Park plant in Detroit, a group of men were assembling flywheel magnetos the way such things had always been assembled: each worker built a complete unit from a pile of parts at his bench, taking about twenty minutes. Someone had the idea of laying the magnetos on a waist-high rail and having each man do one operation and slide the piece along to the next. The first attempt made things worse. The rail was too low, the men stooped, and the slowest worker set the pace for everyone. They raised the rail, rebalanced the work, and eventually added a chain to move the units at a fixed speed. Assembly time fell to roughly a third of what it had been.

That experiment, on a component most customers never saw, is the actual origin of the moving assembly line. Within months the idea had been pushed onto engine assembly, then onto the chassis itself, where a rope and windlass first dragged bare frames across the floor past stationary workers holding parts. The chassis had taken something on the order of twelve hours to put together. By the end of the following year, with the line refined and subassemblies feeding it from overhead conveyors, it took about an hour and a half.

## Everything That Had to Exist First

The moving line is usually told as a flash of genius, and it was not. It was the last piece to drop into place in a system that had been assembling itself for a century, and it would have been useless without the rest.

The first prerequisite was interchangeable parts. Through most of the nineteenth century, a "machine" was a set of components fitted to each other by a skilled hand with a file, and a replacement part meant another session with the file. The American federal armories at Springfield and Harpers Ferry spent decades and enormous sums chasing the ideal of muskets whose lock parts could be swapped between weapons at random, working out along the way the jigs, fixtures, and go/no-go gauges that made it possible. The method spread out of the armories into sewing machines, clocks, typewriters, reapers, and bicycles — the so-called American system of manufactures — until by the time Ford needed it, the practice of making thousands of parts alike enough to assemble without fitting was ordinary industrial competence.

The second prerequisite was the moving overhead conveyor, and it came from an industry that took things apart rather than putting them together. The great meatpacking houses of Cincinnati and then Chicago had for decades been hanging carcasses from a rail and walking them past a line of men, each of whom made one cut. Ford's people knew this. William Klann, who ran engine assembly, went to see a packing plant and came back arguing that if a carcass could be disassembled on a moving rail, an engine could be built on one. The disassembly line was the template; Ford ran it backwards.

The third prerequisite was the shape of the factory itself. Older plants were organized by craft — all the drills in one room, all the lathes in another — because line shafting and leather belting made it convenient to cluster machines around a power source. Electric motors on individual machines cut that constraint, and Ford's production men arranged equipment in the order in which the work was done, so a part moved from operation to operation without backtracking. Walter Flanders had been laying out machinery this way at Ford before the conveyor existed, and the layout is arguably what made the conveyor possible rather than the other way around.

The fourth was a product simple enough to stand still. The Model T was designed to be built, not merely to be driven: few parts, vanadium steel where strength was needed, and no annual restyling. Ford froze the design and then spent a decade reorganizing the factory around it. A moving line cannot absorb a product that changes underneath it.

## The Logic of the Line

What the conveyor actually does is subtle. It does not make any individual worker faster in the sense of moving their hands more quickly. It does three other things.

It eliminates the walking. In bench assembly, a worker spends a surprising fraction of the day fetching parts, setting down one tool and picking up another, and carrying work from one place to another. Bringing the work past a fixed station at a fixed height abolishes nearly all of that. Ford's engineers were fanatical on this point — parts were to arrive at the height of the worker's hands, and a man was never to take a step he did not have to.

It enforces balance. Once the line runs at a set speed, every station has exactly the same amount of time, called the cycle time, and the work must be divided into chunks that fit. This division is where the real engineering lies, and it is why industrial engineers still spend their careers on line balancing. A station that runs long becomes the bottleneck for the entire plant; a station with slack is waste. The moving belt makes imbalance visible immediately, because work piles up in one place and starves in another.

It transfers control of the pace from the worker to the machine. This is the part that has never stopped being controversial. Under the older system, a skilled fitter's output was his own business within limits; supervision was supervision of results. On a line, the conveyor speed is a management decision, and the worker's only real choice is to keep up or stop everything.

The consequence was a radical simplification of the job. Tasks that had taken years to learn were broken into motions that could be taught in minutes, and Ford was explicit about it — the great majority of his jobs, he claimed, required no particular skill and could be filled by a new hire the same day. This opened factory work to immigrants and to workers with no trade, at wages far above farm labor, and it simultaneously destroyed the bargaining position of the craftsman whose knowledge had been the bottleneck.

## The Five Dollar Day and the Problem It Solved

The line worked so well, and was so unpleasant to work on, that Ford nearly could not staff it. Turnover in 1913 was catastrophic — the company had to hire many times over its actual headcount across the year just to keep the stations filled — and every departure meant retraining, mistakes, and lost output. In January 1914 Ford announced a wage of five dollars a day for qualifying workers, roughly doubling the going rate, along with a shorter workday.

It is often described as Ford's scheme to let his workers buy his cars, and he did say things to that effect later. The immediate motivation was plainer. Turnover collapsed, absenteeism collapsed, and a line formed outside the employment office in the Michigan winter. The wage also came with strings that reveal how the company understood the bargain: a Sociological Department sent investigators to workers' homes to verify sobriety, thrift, and domestic arrangements before a man qualified for the full rate. Control of the pace inside the plant extended, for a while, to a claim on life outside it.

## What Followed

Ford's system had one enormous vulnerability, and General Motors found it. A line optimized for a single unchanging product is brutally efficient at making that product and terrible at making anything else. When Alfred Sloan organized GM around a ladder of brands, annual model changes, and colors other than black, Ford's advantage became a trap; converting Highland Park's successor plant from the Model T to the Model A required shutting down for months and rebuilding the tooling almost from scratch. Mass production and product variety turned out to be in tension, and the next several decades of manufacturing engineering were largely about relieving that tension — flexible tooling, faster die changes, mixed-model lines that can build a sedan and a wagon in alternating cycles.

The other consequence took longer. The line concentrated tens of thousands of interchangeable workers in a single building, gave them a shared grievance in the speed of the conveyor, and handed them a weapon nobody had anticipated: a stoppage anywhere halts everything. The sit-down strikes in Flint in the winter of 1936 and 1937, where workers occupied the plants rather than picketing outside them, worked precisely because a handful of key body plants fed the entire corporation. The industrial union was in some sense a product of the same logic that produced the industrial line.

The line itself escaped the auto industry entirely. It reappeared in appliance plants and electronics assembly, in commercial kitchens where a hamburger is built by a sequence of specialists, in mail-order warehouses where the picker walks a route calculated by software, and in the staged pipelines through which software itself is now built and shipped. The mechanism travels well because the underlying insight is not about conveyors. It is that if you can standardize the thing being made, divide the making into equal-sized pieces, and move the work rather than the worker, output rises by a factor that has nothing to do with anyone trying harder.

Whether the trade is worth making has never been settled, and it gets renegotiated every time the mechanism shows up somewhere new.
