# Shinto Shrines, Kami, and Japanese Ritual Practice

The approach to a shrine tells you most of what you need to know before you
reach the buildings. You pass under a torii — two uprights, two crossbars, no
door, nothing to keep anyone out — and the path beyond it is usually gravel,
usually bordered by trees, usually longer than it needs to be. The gate marks no
property line. It marks a change of kind. On one side is ordinary ground; on the
other is ground where a kami is understood to be present, and the walk gives you
time to arrive differently than you would arrive at a shop.

Shinto has no founder, no creed to recite, no scripture that functions the way a
Bible or a Qur'an functions, and for most of Japanese history it had no name.
The word itself — the way of the kami — came into use largely to distinguish
these practices from the imported way of the Buddha. What it labels is less a
system of belief than an inherited set of relations with particular places and
particular presences, maintained mostly by doing things correctly rather than by
thinking things correctly.

## What a Kami Is

Kami is usually translated "god" or "spirit," and both mislead. The classical
description, from the eighteenth-century scholar Motoori Norinaga, is closer:
anything that inspires awe, that possesses a quality of extraordinary power,
whether that thing is admirable or terrifying. Mountains are kami. So are
waterfalls, old trees, particular rocks, storms, rice, the ancestors of a clan,
emperors, a few notable historical figures, and a set of named mythological
figures including Amaterasu, the sun, from whom the imperial line traditionally
descends. A conventional phrase counts them at eight million, meaning
uncountable.

Kami are not omnipotent, not necessarily moral, and not remote. They can be
pleased, offended, invited and settled. A great deal of shrine practice consists
of maintaining conditions under which a kami will remain in residence and remain
well disposed. Some of the oldest holy places have no building for the deity at
all. At Omiwa in Nara the mountain behind the shrine is the object of worship
and there is no inner sanctuary, because there is no need for one. Elsewhere a
rope of twisted rice straw — shimenawa — hung with folded paper streamers marks
a tree or a boulder as a place a kami occupies or may descend into.

## The Grammar of a Visit

Near the entrance stands a stone basin with a bamboo dipper. The visitor rinses
the left hand, the right hand, then pours water into a cupped palm to rinse the
mouth, and finally tips the dipper upright so the remaining water runs down the
handle. This is the small domestic version of a much larger idea. Shinto's
central anxiety is not sin but kegare — pollution, a condition attaching to
death, blood, illness and disorder, which is not wickedness and carries no guilt
but which must be cleared away before approach. Purification, harae, is the
tradition's characteristic act. Priests perform it by waving a wand hung with
paper streamers; at the extreme, practitioners stand under freezing waterfalls
in midwinter.

At the offering hall the standard sequence is a coin tossed into the box, two
bows, two claps, a silent moment, a final bow. The claps are to announce
yourself. Beyond the worship hall is the honden, the inner sanctuary, generally
closed. It houses the shintai — the object in which the kami is understood to
dwell, often a mirror, sometimes a sword or a stone. Almost nobody ever sees it.
At some shrines the priests themselves do not.

The rest of the precinct runs on paper and wood. Small wooden plaques called ema
hang in racks by the hundreds, each with a request written on the back:
university entrance, a safe birth, recovery from an illness, occasionally
something frivolous. Fortunes drawn as printed slips are read, and the bad ones
knotted onto a frame or a branch so the luck is left behind rather than carried
home. Amulets for traffic safety hang from rearview mirrors all over Japan,
including in cars whose owners would describe themselves as having no religion —
a category most Japanese people check, and which coexists without friction with
a New Year shrine visit and a Buddhist family grave.

## Festivals, and the Rebuilt Shrine

A matsuri is the point at which the shrine stops being quiet. The kami is
transferred into a portable shrine, a mikoshi, and carried through the
neighborhood on the shoulders of a shouting crowd, deliberately jostled and
rocked, because the shaking is thought to rouse and please the deity. Sacred
dances are performed, sometimes by shrine attendants, sometimes by villagers in
masks. Food stalls line the approach. The tone is not solemn. The kami is being
shown its territory and the territory is being reminded whose it is.

The most remarkable institution in Japanese religion, though, is a construction
schedule. The Grand Shrine at Ise, dedicated to Amaterasu, is rebuilt every
twenty years. Two adjacent plots alternate; carpenters raise an exact
replacement on the empty one using joinery and unpainted cypress in the ancient
manner, the sacred objects are moved across at night, and the old buildings are
dismantled and their timber distributed to other shrines. The practice has run,
with interruptions during the civil wars, for well over a millennium. The result
is a structure that is always new and always old — continuity carried by
technique and by the training of the next generation of carpenters rather than
by preserved material. Nothing in the buildings is original; the buildings are
original.

## Kami and Buddhas, Together and Apart

Buddhism arrived in Japan in the sixth century, and the two traditions spent
more than a thousand years intertwined rather than in competition. Temples kept
shrines on their grounds for the local kami; shrines employed Buddhist monks to
chant sutras for the kami's own spiritual progress. A sophisticated theory,
honji suijaku, resolved the relationship by treating kami as local
manifestations of buddhas and bodhisattvas — the same reality appearing in a
form the country could recognize. For most of Japanese history the ordinary
person did not experience these as two religions requiring a choice.

The separation was imposed, abruptly, after the Meiji Restoration of 1868. Edicts
ordered kami and buddhas disentangled: Buddhist images removed from shrines,
combinatory titles abolished, priesthoods sorted. A wave of anti-Buddhist
destruction followed in some regions, and a great deal of art was lost. The
purpose was political. A modernizing state wanted an indigenous, unifying
national cult, with the emperor's descent from Amaterasu as its organizing
claim, and shrine rites reframed as patriotic duty rather than religion — a
distinction that allowed the government to require participation while
formally granting freedom of belief. That arrangement, usually called State
Shinto, ended with the occupation after 1945, when the shrines were
disestablished and the emperor publicly set aside claims to divinity. Most
shrines now belong to a voluntary national association, funded by donations and
by the sale of amulets.

The older division of labor survived all of it and is still visible in ordinary
life. Weddings and the blessing of newborns and small children tend to go to
shrines; funerals and memorial rites for ancestors go, overwhelmingly, to
Buddhist temples. Death is the province of the buddhas, in part because death is
precisely what a shrine treats as pollution. Many households keep both a
kamidana, a small high shelf for the kami, and a butsudan, a cabinet for the
family dead. Neither arrangement is felt to contradict the other.

## What Persists

Traces of shrine practice run through things that are not presented as religion
at all. A sumo wrestler throws salt into the ring to purify it, and the roof
suspended above the ring is built like a shrine roof. Construction sites are
blessed by a priest before the ground is broken, including sites for office
towers owned by companies with no religious character whatsoever. New cars get
blessed. Breweries hang a ball of cedar over the door.

Vast numbers of people visit a shrine in the first days of January, and the
crowds at the largest shrines are measured in millions. Ask a participant
whether they believe the kami exists and you will often get a shrug or a
qualification. The question presumes that belief is what the practice is for.
The visit expresses something nearer to membership — in a neighborhood, a
family, a landscape, a year that has begun and needs starting properly.

That indifference to doctrine is what makes Shinto difficult to define from
outside and easy to participate in. The torii at Fushimi Inari outside Kyoto
form a tunnel of thousands of vermilion gates climbing a hillside, each one paid
for by a business hoping for prosperity and inscribed with the donor's name on
the back. Walking up through them, you are moving through an accumulation of
individual transactions with a deity, made by people who mostly would not call
themselves religious, maintained across generations because the alternative —
letting the gates rot and the path close — is unthinkable in a way that has
nothing to do with belief.
