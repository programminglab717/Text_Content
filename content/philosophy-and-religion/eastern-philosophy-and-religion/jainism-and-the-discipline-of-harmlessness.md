# Jainism and the Discipline of Harmlessness

A Jain monk walking a road in Gujarat carries almost nothing — a wooden bowl, a
length of undyed cloth if his order permits clothing, and a small soft broom
made of shed peacock feathers or unspun wool. Before he sits down he sweeps the
ground. Before he sets the bowl anywhere he sweeps that spot too. To a visitor
the gesture reads as fastidiousness about insects, which undersells it. He is
not worried about contamination. He is avoiding an act, because in the Jain
account of the universe acts do not simply happen and pass. They leave a
residue, and that residue is the reason he is here at all.

Jainism is usually introduced as the religion of extreme nonviolence, and the
description is accurate as far as it goes. But ahimsa in this tradition is not
first of all a sentiment about the value of life, though it produces one. It is
a conclusion drawn from an unusually literal theory of what binds a soul to the
world.

## Ford-Makers

Jains do not think of their tradition as founded. They count twenty-four
tirthankaras — "ford-makers," figures who cut a crossing through the river of
rebirth for others to use — stretching back through cosmic time. The
twenty-fourth, Vardhamana Mahavira, lived in the Gangetic plain in roughly the
same period as the Buddha, among the small republics, new cities and
competing ascetic movements that produced both. The twenty-third, Parshvanatha,
is placed by tradition a couple of centuries earlier and is treated by many
historians as plausibly a real person, which would make the Jain community's
memory of itself older than the Buddhist one.

Mahavira was born into a warrior-clan family, renounced in his thirties by the
standard account, and spent twelve years in a severe ascetic discipline before
attaining kevala jnana — omniscience, the state in which a soul, freed of
obstruction, knows everything at once. He taught for about three decades and
died by voluntary fasting. Jains do not regard him as a god, a savior, or an
intercessor. A tirthankara who has achieved liberation is beyond the world and
does nothing for anybody in it. The image in the temple is a model, not an
agent. This is why Jain worship can look intensely devotional while the theology
behind it contains no creator and no ruling deity: the universe was not made, it
will not end, and nobody is in charge of it.

## Karma You Can Weigh

Most Indian traditions treat karma as moral causation of some abstract kind.
Jainism treats it as physics. Karma is a category of extremely fine matter —
real stuff, drifting everywhere. A soul, jiva, is by nature luminous, weightless
and fully knowing. What it is not, in its ordinary condition, is clean. Anger,
pride, deceit and greed make the soul adhesive, and karmic matter sticks to it
the way dust sticks to something oiled. The accumulated coating obscures the
soul's knowledge, determines the body it will next take, and fixes the length of
that life.

That framing changes what counts as a problem. If karma were only a moral
ledger, an accidental injury would be morally neutral. In the Jain analysis even
unintended harm produces some influx, though far less than harm done
deliberately or with relish. The monk's broom follows directly from this. So
does the long Jain avoidance of occupations that destroy systematically —
plowing, which kills soil life by the acre, was ruled out for centuries, and the
concentration of Jain families in trade, banking, gem cutting and later industry
is partly a consequence of that prohibition rather than an accident of class.

Jain thinkers also graded living things by how many senses they possess, and
scaled the gravity of harm accordingly. At the bottom are beings with the single
sense of touch: plants, and the almost inconceivably numerous microscopic
organisms called nigodas, along with the elemental bodies Jains assign to earth,
water, fire and air. Worms have two senses, ants three, bees four; animals and
humans have five, and with the fifth comes mind. Killing a bee is worse than
pulling up a plant, and killing a person worse again, but none of it is free.
The discipline is not an attempt to reach zero, which embodied life makes
impossible. It is an attempt to hold the unavoidable to its minimum.

Liberation becomes a two-part engineering problem: stop the influx of new karma,
and burn off what is already attached. The first is samvara, achieved by
restraint and constant attention. The second is nirjara, the purpose of
austerity — fasting, meditation, endurance of heat and cold, the deliberate
wearing down of a stock of consequences that would otherwise have to be lived
out. A soul that sheds the last of it rises to the top of the universe and stays
there, omniscient and untroubled, among all the others who have done the same.

## Many-Sidedness

For a tradition of such uncompromising practice, Jainism's intellectual
signature is a striking modesty about knowing. The doctrine of anekantavada —
non-one-sidedness — holds that reality has innumerable aspects, and that any
statement about it is made from a standpoint, which means it catches something
real and leaves out a great deal.

The familiar illustration is the elephant examined by people who cannot see it,
each certain from the trunk or the leg or the ear that the animal is a snake or
a pillar or a fan. Jain philosophers turned that intuition into formal
machinery. Syadvada attaches a qualifier — roughly "in some respect" — to every
assertion, and generates a scheme of seven conditioned predications covering
affirmation, denial, their joint application, and what cannot be said at all.
Nayavada catalogs the legitimate standpoints from which a thing can be
described: as substance, as momentary state, as name, as function.

None of this is relativism. Jains are perfectly willing to say that particular
views are wrong. The claim is narrower and harder to dismiss: that a pot is
permanent considered as clay and impermanent considered as pot, and that the old
quarrel between Buddhist momentariness and Vedantic permanence runs forever
because each side has absolutized a partial truth. Some readers take
anekantavada to be the intellectual form of ahimsa — a refusal to do violence to
another person's position by pretending there is nothing in it.

## Two Orders, and the Householder's Version

By the early centuries of the common era the community had divided into
Digambaras, "sky-clad," whose most advanced monks renounce clothing altogether,
and Svetambaras, "white-clad," whose ascetics wear plain white cloth. The
disagreement runs deeper than dress. Digambaras hold that the original teachings
were lost and reject the Svetambara canon; they also hold that liberation cannot
be attained in a female body, which Svetambaras deny — their tradition maintains
that the nineteenth tirthankara was a woman. Within the Svetambara fold the
Sthanakvasi and Terapanthi reform movements rejected image worship and are
recognizable by the muhpatti, a cloth worn across the mouth so that breath and
speech do not injure the minute beings in the air.

Ascetics take five great vows without qualification: non-injury, truth,
non-stealing, celibacy, non-possession. Laypeople take the same five in a
scaled-down household form, supplemented by self-imposed limits on travel,
consumption and enterprise. The dietary discipline that results is the most
visible feature of Jain life: strict vegetarianism, no eating after dark so that
insects are not swallowed unseen, and avoidance of root vegetables, whose
harvesting kills the whole plant and disturbs the crowd of organisms a tuber
shelters. Honey is refused, since taking it robs and injures the hive. Drinking
water is strained.

At the end of a long life, or in terminal illness, a Jain may undertake
sallekhana, a gradual and publicly declared fast to death. The tradition insists
it is the opposite of suicide, which is driven by passion and generates karma;
sallekhana is meant to be entered calmly, with the permission of teachers and
family, and it can be abandoned. It remains legally and ethically contested in
India, and Jains defend it with some heat.

The annual festival of Paryushana concentrates the year's discipline into eight
or ten days of fasting, scriptural recitation and self-examination, and closes
with a day of forgiveness on which Jains ask pardon of everyone they may have
harmed, in a formula whose sense is: whatever offense I have given, knowingly or
not, let it be without effect.

## What a Small Community Left Behind

Jains have never been numerous — a fraction of one percent of India's population
— and their cultural output is wildly disproportionate to that. The marble
temples at Dilwara on Mount Abu and at Ranakpur carry carving so fine that the
ceilings look pierced rather than cut. Shatrunjaya near Palitana is an entire
hill covered in hundreds of shrines, climbed on foot before dawn. At
Shravanabelagola a single granite figure of Bahubali rises nearly sixty feet,
anointed once every twelve years by pilgrims working from scaffolding.

Less visible, and arguably more consequential, are the bhandars — the manuscript
libraries at Jaisalmer, Patan and elsewhere, where Jain communities preserved
palm-leaf and paper texts for centuries, including a great many works that were
not Jain at all. Some of what survives of early Indian literature, grammar and
medicine survives because Jain librarians were meticulous and their patrons paid
for copying. Jain mathematicians, working inside a cosmology that demanded
enormous numbers and distinguished several kinds of infinity, produced early
results in combinatorics and series.

The modern reach of the tradition runs partly through Gandhi, who grew up in
Gujarat among Jain neighbors, corresponded closely with the Jain poet and
thinker Shrimad Rajchandra, and absorbed from that milieu both the primacy of
ahimsa and the habit of treating truth as something approached from more than
one side. What he did with it — turning restraint outward, into public pressure
— is not something a Jain ascetic would recognize as his own project. The
underlying move was familiar enough. Refuse the harmful act, accept the cost of
refusing, and do not pretend your opponent's view is empty.
