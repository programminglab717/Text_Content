# The Sikh Tradition from Nanak to the Guru Granth Sahib

In the last years of his life, the man his followers called Nanak farmed. He had
spent decades walking — to Hindu pilgrimage towns, to Sufi shrines, by some
accounts as far as Mecca and Baghdad — arguing with priests and ascetics and
singing hymns to the accompaniment of a Muslim musician named Mardana who had
traveled with him for most of it. Then he settled on the bank of the Ravi at a
place called Kartarpur, worked the fields, and gathered a village of people who
worked them with him. The ending matters as much as the journeys. Whatever else
Nanak concluded, he concluded that the religious life did not require leaving
ordinary life behind.

Everything distinctive about the tradition he founded is already implied in that
decision. Sikhism has no monasticism, no celibate priesthood, no class of
renunciants, and a persistent suspicion of ritual performed for its own sake. It
asks instead for three things a farmer can do: remember God constantly, earn an
honest living, and share what you earn.

## Nanak's Century

Nanak was born in 1469 in a village in the Punjab, in territory that is now
Pakistan, into a Hindu family of the merchant caste, in a region under Muslim
rule where Sufi devotional currents and the Hindu bhakti movement had been
running alongside each other for generations. Both emphasized direct love of God
over ritual and priestly mediation; both produced poetry in local languages
rather than Sanskrit or Persian; both had produced figures — Kabir the weaver,
Ravidas the leatherworker, Namdev the calico printer — who attacked caste and
ceremony from inside the tradition.

The story Sikhs tell of Nanak's calling has him disappear while bathing in a
river, return after three days, and say that there is no Hindu and no Muslim.
The line is usually misread as an ecumenical pleasantry. In context it is
sharper: the categories people fight over do not describe what God attends to.
What God attends to is conduct and inward orientation.

Nanak's theology is compressed into the opening lines of the scripture he
composed, a passage Sikhs recite daily. It begins with a numeral and a syllable
— Ik Onkar, One Being — and goes on to name that reality as true, creative,
without fear, without enmity, beyond time, unborn, self-existent. There is one
God, formless and not incarnated, present everywhere and knowable through
disciplined remembrance of the divine name. Nanak rejected image worship,
ritual bathing as a means of purification, the sacred thread, caste hierarchy,
and the claim that women are spiritually inferior — a position he stated bluntly,
asking why one should call inferior the person who gives birth to kings.

The institution that carried the argument was practical. At Kartarpur everyone
who came ate together, seated on the floor in rows, from the same kitchen,
regardless of caste. The community kitchen, langar, remains the most recognizable
Sikh institution in the world, serving free meals daily at every gurdwara to
anyone who arrives and removes their shoes and covers their head. It was
conceived as an argument made with food: a Brahmin and an untouchable eating
from one pot in the same line is a statement that no amount of theology can
soften.

## Nine Successors

Nanak did not leave the community to his sons. He chose a disciple, Angad, and
that pattern of chosen succession held through ten Gurus across roughly two
centuries. Each added something structural.

Angad refined and propagated Gurmukhi, the script in which Punjabi and the
scripture are written, cutting the community loose from Sanskrit and Persian
learned traditions. Amar Das built out administration, appointing local
preaching centers, insisting that anyone seeking an audience with him eat in the
langar first, and pressing against widow-burning and the seclusion of women. Ram
Das founded the town that became Amritsar. His son Arjan built at its center a
temple approached by a causeway across a tank of water, with entrances on all
four sides — the Harmandir Sahib, the building later plated in gold and known
abroad as the Golden Temple.

Arjan's larger achievement was editorial. Around the turn of the seventeenth
century he collected the hymns of his predecessors, added his own, and fixed
them in a single authorized volume to prevent spurious compositions from
circulating. Crucially, he included verse by non-Sikhs: Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev,
the Sufi Sheikh Farid, and others, Hindu and Muslim, high caste and low. A
scripture that opens its own pages to a Muslim mystic and a leatherworker is
making a claim about where truth is found.

Arjan was also the first Guru to be executed. Jahangir's court found him
politically inconvenient, and he died in Mughal custody in 1606. His son
Hargobind responded by taking up arms, wearing two swords for spiritual and
temporal authority, and building the Akal Takht facing the temple across the
tank — a seat of worldly decision-making set opposite a house of worship. The
community was now something a state had to reckon with.

The ninth Guru, Tegh Bahadur, was executed in Delhi in 1675 under Aurangzeb,
according to Sikh tradition after intervening on behalf of Kashmiri Brahmins
facing forced conversion — dying, that is, for the religious freedom of people
who were not Sikhs.

## The Khalsa

His son Gobind Singh was nine when that happened. In 1699, at Anandpur, he
assembled the community and asked for a head. One man stepped forward and was
taken into a tent; the Guru emerged with a bloodied sword and asked again. Five
came forward in all. All five walked out alive, in new clothes. The tradition
has always understood the theatrical cruelty of the test as the point: he was
finding out who would die without knowing why.

Those five, the Panj Pyare, were initiated with sweetened water stirred with a
double-edged sword, and then — in the gesture that defines the relationship —
the Guru asked them to initiate him. The Khalsa, the collective body they
formed, took a common discipline and common names: Singh, lion, for men, Kaur,
princess, for women, replacing the surnames that broadcast caste. Initiated
Sikhs keep five articles of faith, each beginning in Punjabi with the letter k:
uncut hair, usually bound under a turban; a small comb; a steel bracelet; a
particular undergarment; and a kirpan, a sword or dagger carried as a commitment
to defend the defenseless. They abstain from tobacco, from intoxicants, and from
meat slaughtered by ritual methods.

Gobind Singh fought the Mughal empire and lost his four sons to it. When he died
in 1708 he ended the line of human Gurus. Authority passed to the scripture
itself, thereafter the Guru Granth Sahib, and to the collective body of the
initiated. There would be no more living masters, no succession disputes, no
charismatic claimants — and, in principle, no priesthood, since any competent
Sikh can read the book aloud and lead worship.

## A Book Treated as a Living Teacher

The Guru Granth Sahib is arranged not by author or topic but by musical mode.
Its hymns are sorted into some thirty-one ragas, each with an emotional
character, because the text was composed to be sung rather than studied. Kirtan
— the singing of these hymns, usually with harmonium and tabla — is the central
act of Sikh worship, and a gurdwara's day is structured around it.

The physical book is treated exactly as a living Guru would be. It rests on a
raised platform under a canopy. An attendant waves a whisk over it. At night it
is carried to a resting room and put to bed; in the morning it is brought back
and opened, and the first hymn the page falls to is read out as the day's word
of guidance. Visitors bow before it. Nobody bows to a person.

That arrangement produced a community with unusual durability and unusual
difficulty in generating orthodoxy. Sikhs in the eighteenth century survived
periods of severe persecution in the Punjab hills and forests, then built a
sovereign state under Ranjit Singh that lasted forty years before the British
annexed it in 1849. Reform movements in the late nineteenth century worked to
sharpen a distinct Sikh identity against absorption into Hinduism and against
Christian missions; in the 1920s an agitation to wrest control of the gurdwaras
from hereditary custodians succeeded, at considerable cost, and placed them
under elected management.

Partition in 1947 cut the Punjab in half and cost the community its heartland
around Nankana Sahib and Kartarpur, both of which ended up in Pakistan; the
migration and the killing that accompanied it are still the defining trauma of
recent Sikh memory, closely followed by the violence of the 1980s. Sikhs have
since become one of the more widely distributed religious communities in the
world, with substantial populations in Britain, Canada, the United States,
Southeast Asia and East Africa, and gurdwaras in all of them running kitchens
that feed anyone who comes.

The three-part formula from Kartarpur has proved portable. Remember the name;
work honestly; share the earnings. During floods, earthquakes and lockdowns,
Sikh volunteer organizations have turned up to cook at scale with a competence
that surprises people encountering the tradition for the first time, and which
is really just the langar doing what it was built to do — feeding strangers
without asking who they are.
