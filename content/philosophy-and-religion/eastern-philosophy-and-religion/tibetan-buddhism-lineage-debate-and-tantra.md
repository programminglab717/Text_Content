# Tibetan Buddhism: Lineage, Debate, and Tantra

Late afternoon in the courtyard of a re-established Gelug monastery in
Karnataka, a few hundred monks are shouting at each other. They are paired off,
one standing and one seated on the gravel. The standing monk poses a question,
then drives his right hand down into his left palm with a crack audible across
the yard, and the seated monk must answer in one of a small number of permitted
ways: I accept, the reason is not established, there is no pervasion. Nothing is
improvised. The exchange is running along a formal syllogistic track, in a
technical Tibetan that a fluent speaker off the street would not follow, and it
may go on for hours.

This is not a performance for visitors. Debate is how the curriculum is
examined, and a monk may spend fifteen or twenty years in this courtyard before
sitting for a degree. It is also the single best entry point into what Tibetan
Buddhism actually is, because the courtyard sits at the intersection of the two
things that define the tradition: an inherited Indian scholastic system,
preserved with extraordinary care, and a set of esoteric practices that the
scholarship is meant to prepare you for.

## Imported Twice

Buddhism arrived in Tibet in the seventh century, under an expansionist empire
whose kings found a literate religion politically useful. The decisive period
came in the eighth, when King Trisong Detsen invited the Indian scholar
Shantarakshita and, according to tradition, the tantric adept Padmasambhava,
whose subjugation of local spirits made construction of the first monastery at
Samye possible. Those local spirits were not dismissed. They were bound by oath
and given jobs as protectors, which is why Tibetan Buddhism carries an entire
population of mountain deities and territorial guardians that have nothing to do
with Indian Buddhism and everything to do with the landscape.

Tradition also places at Samye a debate between an Indian master and a Chinese
Chan teacher over whether awakening is sudden or gradual. The Indian side is
said to have won, and Tibet consequently committed to the gradual, analytic
Indian approach rather than the Chinese one. Historians treat the account as
tidier than the events, but the self-understanding it encodes — that Tibet chose
India — governed everything that followed.

Then the empire collapsed. A ninth-century king remembered as a persecutor was
assassinated, central authority disintegrated, and organized monastic Buddhism
largely disappeared from central Tibet for over a century. What came next is
called the later diffusion: a renewed import of texts and teachers beginning in
the late tenth century, funded by western Tibetan kings, culminating in the
arrival of the Bengali master Atisha in 1042. Every school now existing traces
itself either to the first wave or the second.

## The Translation Project

The most consequential thing Tibetans did with Buddhism was translate it, and
they did it with a rigor that has no real parallel. Royal commissions
standardized vocabulary, issuing lexicons that fixed the Tibetan rendering of
each Sanskrit technical term so that translators working in different places
produced compatible texts. Translation teams paired an Indian scholar with a
Tibetan one. The results are so consistently literal — word order, compound
structure, even particles mapped predictably — that scholars routinely use the
Tibetan to reconstruct Sanskrit originals that have been lost.

What they built is two collections. The Kangyur holds texts attributed to the
Buddha; the Tengyur holds the Indian commentarial literature, a vast library of
philosophy, logic, epistemology, medicine and poetics. When Indian Buddhism was
extinguished in its homeland, most of it survived in Tibetan, and a considerable
amount survives nowhere else.

## Four Schools and a Shared Syllabus

The Nyingma, "ancient ones," trace themselves to the first diffusion and to
Padmasambhava. They are the least centralized, have historically included many
non-celibate householder lamas, and possess a distinctive mechanism for renewal:
terma, treasure texts said to have been concealed by Padmasambhava and recovered
centuries later by discoverers born to find them. Some are physical objects
retrieved from rock or lake; others are revealed in the discoverer's mind. The
best-known abroad is the funerary manual read aloud to the dying and the dead,
usually titled in English as the Tibetan Book of the Dead.

The Kagyu descend from the translator Marpa, who made the trip to India
repeatedly, and from his difficult student Milarepa, a murderer by black magic
in his youth who was made to build and demolish a stone tower again and again
before receiving any teaching, and who became Tibet's most beloved poet-hermit.
The school specializes in mahamudra and in yogic techniques transmitted from
Indian adepts.

The Sakya grew from a single aristocratic family and produced formidable
scholars, one of whom became ruler of Tibet under Mongol overlordship in the
thirteenth century — the first of several arrangements in which a religious
lineage held temporal power backed by an outside patron.

The Gelug is the youngest, founded on the reforms of Tsongkhapa, who died in
1419 after establishing Ganden monastery and a program emphasizing strict
monastic discipline, exhaustive philosophical training, and a particular reading
of Madhyamaka emptiness. Its great monasteries near Lhasa grew to house
thousands of monks each and functioned as universities.

They differ in emphasis, lineage and philosophical detail, but they share a
syllabus inherited from the Indian monastic universities: logic and
epistemology, the perfection-of-wisdom literature with its maps of the
bodhisattva path, Madhyamaka, systematic psychology, and monastic discipline.
The Gelug geshe degree and its equivalents elsewhere certify mastery of these.
Bon, often described as Tibet's pre-Buddhist religion, is better understood now
as a parallel tradition that developed alongside Buddhism, sharing much of its
structure and vocabulary while claiming a different founder and a different
line of transmission.

## Tantra, and Why the Scholarship Comes First

The Vajrayana methods that make Tibetan Buddhism visually distinctive — wrathful
deities with skull garlands, mandalas, mantras, sexual iconography — are not an
alternative to the philosophy. They are held to be a faster vehicle to the same
goal, and dangerous in proportion to their speed, which is the stated reason for
requiring years of preparation and personal transmission from a teacher.

Entry requires empowerment, a ritual in which a qualified lama authorizes the
student to practice a particular deity's cycle. After that the practitioner
undertakes preliminaries: one hundred thousand full-body prostrations, one
hundred thousand recitations of a purification mantra, one hundred thousand
symbolic offerings of the universe, repeated until they are finished. Then comes
the practice proper. In the generation stage the practitioner constructs, in
imagination and in exhaustive detail, a deity and its palace, and then dissolves
ordinary self-identification into that figure — the logic being that awakened
qualities are cultivated by rehearsing them rather than by waiting for them. The
completion stage works with subtle physiology: channels, winds and drops
manipulated through breath and concentration.

The deities are explicitly not gods. A wrathful form with fangs and a necklace
of heads represents the destruction of obstacles and is understood as empty of
inherent existence like everything else — a point the philosophical training is
there to ensure the practitioner does not forget. The sand mandalas that tourists
photograph make the same argument physically. Monks spend days laying colored
sand in patterns of great precision, and then sweep it into a pile and pour it
into a river.

The standard intensive is a retreat of a little over three years, undertaken in
a sealed retreat house, with a fixed daily schedule of sessions.

## Recognized Children

The institution outsiders find strangest is the tulku system. When a major
teacher dies, the search begins for the child in whom the lineage continues,
identified through divination, dreams, oracles, directional indications left in
the predecessor's writings, and tests in which the candidate picks out objects
belonging to the previous incarnation. The practice appears to have begun with
the Karmapa line in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries and spread widely.

Its function was practical. A monastery with property, texts and students needs
succession, and hereditary transmission was unavailable to a celibate
establishment. Recognized reincarnation supplied continuity of authority without
inheritance — and, inevitably, became a prize worth manipulating. The Dalai Lama
line acquired its title from a Mongol ruler in the sixteenth century and became
Tibet's governing institution under the fifth holder, who unified the country
with Mongol military backing in the seventeenth century and began the Potala.
Long minorities between a Dalai Lama's death and his successor's majority left
decades of rule to regents, with predictable results.

After 1959, when the fourteenth Dalai Lama left for India following a failed
uprising, the tradition reconstituted itself in exile. Refugee monks rebuilt the
great Lhasa monasteries in South India, on land granted by the Indian
government, and the debate courtyards filled again. Texts were printed,
curricula restored, and a tradition that had been almost entirely
Tibetan-speaking began teaching in English, Chinese, Spanish and Russian. The
current Dalai Lama has spent decades in conversation with neuroscientists and
physicists, and has said repeatedly that where Buddhist claims about the
physical world are contradicted by evidence, the claims should go — which is an
unusual position for the head of a lineage whose authority rests on transmission
and which not everyone in that lineage shares.
