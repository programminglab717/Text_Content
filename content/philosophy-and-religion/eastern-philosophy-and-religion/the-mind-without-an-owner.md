# The Mind Without an Owner

A Greek king and a Buddhist monk are arguing about a chariot. The king, called
Milinda in the Pali text that records the exchange, has just been told that the
monk Nagasena is not, strictly speaking, anyone. So Nagasena asks how the king
arrived. By chariot. Is the axle the chariot? No. The wheels? The frame, the
yoke, the reins, the goad? No, no, no. Is the chariot something other than all
of these assembled? No. Then, Nagasena says, there is no chariot — and the king
is forced to concede that "chariot" is a designation, a convenient term applied
to parts in a working arrangement, not a further thing sitting behind them.

That is the whole of the Buddhist analysis of mind in miniature, and everything
that follows over the next fifteen centuries is an attempt to make it work in
detail. The conclusion is not that nothing exists. It is that the referent of
"I" is not an owner of experiences but a pattern in them, and the philosophical
labor consists in explaining how a pattern can remember, intend, accumulate
consequences, and eventually stop.

## The Five Heaps

The standard decomposition breaks a person into five aggregates, literally
heaps. Form covers the body and the sense faculties. Feeling is not emotion but
something more primitive: the pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral tone that
accompanies every moment of experience. Perception is the recognizing function,
the one that sorts a patch of color into "blue" or a sound into "my name."
Formations is the widest and messiest category, holding volitions, habits,
attitudes, and dispositions — everything that inclines a mind one way rather
than another. Consciousness is the bare awareness of an object through one of
six channels, the sixth being the mind itself, which in this scheme is a sense
organ that takes thoughts as its objects the way the eye takes shapes.

The list is not a taxonomy for its own sake. Feeling in particular is treated
as the hinge. Contact produces a feeling tone before any deliberation occurs,
and craving fastens onto that tone almost instantly, reaching for more of the
pleasant and away from the unpleasant. The chain from there to suffering runs
through clinging, becoming, and birth, in the twelvefold sequence of dependent
origination that the tradition uses to describe how a life propagates itself.
Each link conditions the next without any of them being an agent. The point of
identifying feeling as the weak joint is that it is the last place where
intervention is still cheap. Attention trained finely enough can catch the tone
before the reaching starts.

## Momentariness and the Bookkeeping Problem

The Abhidharma literature pushed the decomposition much further, cataloguing
the ultimate constituents of experience — dharmas, in this usage meaning
something like irreducible event-types rather than teachings — and arguing
about which ones are real and how they combine. Different schools produced
different lists, running to seventy-five items in one influential compendium
and considerably more in others.

One shared commitment created enormous trouble: momentariness. If everything
composite arises and passes away, and if impermanence is not a slow fraying but
a property of each instant, then a dharma lasts only a moment and is replaced.
The mind becomes a rapid succession of discrete mental events, each conditioned
by the one before.

This solves the self problem and immediately creates a bookkeeping problem. How
does a deed committed now bear fruit decades later, or in another life, if
nothing persists to carry the debt? How is a memory laid down in one moment and
retrieved in one that has no direct contact with it? How does a mind absorbed
in a deep meditative state, in which coarse mental activity has ceased
altogether, resume being that person's mind rather than starting from nothing?

The proposals multiplied. One school held that past and future dharmas exist in
some attenuated way, so the causal connection has something to connect to,
which earned them the name "the ones who say everything exists" and a great
deal of criticism. A rival camp proposed seeds: a deed leaves a trace in the
mental stream that matures under the right conditions, the way a seed carries
an oak without containing a small oak. The Theravada developed an idea of a
resting-state consciousness that runs beneath active cognition, keeps a life
continuous through dreamless sleep, and supplies the substrate that active
mental processes interrupt.

## The Storehouse

Yogacara, worked out by Asanga and Vasubandhu and taking its name from the
practice of yoga, gave the seed metaphor an address. Beneath the six ordinary
consciousnesses it posited a storehouse consciousness: a subliminal, constantly
flowing stream that receives the impressions left by every act of body, speech,
and mind, and from which future experience ripens. It is not a soul. It changes
every moment, is entirely conditioned, and is itself a candidate for
elimination — awakening is described as a turning-about at the base, in which
the storehouse stops functioning as a storehouse and becomes a mirror-like
knowing.

Between the storehouse and ordinary awareness the school inserted a seventh
consciousness, a chronically afflicted faculty whose entire job is to take the
storehouse as an object and mistake it for a self. This is an unusually precise
piece of theorizing. It says the sense of being someone is not an illusion
floating free but a specific, identifiable cognitive operation, running
continuously and below the threshold of notice, and it explains why the
conviction survives the argument. Being persuaded by Nagasena changes what you
believe. It does not, by itself, stop the machinery.

Yogacara is often labeled idealism, and its slogan invites it: what appears is
representation-only. Whether that means the external world is unreal or only
that we never get at objects except through cognitive construction is disputed
by the school's own commentators. The associated doctrine of three natures
points toward the second reading. There is the way things appear when overlaid
with conceptual imputation, the way they actually occur as dependent events,
and what remains when the first is subtracted from the second. Nobody is
claiming that a wall can be walked through.

## Emptiness Applied to the Mind

Running against all of this was Nagarjuna, whose verses on the middle way
turned the Abhidharma's method against its results. The Abhidharma had analyzed
persons into dharmas because persons lack intrinsic nature. Nagarjuna asked
what the dharmas had that would exempt them from the same analysis. Examine any
of them — motion, fire, time, cause, perception — and you find it defined
entirely by relations to what it is not. Nothing has an essence of its own.
Emptiness is that lack, and it applies without remainder, including to itself:
emptiness is not a substrate that the illusions are painted on, and taking it
for one is described as grasping a snake by the wrong end.

For the mind this is bracing. Consciousness is empty. The stream of moments is
empty. Awakening is empty. The two-truths framework keeps this from collapsing
into nihilism — conventionally there are minds, memories, people, and monks
arguing with kings, and none of that is cancelled by the absence of ultimate
findable essences. The conventional is not a lie. It is what remains true when
you stop asking it to be more solid than it is.

## The Light That Sees Itself

The last great phase of the debate was epistemological. Dignaga, and Dharmakirti
after him, restricted valid knowledge to two sources: perception, which is bare,
non-conceptual, and contacts particulars, and inference, which is conceptual and
never contacts anything. Universals are not found in the world; the word "cow"
works by exclusion, carving off everything not-cow, which lets language function
without populating reality with cowness.

Out of this came the sharpest question in the whole tradition. Every cognition,
Dignaga argued, is aware of itself as well as its object — reflexive awareness,
the lamp that illuminates the room and is not itself in darkness. Otherwise how
could you later remember not only the blue patch but the seeing of it? The
argument is elegant and the Madhyamikas hated it. Candrakirti replied that the
memory of seeing blue is explained by the arising of blue-cognition and needs no
extra self-directed function, and that a knife does not cut itself, a fingertip
does not touch itself, and awareness has no more business being its own object.
The exchange continued for centuries and was never resolved, which may be the
correct outcome, since versions of it are still running.

None of these people were doing cognitive science, and the resemblance to bundle
theories of the self is real but shallow enough to mislead. The aim throughout
was practical. The five heaps were not proposed as a model to be confirmed but
as an object for sustained attention, something to be verified by watching the
mind closely enough, for long enough, to see the assembly happening. What the
philosophers supplied was consistency: a way of saying what the meditators
reported that did not smuggle back in the owner they had failed to find.
