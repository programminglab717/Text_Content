# The Shape of Zen Practice

The first thing that happens is a sound. Outside the hall, in the dark, someone
begins striking a suspended wooden board with a mallet — three rolls, each
starting fast and slowing to widely spaced single blows, the last one landing
after a pause long enough that you think the pattern has ended. The board is
cracked and has a date carved into it. By the time the third roll finishes,
everyone is supposed to be seated and still.

Zen has a reputation for spontaneity, iconoclasm, and burning statues for
firewood. Anyone who has actually spent a week in a training hall knows it is
one of the most heavily choreographed activities in religious life. Where you
put your shoes, which foot enters the room first, how you hold the bowls, when
you may cough — all specified. The paradox is real but not deep. The forms
exist so that attention has nothing left to spend itself on.

## Sitting

The posture is the practice; everything else is scaffolding. You sit on a firm
round cushion placed on a square mat, arranged so the hips are lifted above the
knees and the spine can stack without muscular effort. The classic arrangement
is full lotus, each foot on the opposite thigh, which is stable and available
to almost nobody past adolescence. Half lotus, the Burmese position with both
legs folded flat and uncrossed, kneeling astride a low bench, and an ordinary
chair all work. Teachers who insist otherwise are usually confusing endurance
with depth.

What matters is a tripod — two knees and the base of the spine — and the
vertical. The lower back keeps its natural curve, the chin draws in slightly so
the back of the neck lengthens, the shoulders drop. Hands rest in the lap in an
oval, left over right, thumb tips just touching, forearms lightly against the
belly. The thumbs are a gauge: they flatten when you drift off and press
together when you strain, and you can read your own state from them without
thinking about it. The eyes stay open, lowered maybe forty-five degrees toward
the floor or a blank wall, unfocused. Closed eyes invite dreaming.

Then a bell sounds three times and nothing happens for thirty or forty minutes.

The honest account of what follows, for most people for a long time, is
discomfort and tedium. The knees complain. A leg goes numb and you have to
decide whether numbness is damage. Around twenty minutes the mind, deprived of
input, starts generating it — old arguments, song fragments, inventories of
things to do. Some people get visions, lights, the floor tilting, a sense of
the body enlarging or vanishing. The tradition has a word for these and
translates roughly to "diabolical phenomena," and the instruction about them is
uniform across every lineage: ignore them, they are a byproduct of an unusually
quiet nervous system, and treating them as attainments is the standard way to
waste several years.

## What You Do With Attention

Beginners are usually given the breath and told to count. Exhalation one,
exhalation two, up to ten, then start again, and when you notice you are at
forty-six or have been reconstructing a conversation from last Tuesday, return
to one without editorializing. It is simple and nearly impossible, and the
returning is the whole exercise rather than an interruption of it. After some
months the counting is dropped and the breath is merely followed.

From there the traditions diverge. The Soto approach is objectless sitting,
often rendered as "just sitting" — no technique, no focal point, awareness open
and unmanipulated, not suppressing thought and not pursuing it. Dogen, who
brought the lineage from China to Japan in the thirteenth century, insisted
this was not a method for producing awakening later but the activity of
awakening now, and he was fierce about the distinction. Sitting is not the
staircase. It is the room.

The Rinzai approach hands you a koan. The most common first assignment is the
one where a monk asks whether a dog has buddha-nature and the master answers
with a syllable usually left untranslated, meaning something like "no" or
"without." You are not asked to interpret it. You are asked to hold it
continuously, to let it occupy the place where your commentary usually runs,
until the ordinary discursive approach exhausts itself against something it
cannot get purchase on. Hakuin, who rebuilt Japanese Rinzai in the eighteenth
century after a long decline, organized hundreds of these into a graded
curriculum and contributed the most famous one himself, about the sound of a
single hand.

Koan work is tested. Several times a day, at a signal, practitioners line up to
enter the teacher's room one at a time — bells, bows, a fixed exchange — and
present their response. Presenting is not explaining; an answer that arrives as
a clever sentence is usually rung out in under five seconds. Practitioners
describe these interviews with a mixture of dread and affection that is
recognizable to anyone who has had a demanding music teacher.

## The Rest of the Day

Between sitting periods everyone rises and does walking meditation, moving
slowly around the perimeter of the hall in a line, hands held at the sternum,
half a step to each breath. This is functional — it restores circulation to
legs that have stopped reporting in — and it is also where the continuity gets
tested, since attention that survives stillness often evaporates the moment the
body moves.

Meals in a formal retreat are taken in the hall from a nested set of bowls
wrapped in cloth. The unwrapping, the order of serving, the gesture that asks
for more or less, the washing of the bowls at the table with hot water and a
strip of cloth, the drinking of the rinse water so that nothing is wasted — all
of it is prescribed, and the first several times you do it badly and loudly.
There is also work practice: kitchen shifts, cleaning, weeding, splitting wood.
The Chinese master credited with writing the first monastic rules for the
school is remembered for a line about work that his community used against him
when they hid his tools to make him rest, whereupon he refused to eat.

A multi-day intensive retreat compresses all of this. Rising around four,
sitting a dozen or more periods, silence throughout, sleeping little, for five
or seven days. Somewhere around the third day most people hit a wall and then
pass through it into a state that is not pleasant exactly but is unusually
clear. If a monitor sees a sitter drooping, the practitioner may be struck
firmly across the shoulder muscles with a flat wooden stick — by request in
most contemporary halls, preceded and followed by bows, and closer to deep
massage than punishment, though it makes a considerable noise.

## Where the Forms Came From

The school tells its own history as a chain of mind-to-mind transmissions
beginning with a flower held up wordlessly by the Buddha, arriving in China
with a blue-eyed monk who sat facing a wall for nine years, and passing to an
illiterate woodcutter in the south who overheard a line of scripture and
understood it whole. Historians treat most of this as retrospective
construction. The story of the woodcutter, in particular, comes to us through a
text promoted by partisans in a factional dispute about whether awakening comes
suddenly or by degrees, and the "gradual" party it caricatures may not have
held the position attributed to them.

What is clear is that between the eighth and eleventh centuries Chinese masters
developed a distinctive teaching style built on abrupt exchanges, and that by
the Song these encounters had been collected, arranged, and fitted with
commentary and verse into the two anthologies that anchor koan practice to this
day. The freewheeling era and the systematizing era are not the same era, and
the spontaneity we admire reaches us entirely through the editors.

Dogen belongs to the systematizing side. He returned from China convinced that
the practice was being sold short, and wrote both a plain set of instructions
for sitting and an enormous, difficult, recursive body of essays that readers
are still working through. The most quoted lines run that to study the way is
to study the self, to study the self is to forget the self, and to forget the
self is to be verified by the ten thousand things. That is as close to a
statement of purpose as the tradition allows itself.

The twentieth century carried all of this west, mostly through Japanese
teachers and enthusiastic Western students, and in the transfer it changed
shape: lay rather than monastic, mixed across lineages that had been separate
for centuries, dropped into a culture inclined to read it as a technique for
stress. It also arrived with baggage that took decades to face honestly —
institutional complicity with Japanese militarism, and later a run of
authority-abuse failures in Western centers that forced a hard reckoning with
what transmission actually certifies. It certifies less than people assumed. A
person can sit for forty years and see clearly into the nature of mind and
still be a liar, and the tradition is only recently willing to say so plainly.
