# How Daoism Became a Religion

In the early 1990s archaeologists opened a looted tomb near the village of
Guodian, in Hubei, and recovered several hundred bamboo slips that had survived
because the chamber had stayed waterlogged. The burial dates to somewhere
around the end of the fourth century BCE. Among the slips were three separate
bundles of material that corresponds to roughly a third of what we now call the
Daodejing — the same lines, more or less, but in a different order, mixed with
other texts, and with no sign that anyone considered them a single book by a
single author.

Two decades earlier, silk manuscripts from a tomb at Mawangdui had already
shown a version of the text with its two halves reversed, the *de* section
placed before the *dao* section. Put the finds together and a familiar picture
dissolves. There was no sage who wrote eighty-one chapters and handed them over
at a mountain pass. There was a pool of aphorisms, rhymed proverbs, and
political advice that circulated among teachers and was gradually assembled,
sorted, and attributed. The attribution went to Laozi, "the Old Master," a name
that is less a name than a category.

Sima Qian, writing the first real biography of him a couple of centuries later,
could not settle the matter either. He offers three candidates, notes that some
say Laozi lived to a hundred and sixty and others considerably longer, and ends
by admitting nobody knows. He tells the story anyway: the archivist, disgusted
with the decay of the state, riding west; the guardian of the pass who refused
to let him through until he wrote something down; the five thousand characters
produced on the spot; and then the man vanishing, nobody knows where.

## The Politics of Not Doing

Whatever its origins, the resulting text is strange, compressed, and far more
practical than its reputation suggests. A large share of its chapters are
addressed to a ruler. They advise him to tax lightly, to avoid grand projects,
to stop meddling, to leave the people uneducated in the specific sense of
leaving them uninflamed by ambition and novelty. Sharpen a blade too far, it
says, and you cannot preserve the edge. The more laws and prohibitions there
are, the more thieves.

The organizing idea is *wu wei*, ordinarily translated as non-action, though
non-interference or effortless action both come closer. It does not counsel
inertia. It counsels dropping the part of any activity that consists of forcing
— the grasping, the straining, the imposition of a plan on material that has
its own grain. Water is the recurring image, because water takes the low
ground everyone else avoids, yields to every obstacle, and wears through stone.
The uncarved block is another: raw timber that holds every possible form
because it has not yet been committed to one.

Behind this sits a claim about how things move. Reversal is the motion of the
Way. Whatever reaches an extreme turns back toward its opposite; the hard
breaks, the soft endures; the full spills. And the Way itself, the text insists
in its opening lines, cannot be named. A named dao is not the constant dao.
This is not mystification for its own sake. It is a warning that the
distinctions language draws — beautiful and ugly, useful and useless, high and
low — are made by us and then mistaken for seams in the world.

## Zhuangzi's Different Weather

The other foundational text has an entirely different temperature. The Zhuangzi
is funny, digressive, and full of characters: butchers, cripples, cicadas,
robbers, talking trees, and a great many dead people who turn out to be
perfectly happy about it. Only its first seven chapters are generally thought
to come from the man himself, and the rest preserve a school of readers
arguing with him and with each other.

Its best-known moments do philosophical work under cover of anecdote. A cook
carves an ox for nineteen years without sharpening his blade, because he has
stopped cutting and started finding the spaces that were already there. A man
dreams he is a butterfly and wakes uncertain which of the two is dreaming the
other. A gnarled tree survives to enormous age precisely because its wood is
worthless to carpenters, which the text offers as a lesson about the usefulness
of being useless. Crossing a bridge, Zhuangzi remarks on the happiness of the
fish below; his friend the logician Huizi objects that he is not a fish and
cannot know; Zhuangzi replies that Huizi is not Zhuangzi and cannot know what
Zhuangzi knows, and the argument spirals off into a joke about the word "how."

What runs through all of it is a distrust of fixed standpoints. Every creature
judges by its own scale — the small bird laughs at the enormous one, the summer
insect cannot be told about ice. Human categories are one more local scale,
useful within limits and disastrous when taken for cosmic law. When Zhuangzi's
wife died, a friend found him sitting on the ground drumming on a tub and
singing, and reported himself shocked. Zhuangzi said he had wept at first, and
then thought about where she had come from, and stopped.

## Revelation at Crane Cry Mountain

For several centuries this remained literature and conversation. Then, in the
middle of the second century CE, a man named Zhang Daoling, living on a
mountain in Sichuan, reported that the deified Laozi had descended to him and
appointed him Celestial Master on earth. Whatever happened on that mountain
produced an institution.

The Way of the Celestial Masters was an organized church with territory,
officers, doctrine, and dues — members contributed five pecks of rice, which
gave the movement its other name. It divided its region into parishes under
libationers who registered households, kept records, and performed rites.
Illness was understood as the consequence of transgression, so healing meant
confession: the sick wrote out their offenses and the documents were submitted
to the officials of Heaven, Earth, and Water. There were communal feasts, rules
against alcohol, and free lodges stocked with rice and meat where travelers
took what they needed on the understanding that taking too much would bring
sickness.

Around the same period, in the east, a related movement rose in revolt under
the banner of the Yellow Turbans, was crushed, and contributed to the collapse
of the Han dynasty. The Celestial Masters took a different route: their
successor state in the west eventually submitted to Cao Cao, was resettled
across the north, and survived. Its priesthood has continued, with various
ruptures, ever since. Liturgical Daoism starts here, not in the Daodejing, and
its concerns — registers, petitions, talismans, exorcism, the ranking of
deities and the proper paperwork of the invisible world — have only a loose
relationship to anything in Zhuangzi.

## The Body as a Landscape

Later centuries produced further revelations. In the fourth century a medium in
the south received scriptures from perfected beings of the heaven of Highest
Clarity, describing a visionary practice of ascent and union with astral
deities; the Lingbao corpus that followed borrowed Buddhist cosmology and
ritual form wholesale and built a public liturgy around them. The resulting
library eventually became the Daoist canon, a collection of well over a
thousand titles that no single tradition has ever read in full.

Running alongside all of this was the pursuit of physical transformation. The
outer alchemists worked with furnaces and crucibles, refining cinnabar and
other minerals into elixirs meant to confer immortality. The chemistry was
sophisticated and the pharmacology fatal; cinnabar is mercury sulfide, and more
than one emperor died of the cure. By the Tang and Song the emphasis had
shifted inward. Inner alchemy kept the entire vocabulary of furnace, crucible,
lead and mercury, and reassigned every term to something in the body. The
practitioner became the laboratory. Breath, essence, and spirit were refined
and circulated through channels, gathered in three fields below the navel, at
the heart, and in the head, and combined into an embryo of immortality
gestated over years of sitting.

The last great institutional development came in the twelfth century, when a
teacher named Wang Chongyang founded the Complete Perfection order in the
north. It was monastic on a Buddhist model — celibate, communal, vegetarian,
organized around meditation and discipline rather than hereditary priesthood —
and it and the Celestial Masters lineage have been the two main streams ever
since, one monastic, one married and parish-based.

Western scholarship long insisted on splitting all this in half: a pure
philosophical Daoism of the early books, and a degenerate religious Daoism of
priests and elixirs. The distinction flatters the texts we happen to enjoy
reading. It also falls apart on contact with the sources, which quote the
Daodejing in their liturgies, treat Laozi as both author and god, and see
nothing incongruous in reading about the uncarved block on the way to a healing
rite. The people who built the tradition were not confused about which half
they belonged to. They did not think there were halves.
