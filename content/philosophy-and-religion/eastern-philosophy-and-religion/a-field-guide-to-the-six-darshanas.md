# A Field Guide to the Six Darshanas

Tradition says that Shankara, travelling to win arguments, arrived at the house
of the ritualist Mandana Mishra and found parrots in the garden debating
whether the Veda has an author. The contest that followed was judged by
Mandana's wife, who was learned enough to keep score, and the loser was to take
up the winner's way of life. Whether any of that happened matters less than
what it shows about the setting. Classical Indian philosophy was conducted in
public, with rules of procedure, before patrons and audiences, and with
consequences. You were expected to state your opponent's position better than
he could before you attacked it, and a school that could not answer an
objection had to absorb it.

The result is a literature of argument rather than of treatises in isolation.
The six systems conventionally called the darshanas — "views," from a root
meaning to see — are best approached not as six separate philosophies but as
six long-running research programs that spent two thousand years cross-
examining each other and the outsiders.

## What "Orthodox" Actually Means

The dividing line is not belief in God. A system counts as astika, orthodox, if
it grants the Veda authority, and nastika if it does not. That classification
produces results a Western reader will find strange. Samkhya is orthodox and
argues that no creator God exists. Purva Mimamsa, the most scripturally
punctilious school of all, has no use for a deity who does anything. Meanwhile
Buddhism, Jainism, and the materialists of the Carvaka tradition are heterodox,
the last of these cheerfully so — they held that consciousness is a property of
the body the way intoxicating power is a property of fermented ingredients
none of which are individually intoxicating, and that the priests had invented
the afterlife as a livelihood.

The tidy list of six is itself a later convenience, assembled by doxographers
who liked pairing things. Nyaya goes with Vaisheshika, Samkhya with Yoga,
Mimamsa with Vedanta, each pair sharing enough metaphysics that one supplies
the framework and the other the method. The schools did not organize themselves
this way while they were alive, and important thinkers — the grammarians, the
Kashmiri Shaivas, the many bhakti theologians — fit the scheme badly or not at
all.

## Counting the Ways of Knowing

The most useful thing to know about any of these schools is how many sources of
valid knowledge it accepts, because almost every dispute eventually reduces to
that. The Carvakas allowed perception alone, which let them deny anything
unobserved but cost them the ability to justify inference, an embarrassment
their opponents worked hard. The Buddhist logicians allowed perception and
inference. Samkhya added reliable testimony, which lets scripture count.

Nyaya allowed four, adding comparison — the process by which someone told that
a gavaya resembles a cow can identify one on sight. Mimamsa pushed to six,
adding postulation, which infers an unstated fact to make sense of an observed
one, and non-apprehension, on the reasoning that the absence of an elephant in
this room is something you genuinely know rather than merely fail to notice.
That last addition is doing serious work; a school built on the interpretation
of commands needs an account of how injunctions bind in the absence of
observable sanctions.

## Nyaya and Vaisheshika: The Logic and the Furniture

Nyaya is the school of reasoning, and its central contribution is a public
format for demonstration. The inference runs in five members: the thesis, that
the hill has fire; the reason, because it has smoke; the example, as in a
kitchen; the application, and this hill has smoke; the conclusion, therefore it
has fire. Two of these are redundant as logic and indispensable as rhetoric,
since the whole procedure is designed to be spoken aloud at an opponent.

The real technical work went into pervasion — the relation guaranteeing that
wherever there is smoke there is fire — and into the catalogue of ways an
inference fails. A later phase of the school, beginning around the fourteenth
century and generally called the new Nyaya, developed a regimented technical
Sanskrit for stating such relations with absolute precision, prose so dense
that it became the standard instrument for careful argument in every field,
including by philosophers who rejected Nyaya's conclusions.

Vaisheshika supplied the inventory of what there is. Everything falls into a
handful of categories: substances, qualities, motions, universals, ultimate
particularities, and the relation of inherence that binds a quality to the
thing it qualifies. Nine substances were recognized, including earth, water,
fire, and air, each composed of eternal indivisible atoms, plus ether, time,
space, selves, and mind. Atoms combine into pairs, and pairs into triads, the
triad being the smallest thing that can be seen — a mote in a sunbeam. The
selves in this system are numerous, eternal, and, oddly to modern ears,
unconscious in themselves; awareness is a quality that arises in a self when it
is joined with a mind and an object, which means a liberated self has no
experiences at all. Nyaya, unlike most of its neighbors, argued for God, on the
grounds that atoms do not assemble themselves and effects require an
intelligent maker.

## Samkhya and Yoga: Two Ingredients and a Discipline

Samkhya is the oldest of the systems in substance and the most economical. Two
kinds of thing exist. Purusha is pure consciousness: inactive, unchanging,
plural, a witness that does nothing. Prakriti is everything else, including
mind, intellect, and the sense of being an individual, all of which Samkhya
classes as material. Prakriti is a single dynamic substrate in which three
strands are woven — the luminous and light, the active and agitating, the heavy
and obscuring — and every object and mental state is a particular ratio of the
three. Effects pre-exist in their causes, the way oil is already in the seed;
nothing is created, only unfolded.

The unfolding is specified in detail, and the order is the reverse of what a
modern reader expects. Out of undifferentiated prakriti emerges intellect
first, then the sense of I-ness, and only from that do mind, the five capacities
of perception, the five of action, and five subtle essences appear — with the
gross elements of earth, water, fire, air, and ether arriving last. Matter does
not build up toward mind. Mind precipitates out toward matter, and the count of
twenty-five principles that results is the skeleton on which much later Indian
psychology was hung, including by schools that rejected the rest of Samkhya.

Bondage is a category error. Consciousness, which merely witnesses, is
misidentified with the intellect it illuminates, so that the witness seems to
act and suffer. Liberation is discrimination: seeing clearly that these are
two. The school's favorite images are domestic. Purusha and prakriti cooperate
like a lame man riding a blind man's shoulders, one seeing and the other
walking. And prakriti, once she has been truly seen, withdraws like a dancer
who has finished her performance and does not return for the audience of one
who has understood.

Yoga takes this metaphysics wholesale and adds technique. Patanjali's aphorisms
define the discipline in their second line as the stilling of the mind's
turnings, and lay out eight limbs that begin with ethical restraints and
observances, pass through posture and breath regulation and the withdrawal of
the senses, and end in the three internal stages of concentration, sustained
absorption, and the unified state in which the distinction between knower and
known drops away. Yoga also finds room for a God, though a modest one: a
special purusha untouched by the residues of action, offered as an object of
devotion and a useful shortcut rather than as a creator.

## Mimamsa and Vedanta: The Two Halves of Exegesis

Purva Mimamsa is the prior inquiry, into the ritual portion of the Veda, and it
is the most alien of the six to a modern reader. Its subject is dharma, defined
strictly as what the Veda enjoins — not what is good, not what conduces to
happiness, but what the injunctions command. The Veda itself is held to be
authorless and beginningless, not composed even by a deity, which removes the
possibility of error at the source; the relation between a word and its meaning
is likewise eternal rather than conventional. Since rituals plainly do not
produce their promised results on the spot, the school posited an unseen
potency generated by correct performance and maturing later. Mimamsa also holds
that cognitions are valid by default, carrying their own warrant unless
defeated, which is a strong and interesting epistemological position that its
two great commentators developed in rival directions.

Vedanta is the later inquiry, into the closing philosophical portions of the
same corpus, and it eventually swallowed the prestige of the whole field. Its
three authorities are the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita, and the aphorisms on
Brahman — a set of aphorisms so terse that every major interpreter could
produce a commentary supporting a different system, which is precisely what
happened.

Shankara's non-dualism holds that Brahman alone is real, without qualities or
internal distinction, and that the individual self is not similar to Brahman
but identical with it. The world of plurality is a superimposition, like the
snake seen in a coil of rope at dusk — not nothing, since it frightens you, but
not what is there. Ramanuja rejected the whole apparatus of illusion, itemizing
its difficulties in a famous sevenfold attack, and argued instead that souls
and matter are real and stand to Brahman as a body stands to the self that
animates it: genuinely distinct, never independent. Madhva went further and
made difference itself ultimate, enumerating five eternal distinctions that no
realization dissolves. All three claimed the same aphorisms, quoted the same
verses, and accused each other of reading badly.

For all that, the disagreements ride on a common chassis. Nearly every school
assumes that action leaves residues, that residues propel rebirth, that the
cycle is not desirable, and that release is available — Mimamsa being the
holdout, since its original aim was a heaven won by correct ritual rather than
escape from the round altogether, a position its later commentators quietly
revised. Nearly every school also agrees that what binds you is a mistake, some
species of ignorance, and that removing it is therefore a cognitive
achievement rather than a moral or a political one. Practically the entire
argument, across all six systems and the heterodox ones besides, is about what
exactly the mistake is: confusing the witness with the intellect, the self with
the aggregates, the rope with the snake, or the body with the soul that wears
it. They agreed you were asleep. They fought about the dream.
