# Karma and Rebirth as Concepts

The English word arrived sometime in the nineteenth century and has been
misused ever since. In ordinary speech karma means cosmic payback with a short
turnaround — someone cuts you off in traffic, gets pulled over a mile later, and
a passenger says the word with satisfaction. The satisfaction is the giveaway.
Almost none of the traditions that developed the idea treat it as a mechanism
for delivering deserved outcomes on a timescale you can watch, and several of
them would regard the pleasure taken in another person's misfortune as itself
the kind of mental act that keeps the machinery turning.

What karma originally named was much duller and much stranger: the fact that an
action performed correctly produces a result that does not appear at the time.

## From Ritual Act to Moral Law

In the oldest Vedic material, karman means the ritual act — the offering poured
into fire, the syllables chanted in the right order. The priests who thought
carefully about this faced a puzzle their opponents pressed hard. A sacrifice
performed for prosperity does not hand you prosperity when the fire dies. So
where does the efficacy go in the interval? The ritual theorists posited an
unseen potency generated by the act and stored until conditions ripen. They had
invented, for narrowly technical reasons, the idea of a deed that persists after
its performance and produces its effect later.

Rebirth appears to have come from somewhere else, possibly from non-Vedic
communities, and the two ideas were fused in the early Upanishads. There is a
much-discussed scene in which the sage Yajnavalkya, asked in an open assembly
what happens to a person after death, declines to answer publicly and takes the
questioner aside. What they agreed on privately, the text says, was karma: a man
becomes good by good action and bad by bad action. Elsewhere the Upanishads
describe two paths after death — one leading out of the cycle, one leading back
into a womb, the second determined by conduct, with the unpleasant destinations
described in unflattering detail.

The fusion produced a picture that most later Indian thought simply assumed.
Beings die and are reborn indefinitely, in many kinds of body and many kinds of
world. The conditions of each life are shaped by acts performed in earlier ones.
And this whole arrangement — samsara, the wandering — is not a consolation but
the problem. The point is not to secure a better next life. The point is to stop.

## What Exactly Travels

Grant that acts leave traces and that traces shape a later life. Two questions
follow immediately, and the major traditions can be sorted by how they answer
them: what is the trace made of, and what carries it across the gap?

The Brahminical answer is the simplest. An unchanging self, atman, is the
bearer. It is not the personality — not memories, not temperament, not the body
— but something underneath those, which picks up a new set of them each time,
the way one changes clothes. Liberation comes from recognizing what this self
actually is, which in Advaita Vedanta turns out to be not different from the
ground of everything. Later Vedantins subdivided the karmic stock usefully: the
total accumulated over all past lives, the portion already ripening and
determining the present body and circumstances, and what is being generated now.
The middle portion cannot be cancelled even by liberating knowledge. A liberated
sage still has a body, still gets ill, still dies. The arrow already loosed
completes its flight.

The Jain answer, as strange as it is consistent, makes karma literal matter —
fine particles that adhere to the soul and weigh it down, to be blocked and then
burned off by austerity.

The Buddhist answer is the hardest and the most interesting, because Buddhism
denies exactly the thing that would make the problem easy. There is no
unchanging self. There is a stream of physical and mental events, causally
connected, with nothing persisting underneath them. So what is reborn? The
classical reply, worked out at length in a dialogue between a monk named Nagasena
and a Greek-descended king, is: neither the same person nor a different one. A
flame is passed from one lamp to another; the second flame is not the first, but
it is not unrelated to it either, and it would not exist without it. Causal
continuity is all there is here, and all there is in a single lifetime too — the
adult is related to the infant on exactly the same terms.

Buddhism also relocated karma from the act to the intention behind it. This is a
genuine conceptual move, not a softening. A deed done carelessly, in error, or
without volition does not carry the same weight as one done with greed or
hatred. Karma on this reading is closer to the formation of character than to
the recording of debts: each willed act deepens a groove, dispositions harden,
and what one becomes is the cumulative result. The tradition's own image is
agricultural rather than judicial. Seeds are planted; they lie dormant; when
soil and water arrive, they sprout. Nobody is deciding what you deserve.

## The Fatalism Misreading

The most persistent misunderstanding runs in the opposite direction from the
traffic-light version. If present circumstances are the fruit of past deeds,
does anything remain to be done?

The early Buddhist texts treat this as a live and dangerous error and name its
proponents. One contemporary school held that everything unfolds by fixed
necessity, that effort changes nothing, and that beings are purified simply by
running out the clock through an enormous predetermined series of lives. The
Buddha rejected this flatly, and the rejection is structural rather than
rhetorical: a doctrine in which effort is pointless cannot support a path, and
the whole content of the teaching is a path. The Bhagavad Gita handles the
tension differently, arguing that you cannot avoid acting — inaction is itself a
choice with consequences — and that what binds is not the deed but the grasping
after its fruit. Act as the situation demands, release the outcome.

The harsher version of the fatalism problem is social. Karma has been used, in
practice and for a very long time, to explain low birth as deserved and to tell
the poor and the excluded that their position reflects their own prior conduct.
This is not a misreading imposed from outside; it appears in Indian sources, and
it has been one of the standard defenses of caste. The internal critiques are
old too, and they are blunt. The devotional poets whose verse Sikhs and others
preserved — weavers, leatherworkers, printers — wrote from precisely the
positions the doctrine was used to justify and rejected the justification
outright. In the twentieth century B. R. Ambedkar attacked the karmic defense of
caste as its ideological engine, and when he turned to Buddhism he insisted on a
reading of karma confined to this life and this world.

## Merit, Moved Around

Strict karmic individualism has a logical consequence that almost nobody has
been willing to live with: nothing you do can help anyone else's next life. In
practice every Buddhist culture developed methods for transferring merit.
Offerings are made and the good generated is formally dedicated to dead parents,
to all beings, to a specific person in difficulty. Chinese and Southeast Asian
festivals for the hungry dead are built on it. Mahayana Buddhism made the
principle central: a bodhisattva accumulates immense merit across countless
lives and directs it outward rather than cashing it in for release.

Philosophers noticed the tension and argued about it for centuries. Some treated
the transfer as a figure of speech — what actually benefits you is the generous
intention, which is your own karma after all. Others took it at face value. The
argument was never settled, which is itself informative. Traditions rarely abandon
a doctrine that does real work, and the work merit transfer does is to let
people act on behalf of those they love after death has removed every other
means of doing so.

## What the Idea Does

Strip away the metaphysics and something recognizable remains. Karma is a claim
that the moral universe is not arbitrary, that conduct accumulates, that the
person doing the acting is being shaped by the acting. Rebirth extends the
accounting period far enough that the obvious objection — that the cruel often
prosper and the decent often suffer — no longer refutes the claim.

That extension is doing real work and it is also where the idea becomes
unfalsifiable, a cost its defenders have generally been willing to pay. Greek
thinkers reached something similar from a different direction, with Pythagoras
and Empedocles teaching transmigration and Plato closing the Republic with a
vision of souls choosing their next lives and drinking from the river of
forgetfulness before descending. The detail there is worth keeping. The
forgetting is required. A system of consequences that stretched across lives
would be unbearable if you could remember, and useless as a spur if the
punishment were legible as punishment. Whatever else the doctrine is, it is not
designed to be verified from the inside.
