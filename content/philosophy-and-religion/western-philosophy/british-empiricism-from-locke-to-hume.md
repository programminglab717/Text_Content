# British Empiricism from Locke to Hume

Sometime around 1671, five or six friends met in John Locke's rooms in London
to argue about morality and revealed religion. They got stuck almost at once.
Locke's suggestion, as he later described it, was that before going any further
they ought to examine their own abilities and see what sorts of question the
human understanding was actually equipped to settle. He wrote up some notes on
a sheet of paper for the next meeting. The notes turned into the Essay
Concerning Human Understanding, which he published in 1689, roughly twenty
years after that evening.

That reversal of priority is the whole movement in miniature. Instead of
asking what the world is like, ask first what sort of instrument is doing the
asking, and what it can be relied upon to report. The three writers usually
lined up as British empiricism — Locke, then George Berkeley, then David Hume —
did not think of themselves as a school. One was English, one Irish, one Scot;
the later two read the earlier ones as opponents at least as much as allies,
and the tidy three-in-a-row arrangement is a later invention of textbooks and
of Kant's account of what he was answering. But there is a genuine thread, and
it is a thread that pulls tighter with each writer, until it breaks.

## Locke and the Blank Sheet

Book One of the Essay is a demolition of innate ideas. The doctrine Locke
attacks holds that certain principles are stamped on the mind at birth, and his
counter is empirical: if they were, everyone would assent to them, and not
everyone does. Children do not know that whatever is, is. Neither do people
with severe intellectual disabilities, nor, he insists with a traveller's
relish for examples, do whole societies whose moral convictions differ
radically from European ones. A principle that must be taught before it is
recognised is not innate; it is learned.

What is there instead is white paper, as he puts it, void of all characters.
All the furniture comes from two sources: sensation, which reports on external
objects, and reflection, which reports on the mind's own operations. These
supply simple ideas — yellow, bitter, hot, thinking, willing — which the mind
receives passively and cannot manufacture. A man born blind gets no idea of
scarlet however carefully it is described, and no one can invent a new simple
idea any more than he can destroy an existing one. What the mind does actively
is combine, compare, and abstract, building complex ideas out of the simple
stock.

Two moves in Book Two set up everything that follows. The first is the
distinction between primary and secondary qualities. Solidity, extension,
figure, motion, and number really belong to bodies and are inseparable from
them; a divided grain of wheat still has all of them. Colour, sound, taste,
smell, and warmth are not in bodies at all but are powers in bodies to produce
those sensations in us. Locke's illustration is the fire that produces warmth
at one distance and pain at another: nobody supposes the pain is in the fire,
so why suppose the warmth is?

The second move is his account of substance, and it is more embarrassed. When
we try to say what it is that holds a bundle of qualities together, we find we
have no idea of it whatever, only a supposition of something we know not what.
He tells the story of the man who said the world rested on an elephant, and
when asked what held up the elephant, said a tortoise, and when asked about the
tortoise, said something he knew not what. Locke is aware he has put himself in
the same position. He keeps material substance anyway, because he cannot see
how qualities could float free.

## Berkeley Removes the Furniture

George Berkeley read Locke as a young fellow at Trinity College Dublin and
concluded that Locke had stopped one step short of his own conclusion. If
colour and warmth are in the mind, why not extension and figure? Try to
conceive a shape with no colour at all and see how far you get. Berkeley
thought the primary and secondary qualities stand or fall together, and he was
willing to say which.

He also attacked the machinery underneath. Locke's abstraction requires that we
form an idea of a triangle which is neither equilateral nor isosceles nor
scalene but all and none of them at once, and Berkeley simply denies he can do
any such thing. What we actually do is let a particular idea stand in for all
the others of its kind. Take away abstraction and matter goes with it, because
matter is precisely an abstraction: a substance defined as having none of the
sensible qualities by which anything is ever encountered, and therefore
unperceivable by definition. Either the notion is incoherent or it is an idle
wheel that nothing turns.

What remains is his famous formula: to be is to be perceived. Berkeley insisted,
with some justice and no success, that he was the defender of common sense. He
was not denying that apples and stones exist or that stones hurt. He was
denying that they are anything other than orderly collections of ideas, and
pointing out that this is exactly how everyone treats them in practice. The
regularity of those collections, which we call the laws of nature, he explained
as the steady activity of God, who also keeps the tree in the quad in being when
nobody is looking at it. Samuel Johnson's response, kicking a large stone and
announcing that he refuted it thus, is remembered because it is funny rather
than because it lands: the resistance of the stone was never in dispute.

## Hume Follows It All the Way Down

David Hume took the method and dropped the consolations. His starting
distinction is between impressions, which are the lively original perceptions,
and ideas, which are their fainter copies. Every simple idea is derived from a
corresponding simple impression, which gives him a test with real teeth. When a
philosophical term is suspected of meaning nothing, ask from what impression it
is derived. If no impression can be produced, the term is empty noise.

He aims the test at causation. One billiard ball strikes another and the second
moves. Look for the impression of necessary connection and it is not there. What
is there is contiguity in space, succession in time, and the fact that balls
have always behaved this way before. The necessity that seems to sit in the
objects is a feature of the observer: after enough repetitions the mind slides
from one idea to the other, and it mistakes that felt transition for something
it has seen in the world.

The related argument about induction is the most durable thing in the
tradition. Any inference from observed cases to unobserved ones requires the
assumption that the future will resemble the past. That assumption cannot be
demonstrated, since there is no contradiction in supposing tomorrow to be
different. Nor can it be supported by experience, since every such support
would already be using the assumption it is meant to establish. The reasoning
we rely on for everything from bread nourishing us to bridges holding has no
rational foundation at all.

Turned inward, the same test dissolves the self. Hume goes looking for the
impression from which the idea of a continuing I might be copied, and he
reports that whenever he enters most intimately into what he calls himself, he
always stumbles on some particular perception — heat or cold, light or shade,
love or hatred — and never catches himself at any time without a perception.
The mind is a bundle of perceptions in perpetual flux, held together by
resemblance and causation rather than by any underlying owner. In an appendix to
the Treatise, with unusual candour, he admitted he could not make the account
work and did not know how to fix it.

None of this made Hume a sceptic in practice, and he was at pains to say so.
His conclusion is naturalistic rather than despairing: these beliefs are not
grounded in reason and do not need to be, because nature has not left something
so important to the uncertain operations of argument. Custom is the great guide
of human life. He notes that when he leaves his study, dines, plays backgammon,
and talks with his friends, the doubts appear so cold and strained that he
cannot find it in his heart to enter into them any further.

The Treatise, he said later, fell dead-born from the press, and he spent years
repackaging its arguments in shorter and livelier form. A generation on, one
reader in Königsberg found the section on causation impossible to answer and
impossible to accept, and set about building a system designed to show how a
mind could possess concepts that experience could never have supplied. The
empiricists' most lasting bequest was not a doctrine but a question, and it was
sharp enough to break the tradition that produced it.
