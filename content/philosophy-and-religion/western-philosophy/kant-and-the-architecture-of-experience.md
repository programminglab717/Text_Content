# Kant and the Architecture of Experience

Immanuel Kant published almost nothing of consequence until he was well past
fifty. He had been a competent, unremarkable professor in Königsberg for years,
lecturing on logic, metaphysics, physical geography, and anthropology, writing
respectable minor pieces on winds and earthquakes and the nebular origin of the
solar system. Then came a decade of near silence, and at the end of it, in 1781,
the Critique of Pure Reason. He was fifty-seven. Within another ten years he
had also produced the foundations of his ethics and his aesthetics, and every
philosophical movement in Europe for the next century had to work out its
position relative to his.

What broke the silence, by his own account, was reading Hume, who woke him from
what he called his dogmatic slumber. The particular thing that would not let him
sleep was the argument about causation. Hume had shown that no amount of
observing one event follow another can yield the idea of necessity, and that
our confidence in the causal order therefore has no rational ground. Kant
thought the argument was valid on its premises and that the conclusion was
unacceptable, because Newtonian physics was plainly knowledge, and it asserted
universal necessary truths about how bodies behave. Something in the premises
had to be wrong.

## Inverting the Relation

His diagnosis was that both sides of the eighteenth-century deadlock shared an
assumption so basic that nobody had thought to question it: that knowledge must
conform to its objects. The rationalists thought pure reason could work out what
those objects are like; the empiricists thought only the senses could report on
them. Either way the mind is a receiver, and the only question is what it
receives and how reliably.

Suppose instead that objects, as we encounter them, conform to the constitution
of the mind that encounters them. Kant compared this to what Copernicus had
done. Having failed to explain the motions of the heavens on the assumption
that the whole sky revolved around the observer, Copernicus tried supposing
that the observer revolved and the stars stood still. Kant proposed the same
manoeuvre in epistemology. If certain structural features of experience come
from us rather than from the world, then we can know in advance that every
possible experience will have them — and that is exactly the kind of
knowledge, universal and necessary and yet substantive, that the old picture
could not account for.

## A Third Kind of Judgment

To state the problem precisely he needed two cuts through the field of
judgments. The first is analytic against synthetic. In an analytic judgment the
predicate is already contained in the subject: all bodies are extended, all
bachelors unmarried. Such judgments cannot be false, and they tell you nothing
you did not already possess. In a synthetic judgment the predicate adds
something not contained in the concept of the subject, which is why synthetic
judgments are informative and why they can be wrong.

The second cut is a priori against a posteriori: whether a judgment is known
independently of experience or on the strength of it. Kant's marks of the a
priori are necessity and strict universality, neither of which experience can
supply, since experience can only ever tell you how things have been so far.

The empiricists had assumed the two cuts line up, so that all informative
judgments are a posteriori and all a priori judgments are trivial. Kant's
question is whether there is a third box: synthetic a priori judgments, which
are both informative and necessary. He thought mathematics and the foundations
of physics were full of them. Seven plus five equals twelve is his standard
case: however long you stare at the concepts of seven, five, and addition, the
twelve has to be constructed rather than extracted. That every event has a
cause is another: nothing in the concept of an event contains a cause, and yet
no physicist treats it as a generalisation that might fail tomorrow. Later
logicians, Frege in particular, disputed the arithmetic example. The general
question survived the objection.

## Space, Time, and the Categories

The Critique answers the question in two stages, corresponding to the two
faculties Kant thinks are involved. Sensibility receives; understanding judges.
Neither works alone, which is the point of his best-known sentence: thoughts
without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind.

Sensibility contributes space and time, which Kant argues are not things, not
properties of things, and not concepts abstracted from experience, but the forms
in which any sensory content whatever must be received. His evidence is partly
phenomenological — you can imagine space emptied of objects but cannot imagine
the absence of space — and partly mathematical. Geometry makes necessary claims
about space, and an empirical generalisation could never be necessary. If space
is the mind's own format for outer intuition, then geometry describes the
structure of every possible appearance in advance, which explains the otherwise
strange fact that a purely a priori science applies without fail to the physical
world.

The price is severe and Kant pays it openly. If space and time belong to the
receiving apparatus rather than to what is received, then we know things only as
they appear, never as they are in themselves. The thing in itself is not a
hidden object behind a screen; it is the concept of whatever an object is when
you subtract the conditions under which anything can show up for us, and that is
by construction unknowable.

The understanding contributes the categories: twelve pure concepts, organised
under quantity, quality, relation, and modality, derived with some visible
strain from the forms of judgment recognised in the logic of his day. Substance
and causality fall under relation. The hard part, and the part Kant rewrote for
the second edition, is the transcendental deduction, which asks by what right
we apply these concepts to objects at all. His answer runs through the unity of
self-consciousness. The I think, he says, must be able to accompany all my
representations; otherwise something would be represented in me that was not
mine, which is nonsense. But a stream of sensations could only belong to a
single consciousness if it were combined according to rules — if the successive
were sorted into what endures and what changes, what is cause and what effect.
The categories are therefore not read off experience. They are the conditions
under which anything could count as a unified experience in the first place.

That is the reply to Hume. He was right that necessary connection cannot be
found among the impressions, and wrong to conclude it is a mere habit, because
it is among the things that make an ordered field of impressions possible. As
for how an abstract category ever touches a concrete intuition, Kant supplies
the schematism, which translates each category into a determination of time —
substance as permanence, cause as rule-governed succession. He admits the
process is an art hidden in the depths of the soul whose workings nature is
unlikely ever to let us see.

## What Reason Is Not Allowed to Do

The second half of the Critique is the destructive half, and in its own century
it was the more explosive. Reason, Kant argues, has a natural and irresistible
tendency to keep going after the conditions of experience have run out, applying
the categories beyond any possible intuition. The results are not merely false
but systematically deceptive.

Rational psychology infers from the unity of consciousness to a simple,
indestructible soul, which mistakes the formal unity of the I think for the
perception of a thing. Rational cosmology generates the antinomies: pairs of
opposed theses, each supported by an argument that seems watertight. The world
has a beginning in time, and it does not. Matter divides into simples, and it
divides forever. Everything happens by natural causality, and there must also
be freedom. Kant treats these standoffs as a diagnostic. Reason contradicts
itself here because it is treating appearances as though they were things in
themselves, and the contradiction dissolves once the distinction is drawn.
Rational theology fares no better: his objection to the ontological argument,
that existence is not a real predicate, comes with the observation that a
hundred actual coins contain not one penny more than a hundred merely possible
ones, though the state of one's finances differs considerably.

Clearing this ground was, for Kant, an act of construction rather than
vandalism. Because we cannot know that the causal order is the whole of reality,
freedom remains thinkable — I can be an appearance wholly governed by natural
law and also a member of an intelligible order in which I originate action. He
said that he had found it necessary to limit knowledge in order to make room
for faith, and the moral philosophy of the following decade, with its
categorical imperative and its insistence that persons be treated never merely
as means, is built in the space he cleared.

The system did not hold in the form he left it. Friedrich Jacobi complained
almost at once that you cannot get into the Critique without the thing in
itself and cannot stay inside it with one, and the idealists who followed —
Fichte, then Schelling, then Hegel — took the objection as licence to discard
it. Non-Euclidean geometry and, later, relativity made the claim that Euclidean
space is the one necessary form of outer intuition look like a local
observation rather than a discovery about minds. What survived is the shape of
the question. Nobody now assumes that the mind is a passive receiver, and the
work of specifying what a perceiving system must already contribute before
anything can appear to it has simply migrated into other disciplines.
