# Plato's Theory of Forms

Draw a circle on a blackboard. However carefully you draw it, it is not a
circle. The chalk line has width, which a true circle's circumference does not.
The points along it are not all exactly equidistant from the center, and cannot
be, because chalk is made of grains. Smooth the line, shrink the grains, use a
plotter and a laser and the best surface anyone can manufacture, and you will
improve the approximation without ever crossing over into the thing itself.

And yet when a geometer proves that the angle inscribed in a semicircle is a
right angle, the proof is not about the chalk. It is true of every circle,
including all the ones nobody will ever draw, and it was true before anyone drew
any. So the proof is about something. What?

That question, pressed hard, is roughly where Plato's theory of Forms comes
from. It is not primarily a piece of mysticism. It is an attempt to name the
objects that make knowledge possible, in a world where nothing you can point to
sits still long enough to be known.

## Four Problems and One Answer

Aristotle, who spent twenty years in Plato's Academy and had every reason to
know, reported that Plato absorbed early the Heraclitean doctrine that sensible
things are in constant flux, and concluded that if knowledge exists it must be
of something else. That is one of several pressures converging on the same
solution.

The first is the problem of the one over many. Many different things are called
beautiful — a face, a law, a proof, a horse. They share no obvious physical
feature. Either the word is an accident of usage, meaning nothing in particular,
or there is some single thing they share in, and which the word names.

The second is mathematics, the case of the chalk circle. Geometry is manifestly
about something, its results are necessary rather than contingent, and its
subject matter is not available to the senses.

The third is moral evaluation. To call a city unjust is to measure it against a
standard. If the standard is simply what Athenians happen to approve of, then
saying that Athens is unjust becomes incoherent, and reform has nothing to aim
at. Socrates' lifelong demand for definitions presupposes there is something
definite to define.

The fourth is the distinction between knowledge and opinion. A person can hold a
true belief by luck and be unable to defend it, and Plato takes this to be a
different state from knowing. Knowledge, on his account, has to be tethered, and
what it is tethered to had better not be something that changes while you are
looking at it.

A Form answers all four at once. The Beautiful itself, the Circle itself, the
Just itself — each is a single, eternal, unchanging thing; not located anywhere,
not made of anything, accessible to thought rather than sight; and the many
particular beautiful or circular or just things are what they are by
participating in it.

## The Argument from Falling Short

The *Phaedo* gives the tightest version of the case, built from an observation
about how we judge. Take two sticks that look equal. Look again from another
angle, or measure them more finely, and they turn out not to be — they appear
equal to one observer and unequal to another, and in any case no two physical
lengths are exactly the same. So we routinely judge that a pair of sticks falls
short of being perfectly equal.

That judgment is strange. To recognize a shortfall you must already have some
grip on what is being fallen short of. You cannot notice that a drawing is a
poor likeness of someone you have never seen. The senses only ever deliver the
deficient cases, so the standard is not something the senses gave us. Plato's
conclusion is that we must have encountered the Equal itself before birth, and
that learning in this life is recollection — the sight of two near-equal sticks
jogging a memory of the real thing.

The recollection half of the argument has few takers. The structural half is
harder to shrug off, and it recurs in modern form whenever someone asks how
creatures built from sensory experience come to possess concepts that no
possible experience instantiates. Perfect equality, frictionless motion,
infinite series, the ideal gas: physics runs on objects nobody has met.

## The Sun, the Line, and the Cave

In the *Republic* Plato builds the theory into something larger, using three
images in sequence. The sun makes visible things visible and also makes them
grow; likewise the Form of the Good makes intelligible things knowable and also
accounts for their being what they are. Plato goes so far as to say the Good is
beyond being — not one more item in the inventory, but the condition of there
being an intelligible order to inventory at all.

The divided line sorts states of mind by the reliability of their objects. At
the bottom, imagining: shadows, reflections, images of images. Above it, belief
about ordinary physical things, which is where most competent adults live and
where most practical skill operates. Above that, the reasoning of the
mathematician, who argues rigorously but starts from hypotheses he does not
examine and leans on diagrams he knows are inadequate. At the top, dialectic,
which works back to first principles and takes the Forms directly.

Then the cave, which dramatizes the ascent as a personal ordeal rather than an
epistemic ladder. Prisoners chained facing a wall, watching shadows cast by
objects carried behind them, naming the shadows and competing to predict their
sequence. One is freed, and the first result of being turned around is pain and
confusion; the fire dazzles him and the shadows seemed more real. Dragged out
into daylight, he can at first look only at reflections, then at objects, and
only last at the sun. Returning to the cave, his eyes now useless in the dark,
he is worse at shadow-prediction than he was before, and the prisoners conclude
that going up ruins a man. Plato notes, drily, that they would kill him if they
could. His teacher had been dead perhaps thirty years when he wrote it.

The political payoff is direct. If there is a Form of the Good and some people
have made the climb, then rule by those people is not tyranny but competence,
and democratic Athens is a ship whose crew is voting about navigation.

## Plato Takes the Theory Apart

The most damaging attack on the Forms in antiquity was written by Plato. In the
*Parmenides*, a young Socrates lays out the theory to the aged Eleatic
philosopher, who dismantles it point by point while praising the impulse behind
it.

Are there Forms of everything? Socrates is confident about Justice and Beauty,
hesitant about Man and Fire, and embarrassed by hair, mud, and dirt — and
Parmenides tells him the embarrassment is a symptom of youth, that philosophy
will eventually make him stop despising anything.

Then participation. If a thing participates in a Form, does it get the whole
Form or a part of it? The whole cannot be present entire in many separate
places. A part means the Form is divisible, so a thing is large by having a
piece of Largeness — which would be a small piece, and so make things small.

Then the regress now called the Third Man. If large things are large by sharing
in Largeness, consider the set of large things together with Largeness itself:
by the same reasoning there must be a further Form they all share in, and so on
without end.

Then the sharpest one. If Forms are genuinely separate from our world, their
relations are to each other, not to us. Knowledge itself, the Form, would relate
to Truth itself, while our knowledge relates to our world. The Forms would then
be exactly the things we cannot know — and a god who knew them perfectly would,
by the same separation, know nothing of us.

Plato does not answer these objections in that dialogue, and the later works
read as sustained attempts to build something that survives them. The *Sophist*
gives up the picture of Forms as isolated units and works out how they combine,
which kinds blend with which. The *Timaeus* introduces a craftsman who looks to
the Forms and a receptacle in which copies take shape. The *Philebus* treats
limit and unlimitedness as ingredients rather than separate perfect objects.

Aristotle, watching from inside the Academy, made the practical complaint: the
Forms explain nothing. They duplicate the world — an eternal horse alongside the
horses — and doubling a problem is not solving it. Participation, he said, was
empty words and poetic metaphor. His own solution kept form and dropped
separation, putting the form inside the individual thing as its organizing
structure.

The theory nevertheless proved impossible to kill. Plotinus rebuilt it as a
hierarchy descending from the One. Augustine relocated the Forms into the mind
of God, where they stayed for most of a millennium as the divine ideas.
Frege posited a third realm for thoughts that are neither physical nor private.
And working mathematicians, asked whether they invented the prime numbers or
found them, overwhelmingly say found — then look uncomfortable, because saying
so commits them to the existence of something that nobody can locate, that
nothing causes, and that was there before the chalk.
