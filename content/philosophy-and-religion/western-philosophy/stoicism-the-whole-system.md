# Stoicism Was a System Before It Was a Mood

The school is named after a building, and not a grand one. The Stoa Poikile was
a public colonnade on the north side of the Athenian agora, decorated with
paintings of Marathon and the sack of Troy, where people sheltered from the sun
and loitered between errands. Zeno of Citium taught there. He had no walled
garden like Epicurus and no grove like Plato; he lectured in a covered walkway
that anyone could wander into without paying, being introduced, or being Greek.

Zeno was not Greek. He came from Cyprus, probably of Phoenician descent, and
the traditional story has him arriving in Athens by accident, shipwrecked with a
cargo of purple dye. Browsing in a bookseller's, he heard someone reading
Xenophon's recollections of Socrates aloud, and asked where men like that were
to be found. The bookseller pointed at a philosopher walking past in the street.

The school that grew out of that colonnade was, for something like five hundred
years, the most fully worked-out philosophical system in the Mediterranean — a
physics, a logic, and an ethics that were meant to hold each other up. Almost
none of the early writing survives. What reaches most readers now is a handful
of Roman texts about keeping your composure, and a popular version of the
doctrine that consists of one idea repeated: some things are up to you, most
things are not, so concentrate on the first category. That idea is genuinely
Stoic. Detached from everything that supported it, it is also nearly the
opposite of what it originally meant.

## Three Parts That Do Not Separate

The Stoics were fond of images for how their subjects fit together, and the
images all make the same point. Philosophy is an orchard: logic is the wall,
physics the soil and trees, ethics the fruit. Or philosophy is an egg, or an
animal whose bones and sinews are its logic. The versions vary in the reports,
and the variation matters less than the insistence — you cannot take the fruit
and leave the trees.

Their physics is a thoroughgoing materialism with a startling conclusion built
in. Only bodies exist, since only bodies can act and be acted upon. The cosmos
is a single continuous material whole, pervaded by a fine active substance
called *pneuma*, breath or fiery air, which holds things together and gives
each thing its particular tension and character — holding a stone together as
cohesion, a plant as growth, an animal as soul. This active principle is
intelligent and providential. It is the *logos*, and it is God, and it is not
outside the world but identical with it, arranged through it the way a soul is
arranged through a body.

From that follows a strict determinism. Every event is the necessary outcome of
the preceding state of a rational whole, which the Stoics were content to call
fate and equally content to call providence. Chrysippus, the third head of the
school and the man who did most of the systematic work — the ancients said
there would have been no Stoa without him — defended this against the obvious
objection with an image. Push a cylinder and it rolls; push a cone and it spins.
The shove is external, but what the object then does follows from what it is.
Your character is genuinely yours, and your actions genuinely follow from it,
even though the impression that prompted them arrived unbidden.

The epistemology hangs on that word "impression." Zeno demonstrated it with his
hand. Open palm: a sense impression, something the world presents. Fingers
slightly bent: assent, your agreeing that things are as presented. Closed fist:
comprehension, an impression so clear it could not have come from anything other
than what it reports. Left hand clamped over the fist: knowledge, the whole
secured within a structure of other things you understand. The crucial joint is
the second one. The impression arrives on its own; the assent is yours. Nothing
becomes a belief until you sign for it.

## Why the Ethics Is Not Advice

The ethical conclusion follows from the physics with almost no slack. If the
universe is a rational, providentially ordered whole, and you are a fragment of
that rationality, then living well means living in agreement with nature — both
the nature of the whole, whose course you cannot alter, and your own nature as a
rational and social animal.

And then the hard part. The only thing that is good is virtue; the only thing
bad is vice; everything else — health, money, reputation, pain, the survival of
your children — is indifferent. Not unimportant. The Stoics were not ascetics
and did not tell you to stop wanting health. They sorted the indifferents into
preferred and dispreferred, and said that selecting the preferred ones is
exactly what a rational agent does. But the selecting, done well, is the whole
of what is up to you, and success is not part of it.

Their image for this was the archer. The archer's task is to draw, aim, and
release as well as a person can. Once the arrow leaves the string a gust of wind
may take it, and whether it hits is not part of his craft. He has done
everything that was his to do. A Stoic parent who loses a child has been
bereaved of something preferred, not of something good, and the distinction is
supposed to be the difference between grief and ruin.

The account of the emotions is equally uncompromising, and it is the part that
has aged best. The *pathē* — distress, fear, appetite, and the kind of giddy
elation that makes people foolish — are not physical weather that happens to
you. They are judgments, and false ones. Anger is the assent to a proposition
along the lines of: I have been wronged, and it is fitting that I strike back.
Because they are judgments, they can be examined, and withheld. This is not
suppression of feeling; a suppressed emotion is one you have already accepted.
It is refusing to countersign the valuation in the first place. Twentieth-century
cognitive therapy rediscovered this mechanism almost exactly, and its founders
said so.

Nor is the result a cold private self-sufficiency. The Stoics explained moral
development through *oikeiosis*, the natural extension of concern outward from
an infant's instinctive care for itself to family, city, and eventually all
rational beings. Hierocles pictured it as concentric circles around a person,
and described the work of ethics as drawing the outer circles inward, treating
cousins as siblings and strangers as neighbors. Their cosmopolitanism was not a
sentiment but a deduction: if reason is one and universal, the wise are citizens
of the world before they are citizens of anywhere.

## The Roman Turn and the Modern Amputation

Under Rome the school changed register. Seneca, immensely rich, adviser to
Nero, eventually ordered by Nero to kill himself, wrote letters of moral
counsel whose polish has always made some readers suspect their sincerity.
Musonius Rufus argued, unusually for his time, that women should study
philosophy on the same grounds as men, since they have the same reason. His
student Epictetus had been a slave in Rome and was lame, reportedly from
mistreatment; freed, he taught for decades in Greece, and what survives is a
set of classroom transcripts taken down by a pupil. Marcus Aurelius, who held
absolute power over that same empire, wrote his notebooks in Greek, on campaign,
addressed to nobody, and they read like a man conducting maintenance on himself.

That Roman material is what the modern revival draws on, and the selection has
consequences. Almost everything popular Stoicism uses comes from the ethics. The
physics is quietly dropped, usually with a remark that we can keep the practical
wisdom without the outdated cosmology.

But the dichotomy of control changes meaning when the cosmology goes. Epictetus
tells you to accept what happens because what happens is the operation of a
providential reason of which you are a part; your acceptance aligns you with
something, and the alignment is the point. Remove the providence and you are
being told to accept whatever occurs because resisting it is ineffective. The
first is a metaphysical commitment. The second is resignation with a coach's
vocabulary. Likewise, "the only good is virtue" is a shocking claim about the
structure of value, not a productivity tip; a person who keeps the calm and
drops the claim has kept the hardest technique and abandoned the only reason to
attempt it.

The ancient critics went after the same joints. If everything is fated, why act
at all — the lazy argument, which Chrysippus answered by pointing out that
outcomes are co-fated with the actions that produce them, so that a man is not
fated to recover whether or not he calls the doctor. Critics also pressed the
sage, the perfectly wise person who never assents to a falsehood and never feels
a false emotion. The Stoics conceded he was about as common as the phoenix, and
that none of them was one. Which raises the question of what a philosophy is for
when its standard is admittedly unreachable, and leaves the school's actual
practitioners — a banished lecturer, a compromised courtier, an emperor writing
to himself in a tent — in the position of people doing a discipline they knew
they would not complete.
