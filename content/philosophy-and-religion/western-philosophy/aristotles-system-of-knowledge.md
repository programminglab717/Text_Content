# Aristotle's System

Cicero, who had read books of Aristotle that no longer exist, praised his prose
as a river flowing with gold. Anyone opening Aristotle today will wonder what on
earth he was talking about. The surviving text is terse to the point of
rudeness. Arguments start mid-stride, terms arrive undefined and get defined two
chapters later, whole passages repeat with small variations as though someone
were trying a paragraph three ways and had not yet chosen. There are asides that
read like instructions to a room: we have discussed this elsewhere, let that
suffice, let us now take up the next question.

The explanation is that these are not his books. The polished dialogues that
Cicero admired are lost. What came down to us is the working archive of a
school — lecture material, research memoranda, drafts kept because they were
useful, assembled into treatises by editors centuries after the author's death.
A story preserved by Strabo has the manuscripts hidden in a cellar in Asia Minor
for generations, damp and worm-eaten, before resurfacing and reaching Rome,
where Andronicus of Rhodes arranged them into the corpus we now read. The book
we call the *Metaphysics* is named for its place in that arrangement: the
writings shelved after the physics.

What survived the cellar is the most ambitious attempt anyone has made to put
the whole of human knowledge into a single connected order.

## The Blueprint

Aristotle divided the sciences by their aim. Theoretical sciences exist for the
sake of knowing: physics, which studies things that change and exist
independently; mathematics, which studies things that do not change but do not
exist separately; and first philosophy, which studies being as such. Practical
sciences exist for the sake of acting well, and cover ethics and politics.
Productive sciences exist for the sake of making something, and cover rhetoric,
poetics, and the crafts.

Logic sits outside this scheme, which is why the later tradition called the
logical works the *Organon* — the instrument. Before you can study anything you
need to know what a valid inference looks like, what a definition is, what
counts as a demonstration. Aristotle essentially invented this subject. The
syllogism, the analysis of terms into subject and predicate, the systematic
treatment of fallacies, the distinction between the necessary and the merely
true: all of it is new with him, and Kant could still say two thousand years
later that logic had not advanced a step since.

The division carries a rule that gets ignored more often than it is followed.
Each field admits its own degree of precision, and demanding more is a mark of
incompetence rather than rigor. You should no more expect mathematical proof
from a discussion of justice than accept persuasive rhetoric from a geometer.
Ethics, he says, deals in what holds for the most part.

## Matter, Form, and the Rescue of Change

Aristotle's starting point is almost always ordinary observation and what people
generally say — the appearances, and the reputable opinions. He takes seriously
the possibility that a widely held belief is tracking something real, and treats
a theory that contradicts all of them as thereby in trouble. This puts him
directly against the Eleatic argument that change is impossible: nothing can
come from what is not, and nothing can come from what already is, so nothing
comes to be. Things plainly do come to be. The job is to explain how, not to
explain it away.

His solution is the pairing of matter and form. A bronze sphere is bronze
organized in a certain shape. The bronze is the matter, the shape is the form,
and neither exists on its own in the finished object — you cannot hold the
bronze apart from some shape or the shape apart from some stuff. Change is then
matter losing one form and taking on another, with the matter persisting
throughout to be the thing that changed. Nothing comes from nothing, because
there was always the bronze. And a thing that is potentially a sphere becomes
actually a sphere: potentiality and actuality are how he gets a third option
between sheer being and sheer nothing.

This is also where he breaks decisively with the Academy. Form is real and is
what makes a thing what it is, but it is not off in a separate realm. It is in
the horse, doing the work of organizing that particular animal, and it is passed
on when the horse has a foal.

Explanation then branches four ways, because there are four different questions
the word "why" can be asking. What is it made of — the material cause. What is
its structure or definition — the formal cause. What set it going — the
efficient cause. What is it for — the final cause. For an oak: wood and sap;
the organization that makes it an oak and not a beech; the parent tree and the
acorn; and the mature oak itself, which is what the acorn is developing toward
and which explains why its parts grow as they do.

That last cause is the one modernity threw out, and it is worth being clear
about what Aristotle meant. He was not proposing that a designer had plans for
the oak. The end is internal to the organism: acorns become oaks reliably, not
by accident, and pointing at the mature form is the only way to say what the
seedling's root system is doing. Nature, in his phrase, does nothing in vain. A
biologist today who explains the shape of a beak by what the bird eats is
speaking a descendant of this language, with natural selection supplying the
mechanism Aristotle lacked.

At the far end of the physics stands the unmoved mover. Motion in the cosmos is
eternal, so it needs an eternal cause, and that cause cannot itself be moved or
the regress restarts. Aristotle's answer is a being of pure actuality with no
matter and no potential, which moves the heavens the way a beloved thing moves
a lover: as an object of desire, without being touched or altered. Its activity,
being the highest kind, is thinking; and since it must think the best object, it
thinks itself.

## The Man with the Cuttlefish

Biology takes up more of the surviving corpus than anything else, and the
difference in texture is startling. The metaphysical works argue; the biological
works look. Aristotle dissected. He records the four stomachs of ruminants, the
placenta of a species of shark, the way a chick embryo develops day by day with
the heart visible and beating early. He noticed that male octopuses have one arm
modified for transferring sperm to the female — an observation so odd that later
naturalists assumed he had been misled, until the nineteenth century confirmed
it and gave the arm a name.

He classified by degrees of shared structure rather than by the neat dichotomous
division his teacher's school favored, splitting animals first into the blooded
and the bloodless, a cut that maps closely onto vertebrate and invertebrate. He
was also wrong about large things. The heart, not the brain, was for him the
seat of sensation and thought; the brain's job was to cool the blood. His
account of reproduction gave the male the form and the female only the matter,
which did real damage for a long time.

Darwin, near the end of his life, wrote that his own scientific gods, Linnaeus
and Cuvier, were schoolboys next to old Aristotle.

## Living Well as a Skill

The ethics starts from a question about aims. Everything we do is for the sake
of something, and if the chain went on forever desire would be empty, so there
must be something wanted for its own sake. Everyone agrees to call it
*eudaimonia* — flourishing, a life going well — and nobody agrees on what it
consists in.

His route to an answer is the function argument. A good flautist is one who
plays well; a good eye sees well; anything with a characteristic activity is
good when it performs that activity well. The characteristic human activity is
the exercise of reason, so a good human life is one of activity in accordance
with reason, performed excellently, over a complete life.

Virtue, on this account, is not a feeling or a rule but a stable disposition of
character acquired the way skills are acquired: by doing. We become just by
doing just acts, brave by doing brave ones, and there is no shortcut through
understanding. Each virtue lies between two failures — courage between
recklessness and cowardice, generosity between waste and meanness — though the
mean is relative to the person and the situation, not an arithmetic midpoint,
and some acts admit no mean at all. What locates it is practical wisdom, the
capacity to see what this situation calls for, which no amount of general
principle can replace.

He spends more pages on friendship than on almost anything else, and he insists
that the good life needs equipment: health, some money, decent luck, a city
worth living in. Humans are political animals, and a man outside a community is
either a beast or a god. The school collected well over a hundred constitutions
of actual Greek states to study how they worked and why they failed, and one of
them, on Athens, was found on papyrus in the nineteenth century, still doing the
job it was written for.
