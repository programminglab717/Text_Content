# Utilitarianism and the Arithmetic of Happiness

In a glass case at University College London sits the skeleton of Jeremy
Bentham, padded out and dressed in his own clothes, with a wax head resting
above the collar. He left instructions for this in his will. The body was to be
dissected for the instruction of students, then preserved and kept as what he
called an auto-icon, on the reasoning that a corpse is a resource and burying it
wastes it. The attempt to preserve the actual head went badly and it is stored
separately. Very little about the arrangement is dignified, and dignity was not
among the considerations Bentham thought carried weight.

That indifference to what feels appropriate, combined with an absolute
seriousness about what actually helps, is the whole of the doctrine he founded.
Its first premise is stated in the opening sentence of his Introduction to the
Principles of Morals and Legislation: nature has placed mankind under the
governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. They determine what we
shall do and also point out what we ought to do. From there the principle of
utility follows directly. An action is approved or condemned according to its
tendency to increase or diminish the happiness of those affected by it, and
there is no other ground of approval or condemnation available.

## What the Principle Demolishes

The force of this in the 1780s lay in what it ruled out. If consequences for
sentient beings are the only currency, then no practice can defend itself by
appealing to custom, scripture, natural law, the wisdom of ancestors, or the
supposed rights of anyone. Bentham dismissed the rhetoric of natural rights as
nonsense upon stilts, not because he opposed the liberties being claimed but
because he thought asserting a right settled nothing that still had to be shown.

Applied to English criminal law, which at the time prescribed hanging for a long
list of property offences, the principle turned into a programme. Punishment is
itself an evil, since it deliberately inflicts suffering. It can only be
justified by preventing greater suffering, from which it follows that any
severity beyond what deterrence requires is not justice but waste. Bentham and
the circle around him spent decades pressing this argument through the law of
evidence, prison design, the franchise, the poor law, and the sanitary
condition of industrial cities.

Two features of his version were more radical than anything in the reform
politics of the time. The first was strict equality in the counting. Each person
is to count for one, nobody for more than one, so a labourer's suffering enters
the sum at the same weight as a duke's. The second was where he drew the
boundary of the calculation. In a footnote he observed that the question about
animals is not whether they can reason or talk, but whether they can suffer.
Nearly everything argued about animal welfare since is a development of that
sentence. He also wrote an essay arguing that consensual homosexual acts harm no
one and should not be criminal, and prudently left it unpublished; it did not
see print for well over a century.

## The Calculus and Its Problems

Bentham thought the comparison of pleasures could be made systematic. His
felicific calculus lists the dimensions along which a pleasure or pain is to be
measured: intensity, duration, certainty, nearness in time, its tendency to
produce further sensations of the same kind, its freedom from consequences of
the opposite kind, and the number of people affected. Run the columns, sum
them, and you have your answer.

Nobody has ever done this, and the objections are obvious enough that Bentham
half anticipated them. The units do not exist. There is no scale on which the
intensity of one person's relief is commensurable with the duration of
another's boredom, and no way to check the result against anything. Bentham was
also untroubled by the kinds of pleasure involved, remarking that quantity of
pleasure being equal, a simple pub game is as good as poetry. He meant it as a
demonstration of the principle's impartiality. It was read, then and since, as a
reduction of the moral life to the accounting of sensations.

## Mill's Repairs

John Stuart Mill was raised as an experiment in Benthamite education by his
father, learning Greek at three and working through political economy in his
early teens, with almost no company his own age. At twenty he fell into a
depression that he later described with startling directness: he asked himself
whether the complete achievement of all the reforms he was working for would
make him happy, and something inside him answered no. He came out of it slowly,
helped by poetry, and the revisions he made to the doctrine all trace back to
that winter.

The best known is the distinction between higher and lower pleasures. Some
kinds of enjoyment, Mill argues, are more valuable than others independently of
their quantity, and the way to establish which is to consult people who have
adequate experience of both. Such judges, he claims, consistently prefer the
pleasures engaging the higher faculties, even when these come with more
discontent. Hence his line that it is better to be a human being dissatisfied
than a pig satisfied, better Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.
Critics have pointed out ever since that this either abandons the single scale
that gave the theory its power or imports a standard of value that is not
pleasure at all, and that the empirical claim about what experienced judges
prefer is doubtful in any case.

Mill's other repair has worn better. He insists that nobody is supposed to
calculate consequences from scratch before each action. Humanity has been
accumulating evidence about what conduces to happiness for the whole of its
existence, and the results are stored in ordinary moral rules — do not lie, keep
promises, do not steal. These secondary principles do the daily work, and the
principle of utility is consulted only when they conflict. Sailors, he notes,
do not wait until they are at sea to begin calculating the Nautical Almanac.

## The Objections That Stuck

The counterexample that dominated mid-century discussion involves a sheriff in a
town on the edge of a riot, who can prevent a great deal of violence by framing
and hanging one innocent man. The arithmetic appears to endorse the frame-up.
Rule utilitarianism was developed partly in response: assess not individual acts
but the general adoption of rules, and a rule permitting judicial murder has
terrible consequences. The counter-objection followed quickly. If the rules may
be refined with exceptions whenever an exception would produce more good, then
the refined rule eventually says do whatever maximises, and rule utilitarianism
has collapsed back into the act version it was invented to escape.

Deeper objections came later and have not been answered to general
satisfaction. John Rawls charged that summing welfare across people does not
take seriously the distinction between persons: it extends to society a
principle of rational prudence that makes sense only within a single life, where
the one who bears the cost is also the one who gets the benefit. Bernard
Williams pressed the integrity objection, arguing through cases in which a
decent person is offered a chance to do something repugnant in order to prevent
a worse act by someone else. Utilitarianism makes him responsible for
consequences he does not bring about, and requires him to treat his own deepest
commitments as one more input to a calculation, which is to ask him to stand at
a distance from what makes him the person he is.

Robert Nozick attacked the value theory from two directions. His utility monster
is a being that extracts vastly more pleasure from any resource than anyone
else, and to whom the theory therefore says we should give everything. His
experience machine offers a lifetime of any experiences you like, indetectably
simulated. Most people say they would not plug in, which suggests that we care
about actually doing things and actually being connected to other people, not
merely about the quality of our inner states. Derek Parfit then showed how
awkward simple aggregation becomes at scale: a sufficiently enormous population
of lives barely worth living will always outscore a smaller flourishing one on
total happiness, a result he named repugnant and spent years failing to avoid.

## Why It Will Not Go Away

For a theory with that many unpatched holes, utilitarianism has remarkable
staying power, and the reason is that it is the working language of public
decisions. Cost-benefit analysis, quality-adjusted life years in health
policy, discount rates applied to climate damages, statistical valuations of
risk reduction in transport safety — all of these are utilitarian instruments,
routinely operated by people who would reject the philosophy if asked. When a
health service must decide which treatments to fund from a fixed budget, it
cannot avoid comparing benefits across different people. Declining to aggregate
is not a way of keeping one's hands clean; it is a decision with its own body
count, distributed less visibly.

There is also the historical record, which is better than the theory's
reputation. On question after question where the only argument for the existing
arrangement was that it had always been so — flogging, the death penalty for
theft, the legal subjection of married women, the treatment of animals,
religious tests, the criminalisation of private conduct between consenting
adults — the utilitarians were early, and usually right, and got there by asking
the flat, unsentimental question of who is being hurt and what good the hurting
does. The objections to the theory are serious. Nobody has yet produced a rival
that asks that question as persistently.
