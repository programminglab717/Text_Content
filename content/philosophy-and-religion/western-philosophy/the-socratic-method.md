# The Socratic Method

A man stops a decorated general in the street and asks him what courage is.

The general is Laches, a real Athenian commander who had led real troops in the
field, and he gives the answer any soldier would give: courage is standing your
ground and not running away. It is a perfectly good answer. It is also, within
about ninety seconds, in ruins. What about the Scythians, Socrates asks, who
fight on horseback by wheeling away and turning back and wheeling away again?
What about a fighting retreat, which every competent commander has at some
point ordered? Laches revises. Courage is a kind of endurance of the soul.
Better — but foolish endurance, a man holding a position out of sheer stupidity
against odds any officer would recognize, that isn't admirable, is it? No. So
courage is wise endurance. But then the soldier who holds on knowing that
reinforcements are an hour away is braver than the one who holds on knowing
nothing will save him? That seems exactly backwards.

Nicias, another general, tries a different tack: courage is knowledge of what is
genuinely to be feared and what is not. This is more sophisticated, and it fails
more interestingly. If courage is knowing which outcomes are good and which are
bad, then courage turns out to be indistinguishable from virtue as a whole, and
we have explained the part by quietly swallowing the whole.

The dialogue ends with nobody knowing what courage is. Two professional soldiers
and a stonemason's son have talked for an afternoon and produced nothing they
can write down. That is not a failure of the conversation. That is the
conversation working as designed.

## The Shape of a Refutation

The procedure has a recognizable structure, and once you see it you can watch it
run in dialogue after dialogue. Socrates asks a "what is it?" question — what is
piety, what is justice, what is temperance, what is knowledge. The interlocutor
offers an answer. Socrates then secures agreement to two or three further
propositions, usually ones that seem harmless and often ones that seem to have
nothing to do with the topic. He then shows that those propositions, taken
together, contradict the original answer. The interlocutor, who has agreed to
every step, has no choice but to withdraw his definition. A new definition is
offered. The cycle repeats.

Two features of the opening move do most of the work. First, Socrates refuses
examples. In the *Euthyphro*, he meets a young man on the courthouse steps who
is prosecuting his own father for homicide and who is entirely confident this is
the pious thing to do. Asked what piety is, Euthyphro says: this, what I'm doing
right now. Socrates declines the instance and asks for the single character by
which all pious things are pious — the standard you would have to consult to
judge any case at all. Euthyphro tries again: piety is what the gods love. The
gods disagree with each other, Socrates points out, so the same act could be
both. Amended: what all the gods love. And then the question that has outlived
the dialogue by two and a half millennia — is a pious act loved by the gods
because it is pious, or is it pious because they love it? Either the gods are
responding to a standard that exists independently of them, in which case their
approval isn't what piety *is*, or they are not, in which case piety is
arbitrary and could have been anything.

Second, Socrates insists that you answer for yourself. He is not interested in
what your teacher said or what the city believes. He wants the proposition you
actually hold, because the machinery only bites on sincere assent. This
requirement turns the exercise from a debating game into something closer to an
audit of a person. It also explains why the dialogues collapse when someone
refuses it. In the *Gorgias*, Callicles finally sees what is happening, stops
defending his own position, and starts answering merely to move things along;
the argument continues but the philosophy has effectively stopped. In the
*Protagoras*, Socrates threatens to walk out if the great sophist will not
abandon set-piece oratory for short question and answer. He needs sentences
small enough to be individually agreed to. Speeches are immune to refutation
because they never commit to anything you can grip.

## Ignorance as a Working Method

Socrates claims throughout not to know the answers himself. This is the famous
irony, and it has been read as everything from courtesy to a sneer. The version
in the *Apology* makes it a theological puzzle rather than a pose. Chaerephon,
a friend, had asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates,
and was told no one was. Socrates, convinced this could not be right, set out to
find a counterexample, interrogating the politicians and the poets and the
craftsmen. The craftsmen actually knew things — how to make a shield, how to set
a mast — but on the strength of that expertise assumed they also understood
justice and the good life, and did not. Socrates concluded that the god meant
something modest: his own small advantage was that he did not imagine he knew
what he did not know.

Whether or not you take that autobiography at face value, disclaimed ignorance
is structurally necessary to the method. A teacher who knows the answer and is
merely extracting it from you is running an examination, however gently. The
elenchus works only if the outcome is genuinely open, because its product is not
a doctrine but a state — *aporia*, being at a loss, the moment when a
confident person discovers his beliefs do not fit together and he cannot yet see
which one to give up. Meno, stung by this, compares Socrates to a stingray that
numbs whatever it touches, and complains that he could once give fluent speeches
about virtue and now cannot say a word.

Meno also raises the sharpest objection to the whole enterprise. If you don't
know what you're looking for, how will you recognize it when you find it? And if
you do know, what are you looking for? Plato's answer in that dialogue is
recollection: Socrates takes an uneducated household slave and, by questioning
alone, walks him to the construction of a square double the area of a given one.
The boy's first guess — double the side — is shown to produce four times the
area, not twice. Only after that failure, only from inside the confusion, does
he see that the answer is the diagonal. The moral Plato draws is metaphysical,
about the soul knowing before birth. The pedagogical moral is more portable: the
boy could not have been told. He had to be brought to the point where his own
wrong answer became visibly wrong to him.

## What the Method Cannot Do

Refutation is a negative instrument, and the negation is weaker than it looks.
When Socrates shows that your definition contradicts other things you believe,
he has demonstrated that your beliefs are inconsistent. He has not shown which
one is false. Logically, Laches is free to keep his definition of courage and
abandon one of the innocuous-seeming premises he conceded along the way. The
twentieth-century scholar Gregory Vlastos made this the central difficulty of
Socratic argument: the dialogues consistently treat the interlocutor's thesis as
the casualty, when the contradiction by itself does not say so. Something other
than the logic — Socrates' judgment about which commitments run deeper, the
interlocutor's own sense of what he cannot bear to give up — is doing the
selecting.

There is also the matter of who gets refuted. The targets are usually
prominent, usually older, and usually humiliated in front of an audience of
delighted young men. The *Apology* has Socrates cheerfully admitting that the
youth imitate him, go off and cross-examine their elders, and thereby make him
enemies at a steady rate. Aristophanes had already put him on stage in the 420s
as a fraud running a school where students learn to argue the weaker case into
the stronger. By 399 BCE a jury of several hundred Athenians voted to convict
him of impiety and corrupting the young, and then voted to execute him. A
technique that reliably reveals that respected people cannot defend their most
basic commitments is not a neutral technique. It is a political act, and Athens
treated it as one.

The name survives in places that have kept the form and dropped the risk. Law
schools call cold-calling Socratic, though the professor knows the holding and
the student's job is to find it. Classroom "Socratic seminars" are usually
civil group discussions in which nobody is refuted. Cognitive behavioral
therapists practice guided discovery, which comes closest to the original, since
the aim really is to let a person watch one of their own beliefs fail under
their own examination rather than be contradicted from outside.

What none of these inherit easily is the asymmetry that made the thing
dangerous. Socrates was not helping people reach conclusions he had prepared. He
was proposing that a person's whole account of what matters be put on the table
and pulled at until it either held or came apart, with no guarantee of a
replacement and no particular sympathy for how it felt. The general went home
not knowing what courage was. He had known that morning.
