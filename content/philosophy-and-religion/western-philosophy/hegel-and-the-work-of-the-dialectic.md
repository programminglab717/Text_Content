# Hegel and the Work of the Dialectic

Almost everyone who can name one thing about Hegel names the same thing:
thesis, antithesis, synthesis. It is a superb piece of intellectual branding,
compact and memorable and easy to apply to anything. It is also not his. Hegel
used those three terms very rarely, and where he did he was usually describing
somebody else's procedure, Kant's or Fichte's, not his own. The formula reached
the public through popularising lecturers in the decades after his death and
has been circulating under his name ever since, doing more to prevent people
from reading him than any difficulty in the prose — which is considerable, and
which he made no serious attempt to reduce.

The trouble with the formula is not that it is vague. It is that it gets the
direction of the motion backwards. A triad of thesis and antithesis resolved in
a synthesis sounds like two positions colliding and a third splitting the
difference, with the philosopher standing outside as referee, applying a
template. Hegel's claim is that nothing is applied from outside at all. A
position, pressed hard enough on its own terms, generates its own difficulty,
and the difficulty has a determinate shape given by the position that produced
it. The philosopher's job, he says in the introduction to the Phenomenology of
Spirit, is merely to look on.

## Negation That Keeps Something

The engine is what he calls determinate negation. Ordinary negation empties a
claim out: not-this leaves you with nothing in particular. Determinate negation
is the negation of some specific content, and the result carries the content it
negated inside it. If a conception of knowledge collapses because it cannot
account for something, then what you have afterwards is not a blank but that
conception plus the specific reason it failed, which is already a more developed
conception.

His name for the transition is Aufhebung, a word he chose because ordinary
German uses it in contradictory senses: to cancel, to preserve, and to raise
up. He remarks with obvious pleasure that the language itself has arrived at a
speculative truth. What is superseded is not destroyed. It is demoted from a
freestanding truth to a moment within something larger.

The Science of Logic opens with the barest possible illustration. Begin with
pure Being, taken with no determination whatever, because any determination
would be a presupposition. Being with nothing said about it has no content by
which it can be distinguished from Nothing. The two collapse into each other,
and what is actually before us is neither, but the passing of each into the
other — Becoming. Becoming is not a compromise between Being and Nothing. It is
the recognition that neither was ever a stable starting point, and that what
looked like two static concepts was one movement misdescribed.

## Consciousness Testing Itself

The Phenomenology of Spirit, which Hegel finished in 1807 while Napoleon's army
was in the neighbourhood of Jena, is that method applied to knowing. The book
runs through a long series of shapes of consciousness, each of which proposes
both an account of what counts as knowledge and an object that is supposed to
satisfy it. Hegel's device is that the standard of assessment is always the
shape's own. He does not tell sense-certainty that it is wrong; he lets it apply
its own criterion and watch the result come apart.

Sense-certainty is the first and the best miniature of the method. It claims to
be the richest and most immediate knowledge there is, because it adds nothing
and interprets nothing: it simply takes what is given, this, here, now. Ask it
to say what it knows, though, and the trouble begins. Write down what the Now
is. Now is night. Preserve the note and read it at midday, and the truth that
was supposed to be absolutely immediate has gone stale, while the word Now has
survived perfectly well — precisely because it is a universal that holds for
day and night alike. The same happens to This and Here. The most immediate
knowledge turns out to be the most abstract and the emptiest, and consciousness
is pushed onward not by an external argument but by its own attempt to say what
it meant.

## Lord and Bondsman

The best-known episode in the book arrives when consciousness becomes
self-consciousness and discovers that it needs something no object can give it.
A self-consciousness wants to be confirmed as what it takes itself to be, and
only another self-consciousness can do the confirming. Two of them meet. Each
demands recognition without granting it, and the confrontation escalates to a
struggle in which each stakes its life, since a being that will not risk life
has shown that it values mere existence above its own standing. One of them
backs down. The result is lordship and bondage.

The arrangement then fails in a way that no one involved intended. The lord has
won recognition from someone he has defined as not worth being recognised by,
so the recognition is worthless. He also has his relation to things mediated by
the other, who works while he consumes, which leaves him dependent and
unchanged. The bondsman has the worse position and the better prospects. Having
faced the fear of death, he has already had the experience of everything fixed
in him being shaken loose. And through labour he works on objects that resist
him, which forces his intentions to take an external, durable shape. In forming
the thing, Hegel says, he forms himself. He acquires a mind of his own by way
of a discipline he did not choose.

Whether this is an analysis of the structure of self-consciousness or a claim
about history is a question the text does not settle, and readers have taken it
both ways. Alexandre Kojève's Paris lectures in the 1930s pushed the historical
and political reading hard, and through his audience it reached Sartre, Lacan,
Merleau-Ponty, Beauvoir, and Fanon. Much of twentieth-century writing about
recognition, domination, and the formative power of work is a descendant of
those ten pages.

## Spirit, and the Lateness of Philosophy

Geist, usually rendered as spirit or mind, is not a cosmic ghost hovering over
history, whatever the nineteenth-century critics assumed. It is the element of
shared life: language, custom, law, art, religion, institutions — everything
that exists only because a community sustains it, and within which individual
minds are formed. Hegel's objection to Kantian morality follows from this. A
purely formal test applied by an isolated rational will is, he thinks, empty; it
can rule out inconsistency but cannot generate content. Content comes from
Sittlichkeit, the concrete ethical life of family, civil society, and state, in
which duties are already articulated and a person can recognise himself in the
institutions he supports.

This is where Hegel is most often accused of complacency, and the sentence
usually cited is his remark that what is rational is actual and what is actual
is rational. Read one way it sanctifies whatever exists, including the Prussian
state that employed him. Read another way, using his technical sense of
actuality as against mere existence, it says that only what embodies reason
deserves to be called actual, which is a standard that most existing
arrangements would fail. The ambiguity is real and he did little to resolve it.

He was more candid about philosophy's limits elsewhere. Philosophy, he wrote,
always comes on the scene too late to tell the world how it ought to be; it
appears only when actuality has completed its formation. The owl of Minerva
takes flight only as the dusk is falling. Whatever else that is, it is not the
claim of a man who thinks he has a blueprint.

## The Splitting

Within a few years of his death in the cholera epidemic of 1831 his school had
split. The right kept the theology; the left kept the method and turned it
against the theology, with Feuerbach arguing that God is human capacity
projected outward and alienated. Marx, out of that left wing, said the dialectic
in Hegel was standing on its head and needed to be set on its feet: keep the
account of how a social form generates the conditions of its own supersession,
discard the idea that what is developing is spirit rather than the material
organisation of production. Kierkegaard attacked from the opposite side, on the
grounds that an existing individual with a life to lead cannot be a moment in
anybody's system, and that the system's serene comprehensiveness is a way of
avoiding the one decision that matters.

The twentieth century mostly treated him as a warning. Russell and Moore founded
analytic philosophy partly by repudiating the British idealists who had taken
him up, and Popper filed him among the enemies of the open society. That verdict
has come apart over the last few decades, as readers found that the metaphysical
monster they had been warned about was harder to locate in the texts than
expected, and that what is there instead is a theory of how norms get their
authority from mutual recognition and how a tradition can be criticised from
inside without an external standard. The formula about thesis and antithesis
survives too, in politics columns and business books, attached to a method that
was designed to make exactly that kind of external application impossible.
