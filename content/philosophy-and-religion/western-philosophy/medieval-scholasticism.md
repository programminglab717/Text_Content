# Medieval Scholasticism

Twice a year, in Advent and in Lent, the theology masters of the University of
Paris held a disputation at which anyone present could ask anything. The format
was called quodlibetal, from the Latin for "whatever you please," and it was
dangerous. A master sat before a hall of students, rival masters, friars, and
whoever had wandered in, and took questions in no order and on no announced
topic: whether an angel can be in two places at once, whether a prince may
debase the coinage, whether a man who has sworn an unjust oath is bound by it,
whether the damned would prefer not to exist. A bachelor under him fielded the
objections on the day. The master returned, usually the next morning, and
delivered his determination — a structured resolution that had to handle every
objection raised, including the clever ones planted by colleagues who disliked
him.

This was not an eccentric sideshow. It was the characteristic exercise of a
civilization's intellectual life for roughly three centuries, and the books that
survive from that world are mostly transcripts, reworkings, or imitations of it.

## From the Cloister to the Schools

Before there were universities there were cathedral schools, and before those
the monasteries, where reading meant *lectio divina* — slow, prayerful
absorption of scripture and the Fathers, aimed at transforming the reader rather
than resolving a question. The eleventh and twelfth centuries saw a different
kind of reading take hold at Laon, Chartres, and above all Paris: reading that
noticed problems.

Anselm, a monk who became archbishop of Canterbury, set the tone with a
programme he described as faith seeking understanding. He accepted the
doctrines, then asked whether reason alone could reach them, and produced the
argument that God is that than which nothing greater can be conceived — and that
such a being cannot exist only in the mind, since existing in reality would be
greater. A fellow monk named Gaunilo immediately objected that the same
reasoning would conjure up a perfect island, and Anselm answered him in print.
The exchange set a precedent: the objection was published alongside the
argument.

Peter Abelard turned that instinct into a method. His *Sic et Non* collected
roughly a hundred and sixty theological questions and set out, under each, the
authoritative texts that said yes and the authoritative texts that said no.
Fathers of the Church flatly contradicting one another, page after page, with no
resolutions supplied. The provocation was deliberate, but the prologue was
constructive: Abelard laid down rules for handling the conflicts. Check whether
the text is genuine. Check whether the author later retracted the view. Check
whether the same word is being used in two senses. Check what question the
author was actually answering. Doubt, he wrote, leads us to inquiry, and by
inquiry we perceive the truth.

Abelard's own life supplied the era's most famous scandal — the affair with
Heloise, her uncle's men breaking into his room at night, the castration, the
condemnations at Soissons and Sens — but his intellectual legacy was the habit
of treating a contradiction between authorities as a problem to be solved rather
than a scandal to be hushed. Within a generation Peter Lombard had organized the
whole of theology into four books of *Sentences* on that pattern, and lecturing
on the *Sentences* became the standard qualification for a master in theology
everywhere in Europe.

## The Aristotle Problem

The schools were meanwhile absorbing a shock. For centuries the Latin West had
known Aristotle only through a few logical works transmitted by Boethius. Then,
across the twelfth century, the rest arrived — the physics, the biology, the
psychology, the metaphysics, the ethics — translated in Toledo and Sicily and
Constantinople, some from Arabic and some directly from Greek, with William of
Moerbeke eventually producing careful Latin versions at the request of Thomas
Aquinas.

It arrived accompanied. The Arabic and Jewish commentators came with it:
Avicenna, whose account of essence and existence reshaped the Latin discussion;
Maimonides, wrestling with the same scripture-and-philosophy collision from
inside Judaism; and Averroes, whose commentaries were so thorough that Latin
writers simply called him the Commentator, as they called Aristotle the
Philosopher.

The content was alarming. Aristotle's universe was eternal, with no creation in
time. His account of the intellect could be read — Averroes read it this way —
as implying one shared intellect for all humanity, which leaves nothing
individual to survive death. His God was not providential in any recognizable
sense. Paris responded by banning the teaching of the natural philosophy in the
arts faculty early in the thirteenth century, repeatedly, which is a reliable
sign that the ban was not working. By mid-century the prohibited books were the
core of the curriculum.

What followed was the great period. Aquinas took the position that reason and
revelation cannot genuinely conflict, since both come from God, and then did the
laborious work of showing where each operates. Some truths reason can
demonstrate — that God exists, by arguments from motion, causation, contingency,
gradation, and the ordering of things toward ends. Others, like the Trinity or
the Incarnation, reason cannot reach but also cannot refute. On the eternity of
the world he made a characteristically sharp move: philosophy cannot prove that
the world began, and cannot prove that it did not; we know it began because we
are told.

The *Summa Theologiae* is that method frozen into a book, and its architecture
is the disputation on paper. Each article opens with a question, then states
objections to the position Aquinas holds — stated properly, with the best
authorities behind them, sometimes better than his opponents had managed. Then
a counter-authority. Then his own resolution. Then a specific reply to each
objection in turn. A reader who skips to the resolution has skipped the part
that makes the form worth anything, which is the obligation to put the other
side at full strength before you touch it.

## Razors, Condemnations, and Calculators

The synthesis did not settle. In 1277 the bishop of Paris condemned 219
propositions, sweeping up Aristotelian and Averroist theses along with several
that looked uncomfortably like Aquinas's own. The impulse was conservative: the
philosophers were saying that God could not do certain things — could not make a
void, could not make other worlds — because the physics forbade it, and the
bishop's response was that God's power is not bounded by Greek natural
philosophy.

The unintended consequence was a licence to speculate. If God could have made a
void or many worlds, then what would follow *if* he had becomes a legitimate
question, and fourteenth-century writers pursued such hypotheses with real
technical seriousness. Some historians, following Pierre Duhem, have called 1277
the beginning of modern science, which is too strong; but the habit of reasoning
about impossible-according-to-Aristotle cases was genuinely productive.

John Duns Scotus, working in Oxford and Paris, rebuilt the metaphysics with
extraordinary intricacy — arguing that "being" must mean the same thing when
applied to God and creatures or theology cannot say anything at all, and
introducing a principle of individuation, the *haecceitas* or thisness, that
makes each thing this one and not merely an instance of its kind. William of
Ockham then took a scythe to the apparatus, insisting that entities should not
be multiplied without necessity, that universals are not things in the world but
names and concepts by which minds sort individuals, and that God's absolute
power means the connections we observe are contingent all the way down. Ockham's
followers became known as the modern way, the *via moderna*, and the older
schools the ancient way, and universities across Europe split along that line.

Meanwhile at Merton College a group of logicians turned the machinery on motion
itself, proving that a uniformly accelerating body covers the same distance as a
body moving steadily at its mean speed — a result Nicole Oresme in Paris then
demonstrated with a geometrical diagram, plotting velocity against time and
reading off the area. Jean Buridan, arguing against Aristotle's account of
projectiles, proposed that a thrown body carries an impressed *impetus* that
persists, and suggested the heavens might move because God gave them impetus at
creation with nothing to wear it down.

The humanists who came next found all of this contemptible. The Latin was
barbarous, the questions were arid, the terminology — quiddity, haecceity,
suppositio — was ripe for parody. The followers of Duns became the Dunsmen, and
then dunces. The jeer that scholastics argued about how many angels could dance
on the head of a pin appears nowhere in the medieval texts; it was invented later
by people who had stopped reading them. The genuine question behind it, whether
an immaterial substance can be said to occupy a place and whether two of them
can be in the same place, is not a foolish thing to ask if you believe such
substances exist.

What survived the ridicule was the infrastructure. The lecture, the seminar,
the degree, the faculty, the oral examination, the corporate self-government of
scholars, the convention that you set out the objections before you answer them
— all of it comes from those halls, and most of it is still in use by people who
would be startled to learn where it came from.
