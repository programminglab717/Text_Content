# Descartes and the Method of Doubt

On a November night in 1619, a twenty-three-year-old Frenchman attached to a
Bavarian army in winter quarters shut himself in a room heated by a stove and
stayed there the whole day. He was not ill and he was not hiding. René
Descartes had taken a law degree at Poitiers, decided that the books had
taught him nothing he could rely on, and enlisted as a gentleman volunteer
mostly to see the world while the Thirty Years' War got started around him.
That day he thought, and that night he had three vivid dreams which he
interpreted as a mandate. He had glimpsed, he believed, the foundations of a
single unified science. He spent the next thirty years trying to lay them.

What was wrong with the foundations already in place is easy to forget now.
Descartes had been educated at La Flèche, a Jesuit college and one of the best
schools in Europe, on a curriculum built out of Aristotle as digested by
medieval commentators. It was a magnificent structure and it was coming apart.
Copernicus had moved the earth. Galileo's telescope had put moons around
Jupiter and mountains on a body that was supposed to be a perfect sphere. The
physics of substantial forms and natural places, which explained why stones
fall and fire rises by appealing to what stones and fire essentially were, was
not going to survive contact with a mathematics of motion. Descartes came out
of the best education available convinced that the only thing he had learned
that was genuinely certain was mathematics, and that nobody had thought to
build anything on it.

## Emptying the Basket

His solution was not to examine his beliefs one at a time. There are too many,
and most of them are not worth the trouble. Instead he went after foundations.
He compared the procedure to a man who suspects some of his apples are rotten:
rather than inspecting each one in the barrel, he tips the whole lot out and
puts back only those he has examined and found sound. Anything resting on a
principle that can be doubted goes out with the principle.

The demolition in the First Meditation proceeds in three escalating stages.
The first is ordinary: the senses sometimes deceive. A square tower looks round
from a distance, an oar looks bent in water. But this only impeaches the senses
in marginal conditions, and it seems mad to doubt that one is sitting by a fire
holding a piece of paper.

The second stage is the dream. Descartes points out that he has often been
convinced, while asleep, that he was sitting by a fire holding a piece of
paper, and that there is no mark by which he can distinguish the waking state
from the dreaming one while he is in it. This is a much wider cut. It does not
merely discredit distant towers; it discredits the entire testimony of the
senses at once. Yet something survives even the dream. Whether he is awake or
asleep, two and three make five, and a square has four sides. The materials
out of which dreams are assembled may be false in their arrangement, but the
simplest constituents — extension, shape, number, duration — seem untouched.

So he introduces the third stage, which is genuinely radical. Suppose there is
a being of immense power and intelligence who devotes himself to deception. Not
merely the physical world, but the apparent self-evidence of arithmetic, could
be a staged fraud. The demon is not a serious hypothesis about the universe. It
is a device for holding the doubt in place, because Descartes knows that old
beliefs return the moment the pressure comes off, and he wants a fiction
powerful enough to keep them out for the length of the argument.

## The One Thing That Cannot Be Faked

The demon defeats itself. Deception requires someone deceived. However
comprehensively he is being misled, there must be a he who is being misled, and
that much cannot be false at the moment he considers it. In the Discourse on
Method the point appears in the compressed form everyone knows, "I think,
therefore I am," which reads like a syllogism with a suppressed premise. In the
Meditations Descartes states it more carefully: this proposition, I am, I
exist, is necessarily true whenever I put it forward or conceive it in my mind.
It is not an inference from a general rule about thinkers. It is a single act
of recognition in which entertaining the thought and confirming it are the same
motion.

Having found one survivor, he has to ask what it is. Not a body: bodies are
under suspicion. Not a man, if a man means a rational animal, because that
smuggles in a whole biology. What remains is a thing that thinks — which he
unpacks generously as a thing that doubts, understands, affirms, denies, wills,
refuses, imagines, and has sensory experiences. Sensing counts, so long as it is
taken as the seeming and not as a report about the world. That I seem to see
light is certain even if there is no light and no eye.

## The Wax

The Second Meditation contains the most physically specific paragraph in the
Meditations, and it is about a piece of beeswax fresh from the comb. It tastes
faintly of honey, smells of the flowers the bees worked, is hard, cold, and
makes a sound when you rap it. Hold it near the fire. The taste and smell go,
the colour and shape change, it becomes liquid and hot and silent. Every single
sensory property has been replaced, and yet nobody hesitates to say that the
same wax remains.

If not one of the perceived qualities persisted, then what we were tracking was
never a bundle of qualities. Nor is it something we imagine, since the wax is
capable of indefinitely many shapes and no one can run through indefinitely
many images. Our grasp of the wax, Descartes concludes, was always an
inspection of the mind, an intellectual judgment mistaken for an act of seeing.
Then he turns the argument around, and the turn is the real point of the
chapter. Every one of these observations about the wax — clear or confused,
true or false — is also evidence that he exists and is thinking. Nothing can
show him the wax without showing him the mind more distinctly than it shows the
wax. The mind, in his phrase, is better known than the body.

## Buying the World Back

Having burned everything, he now has to repurchase it, and this is where modern
readers usually part company with him. The Third Meditation argues that the
idea of an infinite, perfect being is one he could not have manufactured: the
cause of an idea must contain at least as much reality as the idea represents,
and a finite, doubting, imperfect thing is not a sufficient cause for the idea
of perfection. So a perfect being exists; and a perfect being is not a deceiver,
since deception is a defect. The demon is dismissed. With God's guarantee,
whatever is perceived clearly and distinctly may be trusted, which licenses the
return of mathematics, and then, in the Sixth Meditation, of bodies.

But the world that comes back is not the one that was taken away. What
Descartes recovers is matter as pure extension — size, shape, position, motion
— a world stripped of colours, tastes, smells, and warmth, which are relocated
into the mind as effects. That is not a loss he regrets. It is the entire
purpose of the exercise. A world whose only properties are geometrical is a
world that mathematical physics can describe exhaustively, and Descartes had a
physics to sell.

## The Circle, and the Ghost

Two objections attached themselves to the system almost immediately and never
came off. Antoine Arnauld, given a draft to comment on before publication,
pointed out that clear and distinct perception is validated by God's existence,
while God's existence is proved by clear and distinct perception. Descartes
replied by distinguishing between a conclusion grasped in the present moment,
which cannot be doubted while you hold it, and a conclusion merely remembered,
which needs the divine guarantee. Very few readers have found this convincing.

The second problem was the dualism. Mind is unextended thinking substance, body
is unthinking extended substance, and they have no property in common. So how
does the decision to lift an arm move any matter? Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia
put the question to him in correspondence with complete directness: how can
something with no extension and no surface push anything? Descartes gestured at
the pineal gland as the site of interaction, which answers where but not how,
and he seems to have known it. The difficulty outlived him by four centuries.
A great deal of subsequent philosophy of mind consists of attempts either to
bridge that gap or to deny that one of its two sides exists.

He died in Stockholm in the winter of 1650, having accepted an invitation to
tutor Queen Christina of Sweden, who wanted her philosophy lessons at five in
the morning. He had spent most of his adult life staying in bed until noon,
thinking. The cold got him within a few months.
