# Liberty and Its Limits

A city council takes up an ordinance requiring helmets on rented scooters, and
within twenty minutes the hearing has produced every argument in political
philosophy. A rider says the risk is hers to take. A trauma nurse describes head
injuries and the cost of treating them. Someone points out that the injured are
carried by public ambulances to public hospitals, so the risk is not hers alone.
Another speaker says the real issue is that the scooter companies engineered the
streets into a hazard and the rider's choice is not as free as it looks. A last
one says the rule is fine because almost nobody actually wants to ride without a
helmet — they just forget.

These are not the same argument. They are five distinct theories of when
interference is justified, and they are the whole subject in compressed form.

## Mill's Principle and Its Fine Print

John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty*, published in 1859, contains the sentence most
often cited and least often read in full. The only purpose for which power can
rightfully be exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his
will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, physical or moral, is not
sufficient warrant. He may be remonstrated with, reasoned with, persuaded,
entreated — but not compelled.

Notice how much that sentence concedes before it starts limiting anyone. Mill is
not saying interference for someone's own good is ineffective or unkind. He is
saying it is illegitimate — outside the warrant of the coercer — while explicitly
protecting every non-coercive alternative. You may argue, plead, publish, and
withhold your company. What you may not do is use force or legal penalty.

Two other pieces of fine print get skipped. The first is that Mill grounds the
principle on utility, not on natural rights, which he disclaimed as a foundation.
His case is that a society which permits eccentricity, experiment, and error
discovers more and calcifies less, and that the permanent interests of humanity
as a progressive being are better served by tolerating a good deal of foolishness
than by suppressing it. The second is a set of exclusions modern readers find
embarrassing: the doctrine applies to adults in the maturity of their faculties,
not to children, and Mill excluded what he called backward states of society
altogether, a passage that sat comfortably with his long employment by the East
India Company and does not sit comfortably with anything else in the book.

He also distinguished carefully between harm and things adjacent to harm. Mere
offense does not count. Neither does the ordinary damage of competition — the
shopkeeper ruined by a better shop across the street has been harmed in the plain
sense and has no complaint in the relevant sense. Neither does the distress of
people who simply dislike how you live, which Mill treated with some contempt,
since that distress can always be manufactured in whatever quantity the
disapproving require.

## Harm Does Not Stay Put

The principle's difficulty is that almost nothing is purely self-regarding. Mill
knew it and tried to hold the line by distinguishing conduct that merely affects
others from conduct that violates a distinct and assignable obligation to them.
Your drinking is your affair; your drinking on duty as a soldier or a police
officer is not, because there the drinking breaches a specific duty owed to
specific people.

James Fitzjames Stephen, writing a critique little more than a decade later,
thought the distinction collapsed under pressure. Human beings are not separable
that way. A man's dissipation ruins his family, drains the resources of his
neighbors, and corrupts the example set for his children, and no amount of
analytic carpentry keeps those effects inside his own skin. The modern version of
Stephen's objection arrives as an accounting argument, and it is the one that won
the scooter hearing: where medical costs are shared, any risky private act has a
public price, so the self-regarding sphere is empty and the harm principle
licenses everything.

That argument proves too much, which is the standard reply. If shared costs
convert private choices into public business, then diet, exercise, marriage,
occupation, and sleep are all legitimately regulable, and the principle has
permitted what it was built to forbid. The usual repair narrows what counts: only
harms that violate rights, or that fall on identifiable others, or that are
substantial rather than diffuse. Mill's own instinct was the same. His example
was the sale of poison, which he refused to ban and proposed instead to label and
record — a practice that leaves the transaction available to anyone who wants it
while making sure nobody stumbles into it unawares.

## Negative Liberty, Positive Liberty

Isaiah Berlin's 1958 lecture drew a distinction that reorganized the whole
argument. Negative liberty is the absence of interference: you are free to the
extent that no one is stopping you. Positive liberty is self-mastery: you are
free to the extent that you actually govern yourself, that your actions issue
from your own reason rather than from compulsion, ignorance, addiction, or
manipulation.

Both concepts answer real questions, and the second has an ugly history Berlin
was chiefly concerned to expose. If freedom means acting from one's true self,
someone else can claim to know what your true self would choose and coerce you in
its name — and they will describe this as liberating you. Every regime that ever
jailed people for their own eventual emancipation has run on some version of that
inference. Berlin's warning was about the inference, not about the concept; he
accepted that a person in the grip of an addiction is not straightforwardly free,
and his point was that the step from that observation to the authority of anyone
else to decide on your behalf is a step and not an entailment.

The defenders of positive liberty answer that formal permission without capacity
is thin. A right to travel you cannot afford to exercise is not nothing, but it
is not much. That thought is why arguments about education, health, and material
security keep turning up in disputes ostensibly about freedom, and why the same
policy can honestly be described by one side as an expansion of liberty and by
the other as its restriction.

## Freedom as Non-Domination

A third tradition, recovered from Roman law and the Italian and English
republics, proposes that both of the above miss what unfreedom actually feels
like. On this view — argued at length by Philip Pettit and traced historically by
Quentin Skinner — freedom is the absence of domination, meaning the absence of
anyone's capacity to interfere with you arbitrarily, whether or not they exercise
it.

The test case is the slave with an indulgent master. On a strict negative account
he may be quite free: nobody interferes with him, he does as he likes, his
options are wide. The republican answer is that he is obviously not free, because
everything he has depends on someone else's continued goodwill, and he knows it.
The knowledge changes him. He learns to anticipate, to flatter, to avoid drawing
attention — a posture familiar to anyone who has worked somewhere with an
unpredictable boss and no employment protection.

This recasts what a limit on liberty is. Law that is properly controlled by those
subject to it does not reduce freedom on the republican account; it constitutes
it, because a system of known rules you have a hand in making replaces dependence
on someone's discretion with dependence on rules. What threatens freedom is not
regulation as such but unaccountable power, whether it wears a uniform, signs
your paycheck, or shares your house.

## The Limits People Actually Argue About

Four families of justification for restricting conduct show up over and over, and
Joel Feinberg spent four volumes sorting them.

Harm to others is the uncontested one, and the arguments concern its edges:
whether risk imposition counts before anyone is hurt, how remote an effect may be
and still qualify, and whether harms to future people or to no assignable person
belong on the list.

Paternalism splits in two. The soft version intervenes only where the choice is
not genuinely voluntary — the person is misinformed, incapacitated, or acting
under pressure — and Mill himself allowed it, in the case of a man about to cross
a bridge you know to be unsafe and he does not. Stop him, Mill said, long enough
to tell him; then, if he still wants to cross, let him. Hard paternalism
overrides informed voluntary choice, and that is the genuinely contested
category. Most practical policy lives in the middle: mandatory waiting periods,
disclosure requirements, default enrollment, all of which shape choices without
foreclosing them.

Offense is the messiest. Feinberg thought some offense — profound, unavoidable,
inflicted on a captive audience — could justify regulation, while conceding that
any principle broad enough to cover public indecency risks covering blasphemy and
unpopular political display too.

Legal moralism, the view that immorality is itself grounds for prohibition
regardless of harm, produced the sharpest modern exchange in Anglo-American
jurisprudence. After the Wolfenden report recommended decriminalizing homosexual
acts between consenting adults in private, Patrick Devlin argued that a society
is held together by a shared morality and may defend that morality as it defends
itself against sedition. H. L. A. Hart replied that the public's disgust is not
evidence of anything, that societies survive moral change routinely, and that
Devlin's principle would have licensed every persecution that any majority ever
found comfortable. The law followed Hart. The argument has not gone anywhere, and
it resurfaces intact whenever a majority is certain enough about something.
