# The Social Contract as a Device

The engraved frontispiece of *Leviathan* shows a crowned giant rising over a
landscape of tidy fields and church spires, a sword in one hand and a bishop's
crozier in the other. Look closely at the giant's torso and arms and they are
made of people — hundreds of small figures, packed together, every one of them
facing inward toward the head. The image is the argument. The sovereign is not a
being of a different order set above the population; the sovereign is the
population, reorganized into a single body capable of acting. Thomas Hobbes
published the book in 1651, having spent the English civil war in exile in Paris,
watching from a safe distance while his country demonstrated what happens when
the question of who decides is left open.

That picture did something new to political argument. For centuries, authority
had been justified by descent — from God, from nature, from the patriarchal
authority of Adam. Hobbes justified it by construction. Imagine people without
it, ask what they would rationally agree to, and whatever comes out of that
thought experiment is what legitimate authority amounts to. Once the move was
available, everyone used it, including people who despised his conclusions.

## Hobbes and the Argument from Fear

The state of nature in Hobbes is not a historical claim about cavemen, though he
gestures at the Americas and at civil war as approximations. It is an analysis of
what human beings are like once the machinery of enforcement is subtracted. His
premises are lean: people are roughly equal in strength and cunning, such that
the weakest can kill the strongest by stealth or confederacy; goods are scarce;
and each person's judgment about what they need is their own. From this he
derives not a war of all against all driven by malice, but something worse
because it requires no malice at all. If I cannot be sure you will not strike
first, my safest move is to strike first, and you know that about me, and so your
safest move is the same. Even the moderate are driven to preemption by the
anticipation of others' preemption. The result is a condition in which there is
no industry, no cultivation, no navigation, no arts, and continual fear — and in
which the famous line about life being solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short
is a summary rather than a flourish.

The exit is a covenant in which each person lays down the right to govern
themselves on the condition that everyone else does the same, and transfers it to
a sovereign who is not a party to the agreement and therefore cannot breach it.
Hobbes's sovereign is nearly unlimited, and the reasoning is tight if you accept
the premises: a sovereign bound by conditions requires a judge of whether the
conditions were met, and that judge would be the real sovereign, and the question
merely recurs. Divided sovereignty is the disease that kills commonwealths. The
one right you cannot surrender is self-preservation, because the entire
arrangement exists for it, which means a condemned man may lawfully resist his
executioner and a sovereign who cannot protect you has no claim on you.

## Locke, Property, and the Right to Dissolve

John Locke ran the same construction and arrived somewhere nearly opposite. His
state of nature is not a war but an inconvenience. People there are already
governed by a law of nature discoverable by reason, and already hold rights to
life, liberty, and estate. They even acquire property before government exists:
the earth is given in common, but mixing one's labor with a portion of it makes
it one's own, subject to the provisos that nothing be wasted and that enough and
as good be left for others.

What is missing in that condition is not morality but institutions — a settled
known law, an impartial judge, and a power to execute judgments — so every man is
judge in his own case and disputes escalate. Government is instituted to supply
the deficiency, and it is therefore limited by the purpose. Locke's people do not
transfer their rights to a sovereign; they entrust a power, and a trust that is
abused reverts to the grantor. When a government exceeds its warrant, it is the
government that has rebelled, and the people may dissolve it. The *Two Treatises*
appeared in 1689 with the Glorious Revolution freshly accomplished, though the
work had largely been written earlier, in circumstances dangerous enough that
Locke kept his authorship quiet for the rest of his life.

## Rousseau and the General Will

Jean-Jacques Rousseau accepted the device and rejected almost every assumption
his predecessors had smuggled into it. The people they found in the state of
nature — calculating, acquisitive, anxious about status — were not natural men
but products of society, back-projected onto its origin. Natural man in Rousseau
is solitary, indolent, and moved by self-preservation and an unreflective pity at
the suffering of others. What corrupts him is not scarcity but comparison: the
moment people begin living together and measuring themselves against one another,
*amour de soi*, a healthy self-regard, curdles into *amour-propre*, a need to be
esteemed above others that no amount of getting can satisfy.

The contract in *Du contrat social*, published in 1762, is accordingly not a
device for security but for reconciling authority with freedom. Each associate
alienates himself totally to the whole community, and because everyone gives
himself to all and therefore to no one in particular, each gains over every other
associate exactly what he gives up, and obeys only himself. What the resulting
body wills is the general will — not the sum of private preferences, which
Rousseau calls the will of all, but what the citizens will when they ask what is
good for the association rather than what is good for themselves. From this comes
the line that has troubled readers ever since: whoever refuses to obey the
general will shall be forced to be free. Defenders read it narrowly, as no more
than the coercion of a free-rider by laws he himself voted for. Critics have read
it as the seed of every regime that claimed to know what its subjects really
wanted.

## The Consent Problem

The obvious objection arrived early and has never been fully answered. No one
signed anything. David Hume put it bluntly in his essay on the original contract:
the historical record shows governments founded on conquest and usurpation, not
agreement, and the appeal to tacit consent through residence is empty for anyone
without the means to leave. Asking a poor man whether he consents to his
sovereign by staying, Hume said, is like asking whether a sailor carried aboard a
ship while asleep consents to the captain's authority because he does not jump
into the ocean.

Two repairs have been attempted. The first grounds obligation in benefits
received rather than promises made — you enjoy the roads and the courts and the
protection, so you owe the reciprocal duty — though this runs into the objection
that benefits pressed on you without your asking do not usually generate debts.
The second, and the more durable, abandons the historical claim entirely. Kant
treated the original contract not as an event but as an idea of reason: a test to
which any law can be put, asking whether it could have been agreed to by an
entire people. On this reading the contract never happened and never needed to.
It is a standard, not a story.

## The Device Reborn, and Who Was Assumed to Be at the Table

That is the version John Rawls revived in 1971, and the reason contract theory
became a live enterprise again after a long period in which it was treated as a
historical curiosity. His parties choose principles behind a veil of ignorance
that hides their class, talents, sex, race, and conception of the good, so that
no one can tailor the rules to their own advantage. What makes this a contract is
its structure: principles are justified because they would be chosen by
everyone, in conditions that make the choice fair. What makes it survive Hume's
objection is that nobody claims it occurred.

Robert Nozick, three years later, agreed on method and rejected the outcome,
arguing that a minimal state could arise from a state of nature by an invisible
hand — protective associations competing, consolidating, and compensating those
they disarm — without anyone agreeing to anything, and that nothing more
extensive could be justified.

The sharpest recent criticism does not attack the device but audits its guest
list. Carole Pateman argued that the classic contract stories describe men
contracting with men, and that the resulting civil freedom was built on a prior
and unexamined subordination of women in a domestic sphere the theorists
carefully placed outside politics. Charles Mills pressed an analogous case about
race, reading the canonical contract as underwritten by a tacit agreement among
Europeans about who counted as a full person in the first place. Both arguments
have the same shape, and it is a shape internal to the tradition rather than
opposed to it. If legitimacy comes from what everyone could agree to, then who
was counted as everyone is not a footnote to the theory. It is the theory.
