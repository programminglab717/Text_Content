# Deontology Versus Consequentialism

A hospital runs two systems that contradict each other, and nobody thinks this
is strange. In a mass-casualty event, triage sorts patients by expected benefit:
the person who will die regardless is tagged and set aside so that three who can
be saved are treated first. Numbers govern, explicitly. Meanwhile, three floors
up, the consent rules do not bend for numbers at all. You may not take a kidney
from an unwilling donor to save two people on dialysis, and no calculation of
lives saved will be entertained. Both systems are defended by serious people.
Both are written into law. The quarrel between consequentialism and deontology is
not an academic curiosity; it is the fault line running through the institutions
we have already built.

## Two Different Questions

Consequentialism holds that the rightness of an act is settled entirely by how
its outcomes compare to the outcomes of the available alternatives. Rank the
states of affairs the world could be in, and the right act is whichever one
produces the best-ranked. Everything else — promises, intentions, who did what to
whom — matters only insofar as it changes the ranking. Classical utilitarianism,
in Bentham's and Mill's hands, filled in the ranking with aggregate welfare, but
the structure survives many substitutions. You can be a consequentialist about
preference satisfaction, about equality, about almost anything, provided you
insist that the act's job is to bring about as much of it as possible.

Deontology denies the entailment. It holds that some acts are wrong to perform
even when performing them would produce a better state of affairs, and that some
acts are permitted even when refraining would produce a better one. The word
comes from *deon*, duty, and the family is defined negatively: whatever rejects
the claim that better outcomes always license the act that produces them.

That negative definition is worth pausing on, because the two theories are
usually presented as symmetrical rivals, and they are not. Consequentialism is a
single clean thesis. Deontology is what is left when you deny it, which is why
there are many deontologies and they agree with one another less than their
opponents assume.

## The Case for Counting

The consequentialist argument has a simplicity that is genuinely hard to shake.
If you have the power to make things go better and you deliberately make them go
worse, what exactly were you doing? Every plausible moral theory cares about
suffering. Consequentialism simply refuses to stop caring at any point — refuses
to say that a quantity of suffering which would be decisive if produced one way
becomes irrelevant when produced another.

Its second strength is impartiality. Aggregate welfare does not care whose
welfare it is. No one's pain counts extra for being near, familiar, or ours.
Bentham's willingness to follow that through gave utilitarianism its long record
of being early and right about unpopular things: prison reform, the criminal
treatment of homosexuality, animal suffering, the legal position of women. When a
practice survives only because the people it harms are invisible or
unrepresented, a theory that simply totals up harm tends to notice before the
alternatives do.

Third, it gives clear guidance about institutions. Ask how to allocate a limited
supply of vaccine or organs and the deontological vocabulary of rights and
constraints goes quiet fairly quickly; everyone has a claim, the supply is fixed,
and some comparison is unavoidable. Consequentialist reasoning is the native
language of public health, insurance, safety regulation, and disaster response,
and not because the people doing that work are callous.

## The Case for Constraints

The trouble is the cases where the arithmetic comes out monstrous. A surgeon with
one healthy patient and five dying ones. A sheriff who can stop a lynch mob by
framing an innocent man. A regime that could raise average welfare by treating a
small minority very badly out of public view. Consequentialism does not merely
permit these; if the numbers hold, it requires them. Defenders reply that in the
real world the numbers never hold, that secrets leak and institutions corrode,
and this is often true — but it makes the wrongness of framing the innocent man
contingent on the odds of being found out, which is not what most people think is
wrong with it.

Kant's version of the alternative starts somewhere else entirely. The only thing
good without qualification is a good will, and an act has moral worth when it is
done from duty rather than from inclination or interest. The test he gives in the
*Groundwork* is the categorical imperative: act only on a maxim you could will to
be a universal law. Its most usable formulation tells you to treat humanity,
whether in yourself or another, always as an end and never merely as a means. The
point is not that using people is forbidden — you use the person who sells you
coffee — but that using them *merely* as a means, bypassing their rational agency
and treating them as material for your project, is a distinctive kind of wrong
that no benefit to third parties converts into a right.

This is what the surgeon case is really about. The five dying patients would be
allowed to die, which is bad. The one healthy patient would be used, his body
taken as an input to someone else's outcome, and he would have been treated as
though his existence were a resource. Deontology insists these are different
kinds of fact, not different sizes of the same fact.

## Where Each Theory Bends

Both have spent a century trying to absorb the other's best objections, with
mixed results.

Consequentialism's most famous problem after the monstrous cases is
demandingness. If you should always act so as to produce the best outcome, then
every luxury you buy is a wrong, since the money would do more good elsewhere.
There is no threshold at which you have done enough. Some consequentialists
accept this and change their lives. Others move to rule consequentialism:
evaluate not acts but codes of rules, and act on whichever code would produce the
best outcomes if generally internalized. That rescues promise-keeping and rights
as genuinely binding rules. But it invites the charge of incoherence — when
following the rule is known to produce a worse outcome in this case, the rule
consequentialist must either break it, and collapse back into act
consequentialism, or keep it, and worship a rule for its own sake against the
very value that justified it.

Deontology bends in the opposite direction, at the extremes. Almost no one will
say that a promise must be kept though a city burns. So most deontologists accept
thresholds: constraints hold absolutely up to some magnitude of disaster and then
give way. This is psychologically honest and theoretically awkward, because the
threshold has no principled location, and because a constraint that yields to
sufficiently large numbers has conceded that numbers were always in the running.

There is also the structural puzzle Robert Nozick sharpened. If it is seriously
wrong to violate a right, why is the right thing not to minimize violations —
committing one to prevent five? A theory built on the badness of a violation
should want fewer of them. That deontology forbids the trade is what makes it
deontology, and it shows the constraints cannot be grounded in the badness of the
outcome at all. They are agent-relative: the instruction is not "let there be
less murder" but "do not murder," addressed to you, about what your own hands do.

## The Distinctions the Constraints Require

To sustain that, deontology needs some contested machinery. It needs a
distinction between doing and allowing — between pushing a man under a train and
failing to pull him out — which critics say cannot bear moral weight, since a
lifeguard who watches a swimmer drown has done something monstrous by omission.
It needs a distinction between intending harm and merely foreseeing it, the
doctrine of double effect, which permits the field surgeon to administer morphine
that will shorten life as a foreseen side effect of relieving pain while
forbidding the same dose given in order to end it. And it needs the
agent-relative permissions that let you pursue your own projects, keep your
savings, and favor your own children, none of which impartial aggregation allows.

Philippa Foot's trolley case was invented to probe exactly these seams — why
diverting a runaway trolley onto one person seems permissible while cutting up
the healthy patient does not, when both trade one for five — and the industry of
variations it spawned has been less about trolleys than about isolating which
feature is doing the work.

## What the Disagreement Is Really About

Underneath, this is a disagreement about whether persons can be summed. John
Rawls put it as the separateness of persons: utilitarianism extends to society
the principle of choice appropriate to one person, treating a gain to you as
compensating a loss to me the way tomorrow's health compensates today's exercise.
But there is no experiential subject who undergoes both and comes out ahead.

It is also a disagreement about evidence. Consequentialists ask what justifies
the constraint and find nothing behind it but an intuition that may be a
sophisticated superstition. Deontologists answer that our confidence in the
wrongness of framing an innocent man is higher than our confidence in any
premise of any argument that it might be required, and that when a theory
contradicts a conviction that firm, the rational move is to doubt the theory.

Most people, and most legal systems, end up with a hybrid: outcomes govern within
the space left by constraints. Maximize benefit when allocating scarce goods;
never obtain them by using an unconsenting person. It is not elegant. It is what
survives when you refuse to give up either half of what you know.
