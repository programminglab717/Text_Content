# Breaking the Law in Public

Henry David Thoreau spent one night in the Concord town jail in the summer of
1846, having declined for several years to pay the local poll tax. His reasons
were slavery and the war with Mexico, which he regarded as the same enterprise
under two names, and his method was to withhold the small sum that made him a
shareholder in it. The gesture was interrupted by a relative who paid the tax on
his behalf, to his considerable annoyance, and he was released the next morning.
Out of a single night's inconvenience came the essay now known as "Civil
Disobedience," which argued that a citizen's first obligation is to his
conscience rather than to the legislature, and that a government resting on
majority rule is simply a government resting on whoever is stronger.

Thoreau's night in jail did not change American policy on anything. What it did
was supply a template, and the template travelled further than the man. Roughly
half a century later a lawyer in South Africa read the essay and recognized
something he was already assembling from other materials.

## Gandhi and the Discipline of It

Mohandas Gandhi's contribution was to turn conscientious lawbreaking from a
gesture into a method with rules. He called it satyagraha, usually translated as
truth-force, and insisted it was not the weapon of the weak but a demanding
practice that required training. Volunteers took pledges. They were drilled in
absorbing blows without returning them, in accepting arrest without resisting,
and in maintaining courtesy toward the police who were beating them. Campaigns
were announced in advance, with the authorities told what would be broken and
when. The Salt March of 1930 was choreographed to the last detail: a column
walking for weeks to the coast, at each stage attracting journalists, in order to
pick up a handful of natural salt from the shore in violation of a government
monopoly. The illegality was trivial. The monopoly was not, and the image of a
government arresting tens of thousands of people for gathering salt did more
damage to its claim of legitimacy than a year of speeches.

Two features of Gandhi's method matter for everything that followed. The first is
that the lawbreaking was open. Nobody crept out at night. The point was to be
seen, which meant the action was addressed to an audience rather than executed
against a target. The second is that the penalty was accepted. Filling the jails
was not an unfortunate cost of the campaign, it was the campaign — a public
demonstration that the people breaking the law were willing to bear its
consequences, and therefore were not merely evading it for advantage.

## Birmingham, and the Letter

In the spring of 1963, Martin Luther King Jr. was arrested in Birmingham, Alabama
for marching without a permit and wrote, on the margins of a newspaper and on
scraps supplied by his lawyers, a reply to eight local white clergymen who had
called the demonstrations unwise and untimely. The Letter from Birmingham Jail is
the most carefully argued defence of civil disobedience anyone has produced,
partly because it was written to people who agreed about the injustice and
objected only to the method.

King laid out the sequence a campaign must follow before it breaks anything:
establish that the injustice is real, negotiate, prepare the participants
through what he called self-purification, and only then act directly. He
answered the charge of impatience by observing that "wait" had, in his
experience, always meant "never," and he identified the white moderate who
prefers a negative peace without tension to a positive peace with justice as a
greater obstacle than the open segregationist. Most importantly for the theory,
he drew the line between just and unjust law from Augustine and Aquinas: a law
out of harmony with the moral law is not binding, and in practice an unjust law
is one that a majority imposes on a minority without binding itself by it. And he
was explicit about the condition that makes the whole thing something other than
lawlessness. One who breaks an unjust law must do so openly, lovingly, and with a
willingness to accept the penalty, and in doing so is expressing the very highest
respect for law.

That last move is the hinge. The disobedient is not claiming to stand outside the
legal order. He is appealing to it — to its own stated principles, against its
current practice — and the willingness to go to jail is the proof of the appeal's
sincerity.

## What the Philosophers Made of It

By the time John Rawls came to write about the subject, the American campaigns
had given political philosophy a case to work on. His definition has organized
the academic discussion since: a public, non-violent, conscientious but political
act contrary to law, undertaken to bring about a change in law or policy, and
performed within fidelity to law. Each clause is doing work. Public separates
disobedience from evasion. Non-violent keeps it addressed to the sense of justice
of the majority rather than to their fear. Conscientious excludes the merely
self-interested. Political means the appeal is to shared principles of justice
rather than to personal religious conviction — which is what distinguishes civil
disobedience from conscientious objection, where the objector asks to be excused
rather than asking that the policy be changed.

Rawls also specified when it is justified, and his conditions are tighter than
people remember. The society must be near-just, so that an appeal to its own
principles makes sense; the injustice must be substantial and clear, typically a
violation of equal basic liberties; normal political channels must have been
tried in good faith and failed; and the disobedient should consider whether
widespread resort to the tactic would produce disorder that undermines the
constitution itself.

The conditions have been attacked from every direction since. Why must
disobedience be non-violent? If the test is whether the act communicates rather
than coerces, property damage that harms no person may communicate perfectly
well — and the suffragettes who broke windows and burned letterboxes thought so.
Why must the penalty be accepted? Joseph Raz and others have pointed out that in
a genuinely unjust regime, accepting the punishment simply hands the regime
another prisoner and confers a legitimacy it has not earned. And the near-just
society condition looks strange applied to the American South in 1963, a
situation that was not near-just for the people conducting the campaign; King's
framing survives only if the relevant polity is the nation rather than the state,
which is precisely what was contested.

There is a deeper critique, aimed at the civility requirement itself. The
respectability conditions — be peaceful, be polite, be patient, accept the
handcuffs — were largely written by people who were not suffering the injustice
in question, and they have a convenient tendency to rule out any tactic
uncomfortable enough to work. Historians of the American movement have noted how
quickly a sanitized version of King displaced the actual record, in which the
marches were regarded at the time as dangerous provocations by a large majority
of the country, and in which their effectiveness depended precisely on producing
the crisis that the letter's critics wanted avoided.

## The Cases That Don't Fit

The frame was built for a particular kind of campaign: an identifiable excluded
group, a domestic government responsive to domestic opinion, and a law that could
be broken symbolically at moderate cost. A good deal of recent activism fits it
badly.

Climate protest raises the problem of scale and timing. The grievance is not a
statute that can be repealed by Tuesday but an accumulated global pattern, the
victims are largely unborn or foreign and cannot be enfranchised, and blocking a
motorway inconveniences commuters who are not the wrongdoers in any obvious
sense. Some activists have argued that the communicative model has been tried and
exhausted and that sabotage of infrastructure is the logical next step — which,
whatever its merits, is no longer civil disobedience as Rawls defined it, and its
defenders generally admit as much.

Leaking classified material raises the publicity problem in reverse. The
disclosure is public; the act of disclosure must be covert to succeed, and the
leaker often flees rather than accepts punishment. Whether that is disobedience
with the penalty clause stripped out, or an entirely different category of
political act, is genuinely unclear.

Animal rights activists who remove sick animals from farms and film themselves
doing it have tried to restore the classical form deliberately — open, filmed,
name given to the police, defended in court on the merits — which suggests the
old template still has pull even where it has no obvious application.

What has not changed is the structural bind. A person convinced that a law is
monstrous and that lawful means have failed has three options: comply, and
participate; resist covertly, and forfeit the appeal to shared principle; or
break the law in the open and take what follows. The third is the only one that
tries to have it both ways, and the reason it keeps being chosen is that it is
the only one that treats the people it is arguing with as capable of being
persuaded.
