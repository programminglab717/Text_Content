# What Do We Owe a River

In 2017 the New Zealand parliament passed a statute declaring the Whanganui
River a legal person. The river holds its own interests, has two appointed
guardians — one named by the Crown, one by the Maori iwi whose relationship with
the river the settlement recognized — and those guardians can go to court on its
behalf. The law was the end of a claim pursued for well over a century, and its
central move was to import into a Western legal system an idea the iwi had never
abandoned: that the river is not a resource but an ancestor, and that a person
and a river can stand in a relationship of obligation.

Lawyers found the statute odd and then found it workable, since corporations,
ships and trusts have been legal persons for a long time and nobody thinks a
shipping company has feelings. Philosophers found it harder to ignore. If a river
can hold interests that courts protect, the question of what actually has moral
standing — who or what can be wronged, rather than merely damaged — stops being
an academic exercise.

## Widening the Circle

Western moral philosophy spent most of its history assuming the answer was human
beings and stopping there. Nature mattered instrumentally, as the setting and
supply of human life, and the wrong in cutting down someone's orchard was a wrong
to the owner. The twentieth century opened this up from several directions at
once.

The first and most successful was sentience. Bentham had already suggested that
the question about animals is not whether they can reason or talk but whether
they can suffer, and Peter Singer built a systematic argument on exactly that
foundation: if pain is bad, it is bad wherever it occurs, and discounting it
because the sufferer belongs to another species is a prejudice structurally like
the ones we have already rejected. Tom Regan took a different route to a stricter
conclusion, arguing that mammals of a certain complexity are subjects of a life —
they have beliefs, desires, a welfare that matters to them — and therefore have
rights rather than merely interests to be weighed.

Sentience arguments are powerful and they are also strikingly limited as
environmental ethics. They say a great deal about factory farms and almost
nothing about a river, a wetland, an old-growth stand or a species. Most of what
conservationists care about is not sentient. Worse, a strict sentience view can
cut against ecological management: culling introduced deer to save a forest
causes suffering to beings that can suffer in order to protect beings that
cannot.

Biocentrism pushes the boundary outward again. Albert Schweitzer's reverence for
life and, more rigorously, Paul Taylor's argument that every organism is a
teleological centre of life — something with a good of its own, which can flourish
or be stunted, whether or not it knows it — extend standing to everything alive.
A tree can be harmed in a perfectly ordinary sense. The difficulty is that
nothing in the view tells you what to do when the goods of millions of organisms
conflict, which is every day, and any workable version has to smuggle back in a
ranking that the theory was built to avoid.

## The Last Man

The Australian philosopher Richard Routley proposed a thought experiment in the
early 1970s that has done more work than most arguments in the field. Imagine the
last human being alive, the rest of the species gone, and this last person
spends his final hours setting about the systematic destruction of every living
thing he can reach — felling trees, poisoning rivers, killing animals. No human
will ever be deprived of anything. No future generation will miss the forest.
There is no owner, no user, no spectator.

Most people react to the case with something stronger than distaste. If that
reaction is sound, then nature's value cannot be entirely a function of human
use, because in the last-man case there is no human use left to appeal to. Routley
took this as a demonstration that we need an environmental ethic properly so
called, rather than an extension of existing ethics to new objects.

The argument is an intuition pump and can be resisted. One might say the reaction
tracks a judgment about the man's character rather than about the forest, which
would make it a claim about virtue rather than standing. One might say the
scenario is too remote from anything to generate reliable intuitions. But the
argument establishes at least this much: a purely instrumental account of natural
value is a position with costs, and its defenders have to bite the last-man
bullet rather than pretend it isn't there.

## Wholes Instead of Individuals

Aldo Leopold, a forester who spent his career in American public land management
and wrote the essays collected as A Sand County Almanac, approached the question
from the field rather than the seminar. His famous account of watching a green
fire die in the eyes of a wolf he had shot — and of learning, over decades, that
a mountain stripped of its wolves is eaten bare by deer — is a lesson about
seeing the system rather than the animal. The land ethic he proposed enlarges the
boundaries of the community to include soils, waters, plants and animals, and
supplies the criterion that has organized ecocentric thought since: a thing is
right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability and beauty of the biotic
community, and wrong when it tends otherwise.

Holism of this kind is a better fit for conservation practice than any
individualist theory. It explains why the last twelve members of a species matter
more than twelve deer, why an intact system matters more than the sum of its
parts, why restoration is worth doing. It also has an alarming implication that
Tom Regan named bluntly as environmental fascism: if the community is what
matters and individuals are members whose value depends on their contribution,
then a superabundant species, including a human population, is a problem to be
reduced. Defenders such as J. Baird Callicott have argued that the land ethic
adds an obligation to the biotic community without cancelling the ones we already
have to each other, which is the right answer and does not by itself tell you
what to do when the obligations pull apart.

A quieter problem is that ecology stopped believing in the premise. Leopold's
"stability" reflected a mid-century picture of ecosystems tending toward balanced
climax states. The flux paradigm that replaced it describes systems in constant
disturbance, with no particular equilibrium to return to and no baseline that is
objectively the right one. If there is no natural state of a landscape, then
"integrity" is partly a choice about which past we prefer, which pulls the land
ethic back toward the human judgments it meant to get beyond.

## The People Who Aren't Born Yet

Much environmental argument actually concerns human beings, just ones who do not
exist yet, and this turns out to be philosophically treacherous.

The practical dispute is about discounting. Standard economic appraisal converts
future costs into present values at some rate, and small differences in that rate
produce enormous differences in what climate damage a century out is worth
spending to avoid. The argument is not really technical. A positive pure rate of
time preference says that harm to a person matters less because it occurs later,
which is hard to defend as an ethical claim once stated plainly, and most
economists who use one justify it instead by expected growth — our descendants
will be richer, so a dollar means less to them — or by the chance that the future
never arrives.

Derek Parfit identified a stranger problem. Policies that reshape a society also
reshape who gets conceived: different jobs, different movements, different
partners, different children. A generation living in a depleted world several
centuries hence would not exist at all had we chosen the other path. So they
cannot complain of having been made worse off, since their alternative was
non-existence. The non-identity problem does not show that ruining the future is
permissible. It shows that person-affecting moral principles — the ones that say
an act is wrong only if it is worse for someone — cannot deliver the verdict we
all want, and that the wrong must be located somewhere else: in the quality of
the world brought about, or in a duty owed to no one in particular.

## Why It Stays Hard

Even settled values run into the structure of the problems. Garrett Hardin's
tragedy of the commons described why individually rational herdsmen destroy a
shared pasture, and concluded that only private property or coercion could
prevent it. Elinor Ostrom spent a career documenting communities that had
disproved him — irrigation systems, fisheries, alpine meadows managed
sustainably for centuries under locally devised rules with local monitoring and
graduated sanctions — and identified the conditions that make such arrangements
hold. Those conditions include a bounded resource, a defined group, and the
ability to exclude outsiders, which is precisely what the atmosphere lacks.

Then there is the problem facing the individual. My flight makes no measurable
difference to the climate, and no honest accounting says otherwise. A morality
built on causing harm cannot easily condemn an act that causes none; one built on
integrity, complicity or what kind of person one is becoming can, which is part
of why virtue-based approaches have gained ground in the field. Meanwhile
environmental justice scholarship has kept pressing a question that the standing
debates tend to skip: whoever the forest belongs to morally, the costs of
protecting it and the costs of destroying it land on specific people, usually
poor ones, and an ethic that cannot say which of them should bear what has not
finished its work.
