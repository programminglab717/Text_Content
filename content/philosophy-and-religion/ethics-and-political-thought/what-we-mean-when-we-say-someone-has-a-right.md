# What We Mean When We Say Someone Has a Right

Two drivers arrive at the same parking space. One leans out of the window and
says, "I have a right to park here." The sentence sounds like a single claim.
It is at least four, and which of the four it turns out to be decides entirely
what the other driver owes in response. It might mean: I hold a permit for this
space, so you are obliged to leave it. It might mean: nothing forbids me from
parking here — though nothing forbids you either, and the space goes to whoever
arrives first. It might mean: I own this lot, and I can withdraw your permission
whenever I choose. Or it might mean: I hold a lease the owner cannot revoke, so
neither he nor you can shift me.

Four different relations, one word. An American legal scholar named Wesley
Newcomb Hohfeld noticed the confusion around the time of the First World War and
set about separating the strands, and his scheme has organized the subject ever
since. It is not a moral theory. It is closer to grammar: a set of categories for
saying precisely what is being asserted when someone asserts a right, so that
arguments stop sliding between senses halfway through.

## Four Kinds of Right

The first and strongest is the claim right. If I have a claim that you do
something, you have a duty to me to do it. The two are not separate facts but one
relation seen from opposite ends — my right that you repay the loan and your duty
to repay it are the same thing described from the creditor's side and the
debtor's. Claim rights are what people usually have in mind. They put somebody
under an obligation, and when the obligation is not met the right-holder has been
wronged, not merely disappointed.

The second is the liberty, or privilege. I have a liberty to do something when I
have no duty not to do it. Liberties are weaker than they sound, because a
liberty imposes nothing on anyone else. In a footrace each runner has a liberty
to win; none has a duty to let the others win, and no one is wronged by finishing
second. Hohfeld's term for what corresponds to a liberty on the other side is the
"no-right": you have no right that I refrain. A great deal of everyday rights
talk turns out to be liberty talk, which is why asserting it so often settles
nothing.

The third is the power: the capacity to change legal or moral positions, one's
own or someone else's. Making a will, signing a contract, marrying, firing an
employee, forgiving a debt — all exercises of power. What corresponds to a power
in the other party is liability, which here means no fault of any kind, only
susceptibility to having one's position altered.

The fourth is the immunity: not being liable to have one's position changed by
someone else. A constitutional guarantee is mostly an immunity. It does not so
much instruct the legislature as declare that certain legislative acts will not
take effect at all. Its correlative is disability.

Real rights come in bundles of these. Freedom of speech contains liberties (no
duty to stay silent), claims (against anyone who would forcibly gag you), and
immunities (against a legislature that would like the liberty to disappear).
Property in a house is a larger bundle still — powers to sell or lease, claims
against trespassers, liberties to paint the door any color, immunities against
arbitrary seizure — which is why a legal system can remove one strand, say the
power to sell, and leave the others standing. Tenancies, licences, conservation
easements and trust arrangements are all produced by subtracting particular
incidents from the full bundle and handing them to different people.

## Rights Are Relations, Not Possessions

Because claims and duties are one relation from two sides, any serious assertion
of a claim right has to answer a second question immediately: against whom? For
contracts the answer is easy. Almost everywhere else it is hard.

Rights get sorted into negative and positive, the negative requiring only that
others refrain and the positive requiring that others provide. The division is
useful and nowhere near as clean as its users assume. A right not to be robbed is
filed as negative, yet it is worth very little without police, courts, land
registries, prosecutors, prisons, and the tax system that pays for all of them.
Protecting holdings is expensive and continuously so; a state that enforces
property has not merely stepped back. Run the comparison the other way and a
right to schooling, usually filed as positive, can be stated as a claim against a
named ministry with a named budget line, which makes it no vaguer than a claim
in tort.

The harder problem is what Joel Feinberg called manifesto rights — declarations
that something ought to be everyone's, with no identified party under a duty to
supply it. Onora O'Neill has pressed this objection hard: if nobody in particular
is obliged, then nobody in particular has failed when the good fails to arrive,
and the right has no teeth to show. Defenders answer that such declarations set
the agenda for building the institutions that will eventually carry the duties.
That is a real function. It is just not the function of a claim.

## Where Rights Are Supposed to Come From

The seventeenth and eighteenth centuries produced the idea that certain rights
are natural: held in virtue of being human, prior to any state, and usable as a
standard against which states can be judged and, if it comes to it, dissolved.
The idea did heavy work in the American and French revolutions and drew a famous
reply from Bentham, who thought natural rights were nonsense and imprescriptible
natural rights nonsense upon stilts. Rights, on his account, are the children of
law, and talk of rights antecedent to law is an appetite wearing a costume. Burke
attacked the doctrine from a different angle, preferring the concrete inherited
liberties of Englishmen to any abstraction. Marx attacked it from a third,
arguing that the rights of man as declared were the rights of the isolated
proprietor, protecting separation rather than any shared life.

Natural rights returned after the Second World War in the vocabulary of human
rights, with the practical advantage of not having to win the metaphysical
argument first. The theoretical question survived anyway, and it has settled into
two camps about what a right is fundamentally for.

The will theory says a right is a protected choice. To have a right is to hold a
small-scale sovereignty: the standing to demand performance or to release the
other party from it. Waiver is the test of the thing. The trouble is that
infants, the comatose and animals then hold no rights at all, since they can
waive nothing, and inalienable rights become impossible by definition — a right
you could not surrender would not, on this view, be a right.

The interest theory says instead that a right exists when some aspect of a
person's well-being matters enough to place others under a duty. It handles
infants and inalienability without strain. Its difficulty runs the opposite way:
it threatens to hand rights to anyone who happens to benefit from a duty. If I
promise your mother I will look after you, you benefit, but the promise was made
to her, and most people's sense of the case is that she is the one wronged when I
break it.

## Collisions

Whatever their source, rights are built to resist. Ronald Dworkin's image of
rights as trumps catches the point: a right beats ordinary appeals to aggregate
welfare, so demonstrating that a policy would leave most people slightly better
off is not yet an answer to the person whose right it overrides.

Trumps still lose to other trumps. Expression runs into reputation, the press
runs into privacy, religious liberty runs into anti-discrimination law, and a
defendant's right to confront witnesses runs into a witness's safety. Three broad
responses are on offer. Absolutists hold that a small set of rights — against
torture, say — admits no exceptions at any stakes, and swallow the hard cases
that follow. Threshold theorists hold that rights hold out against consequences
up to some catastrophic level and then give way, which preserves the intuition
at the cost of explaining where the line falls and why. Specificationists deny
that genuine rights ever conflict, arguing that a right correctly described
already contains its own exceptions, so that free speech was never a right to
publish troop movements in wartime.

There is also a worry about inflation. Every interest that gets renamed a right
borrows the word's force, and a currency issued freely enough stops buying much.
The complaint is easy to overstate — most historical expansions of rights
language were expansions of who counted as a person — but the observation
underneath it holds. Rights are one specific moral instrument, not the whole of
morality. They are claimable, they belong to somebody in particular, they can be
insisted upon rudely and without gratitude, and they do not require that anyone
like you or approve of what you are doing with them. That last feature is exactly
what makes them valuable to people with no other leverage, and exactly why the
vocabulary keeps getting picked up by the next group with a grievance and nothing
else to hand.
