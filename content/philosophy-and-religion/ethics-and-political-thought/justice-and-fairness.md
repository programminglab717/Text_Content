# Justice and Fairness

Two children and one cake. The rule that solves it is older than any philosophy:
one cuts, the other chooses. Nobody has to agree in advance on what an equal
share looks like, nobody has to trust the cutter's character, and nobody has to
adjudicate afterward. The procedure is built so that the person with the power to
be unfair has no motive to use it. That is an extraordinary amount of work for
one sentence to do, and it is the reason the example turns up in almost every
serious treatment of the subject.

It also shows the limits of procedure straight away. Cut-and-choose assumes the
cake arrived from nowhere, that both children have equal claim, that neither is
diabetic or starving, and that cake is the only thing being divided. Change any
assumption and the clean answer dissolves. Most real questions about justice are
arguments about exactly those assumptions, conducted in the vocabulary of shares.

## The Formula That Says Almost Nothing

Aristotle gave the standard formal account: treat like cases alike, and unlike
cases in proportion to their relevant difference. It is correct and it is nearly
empty, because everything hangs on which differences count as relevant. Two
applicants differ in test scores, in wealth, in the schools they attended, in how
early someone noticed they were good at something. Which of those licenses
different treatment?

The formal principle does real work in one direction: it puts the burden of proof
on whoever wants to treat people differently. An unexplained inequality is
presumptively unjust, and the defender must name the difference doing the work
and show it is the right kind. Much moral progress has consisted of a difference
that everyone had treated as obviously relevant — sex, ancestry, religion,
legitimacy of birth — failing to survive that demand once it was actually made.
But the principle cannot generate substance on its own. For that you need a
theory of what people are owed, and there are only a few families of answer.

## Rawls's Construction

The most influential modern attempt asks what principles people would choose to
govern the basic structure of society — the constitution, the property system,
the family, the main institutions that shape life chances — if they did not know
who in that society they were going to be. Strip away knowledge of your class,
your race and sex, your natural talents, your generation, even your conception of
what makes a life worth living. What remains is a chooser who cannot rig the
rules in favor of a position they might not occupy.

John Rawls argued that such choosers would not gamble. Facing a one-shot,
irreversible choice with no probabilities to work from and a great deal at stake,
they would reason in a maximin fashion: rank the options by their worst possible
outcome and take the option whose worst outcome is best. You choose the society
you would be willing to enter blindfolded.

What they would choose, on his account, is two principles, and the order between
them matters as much as their content. The first secures an extensive scheme of
equal basic liberties — conscience, expression, the person, political
participation — for everyone. The second governs social and economic inequality:
positions must be open to all under fair equality of opportunity, and remaining
inequalities are justified only if they work to the greatest benefit of the least
advantaged. The first principle takes strict priority. No amount of economic gain
buys a reduction in anyone's basic liberty, which is what separates this from a
utilitarian scheme that would trade the two against each other whenever the
exchange rate looked good.

The second half of the second principle — the difference principle — is the part
that provoked forty years of argument. It does not require equality. It permits
surgeons to earn more than clerks, provided the arrangement genuinely leaves the
worst-off better than a flatter one would. What it denies is that anyone has a
prior claim on the rewards their talents command. Rawls's reasoning is that the
distribution of native ability is a fact of natural lottery, morally arbitrary,
nobody's achievement; and the capacity for hard work and discipline is itself
largely a product of family and circumstance that the person did not select. If
neither your talents nor your work ethic is something you earned, the returns
they generate cannot be yours by desert. They can be yours by agreement, under
rules everyone could accept, which is a different and weaker claim.

## The Entitlement Reply

Robert Nozick's objection was that this whole way of thinking about justice looks
at the wrong thing. It evaluates distributions — snapshots of who has what — when
justice is a matter of history. If people acquired their holdings justly and
transferred them by voluntary exchange or gift, the resulting distribution is
just however lumpy it looks, and if they did not, no amount of surface tidiness
redeems it.

His argument for this is the Wilt Chamberlain case, and it is more awkward than
its critics usually allow. Start from whatever distribution you consider perfectly
just. Now a basketball player is so good that a million people each happily drop
a small extra sum in a separate box at the gate, knowing it goes to him. Nobody
was coerced, nobody was defrauded, every individual transaction was voluntary and
each party preferred the trade to not trading. At the end of the season the
distribution is badly skewed. Which step was unjust? Nozick's conclusion: any
patterned principle of justice — to each according to need, or merit, or the
difference principle — can be maintained only by continuous interference with
what people freely choose to do with what they legitimately hold.

The replies are several and none is knockdown. The pattern being disturbed is a
pattern of the basic structure, not of every individual holding, so a tax scheme
adjusts the rules of the game rather than confiscating a particular ticket sale.
No one buying a ticket consented to the aggregate effect of a million such
purchases, and the aggregate is what shapes the next generation's opportunities.
And the theory's own foundation is fragile: it stands on a principle of just
original acquisition applied to a world in which the actual chain of title runs
through conquest, enslavement, and expropriation, so the historical theory
condemns nearly every existing holding by its own standard.

## Luck, Desert, and the Trouble with Both

A large body of later work took Rawls's observation about arbitrary luck and made
it the whole theory. If inequality traceable to circumstance is unjust and
inequality traceable to choice is acceptable, then justice means neutralizing
brute luck while letting people bear the consequences of their own gambles. This
is elegant and it has a cruel edge. Applied strictly, it means the uninsured
motorcyclist who chose to ride without a helmet has no claim on assistance, and
it makes the welfare system into a tribunal that must decide who deserves help by
investigating how each person came to need it. Elizabeth Anderson pressed exactly
this in an essay asking what the point of equality is, arguing that the aim is
not to equalize fortune but to end oppression — to secure the standing of
everyone as an equal in a shared political community — and that a system which
demands people demonstrate their own blamelessness to get bread has already
failed at that.

The opposite pull comes from desert, which almost everyone believes in and few
can defend cleanly. The intuition is that people who work harder should get more,
and it survives Rawls's argument better in practice than in theory, because what
people actually resent is not inequality as such but inequality that seems
unconnected to contribution. The difficulty is that desert claims need a
background of institutions to be meaningful. What a surgeon deserves depends on
what medicine pays, which depends on how the training system and the insurance
market and the licensing rules were built — all of which are the very
arrangements whose justice was in question. Desert cannot be the criterion for
designing institutions when it only acquires content after they exist.

## What Is It That Should Be Equal

The last and least tidy question is what should be measured. Income is a poor
proxy: two people with identical salaries are not equally well placed if one
uses a wheelchair in a city built with stairs. Amartya Sen argued that the
relevant currency is capability — what a person is actually able to do and to be,
given both their resources and their ability to convert resources into
functioning. On that view a just society equalizes real freedom to live in the
ways one has reason to value, not the stack of goods on the table.

Michael Walzer pushed in a different direction, suggesting there is no single
currency at all. Different goods — medical care, education, political office,
love, hard work, divine grace — belong to different spheres, each with its own
internal logic of distribution. Injustice in his account is mostly domination
across spheres: money, which properly buys commodities, reaching into the sphere
of health or justice or political power and buying those too. What a society
forbids to be bought is a sharper portrait of its values than anything it says
about equality in the abstract.
