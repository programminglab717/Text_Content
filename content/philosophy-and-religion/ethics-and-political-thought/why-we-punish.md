# Why We Punish

A judge says "four years," a clerk types it, and a person is taken away and kept
in a small room for four years against his will. Strip the robes and the
procedure off that sentence and what remains is a set of acts that the same legal
system forbids absolutely to everyone else. Confining someone is kidnapping.
Taking his money is theft. In jurisdictions that retain capital punishment the
state does the one thing it punishes most severely when anyone else does it.
Punishment is the deliberate imposition of suffering on a person by people who
have the option of not imposing it, carried out calmly, in advance-planned
quantities, by officials who bear the offender no grudge.

That is the puzzle, and it is not a rhetorical one. Every other institution can
be defended by pointing at the good it does; punishment has to be defended
against the presumption that hurting people is wrong, and the defence has to
survive the observation that the person being hurt is, by the time we hurt him,
usually not dangerous at that moment and often not going to reoffend. Two
families of answer have been in contention for about three centuries, and neither
has ever managed to eliminate the other.

## Looking Forward

The consequentialist answer is the tidier of the two. Bentham stated it without
ornament: all punishment is in itself evil, and is admissible only insofar as it
promises to exclude some greater evil. The suffering of the offender goes on the
ledger as a cost. The system is justified only if the crimes prevented outweigh
it. This has the attractive implication that punishment should be as mild as will
do the job, and that anything beyond the minimum is gratuitous cruelty rather
than firmness.

Prevention works through three channels. General deterrence aims at everybody
else: the threat of the sentence is supposed to alter the calculations of people
who have not yet offended. Specific deterrence aims at the person punished.
Incapacitation does not rely on anyone's calculations at all — a person in a cell
is not burgling houses, whatever he intends.

The empirical record is uneven in ways that ought to constrain policy more than
it does. Beccaria argued in the eighteenth century that the certainty of
punishment deters far more effectively than its severity, and two centuries of
research have broadly confirmed him. Raising the probability of being caught
changes behaviour; lengthening sentences, past a fairly low point, changes it
much less, in part because the people most likely to offend are the least likely
to be discounting the future carefully. Incapacitation does reduce crime while it
lasts, but it runs into the age-crime curve: offending peaks in the late teens
and early twenties and falls away sharply, so long sentences spend most of their
duration confining men well past the age at which they were dangerous.

Rehabilitation is the third forward-looking justification and has had the most
turbulent history. Through the middle of the twentieth century it dominated
practice in much of the English-speaking world, producing indeterminate sentences,
parole boards and a therapeutic vocabulary in which prisons were treatment. The
model was battered in the 1970s by evaluation research, popularly summarized as
"nothing works," and by a civil-libertarian critique that noticed what
open-ended treatment did to the length of confinement — a man could be held until
the experts pronounced him cured, which for some diagnoses meant indefinitely.
The pendulum swung to determinate sentencing and desert. Later and more careful
evaluation has shown that some programmes, well-targeted, do reduce reoffending;
the slogan was always cruder than the evidence. But the institutional damage was
done, and few systems have gone back.

## Looking Backward

The retributivist answer rejects the ledger entirely. On this account punishment
is justified because it is deserved, not because it produces anything. The
offender has taken an unfair advantage, or violated a standing of equality
between himself and his victim, and the penalty restores what he disturbed.
Kant pushed this to its limit with a thought experiment about an island society
dissolving itself: even then, he said, the last murderer in prison must be
executed first, so that each has what his deeds deserve and no blood-guilt
attaches to those who let him go. Whatever one thinks of the conclusion, the
structure is clear. Desert is not a means to anything.

Retributivism has one very large advantage: it explains the limits. Because the
penalty is owed to the offender rather than extracted from him for society's
benefit, it cannot exceed what he deserves, no matter how much crime a harsher
sentence might prevent. Proportionality falls out of the theory instead of being
bolted on. It also explains why we do not punish the insane, the coerced or the
very young, categories that a pure prevention theory handles awkwardly at best.

Its difficulty is the one every schoolchild raises. How does adding a second
quantity of suffering to the world repair the first? The murdered are still
murdered. If nobody is deterred, nobody protected, nobody reformed, nobody
consoled, what exactly has been accomplished by the offender's misery? Sophisticated
retributivists answer in terms of annulling an unfair advantage or reasserting
the equal standing the crime denied, and these are serious answers, but the
metaphors of balance and restoration do a lot of heavy lifting in them.

## The Innocent Man

The sharpest objection to pure consequentialism is old and simple. If punishment
is justified by its effects, then framing an innocent person should be justified
whenever it produces the same effects — a public execution during a panic,
convincing everyone that the danger is past, with no one the wiser. The inputs
are the same and the outputs are better, since the guilty party, being at large,
will offend again but the innocent one will not.

The standard reply, developed most carefully by Rawls in an early paper, works on
two levels. The question "why have an institution of punishment?" is answered by
consequences; the question "why punish this man?" is answered by the rules of the
institution, which specify guilt. A system in which officials were authorized to
convict the innocent whenever it seemed to pay would not remain secret, would
destroy the trust that makes the institution work, and would on consequentialist
grounds be worse than one with a firm guilt requirement. This is a good answer
and not a complete one, since it concedes that in the rare case where secrecy
held, the framing would have been right.

H. L. A. Hart's response was to stop demanding that one theory do every job. He
separated the general justifying aim of the practice from the principles that
distribute it. The point of having punishment at all is the reduction of crime;
the constraints on who may be punished and how much are matters of desert and
fairness, and they are not derived from the aim. Most working legal systems are
mixed theories of roughly this shape, whether or not anyone in them has heard of
Hart.

## What a Sentence Says

A third tradition holds that both camps have missed the obvious feature of
punishment, which is that it means something. Joel Feinberg pointed out that
hard treatment and condemnation are separable — a fine and a tax can take
identical sums, and the difference is that one brands you — and that punishment's
distinctive work is expressive. It is how a community says, in a voice loud
enough to be heard by the victim, that what happened was not tolerated.

Antony Duff has developed the strongest version, in which punishment is a
communication addressed to the offender as a fellow citizen: a secular penance
intended to bring him to understand what he did, express that understanding, and
be reconciled with the community that imposed it. The theory is appealing and
carries an uncomfortable implication, which Duff accepts. If punishment is a
conversation between a community and its member, it presupposes that the
community has standing to conduct it — that it has not itself abandoned the
person it is now addressing. Sentencing a man raised in conditions the state
tolerated, for an offence largely produced by them, is not obviously a
conversation between equals.

A related movement argues that the whole framing is wrong because it puts the
state at the centre and leaves the victim as a witness. Restorative practices —
conferences bringing offender, victim and affected family together, mediated
agreements about repair, community panels — treat crime as a rupture between
people rather than a breach of a rule owed to the Crown or the People. They
report high satisfaction from victims, who frequently want an explanation and an
acknowledgement more than they want a longer sentence, and modest reductions in
reoffending. Their limits are equally clear. They depend on an offender who
admits the act, a victim willing to be in the room, and an offence of a kind
where repair is imaginable, which excludes a good deal of serious violence. And
the sceptical question lingers: if a restorative conference is voluntary, it may
be too weak to count as punishment at all, and if it is compulsory, it is
punishment with a friendlier vocabulary.

That gap between the theories and the institutions is where the subject actually
lives. Almost no criminal case is resolved by the solemn public weighing any of
these accounts imagines; most are settled by plea bargains negotiated in
corridors under the shadow of a much longer sentence nobody expects to be
imposed. Conviction carries consequences that no judge pronounces and no theory
justifies: lost housing, lost employment, lost voting rights, lost custody,
running for decades after the stated penalty has been served. Any defence of
punishment has to be a defence of the thing that happens, not the thing described
in the statute, and on that measure the gap between what we say we are doing and
what we do is wider than the gap between any two of the theories.
