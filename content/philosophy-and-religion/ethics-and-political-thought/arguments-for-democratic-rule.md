# Arguments for Democratic Rule

Nobody puts surgery to a vote. If you are lying on the table with a hot appendix,
the question of where to cut is not settled by polling the waiting room, and the
suggestion that it should be sounds not merely inefficient but grotesque. Plato
built an entire indictment of Athenian democracy out of that intuition. A ship,
he pointed out, needs a navigator who has studied winds and stars, and the crew
who shout him down and seize the tiller will drown whatever their numbers.
Governing is a craft. Crafts have experts. Handing the tiller to everyone,
including the people who have never thought about it for ten consecutive minutes,
looks like a category error dressed up as fairness.

The objection has never gone away, and it has been sharpened considerably by
survey research on what voters actually know. Sizeable fractions of electorates
in well-established democracies cannot name the parties in their own governing
coalition, have no idea which level of government controls which budget, and hold
views on foreign policy that do not survive contact with a map. Some
contemporary philosophers, Jason Brennan among the most direct, have argued that
this should embarrass us into considering epistocracy — rule weighted toward the
politically competent. If the case for democracy rests on the wisdom of the
voters, it looks shaky. So the interesting question is whether it rests there at
all.

## Counting Heads as a Way of Finding Things Out

One family of defences accepts the terms of the challenge and argues that
democracies decide better, not worse. The clean version comes from Condorcet's
jury theorem, worked out in the late eighteenth century. Suppose a yes-or-no
question has a correct answer, and suppose each voter is slightly more likely
than a coin to get it right. Then the probability that a majority is right climbs
steeply with the size of the group, and for large electorates it approaches
certainty. The mathematics is unimpeachable. The assumptions are the problem.
Voters must be better than chance, which fails for any question on which a whole
society is systematically misled. They must vote independently, which fails badly
in a world of parties, broadcasters and social networks that correlate everyone's
errors at once. And they must vote for what they think is true rather than for
what suits them.

A sturdier version drops the arithmetic and looks at what many heads contribute
that one cannot. Aristotle made the point with an image of a potluck feast: a
meal to which many contribute can be better than one funded by a single purse,
because each guest brings something the others lacked. A large assembly pools
dispersed information about how policies land — which regulation is unworkable in
practice, which shortage is real, which official is corrupt — and no expert, by
definition, has access to that except through the people living it. Work on
collective problem-solving by Scott Page and others has formalized a related
idea: for hard problems, a group of diverse but individually mediocre
problem-solvers can outperform a group of the individually best, because the best
tend to make the same mistakes as each other. Whether the formal results transfer
to electoral politics is contested. The underlying observation — that expertise
narrows the range of considered options as well as improving the analysis of
them — is not seriously in doubt.

A third epistemic claim concerns talking rather than counting. Voting
aggregates preferences that people brought with them; deliberation changes them.
Arguments that cannot be stated publicly without embarrassment tend to lose
force when they have to be stated publicly, which filters out naked
self-interest and leaves reasons that at least purport to be general.
Experiments with citizens' assemblies — randomly selected panels given time,
expert briefings and a facilitator — repeatedly find that participants shift
their positions, become markedly better informed, and produce recommendations
more coherent than anything a referendum on the same question yields. The
qualification is that these conditions are the opposite of ordinary electoral
politics, where deliberation happens in short bursts, under adversarial
incentives, among people who will never meet. Whether the assembly result is
evidence about democracy or evidence about small well-run groups remains open.

## The Argument From Self-Defence

A blunter defence gives up on truth-tracking and concentrates on damage control.
The nineteenth-century utilitarians made it plainly: no class can be trusted with
the interests of another class, and the only reliable protection for a group's
interests is that the group holds a vote. The point is not that voters are wise
but that rulers, however wise, will not reliably attend to people who cannot
remove them.

The evidence for this is stronger than for any epistemic claim. Amartya Sen's
observation that substantial famines do not occur in functioning democracies with
a free press is the sharpest version — not because voters understand agricultural
economics, but because a government facing an election and an uncensored
newspaper cannot afford to let a region starve quietly. Karl Popper reduced the
whole case to its minimum: the virtue of democratic institutions is that they
allow bad rulers to be removed without bloodshed. That is a low bar and an
enormous achievement. Most political systems in history have had no mechanism for
replacing a leadership short of death, coup or civil war, and the transaction
costs of those methods are paid by everyone.

Notice that this argument is indifferent to voter competence in one specific way.
To throw out a government you do not need a theory of monetary policy. You need
to know that things have got worse and that the people in charge were in charge
while they did. Retrospective voting of that crude kind is within almost
everyone's reach, and it is enough to keep rulers nervous.

There is a related claim about what voting does to voters rather than to
rulers. John Stuart Mill thought the strongest reason for extending the franchise
was educative: a person who has to form a view about a public question, defend it
to neighbours, and live with the result acquires habits of judgment that a
subject never develops. On this account the polling station and the jury box are
schools, and the point of participating is partly what participation makes of the
participant. The claim is empirical and only patchily supported — plenty of long
established electorates show no obvious civic flowering — but it identifies
something the other defences miss. A population that has never decided anything
is not merely unrepresented; it is unpracticed, and hands power back the moment
it is offered.

## The Argument From Equality

Both defences so far are instrumental: democracy is good because of what it
produces. If some better-producing arrangement came along, they would have
nothing to say against it. A second family of arguments holds that democracy is
required by the standing of citizens as equals, whatever it produces.

The starting point is that nobody is born with authority over anybody else.
Political power has to be explained, and the explanations that once did the
work — divine appointment, bloodline, natural hierarchy of capacity — have
collapsed one after another. Given that people disagree permanently and in good
faith about justice itself, some procedure must settle what will be done.
Jeremy Waldron has pressed the point that disagreement is not a temporary defect
to be cleared away by better reasoning; it is the condition politics operates in.
A procedure that gives each person's judgment equal weight treats the
disagreement honestly. A procedure that assigns extra weight to some people's
judgment has to justify that assignment to everyone else, and since people
disagree about who the competent are as much as they disagree about everything
else, the justification cannot be given without begging the question.

There is a further point about how being ruled feels and what it means. Thomas
Christiano has argued that public equality matters: it is not enough that my
interests are considered, they must be seen to be considered on the same footing
as everyone else's. Philip Pettit's republican tradition frames the same concern
as non-domination. A benevolent autocrat who happens to do what you want still
holds you at his pleasure, and living at someone's pleasure is a standing
condition of subordination even in the years when he is kind. Voting, on this
account, is less about controlling outcomes than about not being anyone's
subject.

## Where the Arguments Run Out

The defences do not all cover the same ground, and knowing which one you are
using matters, because each has its own failure mode.

The epistemic arguments have thresholds. If voters are systematically worse than
chance on a question — because the issue is technical and the misinformation is
well funded — the jury theorem runs in reverse and large majorities become
reliably wrong. That is an argument for insulating some decisions from direct
majority control, which is what central banks, courts and expert agencies are,
and it is why nearly every functioning democracy is a hybrid rather than a pure
one.

The protective argument justifies removability rather than participation, and it
is satisfied by fairly thin institutions. It supplies no reason for referendums,
citizens' assemblies or anything else beyond a regular chance to eject the
incumbents.

The equality arguments are the most robust against evidence about voter
ignorance, since they do not depend on outcomes at all, but they inherit the
boundary problem. Democracy says decisions belong to the people; it does not say
who the people are. The question of who counts as a member cannot be answered
democratically without assuming an answer, and most of the ugliest disputes in
modern politics — over secession, immigration, devolution, the franchise itself —
live in exactly that gap. The equality arguments also say nothing about a
majority that votes coherently and permanently to subordinate a minority. Every
serious democrat has therefore had to bolt on constraints that are not themselves
democratic: entrenched rights, judicial review, federal structures, supermajority
requirements for constitutional change.

What survives all this is less a proof than a convergence. Several independent
lines of argument, resting on different premises and vulnerable to different
objections, arrive at roughly the same institutional recommendation. That is
usually a sign that a practice is doing more than one job at once, which also
explains why particular reforms keep splitting people who agree about the
fundamentals. A sortition chamber looks excellent by the epistemic argument and
indifferent by the protective one. Compulsory voting looks good on equality
grounds and questionable on epistemic ones. Argue about the reform long enough
and you will find you are arguing about which justification you were relying on
all along.
