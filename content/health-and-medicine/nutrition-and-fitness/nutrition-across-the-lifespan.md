# Nutrition Across the Lifespan

Human milk is not one substance. In the first days after birth it is colostrum,
thick and yellowish, low in volume and loaded with immune proteins. Over the
following weeks it thins and sweetens. Within a single feed it changes again:
the milk that comes first is watery, and the fat content climbs steadily as the
breast empties, so that the end of a feed delivers something closer to cream
than to the drink it started as. Composition also drifts across months, tracking
a baby whose needs are moving. A food that reformulates itself on that schedule
is a good reminder that nutritional requirements are not a fixed list. They are
a moving target, and the shape of the target changes more across a human life
than most people assume.

What follows is a walk along that trajectory, stage by stage, with attention to
what actually changes and why.

## The First Year

An infant is the most metabolically demanding version of a person, per unit of
body weight, that will ever exist. Energy requirements per kilogram run several
times an adult's, partly because growth itself is expensive and partly because a
baby's brain consumes an extraordinary share of resting metabolism — a
proportion that stays high through early childhood and only falls toward adult
values in the school years. Small stomachs must therefore deliver a great deal
of energy, which is why infant diets are unusually fat-rich and why the low-fat
principles that govern adult public health advice do not apply to the very
young.

Milk covers nearly everything for the first half-year, with two conspicuous
exceptions. It contains very little vitamin K, and a newborn gut has not yet
been colonized by the bacteria that make some, which is the reason for the
vitamin K injection given shortly after birth in most maternity units: without
it, a small number of infants suffer bleeding that is otherwise entirely
preventable. Vitamin D is also scarce in milk, which is why supplementation of
breastfed infants is routine in many countries, particularly at high latitudes.

Iron is the other pressure point, and it is a matter of timing. A fetus loads up
on iron during the last weeks of pregnancy, building a store meant to carry it
through early infancy. Milk contains little iron, though what it has is absorbed
unusually well. The stores run down somewhere around the middle of the first
year, which is roughly when solid food is introduced, and this is not a
coincidence so much as a constraint the weaning process has to respect. Babies
born preterm start with a smaller reserve and run out sooner.

Weaning does more than add nutrients. It is a period of extraordinary sensory
learning, when a child encounters textures, temperatures and flavours for the
first time and builds the repertoire of things it recognizes as food. Flavours
from the mother's diet already reach the infant through amniotic fluid and milk,
so the process starts earlier than the first spoonful. Toddlers then pass
through a reliable phase of neophobia, rejecting unfamiliar foods on sight, and
acceptance generally comes through repeated low-pressure exposure rather than
through any single successful meal.

## Growing

Childhood is a long stretch of modest, steady demand punctuated by a sharp
spike. Between the toddler years and puberty, growth is slow and appetite tracks
it reasonably well. Then the adolescent growth spurt arrives and the accounting
changes abruptly. A teenager in peak growth is laying down bone, muscle and
blood volume simultaneously, and requirements for calcium, protein and iron all
climb at once. The skeletal demand is particularly concentrated: a large fraction
of the bone mass a person will carry for life is deposited in these few years,
and it cannot be made up later on the same terms.

Iron deserves separate mention because the two sexes diverge here. Boys gaining
muscle and blood volume need more of it during the spurt and then settle back.
Girls acquire an ongoing monthly loss that persists for decades, which is why
iron deficiency is common in adolescent and adult women and uncommon in adult
men. It is one of the few nutritional requirements where the difference between
sexes is large rather than marginal.

Adolescence is also when eating becomes substantially self-directed for the
first time, and when the gap widens between what a body needs and what a person
chooses. Requirements are at their highest exactly when supervision is at its
lowest.

## Building Someone Else

Pregnancy is the clearest case of a requirement that cannot be met after the
fact. The neural tube — the structure that becomes the brain and spinal cord —
closes within the first month of gestation, often before a pregnancy is
recognized. Adequate folate at that moment substantially reduces the risk of
defects in that closure, and no amount of folate in the fifth month can
retroactively help. That single piece of timing is why many countries fortify
flour with folic acid rather than relying on individual supplementation: it
delivers the nutrient to people who do not yet know they need it.

Iodine works on a similarly unforgiving schedule. Thyroid hormone governs fetal
brain development, and iodine is the raw material thyroid hormone is built from.
Severe deficiency during pregnancy was, for most of history, a leading
preventable cause of intellectual disability, endemic in mountainous and
flood-leached inland regions where soils held little iodine. Salt iodization
addressed it at population scale with striking effectiveness, and remains one of
the most cost-effective public health interventions ever deployed.

Iron demand rises steeply as maternal blood volume expands and the fetus builds
its own stores. Energy demand, by contrast, rises far less than the phrase
"eating for two" suggests — the increase is modest for most of gestation, and
concentrated in the later months. Lactation is in fact the more energetically
expensive stage, and the one where fluid requirements climb noticeably.

## The Long Plateau

Adult nutritional requirements are strikingly boring, and that is the
interesting part. From the twenties onward the body is not building anything new
at scale; it is maintaining, replacing and repairing. Micronutrient needs settle
into a flat stretch that lasts for decades.

Energy expenditure was long assumed to decline gradually across this period, the
familiar story of a metabolism slowing from thirty onward. Careful measurement
of total energy expenditure using doubly labelled water has complicated that
picture: once body composition is accounted for, expenditure appears remarkably
stable through middle adulthood, declining only later in life. What changes in
the meantime is mostly the composition of the body itself — lean tissue slowly
giving way to fat if nothing intervenes — and the amount of movement a person
does. The metabolism is not betraying anyone. The inputs are changing.

The real nutritional story of adulthood is cumulative rather than acute.
Deficiency diseases are rare in well-fed populations; what accumulates instead
is arterial damage, insulin resistance and weight, over timescales of decades,
in response to patterns of eating no single meal of which was remarkable.

## The Last Third

Old age reverses many of the assumptions of the plateau. Appetite tends to
decline, sometimes markedly — the phenomenon has its own name, the anorexia of
ageing — driven by slower gastric emptying, shifts in the hormones that signal
fullness, and blunted taste and smell. Food simply becomes less interesting at
the same time that it becomes more important to eat well.

The nutrient that matters most in this stretch is protein, for a reason that is
not obvious. Older muscle displays anabolic resistance: a given dose of protein
triggers less muscle protein synthesis than the same dose does in a younger
person. Offsetting that requires a larger dose per meal, at a stage of life when
total intake is falling. The result is a squeeze, and on the wrong side of it
lies sarcopenia — the progressive loss of muscle mass and, more consequentially,
muscle power, which is what determines whether someone can rise from a chair or
catch themselves on a stumble.

Absorption changes too. Stomach acid production falls with age and with atrophic
gastritis, and acid is needed to free vitamin B12 from the proteins it is bound
to in food. Deficiency can therefore develop on a diet that contains plenty of
B12, since the vitamin is present but not released. Crystalline B12 from
supplements and fortified foods is not protein-bound and is absorbed normally,
which is why supplementation works even when the underlying problem is
digestive. Skin synthesis of vitamin D declines with age as well, compounded by
spending less time outside.

Taken together these shifts produce a particular kind of difficulty. Energy
needs fall, because there is less lean tissue burning fuel and less movement
being done, while requirements for protein, calcium, B12 and several other
nutrients hold steady or rise. The quantity of food goes down; the amount of
nourishment that has to fit inside it does not. Every mouthful has to carry more
than it did at thirty. A diet that was perfectly adequate in middle age can
become quietly deficient in old age without a single thing about it changing,
simply because there is less of it. Dietitians describe this as a rising demand
for nutrient density, and it is the reason that advice aimed at older adults so
often runs opposite to advice aimed at everyone else — more protein, more fat in
some cases, less emphasis on restriction, more on getting enough at all.

Thirst is the quietest of these changes. The sensation blunts, so older adults
are less likely to feel thirsty at a given degree of dehydration, and the kidney
concentrates urine less efficiently. Dehydration then arrives without the
warning signal that would ordinarily prompt a drink, and it shows up in
emergency departments as confusion, falls and low blood pressure far more often
than as any recognizable complaint about thirst.

Layered on all of it are things that have nothing to do with physiology: poor
dentition making chewing painful, arthritis making cooking difficult, the loss
of a spouse removing the social occasion that structured meals, a fixed income
narrowing what gets bought. A great deal of malnutrition in old age has less to
do with what the body requires than with what the week makes possible.
