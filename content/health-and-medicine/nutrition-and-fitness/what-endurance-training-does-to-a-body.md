# What Endurance Training Does to a Body

Two people lie on a bed in a quiet room. One has a resting pulse of seventy, the
other of forty-two. Both are delivering roughly the same amount of blood to their
tissues per minute, because lying still is not metabolically demanding and the
requirement is fixed by the body, not by the heart. The difference is that the
second heart moves considerably more blood per beat and can therefore afford to
beat less often. That single fact — a larger stroke volume — is the most visible
result of months of aerobic training, and it is only the outermost layer of what
has changed.

Endurance training is not one adaptation. It is a stack of them, occurring on
wildly different timescales in different tissues, and the reason a training
programme is organized the way it is has a great deal to do with which of these
processes is fast and which is slow.

## The Cascade

Oxygen has to travel from the atmosphere to the inside of a mitochondrion, and
at every stage of that journey there is a potential bottleneck. Air is moved into
the lungs. Oxygen diffuses across the alveolar membrane into blood. Haemoglobin
binds it. The heart pumps that blood to working muscle. Oxygen leaves the
capillary, crosses the interstitial space and the muscle fibre membrane, and is
finally consumed by the respiratory chain.

The total rate at which a body can run this cascade at maximum effort is its
maximal oxygen uptake, and it decomposes neatly: the volume of blood the heart
delivers per minute, multiplied by the difference in oxygen content between
arterial blood going in and venous blood coming back. Raise either term and the
maximum rises.

It is worth noticing which stage is not usually the bottleneck. In most people
the lungs are substantially overbuilt for the task; arterial blood leaves them
nearly saturated even at maximal effort, and breathing hard is a sensation rather
than a limit. Training barely changes lung volume at all. The constraint sits
mostly in delivery — in how much blood the heart can move — and secondarily in
how thoroughly the muscle can strip oxygen out of that blood. Only in highly
trained athletes, whose cardiac output is so large that blood spends very little
time in the pulmonary capillaries, does the lung start to lose ground.

## The First Weeks Are Plumbing

The earliest changes have nothing to do with muscle. Within a few days of
starting regular endurance work, the kidney begins retaining more sodium and
water and the liver produces more albumin, drawing fluid into the circulation.
Plasma volume expands, measurably, within a week.

This is a remarkably effective adaptation for its cost. More plasma means more
blood returning to the heart between beats, which stretches the ventricle more
and, by the ordinary mechanics of cardiac muscle, makes the next contraction
eject more. Stroke volume rises before the heart itself has changed shape at all.
It also gives the body a larger fluid reservoir to sweat from and improves the
capacity to dump heat.

It produces one confusing side effect. Plasma expands faster than red cell mass
does, so haemoglobin concentration in a blood sample falls, and a newly trained
athlete can look mildly anaemic on paper while carrying more total haemoglobin
than before. The concentration went down; the absolute quantity went up.

Plasma volume is also the first thing lost. A week of bed rest, or an
interrupted season, and much of it is gone — which is why a layoff feels
disproportionately costly in the first sessions back, and why a good deal of the
initial return is quicker than the original build.

## The Heart Changes Shape

Over months, the left ventricle remodels. The chamber enlarges and the wall
thickens roughly in proportion to it, a pattern cardiologists call eccentric
hypertrophy: a bigger room with walls sized to match. It is driven by volume
loading, hours of moving large quantities of blood at moderate pressure. The
resulting ventricle fills more completely and ejects more per beat.

The contrast with the heart of someone doing heavy resistance work is
instructive. That heart faces brief, enormous pressure spikes rather than
sustained volume, and it responds by thickening the wall without much chamber
enlargement — concentric hypertrophy. Both are adaptations to load. They are
different loads, and they produce different hearts.

One number that training does not move is maximal heart rate. It is set largely
by age and individual variation, drifts slowly downward across a lifetime, and is
essentially indifferent to fitness. Every gain in maximal cardiac output
therefore has to come from stroke volume. The trained heart at full effort is not
beating faster than the untrained one. It is beating the same and moving more.

## Inside the Muscle

The peripheral adaptations are slower and, over the long run, at least as
important.

Repeated contractions generate a cellular signal — a mixture of calcium flux,
energetic stress registered as a falling ratio of ATP to its breakdown products,
and mechanical strain — that converges on the transcriptional machinery
controlling mitochondrial biogenesis. Trained muscle simply contains more
mitochondria, larger and more densely networked, with more of the enzymes of the
citric acid cycle and the electron transport chain per gram of tissue.

Alongside that, the capillary bed grows. New capillaries sprout around trained
fibres, which does two things: it increases the total surface available for
diffusion, and it slows the transit of each red cell through the exchange region,
leaving more time for oxygen to unload. Myoglobin content rises, improving the
shuttle of oxygen from the fibre membrane inward.

The consequence most athletes actually feel is a shift in fuel use. With more
mitochondria and more of the enzymes of fatty acid oxidation, a trained muscle
working at any given absolute pace derives a larger share of its energy from fat
and a smaller share from glycogen. Since glycogen stores are finite and running
out of them is a sharply limiting experience, this shift matters enormously for
anything lasting more than an hour or two. Trained muscle also stores more
glycogen to begin with, and keeps a larger reserve of triglyceride in droplets
right next to the mitochondria that will burn it.

Fibre composition shifts too, though within limits. The most fatigable fast
fibres convert toward a more oxidative fast type, and the whole population
becomes more resistant to fatigue. Wholesale conversion of fast fibres into slow
ones is not something training reliably produces.

## Lactate Was Never the Villain

For most of the twentieth century, lactate was taught as the waste product of
oxygen-starved muscle, the substance that accumulated when you exceeded some
anaerobic threshold and caused both the burning and the slowing. Almost none of
that is right.

Lactate is produced continuously, at rest and at every intensity, by muscles that
have plenty of oxygen. It is a normal branch of glycolysis and it is a genuinely
useful molecule: it can be exported from a fibre, taken up by neighbouring
oxidative fibres, by the heart, or by the liver, and either burned directly or
rebuilt into glucose. The heart, in hard exercise, is a net consumer of lactate
and prefers it.

What changes with intensity is the balance between production and removal. At
low intensities, clearance keeps pace and blood lactate barely moves. Past a
certain workload, production outruns clearance and concentration climbs steeply.
That inflection is what training actually pushes rightward — not by eliminating
lactate production but by building the mitochondria and the membrane transporters
that consume and move it. The acidosis that accompanies heavy exercise comes from
the broader imbalance between ATP hydrolysis and its resynthesis, not from lactic
acid dissociating, and lactate production is closer to a buffer against that
imbalance than a cause of it.

## Where Performance Keeps Coming From

Maximal oxygen uptake responds quickly in untrained people and then flattens.
Athletes with a decade of training behind them often have a figure no higher than
it was several years earlier, while continuing to race faster. The remaining
gains come from two other places.

One is the fraction of maximum a person can hold for a prolonged effort — the
position of that lactate inflection relative to their ceiling. A well-trained
distance runner can hold a much larger share of maximum for an hour than a
novice can.

The other is economy: the oxygen cost of moving at a given speed. Some of this is
neuromuscular, the gradual elimination of wasted motion and co-contraction. Some
of it is elastic. Tendons and the connective sheaths around muscle store energy
on landing and return it on push-off, and they stiffen and thicken with years of
loading, improving that return. This is the slowest adaptation in the stack.
Plasma volume responds in days, mitochondria in weeks, the heart in months,
tendon and bone over years — which is also, uncomfortably, the order in which an
enthusiastic beginner's capacity outruns the tissue meant to carry it.
