# Stretching and the Advice That Reversed

Anyone schooled in the 1980s or 1990s can picture the scene. A row of teenagers
on a cold playing field, one ankle hooked behind them, holding a quadriceps
stretch for a slow count of thirty while a teacher walks the line. Then
hamstrings. Then calves. Only after that were you allowed to run, on the
understanding that stretching cold muscles was what stood between you and a
pulled something.

That sequence has been quietly inverted. Most sports organisations now advise
the opposite order — raise the heart rate first, move through progressively
larger ranges, save long static holds for afterwards or for a separate session
entirely. The reversal happened over roughly two decades, driven less by a
single decisive study than by the accumulated failure of the old advice to
demonstrate the thing it promised. It is one of the tidier examples in exercise
science of a practice surviving on plausibility until somebody checked.

## What Happens Inside a Stretched Muscle

Pull a muscle-tendon unit towards its end range and hold it, and two things
happen that you can feel. Tension in the tissue falls over the first ten to
thirty seconds even though the length has not changed — stress relaxation — and
if you instead hold a constant tension, the tissue slowly lengthens, which is
creep. Both are ordinary viscoelastic behaviours, the same ones a rubber band
and a loaf of bread dough display, and both reverse. Within minutes to an hour,
the tissue is back where it started.

So when someone gains range of motion over weeks of stretching, the obvious
explanation — that the muscle has become permanently longer — turns out to be
hard to support. Measurements of passive stiffness in people who have stretched
diligently for weeks often show very little change. What does change, reliably,
is the point at which the stretch becomes intolerable. Subjects reach further
before the sensation stops them, and they report the same discomfort at the new
end range that they previously reported at the old one. The prevailing
interpretation is that most of the improvement is sensory and neural: a
recalibration of how threatening the nervous system considers a given position,
rather than a structural remodelling of tissue.

Muscle also resists being stretched actively, not only passively. Spindles
embedded among the fibres sense both length and the rate at which length is
changing, and a sudden stretch triggers a reflex contraction — the same circuit a
doctor tests by tapping the patellar tendon with a hammer. This is why ballistic
stretching, bouncing at end range, fell out of favour for a long period: it was
thought to provoke precisely the contraction one was trying to release. That
position has softened, since controlled dynamic movement through range is now
standard practice and the line between that and a gentle bounce is not a sharp
one. What has not come back is the advice to bounce hard into a cold muscle.

Structural change is possible; it is just slower and requires more than a held
position. Immobilise an animal's limb with a muscle fixed in a lengthened
position and the muscle adds sarcomeres in series, genuinely lengthening its
fibres. Fix it short and it removes them. That is a real mechanism, and it
operates over days of sustained loading, not half a minute before football. In
humans, the closest practical analogue is heavy training through a full range,
particularly the lengthening portion, which does appear to increase muscle
fascicle length.

## The Reversal

The first crack in the old model was performance. By the late 1990s laboratories
were finding that a bout of prolonged static stretching immediately before
testing reduced maximal force, power output and jump height. The effect is
modest and depends heavily on duration — holds of under about thirty seconds per
muscle, followed by sport-specific movement, leave little measurable deficit,
whereas several minutes of holding does produce one. But for a sprinter or a
jumper, even a small decrement is the wrong direction, and coaches moved quickly.

What replaced it was the dynamic warm-up: a graded sequence that raises muscle
temperature, increases blood flow and rehearses the movements about to be
demanded, taking joints through their working range under control rather than
parking them at end range. Leg swings, lunges with rotation, skips, strides,
gradually faster. The physiological case for it is sound — warm muscle is less
stiff and contracts faster, nerve conduction speeds up, and rehearsing a
movement pattern primes the coordination for it — and it fits what athletes had
often done informally anyway.

## The Injury Claim, Reconsidered

The stronger claim, and the one that kept the old ritual alive, was that
stretching prevents injury. When this was examined properly — in military recruit
cohorts and sports squads large enough to accumulate injuries — general
pre-exercise stretching showed little detectable protective effect. A related
belief, that stretching afterwards reduces next-day soreness, fared no better; a
systematic review of the question concluded the effect was negligible at best.

This does not mean nothing prevents injury. It means the effective interventions
turned out to be different ones, and mostly involved strength rather than
length. Structured warm-up programmes combining running, strength, plyometrics
and balance work — the football-specific version is the best studied — reduce
injury rates substantially in teams that actually perform them. The single
clearest result in the field concerns the Nordic hamstring exercise, in which an
athlete kneels and lowers their torso forward under control while a partner
anchors their ankles, loading the hamstrings eccentrically at long lengths.
Squads that adopt it consistently suffer markedly fewer hamstring strains. The
muscle is not being made longer by being stretched; it is being made stronger in
the lengthened position where strains actually occur.

There remains a legitimate case for flexibility in sports that demand extreme
range. A hurdler, a gymnast, a martial artist and a swimmer with restricted
ankle or shoulder motion all have technical requirements that ordinary range
cannot meet, and for them flexibility work is skill training. Generalising from
those athletes to everyone else was the error.

## Flexibility Is Not Mobility

The two words get used interchangeably and mean different things, and the
distinction does real work.

Flexibility is passive range — how far a joint can be taken by an external force
when the muscles are relaxed. Mobility, as coaches now generally use it, is how
much of that range a person can enter and control under their own power. The
gap between the two is common and revealing. Someone can have a hamstring that
permits a straight-leg raise to eighty degrees when a clinician lifts it and be
unable to raise it past fifty themselves. Expanding passive range without
building strength and control in the new territory widens the gap, and there is
a reasonable argument that an uncontrolled end range is not much use and may not
be entirely benign.

This is clearest in people who are naturally hypermobile. They have abundant
passive range, often assessed by whether they can bend a thumb to the forearm or
hyperextend elbows and knees, and they are disproportionately represented among
people with joint pain, instability and recurring subluxations. The last thing
most of them need is more stretching. What tends to help is strength work in
midrange and end range, building active restraint where passive restraint is
lacking.

There is also a limit no amount of work will move, and it is made of bone. The
depth and orientation of the hip socket, the angle of the femoral neck, the shape
of the shoulder's glenoid — these vary considerably between individuals and set
hard ceilings on certain positions. Two people attempting the same deep squat
with the same foot position may be limited by entirely different things: one by
tissue tension that will yield to training, the other by femur meeting pelvis. A
stretch cannot renegotiate that, and the sensation of a hard, sudden block at end
range rather than a gradual increase in tension is often the clue.

## What Actually Changes Range

Range does improve with training, reasonably reliably, if the training is
frequent and sustained over months rather than performed as a warm-up ritual.
Static holds work. So does contract-relax stretching, in which a muscle is
stretched, contracted isometrically against resistance, then stretched further —
long explained by reflex inhibition through the Golgi tendon organs, an account
now regarded as too neat, with tolerance again doing much of the work.

More interesting is that resistance training through a full range of motion
produces flexibility gains broadly comparable to stretching, while also building
strength in the positions gained. A deep squat, a Romanian deadlift taken to full
hamstring length, an overhead press from a genuinely overhead start position —
each is a loaded stretch that the body has reason to adapt to. Foam rolling and
similar soft-tissue work reliably produces a short-term increase in range without
the force decrement that static stretching can cause, which makes it useful
immediately before training, though the effect fades within the hour and there is
little evidence it changes anything structural.

Muscles crossing two joints complicate all of this, because their length depends
on both. The hamstrings cross hip and knee; the gastrocnemius crosses knee and
ankle. Stretch a calf with a bent knee and you are largely stretching the soleus
beneath it, since the gastrocnemius has already been slackened at the top. Small
changes in joint position change which tissue is actually under tension, which is
why stretches that look similar can feel entirely different.

The honest position on the office-worker complaint — tight hip flexors, tight
chest, the posture of a decade at a desk — is that the tissue-shortening story is
weaker than its popularity suggests, and that the relief people genuinely feel
from getting up and moving through range is real regardless of whether the
explanation is. Stretching is not useless. It was simply asked, for a long time,
to do a job that belonged to strength.
