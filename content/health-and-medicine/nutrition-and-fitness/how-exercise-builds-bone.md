# How Exercise Builds Bone

Take an X-ray of the arms of a tennis player who started young and kept at it,
and the two sides do not match. The racquet arm carries a thicker shell of
cortical bone. The difference is visible without measurement in extreme cases,
and it has been found again and again in players of racquet sports, in baseball
pitchers, in anyone whose sport loads one limb far harder than the other. The
same person, the same genes, the same diet, the same hormones circulating past
both arms in the same blood. The only variable is mechanical history.

That asymmetry is the cleanest natural experiment in skeletal biology, and it
settles a question that would otherwise be tangled beyond recovery in
confounding: bone responds to being loaded. Not to calcium in isolation, not to
general fitness, not to having been told that exercise is good. To force,
applied locally, in a pattern the tissue reads as demanding.

## Wolff's Observation and Frost's Correction

The nineteenth-century German anatomist Julius Wolff noticed that the internal
struts of spongy bone in the head of a femur are not arranged randomly. They
line up along the paths that stress takes through the joint, arching in curves
that a structural engineer would recognize as the trajectories of principal
stress in a loaded beam. He concluded that bone architecture adapts to the
mechanical demands placed on it, and the idea has carried his name ever since,
usually stated as though bone simply grows where it is needed.

The formulation is too loose to be useful. It does not say what "needed" means,
how much load counts, or why a lifetime of standing does not eventually produce
a skeleton of enormous thickness. The orthopedic surgeon Harold Frost supplied
the missing structure in the 1980s with what he called the mechanostat: the idea
that bone behaves like a thermostat with set points. Below a certain level of
habitual strain, bone is removed. Within a broad middle band, bone is
maintained in a rough steady state. Above the band, formation is switched on and
the bone gets stronger until the strains it experiences fall back into the
middle. Far above it, damage accumulates faster than repair and you get a stress
fracture.

The consequence is that ordinary life sits inside the maintenance band. Standing
and walking are not stimuli, because the skeleton has already adapted to them.
A signal has to exceed what the tissue is used to before anything changes,
which is the same principle that governs muscle, and for much the same reason.

## The Cell That Feels It

For decades the obvious candidates for the sensor were the cells that do the
building and the demolition — the osteoblasts on the surface laying down matrix,
the osteoclasts chewing it away. Both turned out to be downstream. The sensor is
the osteocyte, the most numerous bone cell and the easiest to overlook, because
it spends its life walled into the mineral itself.

Osteocytes are former osteoblasts that got buried by their own product. Each sits
in a small cavity called a lacuna, and from it extends dozens of fine processes
through narrow channels, the canaliculi, reaching the processes of neighbours in
every direction. The result is a connected network threaded through the entire
skeleton, denser than the neural network of many small animals, and bathed in a
thin film of fluid.

When a bone is loaded it deforms. The deformation is tiny — a fraction of a
percent, invisible, well within the elastic range — but it squeezes that fluid
through the canaliculi, and the moving fluid drags at the osteocyte processes.
Shear from flowing fluid appears to be the thing the cell actually detects,
rather than the compression itself. Loaded osteocytes turn down their production
of a protein called sclerostin, which in the unloaded state acts as a brake on
bone formation by blocking a signalling pathway the osteoblasts depend on. Take
the brake off and formation proceeds. Leave a bone unloaded and sclerostin rises,
the brake stays on, and the remodelling balance tips toward removal.

This explains something that a whole-body view cannot: why adaptation is fiercely
local. The osteocyte network reports on the strain in its own neighbourhood. A
tennis serve strains the humerus of the racquet arm; the osteocytes in the other
humerus have no way of knowing anything happened, and no reason to respond. Bone
gained is bone gained where the loading occurred, which is why an exercise
regime is not a general tonic for the skeleton but a set of specific instructions
delivered to specific sites.

## What Counts as a Signal

Three properties of a load seem to matter more than its total amount.

The first is magnitude — how much the bone actually bends. The second is rate:
strain applied quickly provokes a far larger response than the same strain
applied slowly, which is why a hop or a landing does more than a slow heavy hold.
The third is novelty of distribution. Bone adapts to the strain pattern it
habitually sees, so loads arriving from unfamiliar directions, or producing
bending where there was usually compression, register as new information.
Running is better than walking, and running that involves cutting, turning and
changing speed is better than running a flat line.

Duration matters surprisingly little. The mechanosensitive response saturates
quickly: after a modest number of loading cycles the cells stop responding to
further repetitions in the same bout, and long continuous sessions add little
beyond the first minute or two of novel loading. Sensitivity recovers with rest,
so several short bouts separated by hours produce more adaptation than the same
total cycles delivered in one go. Endurance athletes are therefore a mixed
picture. Runners tend to have robust leg bones; competitive cyclists and
swimmers, whose sports are excellent for the heart and nearly weightless for the
skeleton, often do not, and sometimes have lower bone density than sedentary
people of the same age.

The reverse case is stark. Remove load entirely and bone disappears faster than
it can be rebuilt. Prolonged bed rest, spinal cord injury and spaceflight all
produce rapid losses concentrated in the weight-bearing skeleton — the spine,
pelvis and legs — while the skull, which never bore much load in the first place,
is largely unaffected. Astronauts on long missions lose bone from the hip at
rates that would be alarming in an ageing patient on Earth, and the resistance
exercise hardware on spacecraft exists chiefly to fight it. Recovery on return is
slower than the loss, and may be incomplete. The asymmetry is a general feature:
building is a construction project with a long schedule, while removal only
requires letting the demolition crew work uncontested.

## Timing, and the Limits of Loading

The largest returns come early. Most of the bone a person will ever have is laid
down before the mid-twenties, with the steepest accrual during the adolescent
growth spurt, and loading during those years produces changes that persist for
decades. In the racquet-sport studies, the arm asymmetry is far greater in
players who started before puberty than in those who took up the game as adults,
and much of the childhood advantage is still measurable long after they stop
competing. Exercise in adulthood generally maintains bone and produces modest
gains; exercise in childhood shapes the structure itself — wider bones, thicker
cortices — and a wider bone resists bending far better than a merely denser one,
because stiffness depends steeply on how far material sits from the axis of
bending.

Loading is necessary but not sufficient, and the clearest demonstration comes
from athletes who train hard while eating too little for the training they do.
Chronic low energy availability suppresses the reproductive axis; in women this
often shows up as disrupted or absent menstruation and low circulating estrogen,
and bone density falls despite a training load that ought to be building it.
Stress fractures follow. The skeleton, it turns out, treats mechanical demand as
a request rather than a command, and declines the request when the organism is
running a deficit.

A related complication is that the number most people encounter — the bone
density figure from a DXA scan — measures something narrower than strength. The
scan passes X-rays of two energies through the body and infers how much mineral
lies in the path, then divides by the projected area of the bone on the image.
That gives an areal density, in grams per square centimetre, not the true
volumetric density of the tissue. A bone that is wider in cross-section casts a
larger shadow and can score much the same as a narrower one while being
substantially harder to break, because resistance to bending depends on the
distribution of material around the bending axis, not merely on how much there
is. The scan also says nothing about the architecture of the spongy interior,
whether the trabecular struts are still connected into a network or have been
perforated into a set of dead ends, and connectivity matters enormously for how
a vertebra fails under compression. Exercise studies that report only small
percentage gains in areal density may therefore be understating what actually
changed, since some of the benefit shows up as geometry and micro-architecture
that the measurement was never designed to see.

And there is a second half to the fracture problem that loading does not address
at all. Fragility fractures in older adults are the product of two things
meeting: a weakened bone and a fall. Bone density is only one term. Balance,
reaction time, leg power, vision, blood pressure regulation and the sedating
effects of common medications determine whether the hip ever hits the floor.
Resistance and power training happen to improve both terms at once, which is
part of why they show up in fracture prevention research more reliably than
interventions aimed at density alone. A hip that does not get struck does not
break, whatever its scan says.

The tennis player's arm remains the useful image. The skeleton is an accountant
of habits, keeping a running ledger of the forces each region has actually
experienced and adjusting its holdings accordingly. It has no opinion about
intentions.
