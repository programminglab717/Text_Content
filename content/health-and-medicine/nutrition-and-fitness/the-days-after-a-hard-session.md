# The Days After a Hard Session

You can usually date a hard session by the stairs. Going up the morning after is
uncomfortable; going down is worse, and the asymmetry is a clue. Descending
stairs loads the quadriceps while they are lengthening — the muscle is
contracting and being pulled longer at the same time, acting as a brake — and it
is that kind of contraction, the eccentric kind, that produces the most
disruption inside muscle fibres. Lowering a weight slowly hurts more the next
day than lifting it. Running downhill wrecks people who can run uphill all
afternoon.

The folk explanation for the ache is lactic acid, and it is wrong in a way
that is easy to check. Lactate produced during hard exercise is cleared from
blood and muscle within an hour or so of stopping, usually well before the
soreness has begun. Delayed-onset muscle soreness arrives eight to twelve hours
later, peaks somewhere between one and three days, and fades over several more.
Nothing about that timeline fits a metabolite that has already gone.

## The Adaptation Happens Afterwards

Training does not make anyone fitter. Training makes people temporarily worse:
it depletes fuel, disrupts structures, provokes inflammation and leaves the
nervous system less able to produce force. What makes people fitter is the
repair job that follows, and the repair job routinely overshoots. Damaged
proteins are replaced with slightly more of them. Capillary networks extend.
Mitochondria multiply. Tendons and bone lay down matrix along the lines of
stress they have just experienced. The classical picture of this — a dip below
baseline, then a rise above it — is a simplification, because the many systems
involved recover on entirely different clocks, but the central point survives:
the session is the signal, and the adaptation is built in the gap.

That gap is a resource. Too short and the signals pile up on tissue that has not
finished responding to the last one. Too long and each repair job is finished and
decaying before the next stimulus arrives. Most of the practical craft of
training programming is an argument about how long that gap should be for
different tissues and different people.

## The First Day

In the hours immediately after a long or intense effort, the most measurable
deficits are mundane. Muscle glycogen — the branched glucose polymer stored in
muscle itself — can be substantially depleted, and it is the limiting fuel for
sustained high-intensity work. Replacing it is largely a matter of eating enough
carbohydrate; the rate is fastest in the first few hours after exercise, when
muscle is unusually permeable to glucose, and full restoration generally takes
about a day. That timing only matters when the next session is close. An athlete
training twice daily has a real problem; someone lifting three times a week does
not.

Fluid and electrolytes come back over a similar window, at a rate limited less by
absorption than by whether the person drinks. The autonomic nervous system takes
its own path back: heart rate remains elevated for hours after a hard effort,
heart rate variability drops sharply and then climbs over one to several days,
and the sympathetic tilt that comes with it is part of why people who train hard
late in the evening often find themselves lying awake, tired and wired.

The repair process itself begins quickly. Rates of muscle protein synthesis rise
within an hour or two of resistance exercise and stay elevated for a day or
longer, which is the reason the once-ubiquitous advice about a thirty-minute
"anabolic window" has been steadily walked back. The window is wide. What
matters more is the total amount of protein eaten across the day and its rough
distribution across meals — the commonly cited target for people training
seriously sits somewhere around one and a half to two grams per kilogram of body
weight daily, above which the returns flatten out.

Force production itself comes back in two overlapping phases. Part of the
immediate loss is central: the nervous system's drive to the muscle is reduced,
and voluntary activation measured by stimulating the nerve directly falls short
of what the fibres could actually deliver. That component usually returns within
hours. The peripheral part — the muscle's own diminished ability to generate
force after its structures have been disrupted — can take days, and after a
genuinely unaccustomed bout of eccentric work, more than a week. A lifter who
feels perfectly well but cannot reach their usual numbers on the third day is
not imagining it.

Underneath all of this sits total energy intake. Athletes who chronically eat
less than their training demands — sometimes deliberately, often simply because
appetite lags behind a jump in volume — show a recognisable pattern that sports
medicine groups under the heading of relative energy deficiency in sport: bone
density falling, menstrual cycles becoming irregular or stopping, thyroid and
sex hormone output dropping away, immunity and mood suffering, and,
self-defeatingly, performance sliding. No amount of sleep or cold water
addresses it. The body is rationing, and the only remedy is to stop making it.

## Soreness, and the Protection It Buys

Under a microscope, a muscle biopsy taken after unaccustomed eccentric work
shows disordered sarcomeres — the repeating contractile units pulled out of
register, their Z-discs smeared. Calcium leaks where it should not, proteases
activate, and immune cells arrive over the following day to clear debris. The
soreness itself appears to come less from the contractile machinery than from
sensitisation of nerve endings in the connective tissue around and within the
muscle, which is why sore muscles hurt most when stretched or pressed.

The most interesting feature of all this is how quickly the body learns. Do a
bout of unfamiliar eccentric work and you will be wrecked. Do the identical bout
a week later and the damage, the soreness and the strength loss are all markedly
reduced, and the protection lasts for weeks to months. This repeated bout effect
is why soreness is a poor measure of how useful a session was. A novice doing
their first set of lunges will be sorer than a trained athlete completing a
session that produced far more adaptation.

## Sleep Does More Than Anything Sold in a Tub

Of all the interventions studied, the one with the most consistent effect on
recovery is also the least commercial. Sleep restriction reliably degrades
reaction time, raises perceived exertion at a given workload, impairs glucose
handling and worsens mood, and studies that extend athletes' sleep rather than
restrict it have found improvements in sprint times, accuracy and subjective
fatigue. Growth hormone is released in pulses concentrated in the deep,
slow-wave stages early in the night. The immune and inflammatory processes that
clear damaged tissue are strongly tied to the sleep-wake cycle.

Nothing else on the recovery shelf has evidence of that weight. Which makes the
market for it a little odd, since sleep is free and the competition is not.

## The Things That Help Less Than Advertised

Cold water immersion is the most instructive case. It does reduce soreness in
the following days — that finding is solid. But a body of work now suggests that
using it habitually after resistance training attenuates the long-term gains in
muscle size and strength, apparently by damping the very inflammatory and
signalling cascade that carries the message to adapt. The practical reading is
that ice baths are a tool for when performance tomorrow matters more than
adaptation next month: a tournament, a stage race, a second session the same
day. They are a strange default for someone training to get stronger.

The same logic applies, more tentatively, to anti-inflammatory drugs taken
routinely around training, and to very large doses of antioxidant supplements,
which in several studies blunted the endurance adaptations of the people taking
them. Inflammation and oxidative signalling are not simply damage; they are
partly the message.

Massage and compression garments perform respectably on measures of how sore
people feel and much less impressively on measures of what they can do. That is
not nothing — feeling better is worth something, and an athlete who believes a
session will not ruin them tends to train more consistently — but it is a
different claim from restoring function. Light activity on rest days, the sort
that raises blood flow without adding load, is cheap and appears to modestly
reduce soreness.

## When the Gap Stops Being Long Enough

Push repeatedly into a deficit that is not repaid and the picture changes in
stages. Short-term functional overreaching is deliberate and normal: performance
dips during a heavy block and rebounds above baseline after a lighter week.
Non-functional overreaching is the same dip without the rebound, taking weeks to
resolve. Overtraining syndrome sits at the far end, a months-long state of
depressed performance, disturbed sleep, altered mood and frequent illness that
is diagnosed largely by excluding everything else.

The early signals are unglamorous and mostly subjective: sleep getting worse,
irritability, a resting heart rate drifting up, enthusiasm draining out of
sessions that used to be enjoyable, small niggles that do not settle. Heart rate
variability tracked over weeks can help, though day-to-day readings are noisy
enough to mislead anyone who takes a single number seriously.

It also helps to remember that the body keeps a single ledger. Work following
people through examination periods and high-pressure stretches at their jobs has
found that strength recovers more slowly after damaging exercise when
psychological stress is high, and that an identical training load is rated as
harder. A programme that fits comfortably into a calm month can be too much in a
chaotic one without a single session having changed.

Connective tissue deserves its own mention here, because it adapts on a slower
clock than muscle. Tendon and bone remodel over months, not days, while the
muscles pulling on them can gain strength considerably faster. That mismatch is
the mechanism behind a large share of overuse injuries in people who are, by
every other measure, getting fitter. The old coaching rule about increasing
running volume by no more than a tenth a week is not well supported as a precise
number, but the instinct underneath it — that the slowest-adapting tissue sets
the pace — is sound.
