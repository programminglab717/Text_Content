# How Appetite Is Regulated

You finish a meal and put the fork down. Consider what your body actually knows
at that moment. The food is sitting in your stomach, mostly intact. Digestion
has barely begun; almost none of its energy has crossed the intestinal wall into
your blood. By any direct measure of nourishment, nothing has yet happened. And
yet the urge to keep eating, which was perfectly strong twenty minutes earlier,
has switched off. Something decided that enough had arrived, and it decided on
the basis of evidence that does not include the thing it is supposedly
measuring.

That gap — between what is eaten and what is detectable — is the central problem
the appetite system has to solve, and it explains why the machinery is so
layered. There is no single hunger signal, no gauge reading fuel remaining.
There is a set of overlapping estimates, running on different timescales, made
by different tissues, arguing with each other, and with the brain doing
something between arbitration and guesswork.

## The Mouse That Could Not Stop

The first hard evidence that a blood-borne signal governs body weight came from
an unglamorous set of experiments at the Jackson Laboratory in Maine, where
Douglas Coleman spent the 1960s and 1970s working with two strains of
spectacularly obese mice. One strain carried a mutation called *obese*, the
other one called *diabetes*. They looked nearly identical and nobody could say
what distinguished them.

Coleman used parabiosis: surgically joining two mice so that they shared a
circulation. When he joined an *obese* mouse to a normal one, the *obese* mouse
ate less and lost weight, as though it were finally receiving something it had
been missing. When he joined a *diabetes* mouse to a normal one, the outcome was
the opposite and considerably grimmer — the normal partner stopped eating and
starved.

The interpretation was clean. Some factor circulates in the blood that tells the
brain how much fat the body is carrying. The *obese* mouse cannot make it, and
recovers when it is supplied from a partner. The *diabetes* mouse makes it in
enormous quantities but cannot detect it, so it behaves as though starving while
flooding its partner's circulation with a signal that shuts that animal's
appetite down entirely. A hormone and its receptor, deduced from surgery alone,
decades before either could be identified.

The hormone turned out to be leptin, cloned by Jeffrey Friedman's group at
Rockefeller in the mid-1990s, and produced by fat tissue roughly in proportion
to how much of it there is. The *diabetes* gene turned out to encode the leptin
receptor, exactly as Coleman had predicted.

The discovery was greeted as the end of obesity and was nothing of the kind.
Giving leptin to people with the extremely rare condition of congenital leptin
deficiency is transformative — children who were relentlessly, desperately
hungry become normal eaters. Giving leptin to people with ordinary obesity does
almost nothing, because they already have high leptin levels and the brain has
stopped listening. Leptin, it became clear, is better understood as a signal of
scarcity than of plenty. Its dramatic effects come from falling, not rising.
When leptin drops, as it does during weight loss or fasting, hunger climbs,
thyroid activity falls, reproductive hormones dip, and the body behaves as
though a famine has begun. When it rises, comparatively little happens.

## The Argument in the Hypothalamus

Leptin's main destination is a small region at the base of the hypothalamus
called the arcuate nucleus, which sits where the blood-brain barrier is leaky
enough to let circulating signals through. Two populations of neurons there pull
in opposite directions.

One population makes agouti-related peptide and neuropeptide Y, and its firing
drives eating — activate these neurons in a well-fed mouse and it will eat
immediately and voraciously. The other makes pro-opiomelanocortin, which is
cleaved into a peptide that acts on melanocortin receptors and suppresses
intake. Leptin inhibits the first group and stimulates the second. Mutations in
the downstream melanocortin-4 receptor are the most common single-gene cause of
human obesity, and the people who carry them are not lazy or weak-willed; they
are hungry in a way that is difficult for others to imagine.

One of the odder findings about the hunger-driving neurons is how fast they
respond to things that are not food. Recordings in mice show their activity
plunging within seconds of the animal seeing or smelling a meal — long before
anything is eaten, let alone absorbed. The cells are not reporting a nutritional
state. They are making a prediction, and updating it on sensory evidence.

## What the Gut Reports

Below the brain sits a second system operating meal by meal. The stomach carries
stretch receptors whose signals travel up the vagus nerve; distension alone
reduces intake, which is why volume matters and why a large low-energy salad
blunts appetite more than a small dense pastry of the same calorie content.

The stomach also secretes ghrelin, the only well-established hormone that
increases hunger. Ghrelin rises before habitual mealtimes and falls after
eating, and the anticipatory rise is partly learned — it tracks the times you
usually eat, not simply the emptiness of the stomach.

Further down, the intestine responds to nutrients arriving in it. The duodenum
releases cholecystokinin when fat and protein appear, slowing gastric emptying
and signalling fullness. The lower small intestine and colon release peptide YY
and glucagon-like peptide-1 as nutrients reach them, which happens later in a
meal and later after it. GLP-1 slows the stomach further, amplifies insulin
release when glucose is present, and acts on brain circuits that reduce intake.

That last one has become the most consequential piece of appetite biology in
practice. The current generation of weight-loss drugs are long-acting versions
of GLP-1, engineered to resist the enzyme that ordinarily destroys the natural
hormone within minutes, and their effect is not on metabolism so much as on
wanting to eat. People taking them describe the disappearance of a background
noise they had not fully registered as noise — a constant low-level
preoccupation with food that simply stops.

## Wanting Is Not Liking

None of this yet explains dessert. You are full, the homeostatic system has
delivered its verdict, and then the tray comes round and you eat anyway.

Part of the answer is sensory-specific satiety. The pleasantness of a particular
food declines as you keep eating it, while other foods retain their appeal
undiminished. A varied meal therefore supports a larger total intake than a
monotonous one, and the effect is strong enough that it is best thought of as
part of the satiety system rather than an exception to it. In an environment
where variety meant a broader spread of nutrients, eating more when the menu
widened was a reasonable strategy.

The other part is that reward and satiety are separate systems, and within
reward, wanting and liking are separable too. Work in Kent Berridge's lab and
elsewhere has pulled these apart: the dopamine circuits that drive pursuit and
motivation are not the same as the opioid-linked circuits that generate the
pleasure of a taste. An animal can be made to want intensely something it does
not appear to enjoy. Foods engineered to combine fat, sugar, salt and a
particular mouthfeel are optimized for the wanting system, and the wanting
system does not consult the hypothalamus.

Context does a great deal of the remaining work. Portion size reliably shifts
how much people eat, and mostly without their noticing or compensating later.
Energy in liquid form is compensated for poorly — a drink's calories tend to be
added to the day rather than subtracted from the next meal. Familiar foods carry
learned expectations about how filling they will be, which means satiety is
partly a conditioned response to a package, a texture and a portion.

## The Asymmetry

If body weight were regulated around a set point the way body temperature is,
overeating and undereating would provoke equal and opposite corrections. They do
not. The defence against weight loss is fierce; the defence against gain is
feeble.

Lose a significant amount of weight and several things happen at once. Resting
energy expenditure falls further than the smaller body alone accounts for.
Ghrelin rises. The satiety peptides fall. Muscles become somewhat more efficient,
doing the same work for less fuel. Studies following people after substantial
weight loss find these changes persisting for years rather than weeks — the
body does not recalibrate to the new weight and accept it. It continues to treat
the loss as a deficit to be corrected.

Gain weight, and the countermeasures are far weaker. Leptin rises, the brain
largely ignores it, and the new weight is defended almost as stubbornly as the
old one was.

This asymmetry makes evolutionary sense in a world where food supply was the
binding constraint, and it is the single most useful thing to understand about
why deliberate weight loss so often reverses. The reversal is not a failure of
resolve meeting an indifferent physiology. It is resolve meeting a physiology
that has been organized, over a very long time, to win that particular argument.
