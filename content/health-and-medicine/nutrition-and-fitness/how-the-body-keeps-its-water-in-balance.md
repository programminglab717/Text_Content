# How the Body Keeps Its Water in Balance

Sweat starts out as something very close to blood plasma. Deep in the skin, each
eccrine gland is a tiny coiled tube, and at the bottom of that coil cells pump
salt into the lumen and water follows it osmotically, producing a clear, salty
fluid. Then, on the way up the duct toward the surface, other cells reclaim much
of the sodium and chloride and leave the water behind. What arrives on the skin
is dilute — saltier than tap water, considerably less salty than the blood it
came from. A person has a few million of these glands, and in the right
conditions they can collectively deliver well over a litre an hour.

That single arrangement contains most of what makes water balance complicated.
The body is not managing a quantity of water. It is managing a quantity of water
and a quantity of dissolved salt, simultaneously and semi-independently, because
what matters to a cell is not how much fluid surrounds it but how concentrated
that fluid is.

## Two Variables, Two Control Systems

Roughly half to two-thirds of a person's mass is water. Most of it sits inside
cells; the rest is in the spaces between them and in the blood plasma. Membranes
are freely permeable to water, which therefore moves wherever osmotic pressure
sends it, and cannot be pushed around directly. What the body can control is
solute — chiefly sodium, which dominates the fluid outside cells, and potassium,
which dominates inside them. Move the salt and water follows.

The concentration of the extracellular fluid is held within a startlingly narrow
band, somewhere near 285 to 295 milliosmoles per kilogram, and it is defended by
a set of neurons in the hypothalamus that behave like osmometers: they shrink
slightly when the fluid around them concentrates, and that shrinkage is the
signal. They do two things in response. They provoke thirst, and they trigger the
release of vasopressin, which travels to the kidney and inserts water channels
into the walls of the collecting ducts, letting water be reabsorbed rather than
lost. Urine becomes scant and dark.

Volume is a separate problem with separate sensors. Stretch receptors in the
great veins, the atria, and the arterial tree report how full the circulation is,
and when pressure or filling falls, the kidney releases renin and sets in motion
a hormonal cascade that ends in aldosterone, which tells the kidney to hold on to
sodium. Water follows the sodium.

Keeping these two apart clears up a great deal of confusion. Losing blood
depletes volume without changing concentration at all — the fluid lost was exactly
as salty as what remains. Sitting in a sauna without drinking depletes water
faster than salt, so volume falls and concentration rises. Drinking large amounts
of plain water after heavy sweating can restore volume while pushing concentration
too low. The body's responses differ in each case because it is measuring two
different things.

## Why Evaporation Is the Point

Sweat that rolls off the skin and lands on the floor has accomplished nothing
except the loss of the fluid. Cooling comes from the phase change: turning liquid
water into vapour requires a large input of energy, close to six hundred
kilocalories per litre, and that energy is drawn from the skin and the blood
flowing through it. This is why a dry wind feels so much colder than a still one,
and why humid heat is dangerous in a way that dry heat is not. When the air is
already near saturation, evaporation slows to a crawl, sweat production
continues, and the body loses the water without gaining the cooling.

Heat acclimatization, which takes a week or two of repeated exposure, adjusts
this system in several directions at once. Plasma volume expands, giving more
fluid to work with. Sweating begins at a lower core temperature and at a higher
rate, so cooling starts earlier. And the duct cells reclaim sodium more
aggressively under aldosterone, so acclimatized sweat is noticeably less salty —
the body learns to spend water without spending as much salt with it. Anyone who
has moved from a temperate climate to a hot one in summer has felt the first week
and the third week and knows they are not the same week.

## The Ledger

Water leaves by four routes. Urine is the largest and the only one that is
genuinely adjustable — the kidney can produce a small volume of highly
concentrated urine or a large volume of dilute urine depending on what it is
told, though there is a floor, because a certain amount of waste solute has to
be dissolved in something. Sweat is the most variable, ranging from almost
nothing to litres per hour. Then there are the losses nobody notices: water
evaporating continuously from the skin surface independent of sweating, and
water leaving in exhaled breath, which is fully saturated with vapour every time
it goes out. Cold, dry air makes that respiratory loss substantial, which is why
people get dehydrated on mountains and in aircraft. Faecal losses are small in
health and enormous in diarrhoeal disease, which is precisely why such diseases
kill.

On the income side, drinks are the obvious source and food is a surprisingly
large one — fruit and vegetables are mostly water, and so are soups, stews, and
cooked grains. There is also a third source that gets forgotten: metabolism
itself makes water. Oxidizing hydrogen-rich fuel produces water as an end
product, several hundred millilitres a day in a person eating normally. Some
desert rodents push this to its logical conclusion and never drink at all,
subsisting on the water liberated from dry seeds, with kidneys that produce urine
of extraordinary concentration and nasal passages that recondense moisture out of
their own exhaled breath.

## Thirst Is a Lagging Indicator, and a Hasty One

Thirst is triggered by a rise in extracellular concentration of only a percent or
two, which makes it a sensitive alarm. It is also, in two specific ways,
imprecise.

It arrives after the fact. Concentration has to rise before the osmometers
respond, so the sensation reports a deficit that already exists rather than one
that is approaching. During prolonged work in heat, sweat losses can run ahead of
the signal for a long time.

And it switches off early. Within minutes of drinking — long before any of the
swallowed water has been absorbed from the intestine and reached the
bloodstream — thirst subsides. Receptors in the mouth and throat and the
distension of the stomach report that water is on its way, pre-empting the
correction. The system is built to stop drinking before the results are in, which
is sensible in a world where water sources are dangerous places to linger, and
which means people who drink to the point of feeling satisfied often replace less
than they lost.

## Too Much of It

The opposite failure is less familiar and more dangerous. If someone drinks large
volumes of plain water while losing salt in sweat, and if vasopressin remains
inappropriately elevated — as it can be during prolonged exertion, stress, pain,
or nausea — the kidney will not excrete the excess, and the sodium concentration
outside the cells falls. Water then moves into the cells, down its osmotic
gradient, and they swell.

Most tissues accommodate this. The brain cannot, because it is sealed inside a
rigid box with no room to expand. Mild cases produce headache, nausea, and
confusion that are almost indistinguishable from the symptoms of the opposite
problem; severe ones produce seizures and can be fatal. It turns up in endurance
events, and its recognition in the last several decades changed the advice given
at those events considerably, away from drinking as much as possible and toward
something closer to drinking in response to thirst.

There is no single reliable gauge for any of this. Urine colour is a reasonable
rough indicator, because it reflects concentration, but it responds to vitamin
supplements, to certain foods, and to a large drink taken in the last half hour.
Body mass measured at the same time each day tracks short-term fluid swings
fairly well, since nothing else in the body changes that fast. And the familiar
prescription of eight glasses a day has no clear physiological basis and takes no
account of climate, body size, activity, or the considerable amount of water
arriving in food. The kidney, given a functioning thirst mechanism and reasonable
access to fluid, handles the arithmetic continuously and without instruction.
