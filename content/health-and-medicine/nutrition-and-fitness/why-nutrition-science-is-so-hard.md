# Why Nutrition Science Is So Hard to Get Right

A standard instrument in nutritional epidemiology asks you how often, over the
past twelve months, you ate broccoli. Not last week. The past year. It then
wants a portion size, offered as a choice between a half cup, a cup, and more
than a cup, and it asks the same about a hundred or so other foods, and from
your answers a computer will derive your average daily intake of fibre,
saturated fat, folate, and forty other quantities to two decimal places.

Sit with that for a moment. Try to answer it honestly for broccoli. Then
consider that this questionnaire, or something very like it, is the exposure
measurement underlying a large fraction of what anyone claims to know about diet
and chronic disease.

Nutrition science has a reputation for reversing itself, and the usual
explanations — corporate funding, credulous journalists, researchers chasing
headlines — are real but secondary. The primary reason is that the field is
attempting something genuinely, structurally harder than most of medicine, and
the difficulty is not going away with better intentions.

## Nobody Knows What They Ate

Every other branch of epidemiology gets its exposure from somewhere firmer.
Pharmacoepidemiology has prescription records. Occupational studies have
employment histories and sometimes air monitoring. Genetic studies have a
sequence. Nutrition has self-report, and self-report of diet is bad in ways that
are not merely noisy but systematically slanted.

People under-report what they eat, and the shortfall is large. When reported
intake is checked against energy expenditure measured objectively with doubly
labelled water, a substantial share of participants report eating less than
would be required to stay alive at their observed weight. This is not lying. It
is a mixture of forgetting, of not counting what was eaten standing up or off
someone else's plate, and of estimating portions badly, which everyone does.

The trouble is that the error is not random. Under-reporting is more pronounced
in people with higher body weight, and it falls more heavily on the foods that
carry a social judgment. Vegetables get over-reported; snacks, sweets and
alcohol get under-reported. An error that correlates with both the exposure and
the outcome does not wash out with a large sample. It builds in a bias that no
amount of statistical adjustment can reliably remove, because the adjustment
would have to know the shape of a distortion that is invisible from inside the
data.

A handful of nutrients have objective biomarkers — urinary nitrogen tracks
protein intake, urinary sodium and potassium track their own intake reasonably
well over repeated collections, certain fatty acids show up in red cell
membranes. These are expensive, burdensome, and available for a small fraction
of what people want to study. There is no biomarker for having eaten
ultra-processed food.

## Every Question Is Secretly a Comparison

"Is saturated fat bad for you?" has no answer, not because the evidence is
unclear but because the question is incomplete. People eat to a roughly
constrained energy intake. Remove a gram of fat and something takes its place.
The health consequence of eating less saturated fat depends entirely on what
comes in behind it — polyunsaturated fat, refined starch, protein, or simply
more of everything else in the same proportions. Those substitutions do not
point the same direction, and some of them point in opposite directions.

Good analyses handle this explicitly, modelling what happens when one source of
energy replaces another while total energy is held constant. The results are
conditional statements, and conditional statements do not survive contact with
a headline. What reaches the public is "saturated fat exonerated" or "saturated
fat condemned," with the comparison — the entire content of the finding —
stripped out. Two studies that agree completely can be reported as contradicting
each other, simply because they chose different replacement nutrients.

The same structure defeats the search for a control group. There is no
unexposed population. Everyone eats. Whatever you are studying sits inside a
background diet that varies between people and modifies the effect you are
looking for, and the same nutrient behaves differently depending on the food it
arrives in. Calcium in cheese is not calcium in a tablet; the fat, protein and
mineral matrix around it changes absorption and what else comes with it. This
food matrix effect is one reason supplement trials have so often failed to
reproduce the benefits observed for the foods those supplements were extracted
from. Beta-carotene from vegetables and beta-carotene from a capsule are the
same molecule and evidently not the same intervention.

## The Signal Is Small and the Clock Is Slow

The great triumphs of observational epidemiology involved enormous effect sizes.
Heavy smoking multiplies lung cancer risk many times over; a signal that large
shrugs off measurement error and confounding, because no plausible lurking
variable could manufacture it.

Diet-disease associations are nothing like that. The relative risks attached to
individual foods are typically small — the kind of magnitude that could be
produced entirely by residual confounding if the confounding were modestly
mishandled. And the confounding in nutrition is unusually vicious, because
dietary choices cluster with everything else about a life. The person eating
more whole grains is, on average, also exercising more, smoking less, drinking
less, sleeping better, earning more, and more likely to turn up for a
colonoscopy. Researchers adjust for what they can measure, which means they
adjust imperfectly for the measured things and not at all for the unmeasured
ones. The healthy-user effect is not a footnote to nutritional epidemiology; it
is arguably the largest single force in the data.

Latency compounds it. Atherosclerosis and most cancers develop over decades, so
the exposure that matters is a person's diet across long stretches of their
life, not the diet they reported in 2009. Cohorts try to handle this by
re-measuring periodically, which helps and does not solve it.

## Trials Do Not Rescue It

The obvious answer is to randomize, and randomization in nutrition runs into its
own wall.

You cannot blind people to what they are eating. Participants know which arm
they are in, which affects everything else they do — the classic problem where a
person assigned to a diet also starts walking more, and the trial cannot
separate the two.

Adherence decays. Over months and years, intervention groups drift back toward
their habits and control groups, aware they are being watched, drift toward
better ones. The two arms converge, the contrast the trial was built on erodes,
and a genuine effect gets diluted into nothing.

Long trials are ruinously expensive, so most are short. Short trials cannot
measure heart attacks or cancers, so they measure surrogates — cholesterol
fractions, blood pressure, markers of inflammation — and surrogates are promises
about outcomes rather than outcomes. A diet can move a lipid marker
impressively and do nothing for mortality, and there is no way to know from
inside the trial which case you are in.

At the rigorous end sit metabolic ward studies, where participants live in a
research facility and every gram is weighed. These are the closest thing
nutrition has to a clean experiment, and they are necessarily tiny, brief, and
conducted on volunteers willing to be locked in a building. They answer
mechanistic questions beautifully and generalize to free-living life with real
uncertainty.

And whatever a trial finds, it finds on average. Responses to the same food
differ substantially between people — in how much blood glucose rises after an
identical meal, in how cholesterol moves on an identical change in fat intake,
in how much weight is gained on an identical surplus. Some of that variation is
genetic, some reflects gut microbial communities, some is unexplained. An
average effect that combines strong responders with non-responders describes a
person who may not exist, and it will look weak or inconsistent across studies
whose participants happened to be sorted differently. Personalized nutrition is
the attempt to get underneath that average, and it is at an early stage: showing
that people differ is far easier than predicting in advance which kind of person
someone is.

## The Wrong Unit

There is also a conceptual problem inherited from the field's greatest success.
Nutrition science came of age solving deficiency diseases, and that era had a
perfect structure: one missing compound, one distinctive disease, one dramatic
cure. Scurvy, beriberi, pellagra and rickets all yielded to the same logic, and
the logic was that a diet is a delivery vehicle for a list of chemicals.

Extending that framework to chronic disease has gone badly. Heart disease is not
a deficiency of anything, and its relationship to diet runs through patterns of
eating sustained over decades rather than through any single compound. Decades
of single-nutrient research produced a long parade of promising candidates that
worked in observation, failed in trials, and were quietly retired. Recent work
has shifted toward whole dietary patterns, which matches the way people actually
eat but sacrifices mechanistic precision — you learn that something about a
pattern helps without learning what.

Layered on top is the sheer number of questions a nutrition dataset can be asked.
A large cohort contains hundreds of foods and dozens of outcomes, which is tens
of thousands of possible associations, and chance alone guarantees that a great
many will look impressive. Researchers have demonstrated this deliberately by
picking ingredients from an ordinary cookbook and finding, for nearly every one,
published studies claiming it raises cancer risk, lowers it, or both. Journals
prefer findings to null results. Press offices prefer surprises to confirmations.
The machinery downstream of the data is optimized to amplify exactly the noise
the data is most likely to generate.

What survives all of this is a small, dull, remarkably stable core — eat
vegetables and legumes and whole grains, do not smoke, do not drink much, avoid
outright deficiency, and do not eat vastly more than you use. That core has
barely moved in half a century. The reversals occur at the margins, in the
narrow band of questions where the evidence is too weak to settle anything, and
the margins are where the coverage lives.
