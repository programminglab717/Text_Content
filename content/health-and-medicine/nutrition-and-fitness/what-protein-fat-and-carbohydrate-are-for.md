# What Protein, Fat, and Carbohydrate Are For

Dry a peanut, spear it on a pin, set it alight under a small beaker of water,
and you can measure how much heat comes out. The nineteenth-century chemists who
did exactly this — in sealed steel vessels rather than on pins, but the idea was
the same — established the numbers that still sit on the side of every food
package. Fat yields something close to nine kilocalories per gram when it burns.
Carbohydrate and protein each yield about four. Alcohol, which is not a nutrient
in any structural sense, yields about seven.

Even those figures are adjustments rather than raw measurements. A bomb
calorimeter burns protein completely, nitrogen and all, and reports something
closer to five and a half kilocalories per gram. A body cannot burn nitrogen. It
strips it off and excretes it as urea, and the urea carries chemical energy out
of the body with it. Subtract that loss, subtract the fraction that is never
absorbed in the first place, and four is what remains. The number is not a
property of protein. It is a property of protein passing through a human.

Those figures are real, and they are also the least interesting thing about
food. A bomb calorimeter reduces everything to heat, which is precisely what a
body refuses to do. The body takes the same three classes of molecule and sends
them down entirely different routes, stores them in entirely different ways,
and, crucially, cannot always convert one into another. The arrows connecting
them point in particular directions, and some of them only point one way.

## The Fast Account

Carbohydrate arrives as sugars and starches and is broken down, almost
entirely, to glucose. Glucose is the molecule the bloodstream carries and the
molecule most cells will use without complaint. What the body does with a
surplus of it depends on where the surplus lands.

The liver takes the first pass. It stores glucose as glycogen, a branched
polymer that can be unspooled from many points at once, which makes it fast to
mobilize. Liver glycogen is a public utility: when blood glucose falls, the
liver clips glucose units off its glycogen and releases them into circulation
for everyone else to use. Muscle also stores glycogen, in considerably larger
total quantity, but muscle glycogen is private. Muscle cells lack the enzyme
that would let them strip the phosphate off a glucose molecule and export it, so
whatever a muscle stores, that muscle burns. A sprinting leg cannot lend
anything to the brain.

The total store is modest — on the order of a hundred grams in the liver,
several times that spread through skeletal muscle. It is enough to get through a
night's sleep and a hard morning, not enough to get through a week. Once it is
gone, and if none is coming in, the body manufactures glucose from scratch. This
is gluconeogenesis, run mainly in the liver and to a lesser degree in the
kidney, and its raw materials are lactate, glycerol, and amino acids. That last
source is why prolonged starvation eats muscle: there is no other large reservoir
of carbon that can legally be turned into sugar.

A word on fibre, which is carbohydrate that human enzymes cannot open. It passes
into the colon intact and becomes food for the bacteria living there, which
ferment it into short-chain fatty acids that the colon wall absorbs and uses.
So fibre is not quite calorie-free, but the calories are laundered through a
microbial intermediary, and most of what fibre does has to do with transit time,
bulk, and the ecology it supports rather than with energy.

## The Deep Store

Fat is stored as triglyceride — three fatty acid chains hung off a glycerol
backbone — and it is stored in a way that carbohydrate cannot match. Glycogen is
hydrophilic and holds roughly three grams of water for every gram of glycogen,
which means its effective energy density is far below the four kilocalories per
gram the calorimeter reports. Triglyceride is stored essentially dry. Combine
that with fat's higher intrinsic energy content and adipose tissue ends up
storing something like six times as much usable energy per unit mass as glycogen
does. A person of ordinary build carries enough of it to walk for weeks.

Adipose tissue was long treated as inert packing material, which it is not. It
secretes hormones, including leptin, which reports the size of the fat store to
the brain; it participates in inflammation; and it releases fatty acids on
hormonal command, mostly when insulin is low. Those fatty acids travel bound to
albumin, enter cells, and are chopped two carbons at a time in the
mitochondrion — beta-oxidation — feeding acetyl groups into the same central
furnace that glucose feeds.

The brain will not take fatty acids directly; they do not cross into it in
useful quantity. When glucose is scarce, the liver takes the flood of acetyl
groups coming out of fat and condenses them into ketone bodies —
beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate, principally — which are small, water
soluble, and readily crossed into the brain. After a few days without
carbohydrate, the brain runs substantially on these, which spares a great deal
of the muscle protein that would otherwise have to be dismantled to make glucose.

Two fatty acids cannot be built by human enzymes at all: linoleic acid and
alpha-linolenic acid, the parent compounds of the omega-6 and omega-3 families.
They have to arrive in food. Their descendants become components of cell
membranes, where the degree of unsaturation governs how fluid the membrane is,
and precursors of the signalling molecules that coordinate inflammation, blood
clotting, and vessel tone. Fat is not merely fuel with a high energy density; a
substantial fraction of the body's regulatory chemistry is built out of it.

## The Structural One

Protein is different in kind. Dietary protein is taken apart into its component
amino acids, of which twenty are used to build human proteins, and nine of those
cannot be synthesized by adults in adequate quantity. The rest can be made, given
nitrogen and a carbon skeleton.

What distinguishes protein from the other two is that there is no storage depot
for it. There is no protein equivalent of glycogen or adipose tissue. Every amino
acid in the body is either circulating in a small free pool or already built into
something that is doing a job: an enzyme, a transporter, a contractile filament,
a structural fibre, an antibody, a hormone. When the body needs amino acids and
none are arriving, it takes them by breaking down functional tissue — which is
to say, it disassembles working machinery to get parts.

This is less dramatic than it sounds, because proteins are being broken down and
rebuilt constantly anyway. A protein has a half-life; some last minutes, some
last years. Turnover runs continuously, and the useful question is not how much
protein is being made but whether synthesis exceeds breakdown over time. That
balance, measured crudely as the difference between nitrogen going in and
nitrogen coming out in urine, is what tissue gain or loss actually consists of.

Amino acids can also be burned. When they are, the nitrogen has to go somewhere,
because free ammonia is toxic — particularly to the brain. The liver runs the
urea cycle, which packages two nitrogen atoms into a urea molecule that the
kidney can excrete in water. This is an obligatory cost: metabolizing protein
generates a nitrogen disposal problem that fat and carbohydrate simply do not.

## Which Fuel, and When

At any given moment the body is burning a mixture, and the composition of that
mixture is under hormonal control rather than left to chance. After a meal
containing carbohydrate, blood glucose rises, the pancreas releases insulin, and
insulin does several things at once: it opens glucose transporters in muscle and
fat, it tells the liver to build glycogen instead of exporting glucose, and it
shuts down the release of fatty acids from adipose tissue almost completely.
Fuel selection follows. With fatty acids unavailable, tissues burn the glucose
that is abundant.

Hours later, with the meal absorbed and glucose falling, insulin subsides and
glucagon rises. The liver reverses direction, unspooling glycogen and then
running gluconeogenesis; adipose tissue starts releasing fatty acids again; and
muscle, which is perfectly happy oxidizing fat, quietly switches over, leaving
the remaining glucose for the tissues that have no alternative. The switch is
not a metaphor. It can be watched in real time by measuring the ratio of carbon
dioxide exhaled to oxygen consumed, because burning carbohydrate and burning fat
consume oxygen in different proportions. That ratio drifts down through a fast
and jumps up after a meal.

Protein sits slightly outside this cycle. It is oxidized continuously at a low
rate whatever else is happening, and the proportion of total energy it supplies
stays relatively stable — rising when protein intake is high, and rising again,
unhappily, when everything else has run out.

## The One-Way Arrows

The three classes converge on a common intersection — acetyl-CoA, the two-carbon
fragment that feeds the citric acid cycle — which is why all of them can end up
as heat and carbon dioxide. That convergence is genuine but it conceals an
asymmetry.

Surplus carbohydrate can be converted to fat. Human de novo lipogenesis is less
vigorous than it is in, say, a goose being fattened, and on ordinary mixed diets
most stored fat arrived as fat, but the pathway exists and runs. Amino acids can
become glucose, or fat, or both, depending on which amino acid. Glucose can
supply the carbon skeletons for the non-essential amino acids, given a nitrogen
source.

Fat, however, cannot be converted back into glucose in any meaningful quantity.
The step that turns pyruvate into acetyl-CoA is irreversible in animals, and once
a carbon has gone into acetyl-CoA by way of beta-oxidation, it cannot come back
out as sugar. Only the glycerol backbone — a small minority of the mass of a
triglyceride — makes that trip. This single missing enzyme explains why
carbohydrate restriction forces the ketone workaround, why a starving body
consumes its own muscle even with fat still on it, and why the three
macronutrients are not the interchangeable fuel tanks the calorimeter makes them
look like.
