# Vitamins and the Diseases of Absence

In the spring of 1747, aboard a Royal Navy ship called the Salisbury, a surgeon
named James Lind took twelve sailors who were all sick with scurvy in roughly
the same degree, housed them together, fed them the same base diet, and then
split them into six pairs. Each pair got a different addition: cider, dilute
sulphuric acid, vinegar, seawater, a paste of garlic and mustard seed and
horseradish, or two oranges and a lemon. The citrus pair improved so quickly
that within a week one of them was fit for duty and was put to work nursing the
others.

It was not the first observation that fresh fruit cured scurvy — that had been
noted, forgotten, and noted again for two centuries — and Lind himself did not
draw the conclusion cleanly. He thought scurvy was a disorder of digestion and
perspiration, and he ruined most of his lemon juice by boiling it into a
concentrate for storage, which destroyed the very thing that made it work. The
Navy took decades to act, and when it finally standardized on citrus in the
closing years of the eighteenth century, scurvy largely disappeared from its
ships. Then it came back, intermittently, in the nineteenth century, partly
because lemons were replaced with West Indian limes that carried far less of the
active compound, and partly because nobody knew what the active compound was.

That is the shape of the whole story. For a long time people knew empirically
that certain foods prevented certain diseases, and the knowledge kept slipping
away because there was no theory to hang it on. The reigning theory said
diseases were caused by things — bad air, germs, toxins — and a disease caused by
the absence of a thing was close to unthinkable.

## The Rice Problem

By the late nineteenth century, beriberi was devastating the crews of the
Japanese Navy. Sufferers developed numbness and weakness in the legs, then a
staggering gait, then in the worst cases heart failure with massive swelling. A
naval medical officer, Kanehiro Takaki, noticed that officers rarely got it and
ordinary sailors did, and that the difference between their tables was that
sailors ate almost nothing but white rice. He persuaded the Navy to run a
comparison between two long voyages with different rations, adding barley, meat,
and vegetables on the second. Beriberi in the fleet collapsed. Takaki concluded
the missing element was protein, which was wrong, but the ration change worked
anyway.

In Java a few years later, Christiaan Eijkman, a Dutch physician sent to
investigate beriberi as an infection, noticed that the chickens in his
laboratory yard had developed a paralysis that looked remarkably like the human
disease. The birds had been fed leftover polished rice from the military
hospital kitchen. When the supply was cut off and they went back to cheap
unpolished rice, they recovered. Eijkman interpreted this as a toxin in the
starch being neutralized by something in the husk — again not quite right, but
the experiment was reproducible and it moved the question into the laboratory.

The idea that finally organized all of this came from Casimir Funk, a Polish
biochemist working in London, who proposed in the years just before the First
World War that beriberi, scurvy, pellagra, and rickets were all caused by the
lack of specific trace compounds in food. He thought the compounds were amines
and named them accordingly: vital amines, vitamines. Most of them turned out not
to be amines at all, and the final "e" was quietly dropped, leaving a word whose
etymology no longer quite parses. The concept survived the correction.

The compound behind beriberi is thiamine, and what it does is specific. In the
cell it becomes thiamine pyrophosphate, a coenzyme that sits in the active site
of several enzymes at the entrance to aerobic metabolism — including the one that
converts pyruvate into acetyl-CoA. Without it, glucose cannot be fully burned.
Tissues that depend most heavily on continuous aerobic glucose metabolism fail
first, which is why beriberi presents as a disease of nerves and of heart
muscle. The same deficiency shows up today in people with chronic alcohol
dependence, as the neurological syndrome described by Wernicke and Korsakoff.

## Goldberger's Unpopular Answer

Pellagra spread through the American South in the early twentieth century,
concentrated in mill towns, orphanages, prisons, and asylums. It produced a
scaly rash on skin exposed to sunlight, digestive collapse, and eventually
confusion and psychosis — the four Ds that medical students still recite:
dermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia, death. Because it clustered in institutions and
poor communities, nearly everyone assumed it was infectious.

Joseph Goldberger, sent by the United States Public Health Service to
investigate, made an observation that should have settled it quickly: in
institutions where pellagra was rampant among the inmates, the staff who lived
and worked in the same buildings did not get it. The staff ate better. He
produced the disease deliberately in volunteer prisoners fed a monotonous
corn-based diet, and cured existing cases by adding milk, eggs, and meat. To
attack the infection hypothesis directly he and several colleagues, including
his wife, deliberately exposed themselves to material taken from pellagra
patients. None of them became ill.

The resistance he met was not only scientific. Pellagra was a disease of poverty
in a region that did not care to be told it had one, and a dietary explanation
implicated the tenant farming economy that kept people eating cornmeal, molasses,
and fat pork. Goldberger died before the missing factor was identified. It turned
out to be niacin, tracked down through a similar disease in dogs called black
tongue, and the chemistry explained a long-standing puzzle. Maize contains
niacin, but in a chemically bound form that human digestion cannot release.
Mesoamerican cultures had for millennia soaked maize in an alkaline solution
before grinding it — nixtamalization — which frees the niacin. Maize travelled to
Europe, Africa, and the American South as a crop without the processing method,
and pellagra followed it. The body can also manufacture niacin from the amino
acid tryptophan, but maize is poor in tryptophan too, so a maize-dominated diet
closes both routes at once.

## Sunlight, Smoke, and a Vitamin That Is Not One

Rickets was the disease of the industrial city. Children's bones failed to
mineralize properly, so the growing skeleton bent under load: bowed legs,
deformed ribcages, thickened wrists. It was so common in Britain that continental
physicians called it the English disease, and it concentrated in the smoky
manufacturing districts where sunlight barely reached street level.

Two lines of evidence converged in the early twentieth century and initially
looked contradictory. Edward Mellanby produced rickets in dogs kept indoors on
oatmeal and cured it with cod liver oil, which pointed to a dietary factor. Other
researchers cured rickets with sunlight or with ultraviolet lamps, which pointed
to something else entirely. Harry Steenbock then showed that irradiating food
itself made it antirachitic, which tied the two together.

Vitamin D is not really a vitamin. It is a hormone precursor that skin
manufactures from a cholesterol derivative when ultraviolet light of the right
wavelength strikes it. The liver puts one hydroxyl group on it, the kidney puts
on a second, and the finished molecule acts on the intestine to increase calcium
absorption, and on bone and kidney to keep blood calcium within its narrow band.
It becomes a dietary requirement only when the skin cannot do its job — high
latitudes, dark winters, coal smoke, indoor work, heavy clothing, or darker skin
in a place with weak sun.

## What a Vitamin Actually Does

Strip away the history and the category is looser than it sounds. A vitamin is
an organic compound that an organism requires in small amounts and cannot
synthesize in sufficient quantity. That definition is species-specific.
Ascorbic acid is a vitamin for humans, other primates, guinea pigs, and some
bats, all of which carry a broken copy of the gene for the last enzyme in its
synthesis. For a dog or a rat it is simply a molecule they make.

Most vitamins do their work as coenzymes or as the raw material for them. Niacin
becomes NAD and NADP, the electron carriers that shuttle reducing power through
nearly every metabolic pathway there is. Riboflavin becomes FAD. Thiamine becomes
the pyrophosphate. Vitamin C is a cofactor for the enzymes that hydroxylate
proline and lysine residues in newly made collagen, which is why scurvy looks the
way it does: collagen is constantly being replaced, and without the hydroxylation
the triple helix will not hold. Old scars reopen, gums fail around the teeth, and
small vessels leak — not because anything attacked the tissue, but because its
scaffolding stopped being rebuilt.

The split between fat-soluble vitamins and water-soluble ones has practical
consequences. The water-soluble ones circulate freely and the kidney disposes of
surpluses, so the body carries small reserves and deficiency can develop within
weeks or months. The fat-soluble ones — A, D, E, and K — are stored in the liver
and adipose tissue, buffering the body for much longer and making overdose
genuinely possible. Arctic explorers who ate polar bear liver learned this
uncomfortably; its vitamin A concentration is high enough to cause acute
poisoning, complete with peeling skin and splitting headache.

What took so long, in every one of these cases, was the epidemiology. Deficiency
diseases cluster. They appear in ships, prisons, asylums, mill towns, besieged
cities, and among the poor of particular regions — exactly the pattern an
infectious agent produces. For most of the nineteenth century the outbreak curve
pointed one way and the cause lay in the other direction entirely, in something
that was not there.
