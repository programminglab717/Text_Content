# Fibre and the Organisms That Eat It

Pull a string off a stick of celery and you are holding something your body has
no tool for. Human digestion runs on a fairly narrow set of enzymes — amylases
for starch, proteases for protein, lipases for fat, a handful of specific sugar
splitters at the intestinal wall — and between them they can break only a few
kinds of chemical bond. The bonds holding celery strings together are not among
them. Neither are the ones in the skin of a bean, the bran of a wheat grain, the
gel inside a soaked chia seed, or the pectin that makes jam set.

That is the entire definition of dietary fibre: carbohydrate that reaches the
large intestine intact because nothing upstream could cut it. It is a negative
category, defined by an absence, which is why it contains such an oddly assorted
collection of substances. And it explains why fibre is the one nutrient whose
effects are mostly not about you. It is about the several hundred species of
bacteria living in your colon, for whom the material you cannot digest is the
main course.

## The Categories Are Blurrier Than the Packet Suggests

Food labels and public health messaging still lean on a split between soluble
and insoluble fibre, which is a reasonable first cut and a poor guide to what
actually happens.

Insoluble fibres are the structural ones — cellulose, hemicelluloses, lignin —
the scaffolding of plant cell walls. Wheat bran is the archetype. They hold
water without dissolving, add physical bulk, and speed transit through the
colon, which is why bran has been the traditional answer to constipation.
Soluble fibres dissolve or disperse and often form gels: pectin from apples and
citrus peel, beta-glucan from oats and barley, the husk of psyllium seed, gums
like guar. These are the ones that slow gastric emptying, blunt the rise in
blood glucose after a meal, and bind bile acids in the small intestine so that
the liver must pull cholesterol out of circulation to make replacements. The
cholesterol-lowering effect of oats works through exactly that route, and it
depends on the beta-glucan staying viscous — finely milled, heavily processed
oats lose some of the effect.

But solubility is not the property the bacteria care about. What matters to them
is fermentability, and it does not map cleanly onto the older division. Pectin
and inulin are soluble and heavily fermented. Psyllium is soluble, gels
beautifully, and is fermented rather little, which is why it bulks stool without
producing much gas. Cellulose is insoluble and largely passes through. Lignin is
not even a carbohydrate.

Then there is resistant starch, which belongs to the same functional family
without appearing on most labels. Starch can escape digestion for several
reasons: because it is physically locked inside intact cell walls, as in whole
grains and seeds; because its granules are crystalline and raw, as in a green
banana or an uncooked potato; or because it was cooked, gelatinised, and then
cooled, allowing the amylose chains to re-associate into a form the enzymes
cannot grip. That last route is the reason cold cooked potato, day-old rice and
stale bread behave slightly differently in the gut from their fresh equivalents.

## A Fermenter Running at Body Temperature

The colon is a slow anaerobic vat. It is crowded — the microbial cells living
there are roughly comparable in number to all the human cells in the body, and
the density of the population in the distal colon is about as high as microbial
life gets anywhere. Two bacterial groups dominate in most people, the
Bacteroidetes and the Firmicutes, with smaller contributions from
Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria and others, and beneath that phylum-level
description sit hundreds of species and thousands of strains whose exact mix is
close to individual.

These organisms carry an enzymatic repertoire that dwarfs our own. Where the
human genome encodes a couple of dozen carbohydrate-active enzymes, the
collective gut microbiome encodes thousands, organised in some species into
tidy genetic modules that switch on when a particular polysaccharide appears.
A Bacteroides cell can sense pectin, deploy the machinery for pectin, and shut
it down again when the pectin runs out. This is why diet changes the community
so quickly: switching what arrives in the colon changes which organisms can pay
their way, and measurable shifts in composition can appear within days.

The chemistry they run is fermentation, not respiration, because there is no
oxygen down there. Fermentation is a lower-yield process, and its waste products
are the point. Bacteria pull what energy they can from the fibre and excrete
short-chain fatty acids — mostly acetate, propionate and butyrate — along with
hydrogen and carbon dioxide.

## Cross-Feeding, and Why Diversity Matters

Very few species do the whole job alone. Fibre degradation in the colon is a
relay. Primary degraders, often Bacteroides or Bifidobacterium, break the long
polysaccharide chains into oligosaccharides and sugars and ferment them to
acetate and lactate. Those intermediates are then substrate for a second tier —
Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia, Eubacterium rectale and their relatives
— which convert acetate and lactate into butyrate. Neither tier flourishes
without the other. Feed a person inulin and the bifidobacteria bloom first, and
the butyrate producers follow, fed on what the bifidobacteria leave behind.

The hydrogen produced along the way would inhibit fermentation if it
accumulated, so other organisms consume it: methanogenic archaea convert it to
methane, sulfate-reducing bacteria to hydrogen sulfide, and some acetogens turn
it back into acetate. Which of these dominates varies between people and
determines, among other more consequential things, whether their flatus is
flammable.

What the host gets from all this is more interesting than a few extra calories,
though there are some of those too — colonic fermentation recovers a modest
amount of energy from material that would otherwise be waste. Butyrate is taken
up by the cells lining the colon and burned as their main fuel, a curious
arrangement in which a tissue of the body depends on bacterial excretion for its
energy supply. Propionate largely travels to the liver. Acetate reaches the
general circulation. Beyond fuel, these molecules act as signals: they influence
the tightness of junctions between intestinal cells, the production of mucus,
and the behaviour of immune cells in the gut wall, nudging the local immune
system towards tolerance rather than inflammation.

## What Happens When the Fibre Stops

The consequences of starving the community are easiest to see in animals.
Experiments in mice fed fibre-free diets show the microbes switching to the only
carbohydrate left available: the mucin glycoproteins of the mucus layer that
normally keeps bacteria at arm's length from the intestinal wall. The layer
thins. Organisms that specialise in mucin, such as Akkermansia muciniphila,
expand. Bacteria end up closer to the epithelium than they should be, and
susceptibility to gut pathogens rises. Whether the same process plays out in
humans on a typical low-fibre Western diet is not settled, but it offers a
plausible mechanism for a pattern that epidemiology has pointed at for decades:
populations eating large amounts of unrefined plant material have less
diverticular disease, less constipation, lower rates of colorectal cancer and
better cardiometabolic markers than populations eating little.

Most people in wealthy countries eat well under the thirty grams a day that
national guidelines tend to recommend — often around half of it. Closing that
gap is less a matter of finding fibre supplements than of eating the foods that
happen to contain fibre and a great deal else besides: pulses, whole grains,
nuts, fruit with the skin on, vegetables that require chewing.

There is a caveat that anyone who has doubled their bean intake in a week
already knows. Fermentation makes gas, and a community that has not been
supplied with a given substrate for years lacks the species to handle it
efficiently. Increasing fibre gradually gives the population time to adjust. And
for a minority — particularly people with irritable bowel syndrome — certain
rapidly fermented short-chain carbohydrates, the ones grouped under the label
FODMAPs, produce enough gas and osmotic water to cause genuine pain in a gut
that is unusually sensitive to distension. The usual approach there is a
temporary restriction followed by systematic reintroduction, precisely because
the long-term goal is still to feed the organisms, not to evict them.

The celery string, then, is not waste passing through. It is a delivery.
