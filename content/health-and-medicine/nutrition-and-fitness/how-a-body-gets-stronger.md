# How a Body Gets Stronger

Give an untrained person a barbell and eight weeks of sensible practice and they
will very often add a third to what they can lift, while looking in the mirror at
a body that has not visibly changed. No new muscle worth mentioning, no measurable
increase in limb circumference, and yet the weight goes up decisively. This is
the single most useful fact about strength, and it is the one most often lost
under the assumption that strength is stored in muscle the way water is stored
in a tank. Strength is a property of a system. Muscle is one component of it.

The system includes the nervous system that decides how much of the available
muscle to switch on and how fast to drive it, the tendons that transmit force to
bone, the skeleton's particular geometry of levers, and the accumulated skill of
the specific movement being performed. Training changes all of these, but not at
the same rate, and the order in which they respond is what gives early progress
its peculiar character.

## The First Weeks Happen Upstream

A maximal voluntary contraction is not maximal. Stimulate a muscle electrically
during one and you can usually squeeze out a little more force than the person
was producing themselves — less in a trained athlete, more in a novice. The
nervous system holds something back, and a good deal of early strength training
consists of learning to hold back less.

Several things are happening at once. Motor units are recruited in a fixed order
from smallest to largest, and the largest, most powerful ones require the most
drive to reach. Novices frequently cannot generate enough drive to reach the top
of that order at all; practice at high effort gives them access to units they
were not previously using. Firing rates rise, so the units that are recruited
produce more of the force they are capable of. Antagonist muscles, which in an
unpracticed movement fire alongside the prime movers and fight them, learn to
stand down. Stabilizers learn their timing, so less force leaks out of the
system as wobble.

The strangest evidence for the neural account is cross-education. Train one arm
for several weeks and the untrained arm gets measurably stronger too, despite
having done nothing and grown nothing. The effect is modest but it is real and
reproducible, and it has no possible muscular explanation. Whatever improved was
shared between the two sides, which means it was in the brain and spinal cord.

## Then the Tissue Catches Up

Hypertrophy is slower and it has a clear trigger: mechanical tension in a fibre,
sustained, repeated, and high enough to matter. Proteins in the fibre membrane
and within the cytoskeleton register that tension and convert it into a chemical
signal, which converges on a regulatory complex known as mTOR that governs the
rate of protein synthesis. Synthesis rises for a day or two after a hard bout
and then subsides. Muscle is built the way a cathedral is built: one session
adds almost nothing, and the sum of many sessions is the whole structure.

Fibres grow mainly by adding contractile filaments in parallel, packing more
myofibrils into a larger cross-section. That creates a logistical problem,
because each nucleus in a muscle fibre can only service so much surrounding
cytoplasm, and muscle fibre nuclei cannot divide. The solution sits just outside
the fibre membrane: satellite cells, a reserve population of stem cells that
proliferate in response to loading and damage and fuse into the existing fibre,
donating their nuclei to it. A growing fibre is therefore also acquiring
governance for its new territory.

What actually drives this is more specific than "lifting heavy." The common
pathway is that a fibre must be recruited and must then contract against high
tension — which happens when the load is heavy, and also when the load is lighter
but the set is taken close enough to failure that fatigue forces the high
threshold units into play. This is why quite different-looking programmes
produce broadly similar growth, and why the total amount of hard work matters
more than any one variable.

## Why Size and Strength Are Not the Same Number

They are correlated, obviously. Across a population, bigger muscles lift more.
But the relationship is loose enough to be interesting, and the reasons are
worth laying out because they explain a great deal of what looks like unfairness
in a gym.

Force is produced per unit of cross-sectional area, and that figure — specific
tension — varies between people and between muscles. Fibre composition matters,
as does how completely the fibres can be activated. Then there is architecture.
Many muscles have their fibres set at an angle to the tendon rather than running
straight along it; this pennation lets far more fibres pack into a given volume,
at the price of some of their force being directed sideways. Hypertrophy often
increases the pennation angle, so a muscle that has grown may have changed its
mechanical character as well as its size.

Then there is the skeleton, which the individual does not get to choose. A tendon
inserting slightly further from the joint's axis of rotation gives a longer lever
arm, multiplying the torque produced by the same muscular force. Limb proportions
change the distance a weight travels and the angles at which the muscles are
working. Two people with identical muscle mass can differ substantially in what
they can lift because of where their tendons attach.

And strength is stubbornly specific to the task. A person who has trained a
movement is strong at that movement, at that range of motion, at roughly those
speeds. Move the bar's path, change the grip, or test an unfamiliar angle and the
number falls. Part of this is skill and part is that the loaded part of the range
of motion is the part that adapted. Some evidence suggests that training a muscle
in a lengthened position can add sarcomeres in series, shifting the length at
which it produces its best force — a structural change to where a muscle is
strong rather than how strong it is.

## The Slow Tissues

Muscle is the fastest-adapting part of the machine, which is an
underappreciated hazard. Tendon is built from densely cross-linked collagen with
a modest blood supply and a slow turnover rate, and while it does adapt to
loading by becoming stiffer and thicker, it does so over a much longer horizon
than the muscle pulling on it. The same goes for ligaments and for the
cartilaginous surfaces of joints.

Bone adapts too, and more responsively than its appearance suggests. Osteocytes
embedded in the mineral matrix detect strain and fluid movement through the tiny
channels around them, and they signal for deposition where load is high and
resorption where it is not. Loading a bone repeatedly and hard causes it to
thicken in the regions that carry the stress. But the remodelling cycle is
measured in months.

The result is a widening gap during the first year of serious training between
what a person's muscles and nervous system can now do and what their connective
tissue is yet prepared to tolerate. Most of the classic overuse injuries of
beginners and of returning athletes live in that gap.

## What Soreness Is Not

The muscle ache that arrives a day or two after unfamiliar work is caused
primarily by eccentric contraction — muscle lengthening under load, as in the
lowering phase of a lift or in running downhill. It involves microscopic
disruption of the contractile apparatus, an inflammatory response, and
sensitization of the nerve endings in the connective tissue around the fibres.
It is not lactic acid, which is cleared within an hour of exercise and has
nothing to do with a pain that peaks on day two.

Nor is it a measure of how much adaptation was triggered. It fades rapidly with
repetition — the same session that crippled someone in week one barely registers
in week four — through a protective adjustment known as the repeated bout effect,
even as the training continues to produce gains. Soreness reports novelty, not
progress.

Detraining, finally, is not symmetrical with training. Strength falls more
slowly than size does, and both return faster on a second attempt than they
arrived on the first. In rodents the explanation is fairly clear: nuclei acquired
from satellite cells during growth appear to persist in the fibre after it has
shrunk, leaving it primed to rebuild. Whether the same mechanism explains the
phenomenon in humans is still argued, but the practical observation is
uncontroversial to anyone who has come back from an injury and found the lost
ground recovered in a fraction of the time it originally took.
