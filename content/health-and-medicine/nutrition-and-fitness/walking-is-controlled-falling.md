# Walking Is Controlled Falling

In the late 1980s an engineer named Tad McGeer built a machine that walked down
a shallow ramp. It had no motor. It had no battery, no sensors, no controller,
no program. It was a pair of jointed legs with carefully chosen masses and
lengths, and when you set it at the top of a slope of a few degrees and gave it
a nudge, it walked — swinging one leg through, catching its weight, swinging the
other, step after step, with a gait that people who watched it described as
eerily human. Filmed and shown without context, it is hard to tell from a person
walking away from the camera.

The passive dynamic walker is the single most instructive object in the study of
human movement, because of what it proves by omission. Whatever walking is, a
large part of it is not being commanded. It is a property of the legs
themselves, of their mass and length and hinge points, falling under gravity in
a way that happens to repeat. Muscles and nervous systems are not generating the
gait from scratch. They are supplying a modest amount of energy to a mechanical
system that is already inclined to do most of it.

## The Pendulum, Upside Down

The standard model of a walking step is an inverted pendulum. Your body mass
sits atop a stiff supporting leg and vaults over the top of it, an arc rather
than a straight line. Your centre of mass is lowest just after your foot lands,
climbs to its highest point when that leg is vertical beneath you, and falls
again as you pitch forward onto the next foot. If you walk beside a wall and
watch the top of your head against the bricks, you can see it: a shallow rise
and fall, twice per stride.

The efficiency of this comes from timing. Rising, you are converting the forward
kinetic energy of your body into gravitational potential energy — you slow
slightly on the way up. Falling, the potential converts back to kinetic and you
speed up. The two energies are out of phase, trading back and forth like a
child's swing, and only the leakage needs replacing. A well-executed walking
step recovers a large fraction of the energy of the previous one without any
muscle doing positive work at all.

Running works on the opposite principle. There the kinetic and potential
energies rise and fall together, so no pendulum exchange is available, and the
energy is instead stored elastically — stretched tendons, chiefly the Achilles
and the arch of the foot, acting as springs that compress on landing and rebound
on push-off. Two completely different mechanical strategies, with a discontinuity
between them, which is why the change from walking to running feels like
switching gear rather than speeding up.

The arms are part of the mechanism, not decoration. As one leg swings forward
it carries angular momentum about the vertical axis of the body, which would set
the torso twisting if nothing opposed it. The opposite arm swings forward at the
same time and cancels most of it. Hold your arms still and walk, and you will
feel the trunk begin to rotate and the effort go up; measurements confirm the
cost of walking rises when arm swing is restricted, and rises further if the
arms are made to swing in phase with the legs instead of against them. The swing
itself is largely passive, driven by the motion of the shoulders rather than by
deliberate muscular effort, which makes it another piece of the gait that the
body gets almost for free.

The losses in walking are concentrated at the transitions. Every time the
leading foot lands, the body's velocity has to be redirected from falling
forward-and-down to rising forward-and-up, and that redirection costs energy.
It happens during the brief window when both feet are on the ground, which is
the formal definition of walking: in a walk, there is always at least one foot
down, and twice per stride there are two. Lose the double-support phase and you
are running.

## Why You Break Into a Run

There is a speed above which walking stops working, and the reason is a straight
piece of physics.

Going over the top of the stance leg means travelling along a circular arc, and
travelling along an arc requires a centripetal force pointing at the centre of
the circle — downward, toward the hip. Gravity supplies it. But the force needed
grows with the square of your speed, and gravity does not grow at all. Walk fast
enough and the required centripetal force exceeds what gravity can provide. At
that point the body would need to be pulled down onto the arc, and nothing is
pulling it down; instead it leaves the ground. You are airborne, which means you
are running.

The threshold works out at somewhere around two metres per second for adults, a
brisk pace, and it scales with leg length — which is why children break into a
trot to keep up with adults who are merely walking, and why the same physics
predicts the transition speed reasonably well across animals of very different
sizes.

Race walkers live at exactly this boundary. The rules require that one foot
appear to be in contact with the ground at all times and that the leading leg
straighten on contact, which ought to cap their speed near the theoretical
limit. They beat it by flattening the arc: rotating and dropping the pelvis so
that the centre of mass travels a path much closer to horizontal than an
ordinary walk allows. The gait looks peculiar because it is a solution to a
constraint, and it is fast — race walkers cover ground quicker than most people
can run.

## The Cheapest Thing the Body Does

Plot the energy cost of walking against speed and you get a U. Very slow walking
is expensive per unit of distance, because you are spending time holding
yourself up with little to show for it. Very fast walking is expensive because
the mechanics are being strained past their comfortable range. In between there
is a minimum, at a bit over a metre per second for most adults, and left to
themselves people walk at almost exactly that speed. Nobody calculates it. The
nervous system finds the cost minimum through something closer to practice than
to reasoning, and it re-finds it when conditions change — carry a load, walk on
sand, put on a leg brace, and within a few minutes the self-selected gait has
shifted to the new optimum.

Compared with most animals our size, humans walk cheaply. A chimpanzee moving on
two legs spends considerably more energy per distance than a human does, and the
difference lies in the anatomy that makes us awkward at almost everything else:
long legs, a pelvis rotated to hold a body upright over one foot, a foot with a
stiff arch that acts as a lever rather than a grasping hand, a knee that locks
straight so that standing costs almost nothing. We are poor sprinters and
indifferent climbers, and we are extremely good at covering ground slowly for a
long time.

## What the Rest of the Body Contributes

The passive walker overstates the case, since it only works on a ramp, in a
straight line, on a smooth surface, and falls over if anything is unexpected.
Real walking needs a controller, and most of that controller lives below the
brain. Circuits in the spinal cord can generate an alternating stepping rhythm
without any input from above — the reason newborns held upright over a surface
make stepping movements weeks before they can sit, and the basis for treadmill
training in some spinal injuries.

What the brain contributes is everything to do with the environment. Vision
plans foot placement several steps ahead, which is why walking in the dark feels
so tentative. The vestibular system reports head orientation. Sensors in the
skin of the sole and in the ankle report what the ground turned out to be. The
corrections happen fast and mostly unconsciously, and the whole thing runs on a
small enough share of attention that people talk, argue and read while doing it.

Which is why the failure mode is diagnostic. Ask an older adult a demanding
question while they are walking, and if they stop walking to answer, the
postural control system is no longer running cheaply enough to share. Gait speed
turns out to be one of the better single predictors of how an older person is
doing overall — slow walking is associated with subsequent disability and
mortality across many studies, not because walking slowly causes harm but
because a walking speed is a summary statistic for muscle, joints, heart, lungs,
vision, balance and cognition, all reported in one number that takes eight
metres of hallway to collect.

## Ten Thousand Steps

The number everyone knows did not come from research. It came from marketing.
Around the time of the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, a Japanese company sold a pedometer
under a name that translates roughly as "ten-thousand-step meter," chosen partly
because the character for ten thousand resembles a walking figure. The number
was memorable, round, and invented.

It has since been studied properly, which is the unusual part of the story, and
the finding is that the marketing department was not far wrong but had the shape
of the curve incorrect. Benefit accrues steeply at the low end — the difference
between very little walking and a moderate amount is large — and then flattens,
with the plateau arriving well below ten thousand for older adults. The slogan
set a threshold. The data describe a slope, steepest exactly where most
sedentary people are standing.

Long before any of this, walking was a spectator sport. In the 1870s and 1880s
pedestrianism filled arenas in New York and London, where competitors walked in
circles for six days, sleeping in snatches, while crowds bet on the outcome and
newspapers printed hourly mileages. Edward Payson Weston became famous for it,
and was still making long-distance walks across the United States in his
seventies. The whole spectacle collapsed when the safety bicycle arrived and
gave audiences something faster to watch.
