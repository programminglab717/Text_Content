# Metabolism and the Accounting of Energy

Drop a single cashew into a bomb calorimeter — a thick steel vessel packed with
pressurised oxygen and submerged in a measured bath of water — and set it
alight. The nut burns to ash in seconds. The water around the vessel warms by a
small, precise amount, and from that temperature rise you can calculate how much
energy was held in the chemical bonds of that cashew. Food energy figures
descend, ultimately, from measurements like this one. So does a good deal of the
confusion about metabolism, because a body is not a furnace and does not treat a
cashew the way a calorimeter does. It extracts a broadly comparable amount of
energy, but slowly, through dozens of enzyme-catalysed steps, and it spends part
of the proceeds on the extraction itself.

Metabolism is not a dial that some people are fortunate enough to have set high.
It is the entire set of chemical reactions that keeps a body running. Those
reactions divide roughly into catabolism, the controlled dismantling of
molecules to release energy, and anabolism, the assembly of new molecules at an
energy cost. Both run continuously and simultaneously. Even asleep, you are
breaking down and rebuilding proteins, remodelling bone, replacing the lining of
your gut, ferrying ions back across membranes they have just leaked across, and
keeping roughly forty litres of water at a temperature the surrounding air is
constantly trying to change. Almost all of that work is paid for in one currency.

## The Currency Is ATP

Adenosine triphosphate is a small molecule carrying three phosphate groups in a
row, and the last of those bonds is where the useful energy sits. Snap it off,
and the released energy can be coupled to something that would not otherwise
happen: a muscle protein changing shape, a pump pushing sodium uphill, an amino
acid being fastened onto a growing chain. The molecule left behind, adenosine
diphosphate, is then recharged and used again. Almost nothing is stored. The
total ATP present in a body at any instant would fuel only a few seconds of hard
work; what matters is the rate at which it is regenerated. Over a full day the
turnover is enormous — textbooks put the mass of ATP made and spent at something
on the order of a person's own body weight, recycled over and over.

The recharging happens in stages. Glucose is first split in the cytoplasm
through glycolysis, a sequence that yields a modest amount of ATP and works
without oxygen. The products then enter the mitochondria, where the citric acid
cycle strips them of electrons and hands those electrons to carrier molecules.
The carriers deliver them to a chain of protein complexes embedded in the inner
mitochondrial membrane, which use the energy of each electron transfer to pump
protons out, building a gradient across the membrane. The protons flow back in
through a rotary enzyme that spins like a turbine and makes ATP as it turns. At
the end of the chain, the spent electrons are handed to oxygen, which combines
with protons to form water. That last step is the reason you breathe. Fats and
amino acids enter the same machinery at different points, which is why the body
can run on such a variety of inputs, and why the energy content of a food
depends heavily on what kind of molecule it is: fat carries a little over twice
the energy per gram of carbohydrate or protein, because its carbons come more
thoroughly stocked with hydrogen and are further from being oxidised already.

## Where a Day's Energy Actually Goes

If you sat still in a comfortable room, twelve hours after your last meal, and a
technician measured the oxygen you consumed and the carbon dioxide you produced,
you would be measuring resting metabolic rate. For most people this accounts for
the clear majority of daily energy use — commonly cited as something between
sixty and seventy per cent. It is not, as the phrase "resting" suggests, an idle
state. It is the cost of staying alive: breathing, circulating, filtering,
thinking, holding body temperature steady, and replacing tissue.

That cost is not spread evenly across the body. Per gram, the most expensive
organs are the heart, kidneys, liver and brain, and they run at a rate many times
higher than muscle does at rest. The brain in particular is a standout — in an
adult at rest it takes something like a fifth of the body's energy while
representing a small fraction of its mass, and in a young child the share is
higher still. Skeletal muscle is cheap per gram but there is a great deal of it,
so it still contributes substantially, and it is the one tissue whose demand can
rise by an order of magnitude within seconds.

The best single predictor of someone's resting rate is fat-free mass — muscle,
organs, bone and water, everything that is not adipose tissue. Larger people
have higher metabolic rates in absolute terms, which surprises people who assume
the relationship runs the other way. Two adults of the same weight can differ,
but usually by less than the folklore implies, and the differences track body
composition more than temperament.

On top of resting expenditure sit two further accounts. One is the thermic
effect of food: digesting, absorbing and processing a meal itself costs energy,
typically around a tenth of what the meal delivers. Protein is much the most
expensive of the three macronutrients to handle, fat close to the cheapest — a
gram of dietary fat can be moved into storage with very little modification,
whereas an amino acid destined to become glucose has to be dismantled and its
nitrogen packaged for disposal. The other account is movement, and it is the one
that varies most. It includes deliberate exercise, but for most people the larger
and more variable part is everything else: walking to a bus, standing, fidgeting,
carrying shopping, gesturing while talking. Researchers call this non-exercise
activity thermogenesis, and it differs strikingly between individuals living
apparently similar lives.

## Balance Is a Feedback System, Not a Ledger

The arithmetic of energy balance — intake minus expenditure equals change in
stores — is true in the sense that thermodynamics is true. What it conceals is
that the two sides of the equation are connected.

Eat less for a sustained period and expenditure falls. Part of that is simple:
a smaller body costs less to run and less to move. But the reduction tends to
exceed what the change in mass alone predicts. Thyroid hormone output drifts
down, sympathetic nervous system activity falls, muscles become marginally more
efficient at a given workload, and spontaneous movement quietly decreases —
people who are underfed sit more, fidget less, and take the stairs less often
without deciding to. Appetite signalling shifts in the same direction: ghrelin
rises, leptin falls, and hunger becomes harder to ignore. These are not failures
of will; they are a regulatory system doing what it evolved to do in an
environment where food ran out.

There is also good evidence that adding exercise does not simply add its cost to
the total. Studies of hunter-gatherer populations, whose daily physical activity
vastly exceeds that of office workers, found total daily energy expenditure
surprisingly close to that of sedentary people of similar size. The interpretation
that has gained ground is that the body compensates: energy spent on movement is
partly taken back from other budgets, including background inflammation,
reproductive hormone output and restlessness. This does not mean exercise is
pointless — the health effects are large and well established — but it does help
explain why a new running habit often produces less weight change than the
treadmill's calorie display promises.

## The Question of Age

Almost everyone over forty will tell you their metabolism has slowed. The belief
is so universal that it was startling when a large international pooling of
doubly labelled water measurements — a method that tracks the disappearance of
labelled hydrogen and oxygen from the body to calculate real-world energy
expenditure over days — suggested a different pattern. Adjusted for fat-free
mass, energy expenditure appears to be highest in infancy by a wide margin, to
settle by around age twenty, and then to remain remarkably flat through middle
age before beginning a slow decline in the sixties.

If that holds, what people are noticing in their forties is mostly the loss of
muscle and the quiet reduction in movement that accompanies desk work, long
commutes and joint pain, rather than a change in cellular machinery. Which is
oddly encouraging, because the causes are more amenable to intervention than a
mysteriously flagging engine would be.

## What Is Actually Adjustable

Very little of resting metabolism responds to anything you eat. Caffeine and
capsaicin nudge it briefly, cold exposure activates brown adipose tissue in
adults to a degree that remains under investigation, and thyroid disease can move
it substantially in either direction — but no ordinary food raises it enough to
matter against a day's total. What does move is the size of the engine and how
much it is asked to do. Resistance training preserves and adds fat-free mass,
which raises the resting rate modestly and, more importantly, keeps the
machinery of movement intact into old age. Protein at each meal is expensive to
process and helps protect muscle during weight loss. And the least glamorous
lever, the sheer volume of low-intensity movement across the day, is also the one
with the widest range between the person who walks everywhere and the one who
does not.

None of which makes the calorimeter wrong about the cashew. It simply means the
number it gives you is the beginning of the accounting, not the end.
