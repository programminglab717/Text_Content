# How the Food Label Got Its Numbers

In the winter of 1902, in the basement of a government building in Washington,
a dozen young men sat down to a series of carefully weighed dinners. They were
clerks and students, recruited by Harvey Washington Wiley, chief chemist of the
Department of Agriculture, and they had agreed to eat measured doses of the
preservatives then routinely added to American food: borax, salicylic acid,
formaldehyde, copper sulfate, benzoate of soda. Every scrap of what they ate was
recorded. So was everything that came out of them. Newspapers called them the
Poison Squad, which was closer to the truth than the official name for the work,
and the men reported headaches, nausea and, in some cases, an inability to
continue.

Wiley was not trying to establish safe limits. He was trying to make a
political argument with evidence, in a country where a tin might contain almost
anything and say almost nothing. Milk was watered and whitened with chalk.
Coffee was padded with chicory and roasted grain. "Strawberry" jam could be
apple pulp, glucose syrup, timothy seed for pips and coal-tar dye. There was no
requirement to list ingredients because there was no general requirement to tell
the truth.

## Honesty Before Nutrition

The first laws that emerged addressed fraud, not health. The United States
passed the Pure Food and Drugs Act in 1906, the same year Upton Sinclair's
novel about the Chicago meatpacking yards horrified readers who had opened it
expecting a case for socialism. Britain had moved earlier, with sale-of-food
acts in the nineteenth century aimed at adulteration. The principle in all of
them was misbranding: a food had to be what it claimed to be, and could not
conceal filth or poison. What went on the label was largely a negative
requirement — do not lie — rather than a positive one.

The 1938 Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act in the United States, passed after
a sulfanilamide elixir dissolved in diethylene glycol killed more than a hundred
people, strengthened enforcement and introduced something that shaped labels for
decades: standards of identity. If a food met the legal recipe for mayonnaise or
ice cream or ketchup, the recipe itself was the guarantee, and the manufacturer
did not have to list what was in it. The unintended effect was that some of the
most familiar products on the shelf were the least informative. Full ingredient
declaration for ordinary packaged food arrived gradually over the following
decades, and the ordering rule that now seems obvious — by descending weight —
had to be written down.

## From Ingredients to Nutrients

Nutrition information is a much younger idea, and it grew out of a change in
what public health was worried about. Early-twentieth-century food policy
targeted deficiency and contamination. By the 1960s the concerns in wealthy
countries had shifted towards heart disease and what people were eating too much
of. American regulators introduced nutrition labelling in the early 1970s, but
only as a conditional requirement: a manufacturer had to declare nutrients if it
had fortified the product or made a nutritional claim about it. Say nothing, and
you could print nothing.

That arrangement produced a strange landscape of partial information, and it
ended with the Nutrition Labeling and Education Act of 1990, which made a
standardised panel mandatory on most packaged foods and gave it the black-ruled
box, bold "Nutrition Facts" heading and column of indented nutrients that is now
instantly recognisable. Two design decisions in that panel deserve attention
because both have been argued about ever since.

The first was to express everything per serving rather than per hundred grams,
with serving sizes fixed by regulation to reflect the amounts people customarily
eat. The intention was realism. The effect, for a long while, was that reference
amounts set decades earlier drifted away from actual behaviour, and a bottle of
soft drink that any reasonable person drinks in one sitting could report its
sugar in halves or thirds. The second decision was the percent Daily Value
column, which translates each nutrient into a share of a reference intake based
on a two-thousand-calorie diet — a figure chosen as a round approximation and
applying precisely to nobody.

The panel has been revised since. Trans fat gained its own line in the 2000s,
which turned out to be one of the more effective labelling interventions ever
attempted, because manufacturers reformulated rather than print the number. A
larger redesign in the 2010s enlarged the calorie figure, updated serving sizes
towards what people really consume, replaced vitamins A and C with vitamin D and
potassium as the mandatory micronutrients, and added a line for added sugars —
distinguishing, for the first time, the sugar naturally present in milk or fruit
from the sugar someone put in.

## Two Systems With Different Instincts

The European Union arrived at a comparable place by a different route. Its
Food Information to Consumers regulation, adopted in 2011 and phased in over the
following years, made a nutrition declaration compulsory across the bloc and
insisted it be given per hundred grams or hundred millilitres, with per-serving
figures optional alongside. That single choice makes comparison between products
almost trivial, at the cost of requiring a little arithmetic to work out what is
in the packet you are actually holding.

The European list is shorter and blunter: energy in both kilojoules and
kilocalories, fat, of which saturates, carbohydrate, of which sugars, protein,
and salt. Salt rather than sodium — a conversion made deliberately, on the
grounds that people think in terms of salt and cannot be expected to multiply by
two and a half in a supermarket aisle. The same regulation requires the fourteen
substances that account for most food allergy to be emphasised within the
ingredient list itself, usually in bold, rather than buried in a separate
sentence.

Both systems police claims, and here the European approach is the stricter. Under
its rules a health claim must appear on an authorised register before it can be
used, and the assessment that built that register removed a great many
long-standing marketing phrases. Even the word "probiotic" has been treated as
an unauthorised implied health claim in much of the bloc. Nutrient content claims
— "low fat", "source of fibre", "reduced sugar" — have defined thresholds in both
jurisdictions. The word "natural", by contrast, has long been close to
meaningless in American food marketing, having never been given a formal
definition.

## The Fight Moved to the Front

Back-of-pack panels are honest and largely unread. Shoppers spend seconds on a
decision, so the real contest of the last two decades has been over the front of
the package.

Britain popularised the voluntary multiple traffic light, colouring fat,
saturates, sugars and salt red, amber or green. France backed Nutri-Score, a
five-letter scale from a green A to a red E computed from a formula that balances
unfavourable components against favourable ones, and several other European
countries have adopted it. Industry has generally preferred schemes that report
numbers without judgement.

The most aggressive experiment came from Chile, which in 2016 required black
octagonal warnings — high in sugars, high in calories, high in saturated fat,
high in salt — on products crossing set thresholds, and attached consequences to
them. Foods carrying a warning could not be sold or advertised in schools, and
could not use cartoon characters to appeal to children, which is why the tiger
vanished from Chilean cereal boxes. Mexico and several other countries have
since adopted variants. The design logic is the opposite of Nutri-Score's: not a
summary rating to compare products by, but an unignorable flag on the worst
offenders.

## Reading One

None of this makes a label self-explanatory, and a few habits do most of the
work. Find the serving size before anything else, and check how many servings
the package holds. Compare products on the per-hundred-gram column where one
exists, because serving sizes differ between brands in ways that flatter some of
them. Read the ingredient list as a ranking by weight, and notice when the same
substance appears several times under different names — syrups, concentrates,
malts and the many words ending in -ose can each sit below the threshold that
would push one of them to the top of the list.

And be aware that the numbers are rounded, sometimes generously. Rules in some
jurisdictions permit a small amount per serving to be declared as zero, which is
how a spray oil that is entirely fat can advertise no calories, and how the trans
fat line can read nought on a product whose ingredients still include a partially
hydrogenated oil. The label is a legal document written under constraints. It
tells the truth within them.
