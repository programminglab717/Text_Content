# The Principles of Vaccination

In the spring of 1796 an English country doctor took a small amount of fluid
from a sore on the hand of a dairymaid named Sarah Nelmes, who had caught
cowpox from a cow, and scratched it into the arm of an eight-year-old boy named
James Phipps. The boy developed a mild local sore and a brief fever. Some weeks
later Edward Jenner deliberately exposed him to smallpox. Nothing happened.

By the standards of any modern research ethics committee the experiment is
indefensible. By the standards of 1796 it was a careful test of a piece of
rural folklore — the widely repeated observation that milkmaids who had been
through cowpox did not get smallpox — and it worked. Jenner published, the
practice spread across Europe within a few years, and the Latin word for cow,
*vacca*, gave the procedure its name.

## Before Jenner

Jenner did not invent the idea of provoking an infection on purpose. For
centuries, in China, India, parts of Africa and across the Ottoman Empire,
people had practiced variolation: taking material from a smallpox pustule and
introducing it into a healthy person through a scratch or up the nose. Given
deliberately, by that route, in a small dose, smallpox usually produced a
milder illness than the naturally inhaled disease, and survivors were immune
for life. The method worked. It also killed a meaningful fraction of the people
who underwent it, and the inoculated could transmit real smallpox to others.

Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, who had survived smallpox herself and had watched
variolation performed in Constantinople while her husband served as ambassador
there, campaigned to introduce it in England in the 1720s. In Boston in the
same decade, Cotton Mather learned of the practice from Onesimus, an enslaved
West African man in his household who described how it was done where he came
from, and pressed the physician Zabdiel Boylston to try it during an epidemic —
to considerable public fury. What Jenner offered, seventy years later, was the
same protection using a related animal virus that made people only slightly ill
and could not seed a smallpox outbreak. That was the advance: the risk was
decoupled from the disease.

The conceptual generalization took another eighty years. Louis Pasteur, working
on chicken cholera, left a bacterial culture sitting over a summer and found
that the aged, weakened culture no longer killed his birds — and that the birds
it failed to kill were afterward resistant to fresh, lethal culture. He had
stumbled onto attenuation: the deliberate weakening of a pathogen in the
laboratory until it can still be recognized by the immune system but can no
longer cause disease. He went on to attenuated anthrax vaccine for livestock,
demonstrated publicly on a farm at Pouilly-le-Fort in 1881, and then to rabies,
first given to a mauled boy named Joseph Meister in 1885. Pasteur chose to call
all of these vaccines, in explicit deference to Jenner, and the word broke free
of cows for good.

## What the Immune System Is Actually Learning

To see why any of this works, you have to separate two halves of the immune
system. The innate half is fast, generic and inherited: barriers, complement
proteins, phagocytes, and receptors that recognize broad molecular signatures
common to whole classes of microbes. It responds within minutes and learns
nothing.

The adaptive half is slow, specific, and educated by experience. Its working
units are lymphocytes, and the trick that makes it possible is that each B cell
and T cell, as it matures, randomly rearranges the gene segments coding for its
receptor. The result is a population of many millions of cells, each carrying a
different randomly generated receptor, collectively capable of binding almost
any molecular shape that exists — including shapes from organisms that have
never existed. Nothing is anticipated. The repertoire is simply enormous.

When a pathogen arrives, the rare cells whose receptors happen to fit some part
of it are selected: they bind their target, receive confirming signals, and
begin dividing. This is clonal selection, and it takes time — a week or two on
a first encounter. B cells mature into plasma cells that secrete antibody,
proteins shaped to latch onto a specific piece of a pathogen, called an
antigen. Antibodies neutralize viruses by blocking the structures they use to
enter cells, clump bacteria together, and tag everything they bind for
destruction. T cells do different work: helper T cells license and direct the
response, while cytotoxic T cells recognize and kill the body's own cells that
have been infected and are displaying fragments of the invader on their
surface.

In specialized structures called germinal centers, activated B cells then do
something remarkable: they mutate their own antibody genes at high rate and
compete, so that the variants binding the antigen most tightly are the ones
that survive and proliferate. The antibodies produced late in an infection are
measurably better than the ones produced at the start.

Most of these cells die once the threat is cleared. A minority persist as
memory cells — pre-selected, already specific, and primed to respond in days
instead of weeks. That reserve is what vaccination is for. A vaccine is simply
a way of running the education without running the disease.

## Ways to Show the Immune System a Threat

The various vaccine platforms are different answers to one question: how much
of a pathogen, in what form, do you need to present in order to build durable
memory without causing harm?

Live attenuated vaccines use a weakened but still replicating organism —
measles, mumps, rubella, yellow fever, the oral polio vaccine. Because the
agent actually multiplies and infects cells, it triggers both antibody and
cytotoxic T cell responses and usually produces very long-lasting immunity from
one or two doses. The trade-offs are real: they need a cold chain, they are
generally avoided in people with severely weakened immune systems, and an
attenuated virus can in rare cases revert toward virulence.

Inactivated vaccines use the whole organism killed by heat or chemicals. They
cannot replicate or revert, but they present the immune system with a corpse
rather than an invader, so the response is weaker and needs boosting. Subunit
vaccines go further and present only the piece that matters — a surface
protein, a capsid — which is safer still and more easily manufactured, at the
cost of being even less alarming to the immune system on its own. Toxoid
vaccines, used against tetanus and diphtheria, are a special case: the disease
is caused not by the bacterium spreading but by a toxin it secretes, so the
vaccine is that toxin chemically inactivated. It teaches the body to neutralize
a poison.

Conjugate vaccines solved a specific and deadly problem. Several of the worst
bacterial killers of small children, including *Haemophilus influenzae* type b
and the pneumococcus, are wrapped in polysaccharide capsules. Sugars provoke
only a weak, T-cell-independent antibody response, and infants respond to them
hardly at all. Chemically linking the polysaccharide to a carrier protein
brings helper T cells into the reaction and converts a useless response into a
strong, memory-forming one. Childhood meningitis rates in countries that
introduced these vaccines collapsed.

The newer platforms deliver instructions rather than protein. A viral vector
vaccine uses a harmless virus to carry a gene for the target antigen into
cells; an mRNA vaccine wraps the genetic message in lipid nanoparticles that
fuse with cell membranes. In both cases the recipient's own cells manufacture
the antigen for a short period and display it. The appeal is speed of design —
changing the payload is a matter of changing a sequence — and the fact that
antigen made inside a cell is efficiently presented to cytotoxic T cells.

Purified antigens almost always need help, and that help is the adjuvant. The
immune system evolved to respond strongly when it detects damage and microbial
danger signals alongside a foreign molecule; a clean protein injected by itself
looks like harmless debris. Aluminium salts, the oldest adjuvants, and the
newer emulsions and receptor agonists all work by supplying that context of
alarm.

## Why Some Last a Lifetime and Others Do Not

Vaccines differ enormously in durability, and the reasons are instructive.
Measles immunity is effectively permanent because the virus is antigenically
stable and the live vaccine generates a response close to the one a real
infection would. Tetanus needs periodic boosting because antibody levels
against the toxoid decline slowly and there is no natural re-exposure to top
them up. Influenza demands annual reformulation because the virus continually
accumulates changes in its surface proteins, and last season's antibodies bind
this season's strain less well — a process called antigenic drift.

There is also a question of where the immunity sits. Injected vaccines are
excellent at filling the blood with antibody, which is what stops a pathogen
that must travel through tissue to cause disease. They are much less good at
stationing antibody on the wet surfaces of the nose and throat, which is where
a respiratory virus first lands. This is why a vaccine can be superb at
preventing severe illness and considerably less impressive at preventing
infection and onward transmission — a distinction that gets lost in argument
but is entirely predictable from the biology.

The last principle is the one that reaches beyond the individual. Every person
who is immune is not just protected but removed from the pathogen's supply of
hosts, and chains of transmission that would have passed through them simply
end. That is what shields the people who cannot be vaccinated: newborns,
patients on chemotherapy, anyone whose immune system cannot be taught. It is
also what made eradication possible for a disease that lived nowhere but in
human beings. Smallpox was declared gone from the world in 1980, and the
sequence that ended it runs, without a break, back to a dairymaid's hand.
