# How Infectious Disease Spreads

An outbreak in a town of ten thousand does not begin with ten thousand people.
It begins with one. Then two or three, then a dozen, and for the first several
weeks nothing about it looks like an emergency. The arithmetic that turns that
quiet beginning into a crisis is not complicated. What makes epidemiology
interesting is all the specific, physical, human ways that real life bends the
arithmetic.

Every pathogen has one problem to solve: getting out of an infected body and
into a susceptible one before the first body kills it or clears it. The route
it uses is not a detail. It determines who gets sick, how fast, in what
settings, during which season, and what kinds of intervention can possibly
work. A waterborne bacterium and a respiratory virus are not merely two
diseases; they are two entirely different engineering problems.

## Routes Out, Routes In

Respiratory transmission gets the most attention because it is the hardest to
avoid. Breathing, speaking, singing, coughing and sneezing all eject a
continuous spectrum of particles, from large droplets down to specks a fraction
of the width of a hair. The large ones behave ballistically: they arc out,
settle within a meter or two, and land on surfaces or on the person you are
talking to. The small ones evaporate almost instantly into dried residues
light enough to stay suspended, drifting with air currents and accumulating in
a room the way cigarette smoke does. For decades textbooks drew a hard line
between "droplet" and "airborne" diseases, and that line has largely dissolved.
Most respiratory pathogens use both mechanisms, in proportions that depend on
particle size, viral load, and the room. This is why ventilation, occupancy and
time spent indoors turn out to matter so much, and why measles — which can
infect someone who walks into a room after the patient has left — sits at the
extreme end of a continuum rather than in a category of its own.

Fecal-oral transmission is the old killer, and the one that sanitation
engineering has done most to defeat. Cholera, typhoid, rotavirus, hepatitis A
and polio all leave the body in stool and must reach another mouth, usually
through contaminated water or food or unwashed hands. John Snow worked this out
in London in 1854 without any knowledge of bacteria, by plotting cholera deaths
on a street map and noticing that they clustered around a single public pump on
Broad Street. A shared water supply is an extraordinary amplifier: it touches
thousands of people at once, so waterborne outbreaks tend to spike almost
vertically rather than climbing through generations of person-to-person
contact. The shape of the epidemic curve is itself evidence about the route.

Then there is direct contact — skin against skin, as with scabies, ringworm and
impetigo — and contact with contaminated objects, which epidemiologists call
fomites. Norovirus is unusually good at this, surviving on surfaces and
requiring only a tiny dose to infect, which is why it tears through cruise
ships and nursing homes. For many respiratory viruses, though, the fomite route
turned out to be less important than hand-hygiene campaigns once assumed.

Bloodborne and sexually transmitted infections — HIV, hepatitis B and C,
syphilis — require a far more intimate exchange, and their epidemics are shaped
by the structure of social and sexual networks rather than by air currents or
plumbing. Long asymptomatic periods let them spread quietly for years.
Vector-borne diseases add a whole second organism with its own biology: malaria
parasites must complete part of their life cycle inside an *Anopheles*
mosquito, a process that takes days to weeks and runs faster in warm weather,
so the map of malaria is substantially a map of temperature. Dengue rides
*Aedes aegypti*, a daytime biter that breeds in water containers around houses,
which makes it a city disease in a way malaria is not.

Finally, some infections pass from mother to child across the placenta or
during birth, and many of the most alarming new diseases arrive from animals.
Rabies, hantavirus, Ebola and the influenza strains that trigger pandemics all
begin as spillovers. Most spillovers go nowhere: a person is infected, perhaps
dies, and the chain stops. The dangerous transition is the one where the
pathogen becomes competent at human-to-human transmission.

## What R Actually Measures

The basic reproduction number, R0, is the average number of new infections
produced by one infectious person dropped into a population with no immunity at
all. It is not a fixed property of a microbe. It is a product of three things:
how often people come into contact, how likely a contact is to transmit, and
how long a person stays infectious. Change any of them — crowd people into
dormitories, hand out masks, treat patients so they shed less — and R changes.

The threshold is what matters. Above one, each generation of cases is larger
than the last and the outbreak grows. Below one, each generation is smaller and
the chains of transmission die out. Everything that public health does, from
vaccination to quarantine to closing a contaminated well, is an attempt to push
that number under one and hold it there. The fraction of a population that must
be immune to do it with immunity alone is one minus the reciprocal of R0, which
is why a disease as transmissible as measles demands such extraordinarily high
vaccination coverage while less contagious diseases tolerate gaps.

Exponential growth is the part people consistently misjudge. A disease doubling
every four days spends three weeks looking like nothing much and the fourth
week overwhelming a hospital system. The daily increments are small until
suddenly they are not, and because infections detected today reflect
transmission that happened a week or two ago, every decision is made with
out-of-date information.

## Averages Hide the Mechanism

R is an average, and for many diseases the average describes almost nobody. The
distribution of onward transmission is often heavily skewed: the majority of
infected people infect no one at all, while a small minority generate large
clusters. Epidemiologists call this overdispersion, and it has two important
consequences that point in opposite directions.

The first is that outbreaks are fragile. If most introductions fizzle out by
chance, a pathogen may be seeded into a country repeatedly before one seeding
finally catches. The second is that when transmission does happen, it happens
in bursts, in identifiable settings: crowded indoor gatherings, poorly
ventilated workplaces, prisons, nursing homes, wards, choirs. The individuals
involved are rarely biologically remarkable. The circumstances are. This is why
tracing backwards to find the event that infected a case can be more productive
than tracing forwards to find who a case might have infected — backwards
tracing leads to the cluster, and the cluster is where the other cases are.

Some people genuinely are unusual. Chronic carriers shed a pathogen for months
or years without symptoms; Mary Mallon, a cook in early twentieth-century New
York, carried typhoid and infected household after household while remaining
entirely well herself. Asymptomatic and pre-symptomatic infection is the single
most difficult feature a pathogen can have from a control standpoint, because
it breaks the link between feeling ill and being dangerous.

## Timing Is as Important as Number

Two periods matter and they are frequently confused. The incubation period is
the time from infection to symptoms. The latent period is the time from
infection to becoming infectious. Their relationship determines whether simple
isolation can contain a disease.

When infectiousness begins clearly after symptoms appear, people become
contagious at roughly the moment they and everyone around them know something
is wrong. Identify cases, isolate them, and you remove nearly all of their
onward transmission. The SARS outbreak of 2003 had this structure, and that is
the main reason it was stamped out. When infectiousness begins before symptoms,
or in people who never develop them at all, a large share of transmission has
already occurred by the time anyone can act, and isolation alone cannot get R
below one no matter how diligently it is done.

Speed matters too. The serial interval — the gap between one person's symptom
onset and their infectee's — sets the clock of the epidemic. Two diseases with
the same R0 but serial intervals of three days and three weeks produce wildly
different curves; the fast one can burn through a city before a response is
organized, while the slow one gives contact tracers time to get ahead of it.

## Why Outbreaks Stop

Epidemics do not usually end because everyone has been infected. They end
earlier, for several overlapping reasons. As susceptible people are used up,
each infectious person finds fewer targets and effective R drifts downward.
People change their behavior, often well before any official instruction, and
the contact rate falls. Interventions bite. Seasons turn, and with them
humidity, indoor crowding, school terms and mosquito populations.

What happens next depends on whether immunity holds and whether the pathogen
can change. Measles immunity is essentially lifelong and the virus does not
meaningfully escape it, so measles waits for a fresh cohort of children and
returns in waves. Influenza mutates its surface proteins continually, so last
year's immunity is only partly useful and the disease returns annually with a
new face. And where a pathogen has an animal reservoir, it never truly leaves.
Smallpox was eradicable precisely because it infected only humans; there was
nowhere for it to hide once the last chains were broken. Influenza lives in
waterfowl and pigs, and no vaccination campaign on earth will reach them all.

It is worth holding onto the image underneath all of this. A chain of
transmission is a physical thing, built of actual encounters between actual
people in actual rooms. Break any link and every case that would have descended
from it never happens. The whole branch disappears, and nobody ever knows which
ones they were.
