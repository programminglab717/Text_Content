# How Cardiovascular Disease Develops

The heart does not feed itself from the blood passing through its chambers. It
is fed by three arteries that sit on its outer surface, branching over the
muscle like roots over a stone, and at their origins they are about the width
of a drinking straw. Everything depends on them. A muscle contracting
continuously for a human lifetime has no reserve, no capacity to rest and
recover, and no alternative supply. Narrow those vessels and the heart is in
trouble; block one and the tissue it serves begins to die within minutes.

Atherosclerosis, the disease that does the narrowing and blocking, is not a
plumbing problem in any simple sense. The popular image of fat gradually
silting up a pipe until the last gap closes is wrong in almost every
particular. What actually happens is a slow inflammatory process inside the
wall of the artery itself, running quietly for decades, and it usually kills
not by finishing the job of narrowing but by rupturing early.

## The Wall Is a Living Organ

An artery is built in three layers. The outermost is tough connective tissue.
The middle layer is smooth muscle and elastic fibre, which gives the vessel its
tone and lets it absorb the pressure pulse of each heartbeat. The innermost
layer, the intima, is lined by a single sheet of endothelial cells — a surface
so thin it is easy to dismiss and so active that physiologists treat it as an
organ in its own right.

Healthy endothelium is nonstick. It keeps platelets from adhering, releases
nitric oxide that relaxes the muscle beneath it and widens the vessel on
demand, and controls what passes from blood into the wall. It also senses flow.
Where blood runs smooth and fast, endothelial cells align with the current and
behave well. Where flow is disturbed — at branch points, at the inside of
curves, at the carotid bifurcation and the bends of the coronary arteries —
they are subjected to chaotic shear, become more permeable and more
inflammatory, and it is precisely at those spots that disease begins. The
distribution of plaques in the body is, to a first approximation, a map of
turbulence.

## How a Plaque Is Built

The process starts with lipoprotein particles, chiefly LDL, crossing the
endothelium and becoming trapped in the intima by binding to matrix molecules
there. Once trapped and unable to leave, the particles are chemically modified
— oxidized, among other things — and the modified versions are recognized by
the immune system as something foreign.

Monocytes are recruited out of the bloodstream, squeeze between endothelial
cells, and transform into macrophages, whose job is to engulf the debris. They
do so enthusiastically and without an off switch. Stuffed with lipid, they
become foam cells, and a mass of them forms the fatty streak — a yellowish
smear in the artery wall that can be seen with the naked eye. Autopsy studies
of young men killed in twentieth-century wars found these streaks already
present in teenagers and men in their twenties. The disease is not something
acquired in middle age; middle age is when it becomes visible.

Foam cells eventually die, spilling their contents, and a soft core of lipid
and cellular debris accumulates. Smooth muscle cells migrate in from the middle
layer, change character, and lay down collagen over the top, forming a fibrous
cap that separates the mess from the bloodstream. The result is a plaque: a
lipid-rich, inflamed core with a fibrous lid, sitting inside the vessel wall.

For a long time it does not narrow anything. The artery compensates by
remodelling outward, bulging its wall away from the lumen so the channel stays
open even as the plaque enlarges. This is why disease can be extensive before
any test of blood flow finds it, and why an angiogram, which photographs the
open channel rather than the wall, can look reassuring in a heavily diseased
artery.

## Decades of Quiet, Then Minutes

When a plaque does eventually encroach on the lumen, it produces symptoms in a
characteristic way. At rest, the remaining channel delivers enough blood. Under
exertion — a flight of stairs, cold air, a stressful argument — the heart
demands more, the narrowed vessel cannot supply it, and the muscle downstream
becomes ischemic. The result is angina: pressure or tightness in the chest,
often spreading to the jaw or arm, which comes on with effort and settles with
rest. It is reproducible, and it is the body reporting a supply problem
accurately.

But most heart attacks are not the final closure of a severely narrowed vessel.
They begin when a plaque that may have been causing no symptoms at all
suddenly breaks. A thin fibrous cap over a large, inflamed lipid core is
mechanically fragile; enzymes released by macrophages degrade the collagen
holding it together. When the cap tears, or when the endothelium over it simply
erodes away, blood makes contact with the highly thrombogenic material inside.

The clotting system responds exactly as it evolved to respond to a wound.
Platelets adhere within seconds, activate, recruit more platelets, and a fibrin
mesh locks the mass together. A clot can occlude a coronary artery completely
in minutes. Downstream, cardiac muscle deprived of oxygen exhausts its reserves
almost immediately, stops contracting, and begins to die. Cells at the core of
the territory are lost first, and the zone of dead tissue expands outward over
the following hours — which is the reason that reopening the vessel is a race,
and why emergency systems are built around minimizing the time from symptom
onset to restored flow.

The immediate danger is not usually the loss of pumping power. It is
electrical. Ischemic muscle conducts abnormally, and the boundary between dying
and surviving tissue is fertile ground for chaotic rhythms. Ventricular
fibrillation — the heart quivering without pumping — is what kills most people
who die suddenly in the first hour, and it is reversible by defibrillation if
someone is present with a defibrillator, which is the entire rationale for
putting them in airports and shopping centres.

Heart muscle that dies is not replaced. Cardiac myocytes have almost no
capacity to regenerate, so the infarcted region heals as scar: electrically
inert, mechanically stiff, contributing nothing. A large scar leaves the
remaining muscle to do the whole job, and over years of overwork the ventricle
enlarges and weakens. That is one of the common roads to heart failure, a
condition of insufficient output that manifests as breathlessness, fatigue and
fluid accumulation in the lungs and legs.

## The Same Disease in Other Territories

Atherosclerosis is systemic, and the coronary arteries are simply where it
attracts the most attention. The same lesions form in the carotid arteries and
the vessels of the brain, where rupture and thrombosis produce ischemic stroke
— the large majority of strokes — with the dead tissue being neurons rather
than muscle, and the consequence a lost function rather than a lost pump.
Stroke can also arise from a clot formed elsewhere and carried to the brain,
which is why atrial fibrillation matters so much: a fibrillating atrium does
not empty properly, blood stagnates, and clots form in the quiet corners.

In the legs, narrowed arteries produce the walking-distance pain of peripheral
artery disease. In the kidneys they contribute to hypertension. In the aorta,
the wall may weaken and balloon into an aneurysm.

High blood pressure deserves its own mention because it acts on all of these at
once. Sustained pressure damages the endothelium, accelerates plaque formation,
thickens and stiffens the arteries, and forces the left ventricle to hypertrophy
against the load until it becomes stiff and poorly filling. It produces no
sensation whatever until it produces a catastrophe, which is why it is measured
rather than felt.

## What Determines Whether Any of This Happens

The recognized risk factors are not an arbitrary list; each maps onto a step in
the mechanism. Tobacco smoke injures the endothelium directly and makes blood
more prone to clot. Diabetes glycates proteins throughout the vascular wall and
drives inflammation. LDL concentration determines how much lipoprotein is
available to be retained in the intima in the first place — the clearest
evidence for which comes from familial hypercholesterolemia, an inherited
defect in the receptor that clears LDL from the blood, worked out by Michael
Brown and Joseph Goldstein. People carrying it are exposed to very high LDL
from birth and develop atherosclerosis correspondingly early, and the receptor
biology they described is what statin drugs exploit.

Genetics, age and sex set a baseline that nobody chooses. But the long
epidemiological record is consistent on a broader point: populations differ
enormously in how much of this disease they develop, and the differences track
the exposures rather than the ancestry. Groups that migrate acquire the
cardiovascular risk profile of where they moved to, over a generation or two.
The plaque in the wall of a coronary artery is, among other things, a physical
record of the decades that built it.
