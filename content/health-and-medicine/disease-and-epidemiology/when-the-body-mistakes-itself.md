# When the Body Mistakes Itself

Behind the breastbone sits a gland that is largest in childhood and mostly
replaced by fat by middle age. Inside it, immature T cells are shown fragments
of the body's own proteins and killed for responding to them. The thymus is,
among other things, an execution chamber, and the overwhelming majority of the
cells that enter it never leave. This seems like an extraordinary amount of
waste until you consider what the immune system is being asked to do.

It must be able to recognize anything. Not anything on a list — anything,
including pathogens that will not exist for another century. Evolution's
solution was to stop specifying receptors in the genome and instead generate
them by shuffling gene segments at random in each developing lymphocyte, adding
and deleting nucleotides at the joins. The result is a repertoire of perhaps
billions of distinct binding shapes, assembled without reference to what is out
there. It is a beautiful trick, and it comes with an obvious defect. A process
that generates receptors for everything will generate receptors for you.

Paul Ehrlich saw the danger early and gave it a name — horror autotoxicus, the
body's dread of poisoning itself — and assumed some principle must forbid it
outright. He was wrong about the prohibition but right that something had to
give. What actually exists is not a prohibition but a set of filters, layered
and imperfect, and autoimmune disease is what happens when they fail.

## Two Rounds of Quality Control

The first filter is the thymus. Developing T cells there are presented with
self-proteins by specialized cells, and those that bind too strongly are
instructed to die. The trouble is obvious: the thymus is a gland in the chest,
and it has no natural business containing insulin, or myelin, or the enzymes of
the thyroid. How can it screen for reactivity against proteins that exist only
in the pancreas, the brain, or the neck?

The answer turned out to be a single remarkable gene. Certain thymic cells
express a transcription factor, AIRE, that forces the promiscuous production of
thousands of tissue-restricted proteins that have no function there at all.
They are manufactured purely to be displayed, as a kind of rehearsal of the
body. Children born with AIRE mutations develop an autoimmune syndrome that
attacks several endocrine glands at once — an unusually clean demonstration
that this mechanism is doing real work every day in everyone else.

Central tolerance is thorough but incomplete. Some self-reactive cells always
escape, so a second system operates in the tissues. Lymphocytes that encounter
their target without the inflammatory signals that accompany genuine infection
are switched off rather than activated, entering a state of anergy. And a
dedicated subpopulation, regulatory T cells, actively suppresses the others.
Their necessity is again shown by what happens without them: mutations in the
gene that specifies this lineage produce a catastrophic infant syndrome in
which multiple organs are attacked simultaneously. Tolerance is not an absence
of attack. It is an active, ongoing restraint.

## How the Fence Gets Breached

Nobody can give a complete account of why a particular person develops a
particular autoimmune disease at a particular moment, but several contributing
mechanisms are well characterized.

Molecular mimicry is the most intuitive. A microbe carries a protein that
resembles something of yours closely enough that the antibodies and T cells
raised against the infection cross-react. Rheumatic fever is the classic case:
an ordinary streptococcal throat infection provokes a response that goes on to
attack heart valves and joints, sometimes weeks after the sore throat has
resolved. A similar mechanism underlies the paralysis that occasionally follows
certain gut infections, where antibodies against bacterial surface sugars bind
to nearly identical structures on peripheral nerves.

Tissue injury can also expose proteins the immune system never learned to
tolerate because they were never accessible — inside the eye, for instance, or
inside a cell. And inflammation itself lowers the threshold, because the
signals that tell a lymphocyte "this is real, proceed" are supplied generously
during any serious infection, and bystander cells that happen to be
self-reactive can be swept up.

None of this happens uniformly. Genetic background matters enormously, and the
strongest associations are with the HLA genes, which encode the molecules that
display protein fragments to T cells. Certain versions of these genes make
particular diseases far more likely — a well-known variant is carried by the
large majority of people with ankylosing spondylitis, and coeliac disease is
effectively confined to carriers of two specific types. Since these molecules
determine which fragments get shown, the link makes mechanistic sense rather
than being a statistical curiosity.

Then there is the sex ratio, which is one of the more striking unexplained
patterns in medicine. Most autoimmune diseases are substantially more common in
women, and some overwhelmingly so. Sex hormones, the presence of two X
chromosomes and the incomplete silencing of one of them, and the lingering
traffic of fetal cells across the placenta have all been proposed. None has
settled the question.

Finally, incidence has been rising in industrialized countries over several
decades — faster than genetics can change, which points at environment. The
broad suspicion is that immune systems calibrated over evolutionary time by
constant exposure to microbes and parasites behave differently when raised in
cleaner surroundings, though the details remain contested and the label
sometimes attached to this idea oversimplifies it considerably.

## From One Organ to the Whole Body

Autoimmune conditions arrange themselves along a spectrum. At one end are
diseases that pick a single target with great precision. In type 1 diabetes it
is the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas, and nothing else in the
organ is touched. In multiple sclerosis it is the myelin sheath in the central
nervous system, which is why the symptoms are electrical — vision loss,
numbness, weakness — rather than inflammatory in the ordinary sense.

The thyroid supplies the most elegant illustration of how specific the attack
can be. In Hashimoto's thyroiditis, immune destruction of the gland produces
underactivity. In Graves' disease, antibodies bind to the receptor that
normally responds to the pituitary's signal and jam it in the on position,
producing overactivity. The same organ, two autoimmune diseases, opposite
clinical pictures, differing essentially in which molecule the antibodies chose
and whether they block or stimulate it.

Coeliac disease occupies a unique position because its trigger is known and
removable. Gluten proteins from wheat, rye, and barley are modified by an
enzyme in the gut wall in a way that makes them fit snugly into the relevant
HLA molecules; the immune response that follows flattens the absorptive surface
of the small intestine. Remove the protein from the diet and the lining
regrows. No other autoimmune disease has so clean a switch.

At the other end of the spectrum are the systemic diseases. Lupus involves
antibodies against components of the cell nucleus — DNA itself, and the
proteins packaged with it — which means the targets are everywhere, and so is
the damage: skin, joints, kidneys, blood cells, the lining of the heart and
lungs. Rheumatoid arthritis attacks the synovial lining of joints but is
accompanied by antibodies against proteins that have undergone a particular
chemical modification, and it carries systemic consequences including
accelerated cardiovascular disease. These illnesses relapse and remit, resist
neat diagnosis, and frequently cluster in the same families and sometimes the
same patient.

## Treating Without Dismantling

For most of the twentieth century the only option was suppression by brute
force. Corticosteroids work impressively and quickly, and their long-term costs
— bone loss, diabetes, infection, skin thinning — are equally impressive. Drugs
borrowed from oncology and transplantation, given at lower doses, remain
mainstays for many conditions.

The shift since the 1990s has been toward precision. Once the specific
signalling molecules driving inflammation were identified, antibodies could be
engineered to intercept them. Blocking a single cytokine transformed the
outlook in rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease, changing them
from progressively crippling illnesses into ones that are often controlled.
Other agents deplete B cells wholesale, or block the molecular gate through
which lymphocytes cross into the brain, or interrupt the signalling pathways
inside the cell. Each of these narrows the target, and each still carries a
cost, because the pathway being blocked was there for a reason — reactivation
of latent tuberculosis, for example, follows predictably from suppressing the
cytokine that maintains the walls around it.

The ambition beyond this is to restore tolerance to one specific antigen rather
than turning down immunity generally: to teach the system to ignore myelin, or
beta-cell proteins, while leaving the response to pneumococcus untouched. It
has proven far harder than it sounds.

There is an unexpected confirmation that all of these brakes are real and
continuously engaged. Cancer immunotherapies work by releasing the very
checkpoints that restrain self-reactive T cells, on the logic that tumours
exploit them to hide. The drugs succeed, sometimes spectacularly. And a
substantial share of the patients who take them develop inflammation of the
thyroid, the colon, the skin, the pituitary, or the joints — autoimmune disease
manufactured on purpose, as the price of taking the safety off.
