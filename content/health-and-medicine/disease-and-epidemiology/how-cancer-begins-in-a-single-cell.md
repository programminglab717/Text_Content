# How Cancer Begins in a Single Cell

The lining of the human colon renews itself about once a week. Down in the base
of each of its millions of tiny pits sits a small population of stem cells,
dividing steadily, pushing their descendants upward along the wall of the pit,
where they mature, do their job for a few days, and are shed into the gut. It
is an assembly line that never stops, and it runs for eighty years. Every one
of those divisions requires copying three billion base pairs of DNA, and no
copying process is perfect.

Cancer is what happens when one cell in a lineage like that stops taking
instructions. Not because it has been invaded by anything, and not because it
has become something other than a human cell — a tumour is built of the
patient's own tissue, which is precisely why it is so hard to attack. It is a
cell whose control software has been corrupted in a specific and unlucky
combination of places.

## A Disease of Corrupted Information

The genes that matter fall into two broad categories, and they fail in opposite
ways.

Proto-oncogenes are the accelerators: genes for growth factor receptors, for
the signalling proteins that relay a growth instruction inward, for the
machinery that pushes a cell through division. They are entirely normal and
necessary. A mutation that jams one permanently in the "on" position converts
it into an oncogene. Because a single altered copy is enough to produce a
protein that signals constantly whether or not any growth factor is present,
these are gain-of-function changes, and one damaged copy does the job.

Tumour suppressors are the brakes: genes that halt the cell cycle when
something is wrong, that trigger repair, that order a damaged cell to kill
itself. Disabling a brake takes more work, because cells carry two copies of
each gene, and one working copy is generally enough. Alfred Knudson deduced
this from the pattern of childhood retinoblastoma — children with the inherited
form developed tumours early and in both eyes, while the sporadic form appeared
later and usually in one — and concluded that two hits were required. Children
who inherited a damaged copy were born one hit down.

Two tumour suppressors turn up again and again. RB governs the decision point
at which a cell commits to dividing. TP53, often called the guardian of the
genome, is activated by DNA damage and by other stress signals and responds by
arresting the cycle for repair or, if the damage is too great, initiating
programmed cell death. It is the most commonly mutated gene in human cancer,
and losing it removes both a brake and an emergency stop.

## Why It Takes So Long

No single mutation makes a cancer. The number needed varies by tissue, but it
is typically a handful of specific driver changes, accumulated in the same cell
lineage, against a background of many thousands of harmless passenger
mutations. That requirement explains the shape of cancer incidence across a
lifetime: rare in youth, rising steeply with age, because time is what it takes
to assemble the set.

Colorectal cancer has been mapped in unusual detail, and it shows the process
as a sequence rather than an event. Loss of a suppressor called APC produces a
small benign polyp. Activation of a growth-signalling gene in one of the
polyp's cells lets that clone outgrow the rest. Further losses, commonly
including TP53, carry the lesion from benign adenoma to invasive carcinoma over
what is usually a span of years to decades. The intermediate stages are visible
and removable, which is the entire logic of screening.

What drives the sequence forward is competition among the cells of the growing
mass. Each division introduces new variation; a variant that grows a little
faster, ignores a signal to stop, or survives where its neighbours starve
leaves more descendants. A tumour is therefore not a uniform lump of identical
cells but a mosaic of related subclones, some more dangerous than others,
diverging as the mass grows. Two biopsies from opposite sides of the same
tumour can differ genetically in important ways.

Mutations arrive from several directions. Some are simple copying errors, the
unavoidable background noise of cell division. Some come from outside: the
ultraviolet component of sunlight produces characteristic damage in skin cells,
tobacco smoke carries chemicals that bind and distort DNA in the airway lining,
and certain viruses contribute directly — human papillomavirus makes proteins
that specifically disable p53 and RB, which is why infection with particular
strains underlies most cervical cancer. Chronic inflammation raises the local
mutation rate and keeps cells dividing to replace damaged tissue, which is a
large part of why long-running conditions like hepatitis or inflammatory bowel
disease carry cancer risk.

## What a Cell Has to Acquire

Researchers have found it useful to describe malignancy as a set of capabilities
a lineage must acquire, whatever the specific genes involved.

It must generate its own growth signals rather than waiting for them, and
ignore the signals telling it to stop. It must evade apoptosis, the built-in
self-destruct programme that would otherwise be triggered by exactly the sort
of chaotic internal state a would-be cancer cell is in. It must escape the
limit on how many times an ordinary cell can divide: chromosomes end in
repetitive caps called telomeres that shorten with each division until the cell
senesces, and most cancers get around this by switching the telomerase enzyme
back on, restoring the caps indefinitely.

It must also solve a logistical problem. No tissue can grow beyond a millimetre
or two without a blood supply, because oxygen does not diffuse further than
that. Tumours induce the surrounding tissue to build new vessels by releasing
signalling factors, and the vessels they get are hurried and leaky, which
leaves regions of the tumour poorly perfused and acidic. Cancer cells commonly
shift their metabolism toward fermenting glucose even when oxygen is available
— an inefficient way to make energy that nonetheless supplies the raw materials
a rapidly dividing cell needs, and the reason that PET scanning can find
tumours by looking for unusually hungry tissue.

Finally, it must survive the immune system. Lymphocytes do patrol for cells
displaying abnormal proteins, and they eliminate many abnormal cells before
anyone would call them a tumour. Cancers that get established have found ways
around this: hiding the surface molecules that display their internal
fragments, recruiting cells that suppress immune attack, and — the discovery
that reshaped modern oncology — exploiting the switches that normally prevent
immune cells from attacking healthy tissue. Tumours that display the right
ligands can effectively tell an approaching T cell to stand down. Drugs that
block those switches release the brake, and in some patients the immune system
then does the work itself.

## Metastasis, Which Is What Kills

A tumour confined to its tissue of origin is usually a surgical problem. The
overwhelming majority of cancer deaths are caused by spread.

Metastasis is a sequence, and every step is difficult. Cells must break their
attachments to their neighbours and to the underlying basement membrane,
degrade the surrounding matrix, and invade. They must enter a blood or
lymphatic vessel, survive circulating without the anchorage that most cells
require to stay alive, arrest somewhere downstream, exit the vessel into an
unfamiliar tissue, and then grow there. Cells shed into the bloodstream from a
tumour in very large numbers; the fraction that establish a colony is minute.

They are also not distributed at random. Patterns of spread reflect both
anatomy — the first capillary bed a cell encounters catches many of them — and
compatibility between the wandering cell and the tissue it lands in, an idea
the surgeon Stephen Paget put forward in the nineteenth century as the seed and
the soil. Some disseminated cells sit dormant for years before something
permits them to grow, which is why certain cancers recur long after a patient
has been declared clear.

## Why Treatment Keeps Running Into the Same Wall

Classical chemotherapy exploits the one thing all cancers do: divide. Drugs
that damage DNA or disrupt the machinery of mitosis hurt fast-dividing cells
most, which is effective against tumours and also against bone marrow, gut
lining and hair follicles, producing the familiar side effects. The window
between useful dose and intolerable dose is narrow because the difference
between target and bystander is one of degree.

Targeted therapies aim instead at a specific molecular defect. Chronic myeloid
leukaemia is driven by a fusion gene created when two chromosomes swap
segments, producing a permanently active signalling enzyme; a drug designed to
occupy that enzyme's binding site turned a fatal disease into a manageable one.
The catch is the one every such therapy meets. A tumour containing billions of
genetically varied cells will usually contain a few that are unaffected — a
mutation in the binding site, an alternative signalling route — and treatment
that removes their competitors gives them the field. Relapse is often not
failure of the drug but the predictable success of the cells it did not reach.

Which is why the most decisive interventions remain the ones that act before
any of this gets underway: removing the polyp while it is still a polyp,
vaccinating against the virus, clearing the smoke out of the airway lining
while the lineage is still only a few hits down.
