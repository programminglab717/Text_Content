# Antibiotics and the Rise of Resistance

Bacteria have been poisoning each other for a very long time. The compounds we
call antibiotics are, for the most part, not human inventions but molecules
that soil microbes — fungi, and especially the filamentous bacteria called
actinomycetes — evolved to suppress their neighbours in the fierce chemical
politics of a gram of dirt. Which means the countermeasures are ancient too.
Bacteria recovered from caves sealed off from the surface for millions of
years, and from deep permafrost, carry genes that confer resistance to drugs
no human had yet synthesized. We did not start this arms race. We walked into
one already in progress, enjoyed an unusually good run of about eighty years,
and are now discovering the terms.

That framing matters, because it corrects a persistent misunderstanding.
Resistance is not a punishment for carelessness, and it is not something that
would never have happened if everyone had behaved. It is the automatic
consequence of applying strong selection to organisms that divide every twenty
minutes, carry mutation rates high enough to explore alternatives, and trade
genes across species lines. Careless use accelerates it enormously. Perfect use
would only slow it down.

## What the Drugs Actually Do

The reason antibiotics can be taken at all — the reason they are not simply
poisons — is selective toxicity. Each one attacks a structure or process that
bacteria have and human cells either lack or build differently.

The beta-lactams, which include penicillin and its many descendants, block the
enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan, the mesh that forms a bacterial cell
wall. A growing bacterium constantly breaks and remakes that mesh; interfere
with the stitching and internal pressure bursts the cell. Human cells have no
peptidoglycan at all, which is why penicillin is so remarkably well tolerated.
Vancomycin attacks the same wall from a different angle, binding the building
blocks themselves so they cannot be incorporated.

A second large family targets the ribosome, the machine that translates genetic
code into protein. Bacterial ribosomes differ enough from ours in size and
structure that aminoglycosides, tetracyclines and macrolides can jam theirs
while largely sparing ours. A third group attacks DNA handling:
fluoroquinolones trap the enzymes that unwind and untangle chromosomes, leaving
broken DNA behind. A fourth interferes with metabolism the bacterium must
perform for itself — sulfonamides block folate synthesis, a pathway we skip
entirely because we obtain folate from food.

Alexander Fleming noticed in 1928 that a mould contaminating one of his culture
plates had cleared a zone in a lawn of staphylococci, but penicillin sat mostly
unused for more than a decade until Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and their Oxford
group worked out how to purify and produce it in quantity during the Second
World War. Within a generation, infections that had routinely killed healthy
young people — a scratched knee gone septic, bacterial pneumonia, puerperal
fever, endocarditis — became conditions one usually recovered from. Surgery,
transplantation, chemotherapy and intensive care all rest on that foundation.
Every one of them assumes the infection that follows can be treated.

## Four Ways Out

Bacteria escape antibiotics by a small number of general strategies, all of
which have been independently discovered many times over.

The most direct is to destroy the drug. Beta-lactamases are enzymes that snip
open the chemical ring at the heart of penicillin and its relatives, and they
began appearing in staphylococci almost as soon as penicillin came into wide
use. Chemists responded with modified beta-lactams the early enzymes could not
attack, and with inhibitors such as clavulanic acid, which is given alongside
amoxicillin specifically to sacrifice itself to the enzyme. Bacteria responded
with extended-spectrum beta-lactamases, and then with carbapenemases capable of
dismantling the drugs held in reserve for everything else.

The second strategy is to change the target so the drug no longer fits.
Methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* carries an acquired gene for an
alternative wall-building enzyme with low affinity for beta-lactams; the cell
keeps constructing its wall using a tool the drug cannot grip. Vancomycin
resistance in enterococci works by substituting a single chemical group in the
wall precursor, which weakens vancomycin's grip by orders of magnitude while
leaving the precursor usable. Resistance to macrolides frequently involves an
enzyme that adds a methyl group to the ribosome at precisely the site the drug
binds.

The third is to pump the drug out. Efflux pumps are membrane proteins that
export toxic compounds, and because many of them are broad in their tastes, a
single upregulated pump can reduce susceptibility to several unrelated drug
classes at once. The fourth is to keep the drug from getting in — a particular
advantage of Gram-negative bacteria, whose extra outer membrane admits small
molecules mainly through protein channels called porins. Lose or narrow the
porins and the intracellular concentration never reaches a lethal level. This
structural difference is why Gram-negative infections are consistently the
harder ones to treat and why the drug pipeline against them is thinnest.

## Why It Spreads So Fast

If resistance could only be inherited from parent cell to daughter cell, its
spread would still be a problem but a slower and more predictable one. It is
not so confined. Bacteria exchange DNA horizontally, between unrelated cells
and across species boundaries, and this is the single fact that makes the
clinical situation what it is.

The main vehicle is the plasmid: a small circular piece of DNA, replicated
independently of the chromosome, that can be copied directly into a neighbour
through a physical bridge in a process called conjugation. Resistance genes
also ride on transposons, which jump between DNA molecules, and are assembled
into integrons, genetic cassette-collectors that accumulate resistance
determinants in series. The upshot is that a plasmid may carry resistance to
half a dozen drug classes at once, so exposure to any one of them selects for
all of them together. A gut bacterium acquiring such a plasmid from a passing
strain becomes, overnight, a reservoir. When plasmid-borne resistance to
colistin — an old and toxic drug pulled back into service precisely because
nothing else worked — was identified in livestock and then in patients, the
significance was not the single gene. It was that the gene had learned to
travel.

## The Pressure We Apply

Selection acts wherever drugs and bacteria meet, and they meet in more places
than a hospital. Antibiotics prescribed for viral illnesses do nothing for the
patient but everything for the bacteria in that patient's gut, nose and skin,
which are exposed, thinned out, and repopulated by whatever survived. Broad
spectrum drugs given when a narrow one would do sweep a far wider field.
Sub-therapeutic dosing — because of poor quality product, interrupted supply,
or drugs sold loose without diagnosis — creates exactly the concentration
gradient in which partially resistant mutants outcompete everything else.

A large share of global antibiotic consumption is not human at all. Given to
food animals for disease prevention and, historically, for growth promotion,
antibiotics select resistant organisms in barns, which reach people through
food, workers, water and manure spread on fields. Manufacturing effluent does
the same to river sediments downstream of drug plants.

Even the standard advice has turned out to be less settled than it sounded.
The instruction to always finish the course was built on the reasonable idea of
fully clearing an infection, but for a number of common conditions, trials have
shown shorter regimens to be just as effective, and unnecessary extra days of
exposure are themselves a selective pressure on everything else living in the
body. The right course length is a clinical question with different answers for
different infections, which is a less satisfying slogan but a more accurate
picture.

## Holding the Line

There is no solution in the sense of a permanent fix, because there cannot be
one against an evolving opponent. There is only management, and it works better
than fatalism suggests.

Stewardship — matching the drug to the organism, narrowing the spectrum once
cultures return, stopping when the infection is treated — measurably slows
resistance and sometimes partially reverses it, because resistance genes often
carry a metabolic cost that makes their bearers less competitive when the drug
pressure lifts. Faster diagnostics matter enormously, since much unnecessary
prescribing happens in the hours before anyone knows what they are treating.
Infection control in hospitals prevents resistant strains from moving between
patients at all. Vaccines reduce antibiotic use twice over, by preventing
bacterial infections outright and by preventing the viral illnesses that
trigger so many pointless prescriptions. Clean water and sanitation remain
among the most effective antibiotic-sparing interventions ever devised.

What has not worked is the market. A genuinely novel antibiotic, if it is any
good, will be locked away for the worst cases and used as little as possible —
which is the correct clinical policy and a terrible business case. Several
companies that brought new antibiotics through approval have gone bankrupt
afterward. The compounds keep being found, in soil, in unculturable bacteria
coaxed into growing, in the phages that prey on bacteria themselves. The
difficulty is no longer only chemistry. It is that we have built a system in
which the drugs we most need are the ones nobody can afford to make.
