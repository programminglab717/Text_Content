# Clean Water and the Burden of Disease

The handle came off the Broad Street pump in early September 1854, and the
Soho cholera outbreak was already subsiding by then. John Snow's famous
intervention probably shortened the epidemic only slightly. What mattered was
the reasoning behind it: a physician had mapped the deaths street by street,
noticed that they radiated outward from one water pump, and checked the
exceptions. The brewery workers nearby who drank beer were untouched. So were
the inmates of a workhouse with its own well. A widow who had moved to
Hampstead died anyway, because she liked the taste of Broad Street water and
had it carted to her.

Snow went further with a piece of work that impresses epidemiologists more than
the map does. South London was supplied by two competing water companies whose
pipes ran down the same streets, serving neighbouring houses. One drew its
water from the Thames within the tidal reach, where the city's sewage returned
twice a day. The other had moved its intake upstream, above the worst of it.
Households were distributed between them essentially by accident of which
company had signed up which landlord. Snow walked the districts, recorded which
company served each house where a death had occurred, and found death rates
from cholera many times higher among customers of the downstream supplier.
Nature had conducted the randomized trial he was not permitted to run.

He did not win the argument. Cholera was still widely attributed to foul air,
and when Joseph Bazalgette built London's great intercepting sewers after the
Great Stink of 1858 drove Parliament from its own chamber, the stated purpose
was to remove the smell. The sewers stopped cholera anyway, by carrying waste
downstream of the intakes. It is one of the better examples of a correct
intervention justified by an incorrect theory.

## The Fecal-Oral Road

Strip away the specific organisms and the mechanism is monotonous. A pathogen
multiplies in someone's intestine, leaves in their stool, and must reach
another person's mouth. Everything protective in water and sanitation consists
of interrupting one or more of those routes.

Public health teaching lays them out as a diagram of pathways that all begin at
faeces and end at food or drink: fluids, when waste reaches a water source;
fingers, when hands are not washed after defecation or after cleaning a child;
flies, which feed on both excrement and dinner; fields, when crops are
irrigated or fertilized with untreated waste; and food itself, handled by any
of the above. Barriers can be placed at the start — containing waste so it
never enters the environment — or at the end, treating water and washing hands.
Barriers at the start are more effective, because they protect the whole
community rather than one household, and they are harder and more expensive to
build.

The quantities involved differ enormously between organisms and this shapes
which route matters. Some bacteria need only a handful of organisms to
establish infection, which makes person-to-person spread by unwashed hands
entirely sufficient. Cholera generally requires a much larger dose, which is
why it tends to demand a contaminated water supply or food that has allowed the
organism to multiply. And a large fraction of infected people never become ill
at all while still shedding pathogens, which is why outbreak control cannot
consist simply of isolating the visibly sick.

## What the Water Carries

Cholera remains the emblem. The bacterium produces a toxin that locks intestinal
cells into secreting fluid, and the result is watery diarrhoea of a volume that
can kill a healthy adult from dehydration within hours. The great insight of
the twentieth century in treating it was not an antibiotic but oral rehydration
therapy — the discovery that glucose and sodium are absorbed together across
the gut wall, so a simple solution of sugar and salt in clean water will pull
fluid back in even while the toxin is still working. It is among the cheapest
life-saving interventions ever devised.

Typhoid works differently, invading through the gut wall to cause a systemic
fever, and it is notorious for long-term carriers who shed bacteria for years
after recovering. Viruses travel the same road: rotavirus was for decades the
leading cause of severe childhood diarrhoea worldwide, and hepatitis A and E
spread through contaminated water, the latter unusually dangerous in pregnancy.
Protozoa add their own complications. Cryptosporidium forms oocysts that resist
chlorine at the concentrations used in drinking water, so removing it depends
on filtration rather than disinfection — a fact learned expensively through
municipal outbreaks in wealthy countries with otherwise excellent water systems.

Not all waterborne harm is infectious, and the most instructive case is also
the most painful. In Bangladesh and eastern India, millions of tube wells were
installed from the 1970s onward specifically to move people off contaminated
surface water, and they succeeded in cutting diarrhoeal disease. Years later it
emerged that the aquifer in much of the region contains naturally occurring
arsenic, at concentrations that cause skin lesions, cancers, and cardiovascular
disease after long exposure. A public health triumph had created one of the
largest mass poisonings in history, not through negligence in the ordinary
sense but because nobody had tested for something they were not looking for.
Excess fluoride in groundwater damages bone and teeth in parts of Africa and
Asia by a similar route, and lead leaching from old service pipes remains a
live problem in cities that built their networks a century ago.

## Engineering the Barrier

Modern water treatment is organized around the principle of multiple barriers:
no single step is trusted, and each is designed to catch what the others miss.

Protection begins at the source, with catchment controls that keep waste and
runoff away from the intake — the cheapest barrier, and the one most often
sacrificed to development pressure. Water is then usually dosed with a
coagulant that causes fine suspended particles to clump into flocs heavy enough
to settle out. Filtration follows, through sand or increasingly through
membranes, and this is the step that removes the chlorine-resistant parasites.
Disinfection comes last, commonly with chlorine, sometimes with ozone or
ultraviolet light.

Filtration alone was enough to produce one of the starkest natural experiments
in public health. In the cholera epidemic that struck Hamburg in 1892, the
neighbouring city of Altona, which filtered its supply, was largely spared
while Hamburg, which did not, suffered severely — and Altona drew its water
from the river downstream of Hamburg's sewage. The boundary between the two
was visible in the death records. Continuous chlorination of municipal supplies
arrived in the first years of the twentieth century and spread quickly, and the
collapse in typhoid mortality across American and European cities over the
following decades is one of the sharpest improvements in mortality anyone has
recorded.

Chlorine has a second virtue that is easy to overlook: a residual concentration
persists in the distribution network, so contamination entering through a
cracked pipe is partly neutralized. This is why maintaining pressure in the
mains matters so much. An intermittent supply that drops to zero pressure draws
groundwater and sewage in through every defect, and a system that delivers
treated water for six hours a day can be more dangerous than the engineering
suggests.

The other half of the infrastructure carries waste away. Sewers were built
first simply to move the problem elsewhere, which worked only until the
elsewhere belonged to somebody. Treatment plants now screen out solids, settle
what will settle, and then rely on the appetites of bacteria — most commonly
in the activated sludge process, where aerated tanks of microbial culture
consume dissolved organic matter — before a final settling and disinfection
step. The sludge itself must then be digested, dried, and disposed of, a
problem that consumes much of a treatment plant's budget and receives none of
its credit.

## The Harder Half

Water attracts investment because a tap is visible and popular. Sanitation does
not, and it lags almost everywhere. Progress is usually described as a ladder,
climbing from open defecation to unimproved pits, then to improved latrines
that separate people from excreta, and finally to safely managed systems in
which waste is treated. Much of the world sits on the middle rungs, and the
middle rungs contain a trap: a dense settlement of pit latrines still has to
dispose of the pits when they fill, and where emptying services are informal
the contents frequently end up in a drain, which is to say back in the
environment a street away.

Handwashing with soap sits alongside all of this as an unusually cheap
intervention with large effects on diarrhoeal and respiratory illness, and an
unusually stubborn behavioural problem. Access to a handwashing station with
soap and water is not the same as the habit of using it at the moments that
matter.

The chronic damage may exceed the acute. Children in heavily contaminated
environments who never appear to have an episode of severe diarrhoea can still
develop persistent low-grade inflammation of the small intestine, with blunted
absorptive surfaces and increased permeability. This condition is implicated in
childhood stunting and in the reduced effectiveness of oral vaccines in exactly
the populations that need them most. It suggests that the ambient dose of
faecal exposure matters, not just the visible episodes.

That framing helps explain a genuinely surprising finding. Several large,
carefully conducted trials in recent years provided households with improved
water treatment, latrines, and handwashing supplies, and measured child growth
and infection. The effects on growth were considerably smaller than expected —
nutritional supplementation did more. The most persuasive interpretation is not
that sanitation does not work, since the historical evidence on that is
overwhelming, but that it does not work household by household. A clean latrine
in a neighbourhood where the paths, the water, and the soil are contaminated
protects a child only partially, because the child's exposure is a property of
the shared environment rather than the family compound. The barrier has to be
built at the scale of the community, which is why the interventions that
transformed European and North American mortality were municipal works, and why
they remain civil engineering rather than medicine.
