# The Sugar in the Blood: Understanding Diabetes

In 1889, two researchers in Strasbourg removed the pancreas from a dog, mainly
to see what the organ did for digestion. The story told afterwards is that a
laboratory assistant noticed flies clustering around the animal's urine. The
urine was sweet. Oskar Minkowski and Joseph von Mering had not set out to study
diabetes, but they had just produced it experimentally, and in doing so they
pinned a disease that had been described for two thousand years onto a single
organ.

Sweetness was the oldest diagnostic sign there was. Physicians in antiquity
described a wasting illness in which flesh melted into urine, and the name
itself — diabetes, a siphon, something passing straight through — records the
unquenchable thirst and the endless urination. The second half of the name came
much later, when European physicians confirmed by direct tasting that the urine
of these patients was honeyed. Mellitus. It was an accurate observation with no
explanation behind it, and it stayed that way for centuries.

## A Fuel That Has to Be Rationed

Glucose is the body's universal currency of energy, and the brain in particular
is an inflexible customer: it burns glucose almost exclusively under normal
conditions and cannot store more than a few minutes' worth. So the body keeps
the concentration in the blood inside a surprisingly narrow band, roughly
between four and seven millimoles per litre, through hours of fasting, through
a heavy meal, through a sprint. That stability is not passive. It is the
product of a continuous negotiation between two hormones produced a few
micrometres apart.

Scattered through the pancreas, embedded in the tissue that makes digestive
enzymes, are perhaps a million clusters of endocrine cells called the islets of
Langerhans. Beta cells within them sense rising glucose and release insulin.
Alpha cells sense falling glucose and release glucagon. Insulin is the signal
of plenty: it tells muscle and fat cells to pull glucose out of the blood by
moving glucose transporters to their surfaces, tells the liver to lock glucose
away as glycogen, and tells fat tissue to stop breaking down its stores.
Glucagon does the reverse, instructing the liver to dismantle glycogen and
manufacture fresh glucose from amino acids and glycerol. Between meals, the
liver is quietly feeding the brain on glucagon's orders. After a meal, insulin
shuts that production down and opens the doors of the storage tissues.

Remove insulin from this arrangement and two things happen at once, which is
why untreated diabetes is so devastating. Glucose accumulates in the blood
because nothing is taking it up and the liver keeps making more. Meanwhile the
tissues, unable to access that glucose, behave as though they are starving.
Fat is broken down for fuel, and the liver converts the resulting fatty acids
into ketone bodies, which are acidic. Blood sugar climbs high enough to drag
water into the urine osmotically, causing the thirst and the volume. The
patient drinks, urinates, loses weight rapidly, and in the final stage becomes
acidotic and comatose. Before 1922, that was the whole course of the illness in
a child, and it took months.

## Two Diseases Wearing One Name

What unites the conditions we call diabetes is a number: chronically elevated
blood glucose. What separates them is the reason.

In type 1 diabetes, the immune system destroys the beta cells. It is an
autoimmune disease, and by the time symptoms appear the great majority of
insulin-producing capacity is already gone. It most often begins in childhood
or adolescence, though it can start at any age, and it accounts for a small
minority of all diabetes — usually under a tenth of cases. Genetic
susceptibility matters, particularly certain versions of the immune system's
tissue-typing genes, but identical twins are far from perfectly concordant,
which means something environmental is involved. Viral infections have long
been suspected. No single trigger has been established.

Type 2 diabetes is the far more common form and the mechanism is different.
Here the beta cells still work, at least initially, but the tissues respond
poorly to insulin. Muscle and liver become resistant, so the pancreas
compensates by secreting more. For years, sometimes decades, that compensation
succeeds and blood glucose stays normal at the cost of chronically high insulin
levels. Diabetes appears at the moment the beta cells can no longer keep pace —
they are, in effect, worn down by the demand. This is why type 2 arrives
gradually, why it is often discovered incidentally on a routine blood test, and
why a person can have it for years before diagnosis. Excess body fat,
especially around the abdomen and inside the liver, is the strongest modifiable
driver of insulin resistance, along with physical inactivity and age. Heredity
is if anything stronger here than in type 1, which surprises people who assume
the opposite.

The two-category scheme is a simplification that clinicians work around
constantly. Gestational diabetes appears in pregnancy, when placental hormones
deliberately induce insulin resistance to shunt glucose to the fetus, and
resolves after delivery — though it marks a woman as being at elevated risk
later. There are single-gene forms, collectively called MODY, that run through
families in a clean dominant pattern and can be mistaken for either type. And
there is a slowly progressive autoimmune diabetes in adults that looks like
type 2 at first and behaves like type 1 within a few years. Diseases of the
pancreas itself, certain drugs, and some hormonal disorders can produce
diabetes as a side effect.

## How the Damage Accumulates

Glucose is a reactive molecule. At high concentrations it attaches
non-enzymatically to proteins — a process called glycation — subtly changing
their shape and function, and over time those modified proteins cross-link into
stable compounds that stiffen tissues. Combined with the metabolic stress that
high glucose places on cells that cannot regulate their own uptake, the result
is slow injury concentrated in blood vessels.

Small vessels suffer first and most characteristically. In the retina,
capillaries leak, close off, and provoke the growth of fragile new vessels that
bleed; diabetic retinopathy remains a leading cause of blindness in working-age
adults in wealthy countries. In the kidney, the filtration units thicken and
scar, protein begins to appear in the urine, and function declines toward
dialysis. In peripheral nerves, the longest fibres die back first, producing
numbness that begins in the toes and moves upward. That numbness is what makes
diabetic foot ulcers so dangerous: the injury is not felt, the circulation is
poor, and infection has a head start.

Large vessels are affected too. Diabetes markedly accelerates atherosclerosis,
and heart attack and stroke account for more deaths among people with diabetes
than any of the more distinctive complications.

The single most useful measurement in all of this is glycated haemoglobin,
HbA1c. Because red blood cells circulate for about three months and haemoglobin
glycates in proportion to ambient glucose, a single blood sample carries a
weighted average of the preceding weeks. It cannot be gamed by fasting before
the appointment. Large trials in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes have shown
that lowering it reduces microvascular complications substantially, which is
the empirical basis for treating a disease whose early stages produce no
symptoms at all.

## From Death Sentence to Chronic Condition

The transformation came in Toronto over about eighteen months. Frederick
Banting, a surgeon with an idea and little research experience, worked with the
student Charles Best in a borrowed laboratory to extract something from
pancreatic tissue that would lower blood sugar in depancreatized dogs. J. J. R.
Macleod provided the laboratory and direction; the biochemist James Collip
solved the crucial problem of purifying the extract enough to inject into
people. In January 1922 a fourteen-year-old boy named Leonard Thompson, dying
in Toronto General Hospital, received the first successful injections. Children
on the wards who had been comatose woke up. Few medical interventions have ever
produced so visible a reversal.

Insulin came from cattle and pig pancreases for the next sixty years. In the
early 1980s, human insulin made by genetically engineered bacteria became the
first recombinant DNA drug to reach the market, and analogues followed —
molecules deliberately altered so that they either absorb within minutes for
mealtimes or trickle in flatly over a day. Continuous glucose monitors now
report a reading every few minutes from a filament under the skin, and pumps
that read those values and adjust insulin delivery automatically have brought a
partial artificial pancreas into ordinary use.

Type 2 treatment moved differently. Metformin, descended from a medieval herbal
remedy and acting mainly by restraining the liver's glucose production, remains
the usual first drug. More recently two classes developed for glucose control
turned out to do something else as well: drugs that mimic the gut hormone GLP-1
suppress appetite substantially and produce weight loss, and drugs that block
glucose reabsorption in the kidney reduce heart failure and slow kidney
decline, apparently by mechanisms only loosely related to sugar. Trials of
intensive weight management have shown that a meaningful fraction of people
with recently diagnosed type 2 diabetes can push their glucose back into the
normal range and stay off medication entirely, which reframes the condition as
something other than a one-way street.

Where the diagnostic threshold falls is a decision rather than a discovery.
Blood glucose is a continuum, risk rises smoothly along it, and the cut-points
that separate normal from prediabetic from diabetic were chosen — reasonably,
and revised more than once — by committees looking at where retinal damage
starts to appear.
