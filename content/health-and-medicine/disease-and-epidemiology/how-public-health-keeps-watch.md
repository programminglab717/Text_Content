# How Public Health Keeps Watch

In 1662 a London haberdasher named John Graunt published a small book about
death lists. The Bills of Mortality had been compiled weekly by parish clerks
for decades, and their original purpose was crude: they told wealthy Londoners
when plague deaths were climbing so they could remove themselves to the
country. Causes of death were assigned by parish searchers, generally elderly
women paid a pittance to inspect corpses and render a verdict, and the
categories they used — griping in the guts, rising of the lights, teeth — were
not diagnoses in any sense a physician would accept.

Graunt did something nobody had thought to do with this material. He added it
up across years and looked for structure. He found it. Roughly the same
proportion of deaths in each category recurred annually. More boys were born
than girls, consistently. Deaths in London exceeded births, so the city grew
only by swallowing migrants from the countryside. He constructed something
close to the first life table, estimating how many of a hundred people born
would survive to each decade. From records assembled by untrained observers for
a different purpose entirely, he extracted the regularities of a population.

That is surveillance in a sentence: continuous, systematic collection of
imperfect health data, analysed so that it can be acted upon, and fed back to
the people in a position to act.

## Farr and the Registrar's Ledger

The modern apparatus began in Britain in the late 1830s, when civil
registration of births, marriages, and deaths became compulsory, and a young
physician named William Farr was hired to compile the abstracts. Farr held the
post for four decades and turned a clerical job into an instrument of
government.

His first insight was that a death record is useless for public health unless
the cause is written down in a standard vocabulary. He built classification
schemes, argued them out with physicians, and revised them repeatedly; the
international classification of causes of death agreed at the end of the
nineteenth century, and the coding system that hospitals still use today,
descend directly from that work. His second insight was that the numbers should
be published fast and regularly, so the weekly returns became a running
account of the nation's health rather than a historical record.

Farr then used them as ammunition. He compared mortality between districts,
between occupations, and between elevations above the Thames, and he pressed
those comparisons into debates about sewage, housing, and water supply. He was
wrong about some of the mechanisms — he long favoured the idea that disease
arose from foul air rather than contaminated water — but the method survived
the error. Once you can compare death rates between places and watch them move,
you can argue about what to do.

## What Gets Counted, and Why It Is Hard

Every country now requires clinicians and laboratories to report certain
diseases to public health authorities. Which diseases are notifiable varies, but
the logic is constant: report the things where a single case might mean an
outbreak, where early intervention changes the outcome, or where the pattern
over time matters for policy.

Making this work requires a case definition — an explicit statement of what
counts. Definitions typically distinguish confirmed cases, with laboratory
proof, from probable and suspected cases meeting clinical or epidemiological
criteria. The point is not precision for its own sake. It is comparability: a
count only means something if everyone counting is applying the same rule, and
when a definition changes mid-outbreak, the curve jumps for reasons that have
nothing to do with the disease.

Underneath every reported number sits a pyramid of attrition. Some people
infected never develop symptoms. Some develop symptoms and do not seek care.
Some seek care and are not tested. Some are tested and the result never reaches
the health department. Reported cases are the visible tip, and the ratio between
tip and base is neither fixed nor knowable in real time. This is why a rise in
reported cases is ambiguous — it may reflect more disease, or more testing, or
a new reporting requirement, or a journalist's story that sent worried people to
clinics. Experienced analysts watch the proportion of tests returning positive
alongside the raw counts, because that ratio moves differently depending on
which explanation is true.

Then there is the denominator. A hundred cases in a city of ten million and a
hundred cases in a town of four thousand are not the same event, and the
population figures used as denominators come from censuses that may be years
old. And there is lag. A person infected today develops symptoms in days, seeks
care after that, is tested, waits for the result, and enters the system
sometime later still. Every surveillance curve is a picture of the past, and
knowing how far in the past is part of reading it correctly.

## The Instrument Panel

Contemporary systems run several channels at once, because each has a different
combination of speed, specificity, and coverage.

Notifiable disease reporting is specific but slow. Sentinel surveillance
recruits a sample of clinics to report in detail on everything they see —
standard practice for influenza, where a network of general practices records
consultations for influenza-like illness and swabs a subset. You lose complete
coverage and gain timeliness and quality. Syndromic surveillance goes faster
still by watching proxies that require no diagnosis at all: emergency
department presenting complaints, ambulance calls, school absences, sales of
antidiarrhoeal medication. It cannot tell you what the pathogen is. It can tell
you within a day that something is happening in a particular postcode.

Laboratory networks supply the biological detail. Since the early 1950s, a
global influenza network has shipped isolates to collaborating centres that
characterize circulating strains, and the twice-yearly meetings that decide
what goes into the next season's vaccine are a direct product of that
surveillance. Cheap genome sequencing has extended this enormously. Sequencing
lets public health laboratories recognize that listeria isolates from four
states are the same strain and therefore the same food source, a link that would
have been invisible when typing methods were coarse.

Two further channels deserve mention because they sidestep the pyramid
entirely. Wastewater sampling measures pathogen fragments shed into the sewer
by everyone in a catchment, whether or not they have symptoms, seek care, or
test. It has been used for decades to hunt poliovirus in places where paralysis
cases had stopped appearing, and it now tracks respiratory viruses and drug
metabolites as well. And excess mortality — comparing total deaths in a period
against what the preceding years would predict — is the bluntest and most
robust measure available, because a death is difficult to miss and impossible
to leave untested. It does not say what killed people. It says how many more
died than should have, which during a crisis is often the only figure not
distorted by testing capacity or reporting rules.

Alongside all of this sit registries: cancer registries that record every
diagnosis in a defined population, birth defect registries, trauma registries.
They are slower, but they support the long-horizon questions surveillance
otherwise cannot answer.

## From Signal to Action

A surveillance system that only produces charts has failed. The point is the
response, and outbreak investigation follows a sequence refined over a century.

Confirm the diagnosis first, because clusters of laboratory error are more
common than clusters of disease. Establish that there really is an excess over
the expected baseline. Write a case definition and use it to find cases
systematically rather than relying on whoever happened to be noticed first.
Then describe what you have by person, place, and time — who is affected, where
they are, and when they fell ill.

The epidemic curve, a simple histogram of cases by date of onset, carries more
information than its simplicity suggests. A sharp rise and fall spanning roughly
one incubation period indicates a point source: everybody exposed at one event,
one meal, one contaminated batch. A series of successive peaks separated by an
incubation period suggests person-to-person transmission. A long plateau points
to a continuing common source that has not yet been removed.

From the description comes a hypothesis, and from the hypothesis an analytic
study — usually comparing what the ill ate or did against what the well ate or
did. Control measures follow, and often precede the statistics, because a
suspected food is withdrawn on reasonable grounds rather than waiting for a
p-value.

The final step is the one most often neglected: reporting back. The clinicians
and laboratories that feed a surveillance system are doing unpaid administrative
work, and when nothing ever returns to them, compliance decays. Systems that
publish timely, useful summaries to their own reporters get better data than
systems that do not.

Surveillance also runs into genuine tensions. It requires collecting
identifiable information about people who did not consent and often do not know
they are in a database, which is justified by necessity and constrained by law,
but the justification has to be renewed rather than assumed. Countries under
economic or reputational pressure have incentives to report less. And the
deepest problem is structural: a surveillance system doing its job produces
outbreaks that were contained early and epidemics that never happened. The
budget line is visible. The catastrophe it prevented is not.
