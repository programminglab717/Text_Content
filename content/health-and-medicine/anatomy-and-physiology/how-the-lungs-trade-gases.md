# How the Lungs Trade Gases

Breathe onto a cold window and you get a patch of fog that shrinks from the
edges inward. That fog is a small piece of evidence about what your lungs have
been doing. The air you exhaled left the room at whatever temperature the room
happened to be, and came back out at body heat, fully saturated with water. In
the second or so it spent inside you it was warmed, humidified, stripped of
about a quarter of its oxygen, and loaded with something like a hundred times
the carbon dioxide it went in with. None of that involved a pump, a membrane
channel, or any expenditure of energy on the gases themselves. It happened
because molecules drift from where they are crowded to where they are not.

Diffusion is a superb mechanism over micrometers and a hopeless one over
centimeters. A molecule of oxygen will cross the width of a cell almost
instantly and take the better part of a day to cross a room. Every structural
oddity of the lung follows from that single constraint: the gas and the blood
must be brought within a couple of micrometers of each other, across an
enormous area, inside a container the size of a ribcage.

## The Geometry of a Very Large Surface

The airway solves this by branching. The trachea divides into two bronchi, those
into smaller bronchi, and the division repeats something like twenty times over,
each generation shorter and narrower than the last but more numerous, so that
total cross-sectional area grows at every step. By the final generations, air is
no longer flowing in any useful sense. It has slowed to the point where diffusion
alone carries molecules the rest of the way.

The last branches open into alveoli — thin-walled sacs, several hundred million
of them, clustered like the chambers of a wasp nest. Spread flat, the exchange
surface of a pair of human lungs would cover something close to a tennis court.
The wall separating air from blood consists of a flattened alveolar cell, a
shared basement membrane, and the flattened cell of a capillary: thinner, in
total, than a wavelength of red light. A red cell squeezing through that
capillary is in contact with the air for less than a second, and under resting
conditions it finishes equilibrating in about a third of that time. The lung is
built with a substantial reserve, which is why people can lose a great deal of
it before noticing anything amiss.

Surfaces that thin and that wet have a problem, though. Water's surface tension
pulls any small curved film inward, and the pull grows stronger as the radius
shrinks. Left to physics alone, small alveoli would empty themselves into
larger neighbors and the lung would collapse into a few big bubbles. Certain
alveolar cells secrete a detergent-like mixture of lipid and protein that
disrupts the water surface and lowers its tension, and — critically — lowers it
further as the alveolus gets smaller and the molecules crowd together. Small
alveoli therefore hold themselves open. Premature infants who have not yet
begun producing this substance struggle for every breath, which is what made
its discovery so consequential.

## The Bellows Around It

The lungs contain no muscle capable of moving air. They are elastic bags that
recoil inward, and they stay inflated because they are stuck to the inside of a
chest wall that springs outward. Between the two lies a thin film of fluid in a
sealed space; the opposing pulls hold that space at a pressure slightly below
atmospheric, and the liquid film lets the surfaces slide without separating,
like two wet panes of glass.

The diaphragm does most of the work. It sits as a dome under the lungs, and when
it contracts it flattens, lengthening the chest cavity downward and pressing the
abdominal contents down and out — which is why the belly of a relaxed sleeper
rises rather than the chest. Muscles between the ribs swing the ribcage up and
outward at the same time. The cavity enlarges, the pressure inside drops below
that of the outside air, and air flows in. Quiet exhalation takes no effort at
all: the muscles simply stop, and stretched elastic tissue does the rest.
Forceful exhalation, in coughing or shouting or hard exercise, recruits
abdominal muscles to drive the diaphragm upward.

Because the whole arrangement depends on the seal, puncturing the chest wall
undoes it immediately. Air enters the pleural space, the two opposing pulls are
released, and the lung on that side balls up toward its own root while the
ribcage springs outward.

Not all the air that enters the nose reaches an alveolus. Roughly the first
third of a quiet breath only refills the conducting airways, where no exchange
occurs — dead space, in the unflattering technical term. This is why rapid
shallow panting moves plenty of air and refreshes very little of it, and why
slow deep breaths are more efficient per liter. Nor does the lung ever empty.
Even after the hardest exhalation, a substantial volume stays behind, and the
fresh air of each breath mixes into it rather than replacing it. Alveolar gas is
therefore remarkably stable from moment to moment, which is exactly what a
diffusion-driven system needs.

## Carrying What Was Collected

Oxygen dissolves poorly in water, and plasma is mostly water. If blood carried
oxygen in simple solution, a resting adult would need a cardiac output of
something like eighty liters a minute to stay alive. Hemoglobin raises the
carrying capacity roughly seventyfold.

Its trick is cooperation. Each hemoglobin molecule has four binding sites, and
binding at one site tugs the protein into a shape that makes the others bind
more readily. Plot saturation against oxygen pressure and you get an S-shaped
curve rather than a straight line, and the shape is the whole point. In the
lung, where oxygen pressure is high, the curve is flat on top — so a fall in
alveolar oxygen, whether from mild lung disease or from being at altitude, costs
surprisingly little saturation. In working tissue, where oxygen pressure is low,
the curve is steep, so a small further drop releases a large amount of oxygen.
The curve also shifts. Heat, acidity, and carbon dioxide all loosen hemoglobin's
grip, which means exercising muscle — hot, acidic, and generating carbon dioxide
— strips oxygen from passing blood more aggressively than resting tissue does,
without any signal being sent anywhere.

Carbon dioxide travels differently. A little dissolves, a little rides on the
hemoglobin protein itself, but most is converted inside red cells into
bicarbonate by an enzyme that accelerates the reaction enormously. That
conversion releases a hydrogen ion, which hemoglobin mops up — more readily
when it is not carrying oxygen, so blood unloading oxygen is simultaneously
better equipped to pick up carbon dioxide. In the lung every step runs backward:
bicarbonate is reconverted, carbon dioxide diffuses into the alveolus, and
hemoglobin, reloaded with oxygen, sheds the hydrogen ions it was holding.

Since carbon dioxide and acid are chemically interchangeable in this system,
breathing is also the body's fastest control on blood pH. Hold your breath and
your blood turns measurably more acidic within a minute. Breathe hard and
deliberately for the same minute and it turns alkaline, which is why
overbreathing produces tingling hands and lightheadedness — an acid-base shift,
not a shortage of oxygen.

## Matching Air to Blood, and Deciding to Breathe

Ventilating a region of lung that has no blood flow is wasted effort; perfusing
a region that has no air is worse, because that blood returns unoxygenated and
dilutes everything else. The lung addresses this with a reflex that runs
opposite to the rest of the body's vessels. Everywhere else, low oxygen makes
arterioles dilate to bring in more blood. In the lung, low oxygen in an air
space makes the local vessels constrict, diverting blood toward better
ventilated regions. It is a sensible local rule that becomes a liability when
applied to the whole lung at once — at high altitude it raises pressure
throughout the pulmonary circuit and strains the right side of the heart.

The decision to breathe is made in the brainstem, by networks that generate a
rhythm without any conscious involvement and keep generating it through sleep,
anesthesia, and distraction. What they respond to most sensitively is not
oxygen. Cells near the surface of the brainstem monitor the acidity of the fluid
around them, and since carbon dioxide crosses into that fluid freely while
bicarbonate does not, they are in effect reading carbon dioxide directly. A
small rise in it produces a large increase in breathing. Oxygen is monitored
separately, by small clusters of tissue at the carotid arteries and the aortic
arch, but those only assert themselves when oxygen falls substantially.

The consequence is that the urgent, unbearable need to breathe is a carbon
dioxide signal, not an oxygen one. The two normally rise and fall together, so
the substitution works. When they are decoupled the safeguard fails silently:
breathing hard before a long underwater dive flushes out carbon dioxide without
storing any extra oxygen, and a swimmer can therefore feel comfortable right up
until the moment the brain runs out. Breathing air in which oxygen has been
displaced by an inert gas feels like nothing at all, because the exhaled carbon
dioxide is carried away normally. The alarm is wired to the exhaust, not the
fuel.
