# The Kidney and the Logic of Filtration

Start with an arithmetic problem. A pair of adult kidneys weighs less than a
pound together, roughly the size and shape of two computer mice, and between
them they push something in the region of 180 litres of fluid out of the
bloodstream every day. The same person produces a litre or two of urine. Which
means that about ninety-nine percent of everything the kidney filters, it
immediately hauls back in — water, salt, sugar, amino acids, bicarbonate — using
a great deal of energy to undo work it just finished doing.

No engineer would design it this way. If you wanted to remove a short list of
unwanted compounds from a fluid, you would build selective extractors for those
compounds and leave everything else alone. The kidney does the opposite: it
indiscriminately dumps nearly everything small enough to fit through a sieve,
then rescues the items on an approved list and lets the rest wash away.

The apparent waste is the whole strategy. A selective extractor needs to know in
advance what it is looking for, and an animal cannot know in advance what it
will eat, inhale, or be poisoned by. A blanket filter followed by a specific
recovery system requires no such foresight. Anything the body has evolved
transporters for gets kept; anything novel, unrecognized, or simply present in
excess departs by default. It also makes regulation fast. If the recovery
machinery is running at full stretch all the time, then dialling it down even
slightly produces an immediate, large change in output — which is how a person
who drinks a litre of water can be rid of it within the hour.

## The Sieve

Each kidney contains on the order of a million filtering units, and each begins
with a knot of capillaries pushed into a cup at the end of a tubule. Blood
enters that knot through a small artery and leaves through another small artery
rather than a vein, which is an unusual arrangement and an important one. Having
a resistance vessel on both ends lets the kidney set the pressure inside the
capillary tuft independently of the pressure elsewhere in the body. Squeeze the
outflow and filtration pressure rises; squeeze the inflow and it falls.

The barrier itself has three layers. The capillary lining is perforated with
pores large enough to pass plasma freely while holding back blood cells.
Underneath sits a meshwork of matrix proteins carrying a strong negative charge,
which repels the negatively charged proteins of plasma. Draped over that are
extraordinary cells with interlocking foot-like processes, leaving slits bridged
by a fine protein zipper. Water, salts, glucose, urea, and small drug molecules
pass. Albumin — abundant, medium-sized, and negatively charged — is almost
entirely excluded. When protein does show up in urine, it usually means that
barrier has been damaged, and testing for it is one of the oldest and cheapest
ways to learn something about a kidney.

The filtration rate is defended stubbornly against changes in blood pressure.
The inflow artery constricts on its own when stretched, damping surges. And a
patch of sensor cells sits where each tubule loops back to touch its own
glomerulus, tasting the salt concentration of the fluid that unit has just
produced. If it is too salty — a sign that this unit is filtering too fast for
the tubule to keep up — the sensors signal the inflow artery to narrow. Each
filtering unit throttles itself, individually, against its own output.

## Taking It All Back

The first stretch of tubule does the bulk work. Its cells are crammed with
mitochondria and fringed with a dense brush of microvilli that multiplies their
absorbing surface, and they reclaim roughly two-thirds of the filtered water and
sodium, along with essentially all the glucose and amino acids, before the fluid
has gone more than a few millimetres.

Almost all of it runs off one gradient. Pumps on the blood-facing side of each
cell drive sodium out, keeping sodium low inside. Sodium then falls into the
cell from the tubule, and the transporters that let it in are built to carry
something else along for the ride — glucose, phosphate, an amino acid. The cell
spends energy only on the pump; the rest is paid for by the gradient the pump
creates. Water follows the salt osmotically, and other solutes get dragged along
in the flow.

These transporters can be saturated, which has a famous consequence. Below a
certain blood glucose level, every filtered sugar molecule is recovered and
urine contains none. Above it, the carriers are working flat out and the
remainder passes through, taking water with it by osmosis. That is the origin of
the enormous thirst and urine volumes of untreated diabetes, and of the disease's
second name — mellitus, honeyed — from an era when physicians diagnosed it by
taste.

All that pumping makes the tubule a heavy consumer of oxygen. The kidneys take
a startling share of cardiac output, far more than their metabolic needs alone
would justify, because they are being perfused for filtration rather than for
nourishment. Even so, the regions doing the most transport work sit in tissue
where oxygen is relatively scarce, and they are among the first structures in
the body to fail when blood pressure collapses.

## The Countercurrent Trick

Reclaiming water is easy when you only need to match the concentration of blood.
Producing urine substantially more concentrated than blood is a harder problem,
and it is solved by a hairpin loop that dips down from the outer kidney into its
inner core.

The descending arm is permeable to water but not salt. The ascending arm is the
reverse: waterproof, and actively pumping salt out into the surrounding tissue.
Fluid coming down the descending arm therefore loses water to that salty tissue
and grows more concentrated; when it rounds the bend and starts up the ascending
arm it gives up salt, making the tissue saltier still, which in turn draws more
water out of the descending arm behind it. Each small step is modest. Run in a
loop, with fluid constantly flowing, the steps compound, and the result is a
steep gradient of saltiness increasing from the kidney's outer rim to its
centre. Urea, recycled from the far end of the system, deepens it further.

The tubule then runs a final duct straight back down through that gradient on
its way to the ureter. Whether water leaves the duct on this last pass is
controlled by a single hormone released from the base of the brain when blood
grows too concentrated or blood volume falls. The hormone tells the duct cells
to insert water channels into their walls. With the channels in place, water
pours out into the salty core and a small volume of concentrated urine emerges.
Without them, the duct stays sealed and dilute urine runs straight through. The
blood vessels supplying the region are looped in the same hairpin fashion so
that they can deliver oxygen without washing the hard-won gradient away.

The depth of the gradient depends on how long the loops are, and loop length
tracks ecology with some precision. Desert rodents that never drink have
extremely long loops and produce urine several times more concentrated than a
human can manage. Beavers have short ones.

## Everything Else the Organ Does

Calling the kidney a filter undersells it, because filtration is only the
mechanism. What the organ actually manages is the composition of the internal
environment, and the list of variables it holds steady is long.

It sets blood volume and pressure over the long term. Cells in the wall of the
inflow artery release an enzyme when pressure or salt delivery drops, kicking
off a hormone cascade that constricts vessels body-wide and instructs the far
end of the tubule to hold onto sodium — and therefore water. Most drugs for high
blood pressure act somewhere on that cascade or on the tubule's salt handling.

It controls acidity. Metabolism produces a steady acid load that breathing
cannot remove, since it is not carbon dioxide. The tubule secretes hydrogen ions
into the urine, regenerates bicarbonate back into the blood, and manufactures
ammonia to buffer the acid it is excreting, so that urine can be made
substantially acidic without dissolving the plumbing.

It manages potassium, which matters more than its low profile suggests. The
concentration outside cells is small and the concentration inside is large, and
nerve and heart muscle set their excitability against that ratio. Move it a
little in either direction and the heart's rhythm changes. The late tubule
trades potassium out in exchange for sodium retained, under the same hormonal
control.

And it is an endocrine organ in its own right. Cells in the kidney sense oxygen
levels and release the hormone that tells bone marrow to make red blood cells,
which is why chronic kidney failure brings anemia with it. The kidney also
performs the final activation step on vitamin D, without which dietary calcium
is poorly absorbed and bone is poorly maintained.

This is why a dialysis machine, which does the filtering competently, is not a
replacement kidney. It can clear urea and correct salt and water. It does not
sense oxygen, does not finish vitamin D, does not adjust itself hour by hour to
a meal or a hot afternoon. The blood's composition is determined far less by
what goes into the mouth than by what these two organs decide, continuously and
without consultation, to keep.
