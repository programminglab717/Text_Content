# The Wiring of the Nervous System

A doctor taps the tendon below your kneecap with a rubber hammer and your lower
leg kicks. You did not decide to kick, you cannot easily suppress it, and the
whole event is over before you have registered the tap. What happened is almost
embarrassingly simple: the tap stretched the muscle, a sensory fibre in the
muscle fired, that fibre ran up to the spinal cord and made direct contact with
a motor cell, and the motor cell told the muscle to shorten. Two cells, one
junction, no brain. The brain learns about it the way you do — afterwards, from
a separate set of fibres reporting on what the leg just did.

That reflex is a useful corrective to the picture of the nervous system as a
brain with cables attached. A great deal of the work is done locally, by
circuits that handle their own business and escalate only what needs escalating.
The brain behaves less like a central processor polling its peripherals and more
like a management layer over a large number of semi-autonomous departments.

## The Signal Is Not Electricity Running Down a Wire

Nerves conduct, but not the way copper does. A neuron at rest maintains a
voltage across its membrane — the inside sits at roughly seventy millivolts
below the outside — by pumping sodium out and potassium in, and then letting
potassium leak back out through channels that are always open. The result is a
charged capacitor a few nanometres thick, held ready.

Sitting in that membrane are channels that open in response to voltage itself.
If something nudges the membrane voltage upward past a threshold, sodium
channels fling open, sodium rushes in down its gradient, the voltage swings
sharply positive, and that swing opens the channels immediately adjacent. A wave
travels. Behind it, the sodium channels snap shut and become briefly
unresponsive while potassium channels open to restore the resting voltage, so
the wave cannot double back on itself. It only goes forward.

Two features of this fall out of the mechanism. First, the signal is
all-or-nothing: the channels either reach threshold or they do not, and the
spike that results is the same size whether the stimulus was barely sufficient
or overwhelming. A neuron cannot report intensity by shouting louder. It reports
intensity by firing faster, and much of the nervous system's code is written in
firing rates and timing patterns rather than amplitudes. Second, because the
signal is actively regenerated at every point along the way, it does not fade
with distance. A spike arrives at the end of a metre-long axon exactly as strong
as it started.

The cost is speed. Regenerating the signal takes time, and a bare axon conducts
at something like the pace of a brisk walk — fine for a jellyfish, inadequate
for an animal that needs to catch itself when it stumbles. The solution is
insulation. Support cells wrap themselves repeatedly around the axon, laying
down dozens of layers of fatty membrane, leaving short bare gaps at regular
intervals. Between the gaps the current spreads passively and almost instantly;
the spike only has to be rebuilt at each gap, so it appears to leap from one to
the next. Insulated fibres conduct more than a hundred times faster than bare
ones. When that insulation is attacked — as it is in multiple sclerosis —
signals do not stop so much as arrive late, unevenly, and out of order, which is
why the symptoms can be strange and scattered rather than a clean loss of
function.

## The Deliberate Gap

Where one neuron meets the next there is usually no continuity at all, just a
cleft narrower than the wavelength of light. The arriving spike opens calcium
channels; calcium entering the terminal causes small packaged bubbles of
chemical to fuse with the membrane and spill their contents into the gap; the
chemical drifts across and binds receptors on the far side.

Building the junction this way seems like a needless complication, and it costs
perhaps a millisecond every time. What it buys is control. A chemical synapse
can amplify, so that a small presynaptic event triggers a large postsynaptic
one. It can invert the sign: some transmitters open channels that let chloride
in or potassium out, pushing the receiving cell away from threshold rather than
toward it, which is how inhibition — the nervous system's most underrated
operation — is implemented. It can act slowly and diffusely, through receptors
that trigger internal chemical cascades lasting seconds rather than opening a
pore for a millisecond. And it can change. Synapses that are repeatedly used
grow stronger and those that are not weaken, which is the physical substrate of
learning.

The consequence is that a neuron is not a relay. A single cell in the cortex may
receive thousands of inputs, excitatory and inhibitory, arriving at different
distances from its output zone and at different moments, and what it does is sum
them continuously in space and time and fire only if the running total crosses
threshold. Nearly every drug that alters mood, perception, pain, or alertness
works somewhere in this gap — blocking a receptor, mimicking a transmitter,
preventing reuptake so a signal lingers, or damping the machinery of release.

## How the System Is Divided

Anatomists split the nervous system into the brain and spinal cord on one hand
and everything else on the other, and the division is more than bookkeeping: the
two behave differently when damaged, and they are protected differently. The
peripheral half carries sensation inward and commands outward, and its cell
bodies for sensation sit in small swellings just outside the spinal cord rather
than within it.

The outgoing half divides again. One branch drives skeletal muscle and is under
voluntary control, with a single long neuron running from the spinal cord to the
muscle fibre. The other manages everything you do not consciously operate —
heart rate, gut motility, sweating, pupil diameter, the calibre of blood
vessels — and reaches its targets through a two-neuron relay with a junction in
a ganglion along the way.

That autonomic branch is conventionally described as two opposing systems, one
for fight-or-flight and one for rest-and-digest, and the shorthand is serviceable
if not pressed hard. The arousal side has its ganglia in chains near the spine,
so a single alarm signal fans out widely and the response is body-wide: heart
faster, airways open, pupils wide, blood shunted from gut to muscle. The calming
side has its ganglia on or inside the organs themselves, mostly reached by one
long wandering nerve from the brainstem, and it can therefore act on one organ
without involving the others. They are not simply antagonists — some functions
receive only one, and some, like sexual response, require both in sequence.

The gut runs its own network on top of this, a mesh of a few hundred million
neurons in the intestinal wall capable of coordinating digestion with the
connection to the brain severed entirely. It senses stretch and chemistry, runs
the wave of contraction that moves food along, and argues continually with the
brain in both directions.

## Layers of Command

The central nervous system is arranged roughly as a hierarchy, with older
structures underneath. The spinal cord handles reflexes and hosts pattern
generators capable of producing rhythmic walking movements largely on their own.
The brainstem sits above it, running breathing, blood pressure, swallowing, eye
movement, and the general level of arousal — the reason damage there is so
catastrophic while much larger injuries higher up may not be.

Behind the brainstem, the cerebellum receives a torrent of information about
intended movements and actual positions and compares them, correcting timing and
smoothing execution. It contains more neurons than the whole of the rest of the
brain combined, packed into a tightly folded sheet with a strikingly regular
circuit repeated across it. Damage does not cause paralysis; it causes movements
that overshoot, oscillate, and arrive in the wrong order.

Deeper in sit the thalamus, through which almost all sensory traffic is routed
and gated on its way up, and the basal ganglia, which appear to handle the
selection of actions — releasing the ones chosen and suppressing competing
alternatives. The degeneration of a small dopamine-producing nucleus feeding
that system produces the rigidity and difficulty initiating movement seen in
Parkinson's disease.

The cortex on the outside is a sheet a few millimetres thick, crumpled to fit
the skull, organized into six layers and into columns running through them. Much
of it is laid out as maps: adjacent patches of skin project to adjacent patches
of cortex, with area allotted according to sensory density rather than physical
size, which is why the hands and lips occupy so much territory. The visual
system, the auditory system, and the motor system all show the same principle of
orderly spatial correspondence.

## The Cells That Are Not Neurons

Roughly half the cells in the brain are not neurons at all. Star-shaped
astrocytes wrap around blood vessels and synapses, buffer potassium released by
firing, shuttle metabolic fuel to active neurons, recycle transmitter out of the
cleft, and help enforce the tight sealing of brain capillaries that keeps most
molecules in the bloodstream from entering the tissue. That barrier is why the
brain is chemically insulated from the rest of the body, and why designing drugs
that reach it is a discipline of its own.

Other glia build the myelin sheaths. Smaller mobile cells patrol as the brain's
immune force, clearing debris and pruning synapses during development. And the
whole organ is metabolically extravagant — a couple of percent of body weight
consuming something like a fifth of the oxygen used at rest, with almost no
capacity to store fuel, which is why a few seconds of interrupted blood flow
produces unconsciousness.

The repair capacity of the two halves diverges sharply. Cut a peripheral nerve
cleanly and the severed axons will grow back down their original sheaths at
roughly a millimetre a day, so that a hand may recover useful sensation over the
course of a year. Cut the spinal cord and nothing of the sort happens. The
environment there actively inhibits regrowth, and the reasons — scar chemistry,
inhibitory molecules in the myelin itself, the sheer difficulty of a fibre
finding its old target among billions — remain the central obstacle in one of
the most stubborn problems in medicine.
