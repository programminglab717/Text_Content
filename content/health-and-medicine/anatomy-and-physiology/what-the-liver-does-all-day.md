# What the Liver Does All Day

In a museum in northern Italy there is a bronze sheep's liver, cast by Etruscan
hands more than two thousand years ago, its surface divided into regions and
inscribed with the names of gods. It was a reference model. A diviner examining
a freshly sacrificed animal would compare the real organ against it, noting
which region looked swollen or discoloured, and read the finding as a message
about the future. Babylonian priests worked from clay models of the same kind,
centuries earlier. Across the ancient Mediterranean and Near East, if you wanted
to know the disposition of the heavens, you looked at a liver.

They were wrong about the forecasting, and they were not entirely wrong about
the organ. Ancient physicians treated the liver as the source of blood and the
seat of the body's vital processes, a view Galen systematized and Europe
inherited for well over a millennium. Strip away the metaphysics and something
defensible remains. The liver is where the chemistry of the body is actually
transacted. It is the largest internal organ, a soft reddish-brown wedge of
perhaps a kilogram and a half tucked under the right side of the ribcage, and it
is running several hundred distinct chemical operations at any given moment.

## Plumbed Into the Gut

Most organs are supplied by an artery and drained by a vein. The liver has two
inflows, and the smaller one is the artery. Roughly three-quarters of the blood
arriving comes up the portal vein, which is not fed from the heart at all: it
collects the venous drainage of the stomach, intestines, pancreas, and spleen,
and delivers it — already stripped of much of its oxygen, but loaded with
whatever was absorbed from the last meal — straight into the liver before it can
reach anywhere else.

That arrangement puts the liver between the gut and the rest of the body. Sugar,
amino acids, drugs, alcohol, and bacterial products that cross the intestinal
wall all pass through this checkpoint first. Pharmacologists call the
consequence first-pass metabolism, and it explains why some drugs are useless
when swallowed and must be injected or absorbed under the tongue: the liver
dismantles them before they get a chance to circulate.

Inside, the tissue is organized into repeating hexagonal units. Branches of the
portal vein and the hepatic artery run along the corners, their blood mixing and
seeping inward through leaky channels toward a central vein at the middle. The
working cells are stacked in plates one or two cells thick on either side of
those channels, so that every cell fronts directly onto the flow. The lining of
the channels is deliberately full of gaps, with no tight barrier between blood
and cell — the liver is one of the few places where plasma is allowed to bathe
tissue directly. Large resident scavenger cells sit in the channels straining
out bacteria and debris arriving from the gut.

Because blood travels from the corners to the centre, oxygen falls steadily
along the route, and the cells divide their labour accordingly. Those near the
inflow, well oxygenated, handle the energy-expensive jobs. Those near the
central vein specialize in different reactions and, running on the leanest
oxygen supply, are the first to die when blood flow drops or a poison arrives.
Pathologists can often read the cause of an injury from which ring of cells was
damaged.

## The Shift Change After a Meal

Blood glucose stays within a narrow band whether you have just eaten a plate of
rice or not eaten for a day, and the liver is what holds it there. After a meal,
insulin instructs liver cells to pull glucose from the portal blood and chain it
into glycogen, a bushy storage polymer packed into granules. Hours later, when
insulin falls and glucagon rises, the same cells break the chains down and
release glucose back. When that reserve runs out — it lasts less than a day —
the liver switches to building glucose from scratch out of lactate, glycerol,
and the carbon skeletons of amino acids. Keep fasting and it starts converting
fat into ketone bodies, a fuel the brain can burn when sugar is short.

Fat and protein go through the same offices. The liver assembles cholesterol and
packages fats into the lipoprotein particles that carry them through the
bloodstream, and it clears those particles when they return. It takes apart
surplus amino acids, which means stripping off their nitrogen, which produces
ammonia — a substance toxic to the nervous system at low concentrations. A
cycle of reactions unique to the liver converts that ammonia into urea, an inert
and highly soluble molecule that the kidneys dispose of without difficulty.

The liver also manufactures a large share of the proteins dissolved in plasma.
Albumin, the most abundant, provides the osmotic pull that keeps fluid inside
capillaries; most of the clotting factors are made here too. This is why serious
liver disease shows up as bruising and bleeding, and as fluid accumulating in
the ankles and abdomen. The failure is not of the blood or the circulation. It
is of a factory that has stopped shipping.

## What Detoxification Actually Means

The word has been thoroughly abused, but there is a real process underneath it,
and its logic is straightforward. The body excretes waste in water — in urine,
or in bile that leaves with the stool. Many unwanted compounds, including most
drugs and a great many plant and environmental chemicals, are fat-soluble. Left
alone they would diffuse back across membranes and be reabsorbed endlessly. The
liver's task is to make them water-soluble enough to leave.

It does this in two stages. First a large family of enzymes, embedded in the
internal membranes of liver cells, attacks the molecule and installs a reactive
chemical handle on it — usually by oxidation. Then a second set of enzymes
staples a bulky, water-loving group onto that handle: a sugar acid, a sulfate, a
small peptide. The finished product is soluble, biologically inert, and
recognizable to the transporters that pump it into bile or hand it to the
kidneys.

Two complications follow. The first is that these enzymes are adjustable. Some
substances ramp up their production, so a person taking them clears other drugs
faster than expected; others block the enzymes, so ordinary doses accumulate.
Grapefruit juice is the well-known example of the second kind, inhibiting an
enzyme that handles a long list of common medications.

The second complication is that stage one can make a compound more dangerous
than it was. A small fraction of ordinary paracetamol is converted by these
enzymes into a highly reactive intermediate, which the liver normally neutralizes
immediately by binding it to glutathione. The system works perfectly until the
glutathione supply is exhausted, at which point the reactive product attacks the
cells that made it. The poisoning is not caused by the drug. It is caused by the
liver's own attempt to process it running past the end of its safety margin.

## Bile, and the Colour of Everything

The liver's other output leaves by a different door. Bile is a greenish fluid of
salts, cholesterol, phospholipids, and pigment, secreted continuously into tiny
channels running between the cells, and collected into ducts that converge on
the gut. The gallbladder is a side pocket on that route where bile is stored and
concentrated between meals; fat arriving in the small intestine triggers a
hormone that squeezes it out.

Bile salts are detergents. Fat entering the intestine as large droplets would be
almost unreachable by water-soluble digestive enzymes, so the salts break it
into a fine emulsion, multiplying the surface available for attack. Without
them, fat and the vitamins dissolved in it pass through largely unabsorbed.

The pigment is a disposal product. When a red blood cell reaches the end of its
few months in circulation, its hemoglobin is broken up; the iron is recovered
and recycled, and the ring structure that held it becomes bilirubin. Bilirubin
is yellow, fat-soluble, and useless, and the liver conjugates it in the standard
way and dumps it into bile. Gut bacteria then transform it into the pigments
that give stool its brown colour, while a portion is reabsorbed, filtered by the
kidneys, and contributes to the yellow of urine. Everything visible about this
system is diagnostic: if bilirubin backs up into the blood, the skin and the
whites of the eyes turn yellow, and whether the pigment is conjugated or not
tells a physician whether the problem lies before the liver, inside it, or in
the ducts below.

## The Organ That Grows Back

The liver is the only large human organ that regenerates substantially. Remove a
sizeable portion and the remainder enlarges over weeks until the original mass
is restored — not by regrowing the lost lobe in its old shape, but by expanding
what is left. This is what makes living donation possible: a lobe is taken from
a healthy adult, and both the donor and the recipient end up with a functioning
liver. It is difficult not to think of Prometheus chained to the rock with an
eagle at his liver each day and the organ whole again each night, though whether
the Greeks knew anything real about hepatic regeneration or simply chose the
organ for its ancient significance is not something the myth will settle.

Regeneration has a limit, and it is architectural rather than cellular. Injury
repeated over years — from alcohol, from chronic viral infection, from fat
accumulation and the inflammation that follows it — lays down scar tissue
between the working plates. The cells still divide, but they do so inside a
distorted framework, forming nodules that lack the neat corner-to-centre plumbing
the tissue depends on. Blood backs up in the portal vein, forcing itself through
small collateral veins that were never built for the pressure. Fluid collects in
the abdomen. Ammonia bypasses the urea cycle and reaches the brain, producing
confusion and tremor in someone whose liver is still, technically, doing most of
its job. Reserve capacity is so large that the organ can be more than half
destroyed before any of this becomes obvious, which is the cruelty of liver
disease: it is quiet for years, and then it is not.
