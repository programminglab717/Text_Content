# What Happens When a Muscle Pulls

A muscle can only pull. It has no mechanism for pushing, no way to extend
itself, and the only reason your elbow straightens after it bends is that a
different muscle on the other side of the joint pulls it back. Everything a body
does mechanically — walking, breathing, the small corrections that keep a full
glass level on the way to your mouth — comes out of that one primitive action,
repeated across several hundred muscles and scaled from the milligram tugs of
the middle ear to the enormous forces a thigh generates in a bad landing. The
pull itself happens at the scale of single protein molecules. All the force you
can produce is an extremely large number of those molecules taking extremely
small steps at roughly the same time.

## Stacked in Register

Under an ordinary light microscope, skeletal muscle is striped. The stripes run
across the fibres, evenly spaced, dark band alternating with light, and they are
the visible signature of a structure repeated with obsessive regularity all the
way down.

A muscle fibre is a single cell, unusually large and containing many nuclei
because it forms by the fusion of many precursor cells during development. Some
fibres run the entire length of the muscle. Packed inside each one are
myofibrils, and each myofibril is a chain of identical contractile units called
sarcomeres, laid end to end like carriages. A sarcomere is roughly two
micrometres long. The fibre looks striped because the sarcomeres in neighbouring
myofibrils sit in register with each other, so their internal bands line up
across the whole cell.

Each sarcomere is bounded by a dense plate, the Z-disc. Anchored to the Z-discs
and projecting inward from both ends are thin filaments, built mainly from
actin. Floating in the middle, overlapping the thin filaments but not touching
the Z-discs, are thick filaments assembled from several hundred myosin
molecules, each of which sticks a pair of hinged heads out into the gap between
filaments. The dark band is the territory of the thick filaments; the light band
is thin filament only.

For decades it was assumed that contraction meant the filaments themselves
coiled up or folded. In the mid-1950s two research groups, working
independently and publishing alongside each other, showed this was wrong. When a
muscle shortened, the dark band kept exactly the same width. The light bands
narrowed, and so did the gap in the middle of the dark band where thick
filaments had no thin filaments to overlap. Nothing was shortening. The two sets
of filaments were sliding past each other, and the sarcomere was closing like a
pair of interleaved combs being pushed together.

## The Stroke

The sliding is done by the myosin heads, and each one runs a cycle with a
strict order of operations.

A head carrying a freshly bound molecule of ATP is detached from actin. It
splits that ATP into ADP and phosphate but holds both products, and the energy
released cocks the head into a strained, high-energy position — a mousetrap set
rather than sprung. In this state the head binds weakly to the thin filament
beside it. If the binding site on actin is available, that weak attachment
tightens, the phosphate leaves, and the head snaps through its power stroke,
swivelling at its hinge and dragging the thin filament a few nanometres toward
the centre of the sarcomere. The ADP then leaves, and the head is stuck fast to
actin in what biochemists call the rigor state. It stays stuck until a new ATP
arrives and binds, which is what releases it to start again.

That last detail explains rigor mortis. When a body stops making ATP, every
myosin head that completes its stroke has nothing to unbind it. The heads lock
onto actin and the muscle stiffens, staying that way until the proteins
themselves begin to break down.

One stroke moves a filament a distance you would need a good electron microscope
to see. But a single thick filament carries hundreds of heads, a myofibril
contains thousands of filaments side by side, and the heads cycle out of step
with one another, so at any instant some are pulling while others are resetting.
The force comes out smooth. And because sarcomeres are chained in series, each
one shortening a fraction of a micrometre, the small displacements add up along
the length of the fibre into centimetres of real movement.

## The Switch That Holds It Off

Myosin and actin bind each other readily. Left to themselves they would lock
together permanently, so the binding sites on the thin filament are kept
covered. A long, ropy protein called tropomyosin lies in the groove of the actin
helix, physically blocking the sites, and it is held in position by a three-part
troponin complex stationed at regular intervals along the filament.

Calcium is the key. When calcium ions bind to one component of troponin, the
complex changes shape and pulls tropomyosin deeper into the groove, uncovering
the binding sites and letting the cycle run. Remove the calcium and tropomyosin
slides back. This is why the cytoplasm of a resting muscle cell holds almost no
free calcium, and why the cell devotes an elaborate internal membrane system,
the sarcoplasmic reticulum, to hoarding it.

Getting the signal from a nerve to that reservoir takes a few milliseconds. A
motor neuron releases acetylcholine onto the fibre, which triggers an electrical
impulse that sweeps along the cell's surface membrane. A surface signal alone
would be useless — the interior of a fibre is far too thick for a message to
diffuse inward fast enough — so the membrane dives into the cell at regular
intervals in narrow tubes called T-tubules, which run past the sarcoplasmic
reticulum deep in the fibre. Voltage sensors in the tubule wall sit directly
against calcium release channels in the reticulum membrane. When the impulse
arrives, the sensors shift, the channels open, and calcium floods the
myofibrils almost everywhere at once.

Relaxation is not passive. Pumps in the reticulum membrane haul calcium back
into storage against a steep concentration gradient, burning ATP to do it, and
a substantial part of the energy a working muscle consumes goes into this
retrieval rather than into the pulling. It is also a failure point: certain
inherited faults in the release channel let calcium leak uncontrollably when
triggered by particular anaesthetics, and the muscle then contracts and burns
fuel until the body's temperature climbs dangerously.

## Turning the Force Up

A motor neuron and all the fibres it controls form a motor unit, and the unit is
all-or-nothing — when the neuron fires, every fibre it reaches contracts. Muscles
that need precision are built from very small units, a handful of fibres each,
so the nervous system can add force in fine increments. Muscles built for power
use units containing hundreds or thousands of fibres.

Force is then graded two ways. The first is recruitment, and it follows a
consistent order: the smallest, slowest, most fatigue-resistant units are
switched on first, and larger ones join only as demand rises. The second is
firing rate. A single impulse produces a twitch that fades as calcium is pumped
away; impulses arriving faster than the muscle can clear the calcium pile up on
one another until the twitches fuse into a smooth, maximal contraction. Between
those two dials, the same muscle that pours tea can throw a punch.

The fibres themselves differ. Slow fibres are dense with mitochondria and
myoglobin, which makes them reddish, and they resist fatigue because they work
aerobically. Fast fibres carry a myosin variant that splits ATP more quickly,
generate force in a hurry, and tire fast. Because recruitment starts small, slow
fibres do nearly all of the day's ordinary work, and the fast ones stay in
reserve.

## Length, Speed, and Fuel

How much force a muscle makes depends on how far it is stretched. There is an
overlap that suits the sarcomere best: enough thin filament sitting alongside
thick filament for the maximum number of heads to find sites. Pull it longer and
the overlap shrinks. Squash it shorter and the thin filaments collide in the
middle while the thick ones run into the Z-discs. Joint geometry generally keeps
muscles working near the middle of that range.

Speed matters too. A muscle shortening quickly makes less force than one
shortening slowly, because each head spends less time attached before it has to
let go. The reverse case is stranger: a muscle being forcibly lengthened while
still active — the quadriceps on a long downhill walk, absorbing each step —
produces very high force for very little metabolic cost, because the heads are
being torn off their binding sites rather than doing the work of detaching.
That is also the kind of contraction that leaves you sore two days later, from
mechanical damage to the fibres and the inflammation that follows, not from
lactate, which is cleared within an hour of stopping.

The fuel supply is arranged in tiers. The ATP sitting in a muscle at rest would
last a couple of seconds of hard effort. Phosphocreatine regenerates it for a
few seconds more. Glycolysis covers the next stretch, up to a minute or two,
producing lactate as it goes. Anything sustained beyond that has to come from
oxidative metabolism in the mitochondria, which is why endurance is ultimately a
question of delivering oxygen rather than of the contractile machinery itself.

Which means that standing still is not rest. Holding a position against gravity,
nothing visibly moving, the heads are still attaching, pulling, releasing and
resetting thousands of times a second, and the pumps are still working to keep
the calcium where it belongs.
