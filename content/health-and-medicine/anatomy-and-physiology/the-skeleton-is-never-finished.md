# The Skeleton Is Never Finished

A dry femur from a teaching collection feels like an object rather than a
tissue. It is light, hard, faintly waxy, and it has the look of something
manufactured and then left alone. That impression is almost exactly backwards.
Bone is among the busiest tissues in the body, under continuous demolition and
reconstruction by crews of specialist cells, and the skeleton you are carrying
now shares very little actual material with the one you were carrying a decade
ago. It has the same shape. It is not the same bone.

## A Composite, Not a Mineral

The starting point for understanding any of this is that bone is not stone. It
is a composite of two materials with opposite mechanical personalities, and
nearly everything interesting about it follows from the combination.

The organic phase is collagen, laid down in fibres that are themselves twisted
from triple-helical molecules. Collagen is tough and springy; it resists being
pulled apart and it absorbs energy by deforming. The mineral phase is a calcium
phosphate closely related to the mineral hydroxyapatite, deposited as tiny
plate-like crystals in and around the collagen. Mineral is stiff and resists
compression, and on its own it is brittle.

You can separate them crudely in a kitchen. Leave a chicken bone in vinegar for
a few days and the acid strips out the mineral, leaving a rubbery object you can
bend or tie into a loose knot. Put a bone in a hot fire instead and the organic
phase burns away, leaving something rigid, chalk-white, and so brittle it
crumbles under a thumb. Intact bone is neither: stiff enough to hold a shape
under load, tough enough to take a knock without shattering.

The arrangement scales up. The dense outer shell of a long bone, cortical bone,
is built from concentric cylinders called osteons, each wrapped around a central
channel carrying a blood vessel. The boundaries between osteons, called cement
lines, are relatively weak, and this is a feature rather than a flaw: a crack
running through cortical bone tends to reach a cement line and turn sideways
rather than continue straight through, which blunts it. Inside the shell, at the
ends of long bones and throughout the vertebrae, is trabecular bone, an open
lattice of struts and plates that provides stiffness for very little weight.

The orientation of those struts is not random, and noticing that is what opened
the whole subject. In the nineteenth century an anatomist showed drawings of the
trabecular pattern in the upper femur to an engineer, who observed that the
struts followed much the same lines as the stress trajectories in a loaded
curved crane. The German surgeon Julius Wolff generalized the observation into
what is still called Wolff's law: bone adapts its architecture to the loads
placed on it. He was describing the result without knowing the mechanism. The
mechanism took another century.

## The Crew

Three cell types do the work, and they are easiest to keep straight by their
jobs.

Osteoblasts build. They secrete unmineralized matrix — collagen and associated
proteins, called osteoid — and then manage its mineralization over the following
weeks. Most osteoblasts die once a patch of building is finished. Some flatten
into quiet lining cells on the bone surface. And some get walled into the matrix
they are producing.

Those walled-in cells become osteocytes, and they are by a wide margin the most
numerous cells in bone. Each sits in a small cavity with long thin processes
extending through microscopic channels to touch its neighbours, forming a
connected network through the entire mineralized mass. When a bone is loaded it
deforms very slightly, and that deformation drives fluid through the channels
around the osteocyte processes. The osteocytes sense the flow. They are the
strain detectors, and they issue chemical instructions accordingly — including a
protein called sclerostin, which suppresses bone formation and which osteocytes
produce less of when a bone is being worked hard. Rare inherited failures to
make sclerostin at all produce people with extraordinarily dense, heavy
skeletons.

Osteoclasts demolish. They are large, multinucleate cells derived from the same
lineage as macrophages, and they work by clamping a sealed ring onto the bone
surface and pumping acid into the sealed compartment underneath. The acid
dissolves the mineral; enzymes released into the same space digest the exposed
collagen. An osteoclast leaves behind a scooped pit.

The interesting part is that these cells are coupled. Osteoblast-lineage cells
control osteoclast formation by displaying a signalling protein that drives
precursor cells to fuse and mature — and they also secrete a decoy version of
its receptor that soaks the signal up. The ratio between those two, more than
the absolute amount of either, sets how much demolition happens. Bone builders
license the wreckers.

## Why Tear Down Good Bone at All

Remodelling in the adult skeleton proceeds in discrete packets. A team of
osteoclasts opens a resorption site — tunnelling through cortical bone, or
excavating a pit on a trabecular surface — and takes a few weeks to finish.
Osteoblasts then move in and spend several months refilling the cavity. The
asymmetry in those timescales matters enormously, as we will see.

Three purposes justify all this effort. The first is damage control. Bone
accumulates microscopic cracks from ordinary use, the way any material under
cyclic load does, and unrepaired fatigue damage eventually propagates. Osteocytes
in the path of a microcrack die, and their death is itself a signal that summons
resorption to that precise spot. The old bone containing the crack is removed
and replaced.

The second is adaptation. Loading a bone habitually harder causes it to be
rebuilt thicker, and in the trabecular lattice it causes struts aligned with the
dominant load to be reinforced while poorly aligned ones are trimmed. The clean
demonstration is in people whose two arms do different jobs. The playing arm of
a career tennis player carries visibly more cortical bone than the other, and
the difference is greatest in those who started young.

The third purpose has nothing to do with mechanics. The skeleton holds nearly
all of the body's calcium, and blood calcium has to be held within a narrow
range because nerve and muscle function depends on it. When it drops, the
parathyroid glands release a hormone that increases calcium recovery in the
kidney, promotes the activation of vitamin D so the gut absorbs more, and — if
it stays elevated — mobilizes calcium from bone. The skeleton is a structural
member and a reserve account at the same time, and when the two roles conflict,
the account wins.

## Building It in the First Place

Adult remodelling replaces bone without changing its shape. Growth requires
something different, and anatomists distinguish it as modelling: resorption on
one surface and formation on another, unpaired, so that the outline of the bone
migrates.

Consider what has to happen for the shaft of a femur to get wider while staying
hollow. Osteoblasts add bone to the outer surface, and osteoclasts simultaneously
remove it from the inner surface of the marrow cavity, so the tube expands
outward and its wall stays a sensible thickness. Consider also the flared end of
a long bone, where the shaft widens into the joint surface. As the bone
lengthens at its growth plate, that flare is constantly being left behind, and
the bone has to be carved back down to shaft diameter behind the advancing end.
A growing skeleton is not simply accreting material. It is being sculpted from
several directions at once, and the sculpting continues for as long as the
growth plates stay open.

Those plates are cartilage, and they work by a small assembly line: cartilage
cells proliferate in columns on the epiphyseal side, swell enormously, die, and
leave a mineralized scaffold that osteoblasts arriving from the shaft side use
as a template. Sex hormones at puberty drive a growth spurt and then close the
plates for good by exhausting them, which is why the timing of puberty
influences final height.

Bone mass keeps accumulating for some years after height stops changing,
reaching a peak in early adulthood. Everything after that is a question of how
slowly it is spent, and the size of the peak matters as much to late-life
fracture risk as the rate of subsequent loss — which puts a surprising amount of
weight on how much a person ran, jumped and climbed as a child.

## When the Balance Tips

Because resorption is quick and refilling is slow, anything that increases the
rate of remodelling tends to reduce bone mass, simply because more sites are
sitting open at any moment. And anything that causes each refill to fall a
little short of each excavation produces a steady, cumulative loss.

Both happen after menopause. Oestrogen restrains osteoclasts, and its withdrawal
lets turnover accelerate sharply. More sites open, the pits go deeper, and the
refills lag. In trabecular bone this produces a particular and irreversible kind
of damage: a resorption pit that cuts all the way through a thin strut severs
it, and osteoblasts cannot rebuild a strut that is no longer there because they
have nothing to build on. Bone mass measured by a scan may look only modestly
reduced while the lattice has lost a disproportionate share of its strength,
which is part of why fracture risk climbs faster than density alone would
predict.

Disuse does something similar from the other direction. Without the strain
signal, osteocytes stop restraining resorption, and bone is lost from precisely
the sites that were carrying load — the hip, the spine, the heel. Long bed rest
does it. So does spaceflight, at rates that make it one of the harder problems
in keeping people in orbit for extended periods, and the losses take
considerably longer to recover on return than they took to accrue.

Fracture repair runs the same machinery in a hurry. A break fills with a clot,
then with a soft cartilage callus that splints the ends together, then with
woven bone — laid down fast, with collagen oriented every which way, mechanically
mediocre but quick. Over the following months remodelling replaces the woven
bone with properly organized lamellar bone and gradually carves the callus back
toward the original contour. In a child the contour can be restored so
completely that the fracture becomes hard to find. Stress fractures are the same
system failing in the opposite direction: repeated loading opens resorption
sites faster than they can be refilled, the bone is temporarily weakened by its
own repair effort, and a crack propagates through the construction site.

None of which is visible in the dry femur on the table. What that object records
is a long negotiation, settled in favour of whatever loads the person actually
put through it, and now stopped at the last position it happened to be holding.
