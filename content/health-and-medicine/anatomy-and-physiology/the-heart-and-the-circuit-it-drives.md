# The Heart and the Circuit It Drives

Put a stethoscope on someone's chest and the first thing you notice is that a
heartbeat has two sounds, not one. Lub, then dub, then a pause a little longer
than you expect. Neither sound is the muscle contracting. Muscle contracting is
silent. What you are hearing is valves shutting — first the pair between the
upper and lower chambers, then, a fraction of a second later, the pair guarding
the exits into the great arteries. The heart announces itself by closing doors.

That detail is a reasonable way into the whole organ, because the heart's
difficulty is not generating force. A skeletal muscle of the same mass could
squeeze about as hard. The difficulty is making the squeeze go somewhere:
ensuring that every contraction drives blood forward rather than sloshing it
back the way it came, and doing so roughly once a second, for decades, without
a maintenance window.

## Two Pumps in One Housing

The common picture of the heart as a single pump is misleading. It is two pumps
bolted together, sharing a wall and a schedule but serving entirely different
circuits. The right side receives dark, oxygen-poor blood returning from the
body, and sends it a short distance to the lungs. The left side receives the
freshly oxygenated blood coming back from the lungs and drives it out to
everything else — brain, gut, kidneys, muscle, skin, and the heart's own walls.

The two circuits are in series, which has a consequence people often miss: over
any meaningful stretch of time the two sides must pump exactly the same volume.
A mismatch of even a few percent, sustained, would either flood the lungs or
drain them. Nothing supervises this balance from outside. It is handled by the
muscle itself, as we will come to.

What differs between the two sides is pressure. The pulmonary circuit is short,
wide, and low-resistance; it runs at perhaps a quarter of systemic pressure. It
has to. The membrane where gas exchange happens is thinner than a sheet of
tissue paper, and if you pushed arterial pressures through it, fluid would weep
into the air spaces. The systemic circuit has the opposite job — it must
maintain enough head of pressure to reach the toes and the top of the skull, and
to keep flowing through organs that are constantly changing their demands. So
the left ventricle's wall is several times thicker than the right's. Cut a heart
crosswise and the asymmetry is unmistakable: a dense ring of muscle on one side,
a thin crescent draped over it on the other.

## The Beat Is Scheduled, Not Commanded

A heart removed from the body and kept warm and perfused will go on beating. It
does not need instructions from the brain. Tucked into the wall of the right
atrium is a small patch of specialized cells — the sinoatrial node — that cannot
hold a steady resting voltage. Ions leak inward across their membranes and their
charge drifts upward until it crosses a threshold, at which point the cell fires
and the drift begins again. The slope of that drift sets the tempo.

Nerves modulate this rather than dictate it. The vagus nerve, running down from
the brainstem, steepens or flattens the drift to slow the rate; sympathetic
fibers and circulating adrenaline do the reverse. Left entirely alone, the node
would run faster than a typical resting pulse — which is why a transplanted
heart, disconnected from those nerves, settles at a briskly elevated resting
rate and responds to exertion slowly, through hormones rather than reflexes.

The signal spreads across both atria, which contract, and then hits a problem
the heart solves elegantly. A ring of fibrous tissue separates atria from
ventricles and does not conduct electricity. The only electrical crossing is the
atrioventricular node, and that node conducts slowly on purpose. The delay it
imposes — around a tenth of a second — gives the atria time to finish emptying
before the ventricles clamp down. Past the node, the signal enters a fast
conducting network, down the septum and out through fibers spread beneath the
inner lining, so that the ventricular muscle depolarizes almost as a unit,
starting near the apex and sweeping upward, wringing blood toward the outflow
valves rather than merely compressing it. An electrocardiogram is a recording of
this sequence from the skin: a small bump for the atria, a sharp spike for the
ventricles, a broader wave as the ventricles reset.

## One Beat, Mechanically

Most ventricular filling is passive. Blood returning from the veins runs through
the open atria into the relaxed ventricles under its own modest pressure; the
atrial contraction that follows tops off the last portion rather than doing the
bulk of the work. This is why people can live reasonably well in atrial
fibrillation, where the atria quiver uselessly — they lose a slice of filling,
not all of it.

Then the ventricle contracts. Pressure inside rises above pressure in the atrium
and the inlet valves snap shut, producing the first sound. For a brief interval
no blood moves at all: every valve is closed, the muscle is tightening, and
pressure climbs against a sealed chamber. Only when ventricular pressure exceeds
the pressure sitting in the aorta do the outlet valves open and ejection begins.
Even then the chamber does not empty. A healthy ventricle expels something like
half to two-thirds of its contents per beat, keeping a reserve it can draw on
when demand rises.

The inlet valves have a mechanical problem worth noticing. They are large,
floppy leaflets facing enormous backward pressure, and nothing about their shape
prevents them from flipping inside out. They are held by fine cords running down
to small muscular knobs on the ventricle floor, and those knobs contract along
with the rest of the chamber, tensioning the cords exactly when the pressure
arrives. It is the same principle as the lines on a parachute.

Underneath all of this sits a self-correcting mechanism that requires no nerves
and no hormones. Stretch heart muscle further and it contracts harder. So a
ventricle that receives more blood during filling ejects more on the next beat.
This is how the two sides of the heart stay matched without any coordinating
signal: if the right side sends a little extra to the lungs, the left side
receives a little extra, stretches a little more, and puts out a little more in
turn. The volume ejected per beat, multiplied by the rate, gives cardiac output
— around five liters a minute at rest, and four or five times that in a trained
athlete at full effort.

## What the Vessels Do With It

Ejection is violently intermittent, but capillary flow needs to be smooth. The
aorta handles the conversion. Its wall is loaded with elastin, so it balloons
during ejection, absorbing a share of the stroke volume, and recoils during the
pause, pushing that stored blood onward. This is why blood pressure has two
numbers and why the lower one is not zero. As arteries stiffen with age, the
reservoir works less well, the pulse arrives more abruptly, and systolic
pressure climbs.

The arterioles are where the circulation is actually governed. They are small,
muscular, and densely innervated, and the steepest fall in pressure across the
entire circuit happens in them. Tightening them raises resistance and pressure
generally; relaxing the ones feeding a particular tissue sends flow there
specifically. Much of that local control is chemical and automatic — working
muscle accumulates metabolites that dilate its own supply vessels, so blood
follows demand without anything needing to decide.

Capillaries are a single endothelial cell thick, and in most tissues no cell
lies more than a short diffusion distance from one. Their combined cross-section
is so vast that blood slows almost to a crawl on passing through, which is the
point: exchange needs time. Fluid pushes out through the capillary wall at the
upstream end, where hydrostatic pressure dominates, and is drawn back at the
downstream end by the osmotic pull of plasma proteins too large to escape. The
balance never quite closes, and the surplus is collected by lymphatic vessels
and returned to the veins near the collarbones.

Veins are not simply return plumbing. They are compliant, they hold the majority
of the body's blood at any moment, and constricting them shifts volume back
toward the heart. Since venous pressure is low, they rely on one-way valves and
on being squeezed — by contracting leg muscles, and by the pressure swings of
breathing. Stand rigidly still for long enough, as soldiers on parade
occasionally discover, and that pump stops working, venous return falls, and the
brain runs short.

## The Organ's Own Supply

The heart is full of blood and cannot use any of it. The muscle is too thick and
too dense for oxygen to diffuse inward from the chambers, so it needs a delivery
system of its own. The coronary arteries are the very first branches off the
aorta, leaving just above the outlet valve.

Their flow pattern is peculiar. When the left ventricle contracts, it squeezes
the vessels buried in its own wall nearly shut, so the muscle is largely
perfused between beats rather than during them. A fast heart rate shortens the
resting interval disproportionately, which means the heart's own blood supply
falls off precisely when its workload is highest. That is the mechanism behind
chest pain that appears on a hill and fades at the top.

The coronaries also carry little redundancy. Downstream of a blockage there is
usually no alternative route, and muscle deprived of flow begins dying within
minutes. What replaces it is scar — tissue that neither contracts nor conducts,
leaving both a weaker pump and a rearranged electrical map. The organ with the
most privileged access to the body's blood is the one least able to survive
losing it.
