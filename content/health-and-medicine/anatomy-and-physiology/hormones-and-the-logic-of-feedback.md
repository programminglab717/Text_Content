# Hormones and the Logic of Feedback

In 1902, two physiologists at University College London were investigating how
the pancreas knew when to release its digestive juice. The reigning answer was
nerves. Acid arriving in the intestine was thought to trigger a reflex that ran
up to the central nervous system and back down again, and the great Russian
school of digestive physiology had produced a body of elegant evidence for
exactly that.

William Bayliss and Ernest Starling did the obvious control experiment. They
took a loop of intestine in an anaesthetized animal and cut every nerve running
to it, leaving only its blood supply. Then they put acid into the denervated
loop. The pancreas, some distance away and with no nervous connection
whatsoever to the loop, promptly secreted. Something had travelled through the
blood. They ground up intestinal lining, injected the extract into a vein, and
got the same response. They called the substance secretin, and a few years later
Starling gave the general class a name borrowed from a Greek verb meaning to
rouse or set in motion: hormone.

What that experiment established was the existence of a second communication
system, working on entirely different principles from the first.

## Two Ways to Send a Message

Nerves are wires. A signal travels a specific path to a specific destination,
arrives in milliseconds, and stops when the firing stops. That is the right
design for catching a dropped glass.

Hormones are broadcast. A gland releases a molecule into the bloodstream, where
it is diluted enormously and carried everywhere without discrimination, reaching
the liver and the bone and the skin and the brain alike. It is slow, it is
imprecise about routing, and it is impossible to switch off quickly. These look
like defects until you notice what the system is for. Growth, puberty,
pregnancy, the response to a week of starvation, the daily alternation of
burning fuel and storing it — these are not events but states, and a state has
to be held across every tissue simultaneously for hours or years. You cannot
wire that. You broadcast it.

The two systems are not cleanly separate. The inner part of the adrenal gland is
essentially a sympathetic nerve ganglion whose cells, instead of releasing their
transmitter onto a neighbouring target, dump it into the bloodstream, where it
becomes the hormone adrenaline. And the back of the pituitary gland is not
glandular tissue at all but the ends of nerve cells whose bodies sit in the
hypothalamus and which secrete directly into blood. The boundary is a matter of
delivery, not of chemistry.

## Chemistry Sets the Timescale

Hormones come in two broad chemical families, and the difference determines
almost everything about how they behave.

Peptide and protein hormones — insulin, growth hormone, the pituitary's
signalling hormones, oxytocin — are chains of amino acids. They are made in
advance, packed into vesicles, and released in seconds when the cell gets the
order. Being water-soluble, they dissolve straight into plasma and travel free,
but they cannot cross a cell membrane, so their receptors sit on the target
cell's outer surface. Binding triggers a relay inside the cell: a chain of
enzymes that modify proteins already present. The effect begins within seconds
to minutes and ends nearly as fast, because these hormones are cleared from the
blood quickly. Adrenaline is gone in a couple of minutes.

Steroid hormones — cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, oestradiol — are built
from cholesterol, and they are greasy. They cannot be stored in vesicles because
they leak straight out through membranes, so a steroid gland regulates output by
regulating synthesis. Being poorly soluble in water, they are carried through
the blood attached to transport proteins, with only a small free fraction
actually able to act. And because they pass through membranes freely, their
receptors are inside the cell. A steroid–receptor pair binds DNA and turns genes
on or off, which means the response requires new protein to be manufactured. The
effect takes tens of minutes to hours to appear and persists long after the
hormone has gone.

Thyroid hormone is a hybrid: built from an amino acid but iodinated and
fat-soluble, carried on binding proteins, acting on receptors in the nucleus. It
is released mostly in a long-lived form that tissues convert locally into the
more active version, and its circulating half-life is around a week. That is why
the effects of a change in thyroid function take weeks to become obvious and
weeks to correct.

## Addressed by Receptor, Not by Route

If a hormone reaches every cell, how does it act on only some of them? The
answer is that the address is at the destination. A cell responds only if it
carries a receptor of the right shape, and the pattern of receptors a tissue
expresses is what makes it a target.

This lets one molecule mean different things in different places. Adrenaline
widens the arteries feeding skeletal muscle and narrows those feeding the skin
and gut, and it does both at once, because those beds carry different receptor
subtypes that hook into different internal machinery. Drug design exploits the
same gap: a compound that blocks one adrenaline receptor subtype can slow the
heart without much affecting the airways, and one that misses the distinction
causes exactly the trouble you would predict.

Receptors also explain the extraordinary sensitivity of the system. Hormones
circulate at concentrations that would be undetectable by taste or smell, yet
they produce large effects, because each step of the internal relay multiplies.
One bound receptor activates many signalling proteins, each of which switches on
an enzyme that manufactures many small messenger molecules, each of which
activates a kinase that modifies many targets. A few hundred molecules arriving
at a cell can reorganize its metabolism.

The flip side is that receptors are adjustable. A cell bathed continuously in a
hormone tends to reduce the number of receptors it displays, or to uncouple them
from their internal machinery. Signals that arrive constantly stop being heard.

## Loops That Hold a Value

The real architecture, though, is not the message. It is the loop.

Most endocrine control runs through a three-tier arrangement. The hypothalamus
releases a small quantity of a releasing hormone into a short private circulation
running to the front of the pituitary. The pituitary responds by secreting a
signalling hormone into the general bloodstream. That hormone drives a target
gland — thyroid, adrenal cortex, ovary or testis — to produce the hormone that
does the actual work. And the final hormone travels back and suppresses both
levels above it.

Negative feedback of this kind holds a variable near a set point without anyone
having to specify what the output should be. If thyroid hormone falls, the
suppression weakens, pituitary output climbs, the thyroid is driven harder, and
the level comes back up. If it rises, the reverse.

One practical consequence is that the informative measurement is often the
control signal rather than the controlled one. To assess a thyroid gland, the
first thing worth measuring is the pituitary hormone that drives it: a high
value means the pituitary is shouting, which means the gland is not delivering.
The number reads backwards from the intuition, which is a reliable sign that you
are looking at a control system rather than a quantity.

Not every hormone needs three tiers. Insulin and glucagon are regulated by what
they regulate, with no pituitary involvement at all — the cells that secrete them
sense blood glucose directly and respond to it. And glands turn up in
unexpected places: fat tissue secretes a hormone reporting how much fat there
is, and the heart secretes one when its chambers are over-stretched, telling the
kidney to shed salt and water.

## Rhythm, Pulses, and the Loop That Runs Away

Steady output is the exception. Cortisol rises in the hours before waking and
falls through the evening. Growth hormone comes out in bursts, most of them
during deep sleep. The hormone that drives reproduction is released by the
hypothalamus in discrete pulses roughly every hour or two, and the pulsing is
not incidental — it is the message. Supply the same hormone continuously instead
of in pulses and the pituitary, receptors dwindling, stops responding and the
entire reproductive axis shuts down. That paradox is used deliberately in
medicine, where drugs mimicking the natural pulse hormone are given
continuously in order to suppress the system they superficially resemble.

Positive feedback exists but is rare, because a loop that amplifies itself has no
resting state and must end in something. Childbirth is one case: stretch of the
cervix triggers oxytocin release, oxytocin strengthens contractions, contractions
increase the stretch, and the cycle escalates until delivery breaks it. The other
is the mid-cycle surge that triggers ovulation, where rising oestradiol, which
normally suppresses the pituitary, flips above a sustained threshold into
stimulating it instead — a feedback sign reversal that converts a stable loop
into a brief, decisive explosion.

Which leaves the least glamorous half of the whole arrangement. A system whose
messages persist has to be as good at ending them as at sending them, and the
liver and kidney spend a considerable effort inactivating and excreting hormones
that have already done their job. Failure there produces the same picture as
over-secretion, from the opposite cause, and a signal that cannot be cleared is
in the end no more useful than one that cannot be sent.
