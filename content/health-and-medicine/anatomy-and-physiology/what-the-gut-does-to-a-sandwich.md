# What the Gut Does to a Sandwich

Start with a fact that sounds like a trick and isn't: the food in your stomach
is not inside you. The digestive tract is a tube running through the body from
mouth to anus, open at both ends, and its contents are topologically outside in
the same way that the hole in a doughnut is outside the doughnut. Nothing you
swallow has entered you until it crosses the wall of that tube and gets into
blood or lymph. Everything between the first bite and that crossing is the
business of taking a structured object — bread, cheese, a slice of ham, a leaf
of lettuce — and reducing it to molecules small enough to be let through a door.

## Before It Gets Anywhere

The preparation begins before the food does. The smell of toast, or simply the
expectation of lunch, sends signals down the vagus nerve that start saliva
flowing and prompt the stomach to begin acidifying. By the time the sandwich
arrives the receiving end is already running.

Chewing is mechanical, and its value is surface area. Enzymes work only at
surfaces, so a mouthful broken into small fragments will be digested far faster
than one swallowed whole, which is a good part of why cooking and grinding food
was such a metabolic bargain for our ancestors. Saliva contributes mucins for
lubrication, bicarbonate to buffer acids, and an enzyme called amylase that
starts cutting starch into shorter sugar chains straight away. Hold a piece of
plain bread in your mouth without swallowing and after a minute or so it turns
faintly sweet: that is amylase releasing maltose from the starch.

Swallowing is the most precisely choreographed reflex in the body, because the
route to the stomach crosses the route to the lungs. The tongue pushes the bolus
back, the soft palate lifts to seal off the nose, the larynx rises and the
epiglottis folds over the airway, breathing stops for a fraction of a second,
and the upper oesophageal sphincter opens. What follows is a ring of contraction
that travels down the oesophagus and pushes the bolus ahead of it. Peristalsis
of this kind doesn't rely on gravity; an astronaut can eat in free fall, and you
could swallow standing on your head.

## The Acid Tank

The stomach is not primarily an absorbing organ. It is a holding tank with
aggressive chemistry and a slow, deliberate outlet valve.

Its upper part relaxes as food arrives so that volume can increase without
pressure rising much. Its lower part grinds. Meanwhile parietal cells in the
lining pump hydrogen ions into the cavity using a dedicated molecular pump,
driving the contents to a pH low enough to sting a wound. Acid does three
things. It kills most of the microorganisms that came in with the food. It
unfolds proteins, stretching them out so their peptide bonds are exposed. And it
converts an inactive precursor, pepsinogen, secreted by neighbouring cells, into
the active protein-cutting enzyme pepsin — which works only in acid, and switches
itself off later when it reaches the neutral conditions downstream.

The obvious question is why the stomach doesn't digest itself. The answer is a
layer of mucus with bicarbonate trapped in it, held against the lining so that
the surface cells sit at near-neutral pH even while the cavity a fraction of a
millimetre away is strongly acidic, plus an epithelium that replaces itself
every few days. Damage to that defence, rather than excess acid as such, is what
produces most ulcers.

The stomach absorbs very little — alcohol and a few drugs get through, which is
why a drink on an empty stomach hits quickly. Its one irreplaceable chemical
contribution is a protein called intrinsic factor, secreted by the same cells
that make acid, without which vitamin B12 cannot be taken up much further along.

The exit is metered. The pylorus releases only a few millilitres at a time and
only particles that have been ground below roughly a millimetre; larger pieces
get pushed back for more work. Liquids leave quickly, a fatty meal slowly. A
sandwich takes a few hours to clear.

## Where the Work Actually Happens

What arrives in the duodenum is acidic, partly broken-down slurry, and the first
task is to make it workable. Sensors in the duodenal wall detect the acid and
release a hormone that tells the pancreas to flood the region with bicarbonate;
other sensors detect fat and protein and release a second hormone that squeezes
the gallbladder and calls for pancreatic enzymes. Within minutes the pH has been
brought up to something close to neutral and the real digestive chemistry
begins.

Bile is not an enzyme and digests nothing. Bile salts are detergent-like
molecules, water-friendly at one end and fat-friendly at the other, and their job
is to break fat into a fine emulsion. This matters because fat and water do not
mix and lipase can only act at the boundary between them; turning one large
globule into millions of tiny droplets multiplies that boundary enormously.

The pancreas supplies the heavy enzymes: more amylase for starch, lipase for
fat, and a set of proteases. Those proteases are secreted as inactive precursors
and only switched on after they arrive, by an activating enzyme anchored to the
intestinal lining that clips the first one, which then clips all the rest in a
cascade. The safety logic is obvious, and so is what happens when it fails
inside the gland.

Then the surface. The small intestine is folded, the folds are carpeted with
finger-like villi, and every cell on every villus carries a dense brush of
microvilli on its exposed face. Three nested levels of texture turn a tube a few
centimetres across into an absorbing surface of tens of square metres — not the
tennis court of older textbooks, but still an area out of all proportion to the
space it occupies. Embedded in the brush border are the final enzymes, the ones
that do the last cut just as the product is about to be absorbed: peptidases,
and the sugar-splitters including lactase, sucrase and maltase. Most of humanity
switches lactase off after weaning, which is the ancestral condition; keeping it
on into adulthood is the recent mutation.

Absorption itself is a set of specific transporters, not a filter. Glucose is
hauled in alongside sodium by a shared carrier, and that single fact is the
basis of oral rehydration therapy: salt and sugar dissolved together are
absorbed far better than either alone, and water follows. Amino acids and short
peptides have their own carriers. Fats are different again — the products of
lipase are ferried to the cell surface inside bile salt aggregates, absorbed,
reassembled into triglycerides inside the cell, packaged with proteins into
droplets and exported not into blood but into lymph vessels inside each villus,
joining the bloodstream near the heart and bypassing the liver entirely.
Everything water-soluble goes the other way, into the portal vein, and is
presented to the liver for inspection before it reaches general circulation.

Two things are saved deliberately at the far end of the small intestine. Bile
salts are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum and shipped back to the liver for
reuse, recycling several times over a single meal. And B12, still bound to the
intrinsic factor it met in the stomach, is taken up by a receptor found
essentially nowhere else.

## The Nervous System You Don't Notice

The gut runs its own affairs. Embedded in its wall are two interconnected
networks of nerve cells, numbering in the hundreds of millions, which together
constitute a nervous system large enough that people reasonably call it a second
brain. A length of intestine removed from the body and kept alive will still
propagate a peristaltic wave when something distends it: the reflex is local,
requiring nothing from the spinal cord.

The brain gets reports rather than giving orders. The great majority of fibres in
the vagus nerve carry traffic upward — stretch, chemistry, irritation — and only
a minority carry instructions down.

Motility varies by task. The small intestine mostly does segmentation, rhythmic
pinching that mixes contents back and forth against the absorbing surface
instead of hurrying them along. Between meals a different pattern takes over: a
slow wave of strong contraction that sweeps from the stomach down the whole
length of the intestine every hour or two, clearing out debris and bacteria. The
rumbling of an empty stomach is that housekeeping wave, not hunger as such.

## The Fermentation Chamber

By the time material reaches the ileocaecal valve, most of what was chemically
useful has been extracted. What remains is water, minerals, dead cells, bile
pigments, and the parts of the lettuce and bread that no human enzyme can cut —
the fibre.

The colon does two things with it. It reclaims water and salt, converting a
liquid slurry into something progressively more solid, and it hosts the enormous
microbial population that can do what we cannot. Gut bacteria ferment fibre and
resistant starch, producing gases and short-chain fatty acids, and the cells
lining the colon take up one of those acids, butyrate, as their preferred fuel.
A tissue of your body runs largely on bacterial waste product. The same
populations make small amounts of vitamin K and several B vitamins.

Movement here is unhurried — mixing contractions most of the time, with a few
strong mass movements a day, often triggered by the arrival of a meal at the
other end of the tube. A sandwich eaten at noon is finished with the stomach by
mid-afternoon, through the small intestine by evening, and will spend the next
day or so in the colon, which is where the majority of the total journey time
goes.
