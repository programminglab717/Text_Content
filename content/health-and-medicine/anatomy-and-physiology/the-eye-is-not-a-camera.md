# The Eye Is Not a Camera

Close your eyes and press gently on the outer corner of one of them. A faint
glowing disc appears — and it appears on the inner side of your visual field,
opposite to where your finger is. You have just triggered photoreceptors
mechanically instead of with light, and their location has been reported to the
brain according to the usual rule, which is that the image on the retina is
upside down and left-right reversed. Light from your left lands on the right of
the retina. The brain has no independent way of knowing this and no need to know
it; it simply interprets a signal from one patch of retina as coming from the
corresponding place in the world, and a fingertip gets interpreted the same way.

The camera comparison is the standard one, and for the front half of the eye it
is a fair description. For the back half it is misleading in a way that hides the
most interesting thing about vision, which is that a substantial amount of
processing has already happened before any signal leaves the eyeball.

## The Front End Is Genuinely Optical

Most of the bending of light happens at the very first surface. The cornea is a
transparent dome sitting in air, and because the jump in refractive index
between air and tissue is large, that single interface supplies roughly
two-thirds of the eye's focusing power. The lens, immersed in fluid on both
sides, has a much smaller index difference to work with and contributes less.

This is why human vision underwater is so poor. Water has nearly the same
refractive index as the cornea, the air interface disappears, and most of your
focusing power goes with it. Goggles restore it by reintroducing a pocket of
air.

The cornea's power is fixed. The lens is the adjustable element, and it adjusts
in a way that most people get backwards. It is suspended by a ring of fine
fibres attached to a circular muscle. When that muscle is relaxed, the ring
around the lens is at its widest, the fibres are under tension, and they pull
the lens flat — which is the setting for distant objects. To focus on something
close, the muscle contracts, and because it is a ring, contracting it makes its
diameter smaller. The fibres slacken, and the lens, which is elastic and would
prefer to be round, bulges into a more strongly curved shape. Focusing on your
own hand takes muscular effort; staring at the horizon does not.

The lens stiffens steadily throughout life. The loss is gradual and starts in
childhood, but it only becomes a problem when the closest point you can focus on
drifts past normal reading distance, which happens somewhere in the forties and
tends to feel sudden because that is when the arm runs out of length.

Focus can also fail geometrically. If the eyeball grows slightly too long during
childhood, the image forms in front of the retina and distant objects blur:
short-sightedness, which has become dramatically more common in much of the
world within a couple of generations. The best-supported explanation is not
reading as such but a shortage of time outdoors, with bright daylight appearing
to supply a signal that regulates how far the eye elongates while it grows.

## Aperture, and the Range Problem

The iris adjusts the pupil over a range of a few millimetres, changing the
amount of light admitted by something over tenfold. That sounds useful, and it
is, but set it against the task: between a moonless night and a snowfield at
noon, the light available spans something like ten orders of magnitude. The
pupil handles a rounding error.

Almost all of the adaptation happens further back, chemically and neurally. In
bright light the visual pigment in the photoreceptors is being bleached faster
than it can be regenerated, so the cells sit at low sensitivity. Move into
darkness and the pigment gradually rebuilds. Cones finish this within a few
minutes. Rods take twenty minutes or more, which is why the sky keeps acquiring
new stars for a long time after you sit down outside, and why a single glance at
a phone screen costs you most of the progress.

## A Retina Built Backwards

Here the camera analogy starts to fall apart. The retina is installed the wrong
way round. Light entering the eye passes first through the layer of output
neurons, then through the interneurons, and only then reaches the
photoreceptors, which point away from the light with their sensitive ends buried
in a dark pigmented layer at the very back.

This looks like poor engineering and gets cited as such. The reason it works is
that photoreceptors are metabolically extravagant — they continuously rebuild
their light-sensitive structures, shedding and replacing the tips daily — and
they depend on the pigment layer behind them to recycle their pigment, dispose
of the shed material, and connect them to the rich blood supply of the tissue
outside. Putting them at the back puts them next to their support system. The
overlying neural layers are thin and nearly transparent, and elongated glial
cells appear to act as light guides, funnelling light through the clutter to the
receptors below.

One cost is unavoidable. Since the output neurons lie in front of the
photoreceptors, their axons must gather on the inner surface of the retina and
then dive through it to leave the eye. At that exit there can be no receptors,
and the result is a genuine blind spot in each eye a little to the outer side of
centre. You do not notice it because the other eye covers the gap, and because
the visual system confidently fills the hole with whatever surrounds it.

## One Photon Is Enough

The chemistry of detection rests on a single trick. A photoreceptor packs its
membranes with a protein bound to a derivative of vitamin A, a small molecule
with a kink in it. When a photon strikes, the molecule straightens. That is the
entire photochemical event; everything afterwards is the cell reacting to a
change in the shape of a protein.

The reaction is a cascade, and it amplifies enormously. The altered protein
activates a relay protein, which activates an enzyme, which destroys a small
signalling molecule that had been holding ion channels in the cell membrane open.
The channels shut and the cell's electrical state changes. Because each step
acts on many copies of the next, a single absorbed photon produces a measurable
response in a rod, and a person in a properly dark room can reliably detect
flashes of only a handful of photons.

The polarity is the surprise. In darkness those channels stand open and the
photoreceptor sits depolarized, continuously leaking current and continuously
releasing neurotransmitter onto the cells downstream. Light closes the channels
and quiets it. Photoreceptors signal by falling silent. A retina in total
darkness is not a resting retina; it is one working at full output, which is
part of why the outer retina consumes so much energy.

The dependence on vitamin A is direct, and its deficiency shows up first as
difficulty seeing in dim light.

## What Actually Leaves the Eye

The retina holds something over a hundred million photoreceptors. The optic
nerve contains roughly a million fibres. Whatever the eye sends onward, it is
not a pixel-by-pixel transmission, and the compression is not uniform.

At the centre of gaze is the fovea, a small pit no bigger than your thumbnail
held at arm's length, containing only cones, packed at maximum density, with the
overlying neural layers pushed aside so light reaches them more cleanly. Foveal
cones get nearly private lines to the brain, which is why acuity there is
superb. Everywhere else, and especially in the far periphery, many receptors
funnel into each output neuron. That pooling throws away detail and buys
sensitivity, which is why a faint star is easier to see slightly off to the side
of where you are looking than dead centre. Astronomers learn the trick early.

Colour works by comparison rather than by measurement. There are three cone
types, with broad and heavily overlapping sensitivity ranges, and no single one
of them detects a colour. A long-wavelength cone responds to green light too,
just less strongly. Hue is inferred from the ratio of responses across the
three, which is why entirely different mixtures of wavelengths can be
indistinguishable to the eye — the basis on which every screen you own gets away
with faking the visible spectrum using three primaries.

The retina then recodes those signals before sending them. Circuits of
horizontal and amacrine cells subtract neighbouring responses from one another,
so that a typical output neuron reports not how bright its patch of the world is
but how much brighter it is than the ring around it, and colour is repackaged
into opposed pairs — red against green, blue against yellow, light against dark.
The eye transmits contrast and difference. It largely discards absolute
brightness, which is a sensible economy given that the same white paper reflects
wildly different amounts of light indoors and out, and it is why a great many
visual illusions work by manipulating surroundings rather than targets.

One last thing keeps it all running. The eye is never still: it jumps several
times a second, and between jumps it trembles. This is not sloppiness. A retinal
image held perfectly motionless on the receptors fades out within seconds, since
a system built to report change has nothing to report. Vision depends on the
picture being constantly jiggled.

So what travels up the optic nerve is not a picture at all. It is a stream of
reports about edges, movements, and differences, gathered at wildly uneven
resolution by a sensor that spends most of its energy in the dark and never
holds still — and the coherent, stable, detailed scene you believe you are
looking at is assembled somewhere downstream, out of considerably less than it
appears to contain.
