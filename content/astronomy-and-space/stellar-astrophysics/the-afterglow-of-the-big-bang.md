# The Afterglow of the Big Bang

In the mid-1960s, two radio astronomers at Bell Labs in New Jersey, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, were trying to calibrate an unusually sensitive antenna, originally built for satellite communication, for use in radio astronomy. No matter where they pointed it, they kept picking up a faint, steady hiss of microwave noise they could not get rid of. They checked the electronics, ruled out radio interference from nearby cities, and even climbed into the antenna to clean out a pair of pigeons and their droppings, suspecting the birds might somehow be responsible. The noise persisted regardless of the direction they pointed the antenna, regardless of the time of day, and regardless of the season, which ruled out the sun, the galaxy, or any localized source. Whatever it was, it seemed to be coming from everywhere at once, uniformly, across the entire sky.

What Penzias and Wilson had stumbled onto, without initially realizing it, was the cosmic microwave background: the faint, cooled remnant of radiation left over from the hot, dense early universe, now regarded as one of the strongest single pieces of evidence for the Big Bang.

## An Unwanted Hiss

Penzias and Wilson were not cosmologists and were not looking for evidence of the universe's origin; they simply wanted their antenna's background noise low enough to do useful radio astronomy, and this particular noise refused to go away no matter what they tried. Frustrated, they eventually made contact with researchers at nearby Princeton University, who, as it turned out, had been independently working toward building an instrument specifically to search for exactly this kind of signal, based on theoretical predictions that had been sitting largely unexamined for over a decade.

The Princeton group had reasoned that if the universe really had begun in an extremely hot, dense state, as the general theory of an expanding universe implied, then the intense radiation filling that early universe should still be around today, tremendously cooled and stretched out by billions of years of cosmic expansion into faint microwave wavelengths, arriving equally from every direction in the sky. When the two teams compared notes, it became clear that Penzias and Wilson's stubborn, unwanted hiss was exactly that signal.

## A Prediction Already on the Shelf

The theoretical case for this radiation actually predated its discovery by close to two decades. Physicists working on the physics of the early universe, including George Gamow and his collaborators Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman, had calculated that a hot early universe should leave behind a relic bath of radiation, and had even estimated roughly what temperature it should have cooled to by the present day. Their prediction attracted relatively little attention at the time and was largely forgotten by the broader astronomical community, in part because the technology needed to detect such a faint, uniform signal did not yet exist.

By the time Penzias and Wilson's discovery was published, alongside a companion paper from the Princeton group explaining what it meant, the pieces fell into place almost immediately: a prediction made decades earlier, nearly forgotten, was confirmed by an accidental discovery made by researchers who had not set out looking for it at all.

## What the Afterglow Actually Is

For the universe's first few hundred thousand years, matter was too hot for atoms to hold together; any electron that settled into orbit around a nucleus was immediately knocked loose again by the intense surrounding radiation, leaving matter in an ionized state that scattered light constantly, making the early universe opaque, like the inside of a thick fog. As the universe expanded, it cooled, and a few hundred thousand years after the Big Bang, it finally cooled enough for electrons and nuclei to combine permanently into stable atoms, an event cosmologists call recombination. With the free electrons gone, light could suddenly travel freely without being scattered, and the universe became transparent almost overnight.

The light released at that moment has been traveling through space ever since, stretched by the ongoing expansion of the universe from what was once visible and infrared light into the microwave wavelengths detected today. That stretched light is the cosmic microwave background, and because it comes from a single, well-defined moment in the universe's early history, arriving from every direction at once, it effectively offers a snapshot of what the entire universe looked like at that specific age, long before any star or galaxy had formed.

## Reading the Fluctuations

The cosmic microwave background as Penzias and Wilson found it appeared almost perfectly uniform in every direction, but almost was the operative word. Satellite missions launched over the following decades, beginning with COBE and continuing with more sensitive successors like WMAP and Planck, mapped the sky in increasingly fine detail and revealed extremely faint temperature variations from spot to spot, differences amounting to only tiny fractions of the background's overall temperature. Those fluctuations are not noise; they are the fingerprint of slight density variations in the early universe, regions fractionally denser or sparser than average, that would go on, over the following billions of years, to collapse under their own gravity and grow into the galaxies and galaxy clusters observed today.

The precise pattern of those fluctuations, including the characteristic sizes at which they are most pronounced, encodes an enormous amount of information about the universe's overall composition and geometry, and analysis of that pattern is part of what convinced cosmologists that ordinary matter makes up only a modest fraction of the universe's total mass, with the remainder consisting of dark matter and, on the largest scales, the mysterious dark energy driving the universe's accelerating expansion.

Beyond mapping temperature variations across the sky, the COBE satellite also confirmed something more basic about the background's overall spectrum: it matches, to remarkable precision, the smooth curve physicists call a blackbody spectrum, the same characteristic shape of light radiated by any object in thermal equilibrium, scaled down to the frigid temperature the universe has cooled to today. That clean blackbody shape is itself a strong piece of evidence for the Big Bang picture, since it is exactly what would be expected from light that was thoroughly scattered and thermalized in the hot, dense, opaque plasma of the early universe before recombination let it escape.

## A Puzzle the Background Poses

The cosmic microwave background's near-perfect uniformity across the sky is itself something of a puzzle. Regions on opposite sides of the observable sky are, in an important sense, far enough apart that light would not have had time to travel between them since the Big Bang, given the universe's age and the finite speed of light. Yet those regions show almost exactly the same temperature, to a remarkable degree of precision, which is strange if they were never in contact with each other long enough to reach the same temperature by ordinary physical processes, the way a room eventually reaches a uniform temperature through the exchange of heat.

This puzzle, sometimes called the horizon problem, is one of the main reasons cosmologists proposed that the universe underwent a brief, extraordinarily rapid period of expansion, called inflation, in a tiny fraction of a second after the Big Bang, long before the era that produced the cosmic microwave background itself. If regions of space that are now far apart were once close enough together to reach the same temperature, and inflation subsequently stretched that already-uniform region out to enormous size faster than light could have crossed it under ordinary expansion, the observed uniformity stops being a coincidence and becomes an expected consequence.

Inflation, though not directly observed the way the background radiation itself has been, makes additional predictions about the statistical pattern of the background's tiny temperature fluctuations, and measurements of that pattern by later satellite missions have so far matched what simple inflationary models predict reasonably well, lending indirect support to an idea that was originally proposed mainly to solve puzzles like the uniformity problem rather than to match any specific observation that existed at the time.

## Why It Still Matters

The discovery of the cosmic microwave background settled, essentially overnight, a long-standing debate between the Big Bang model of an expanding universe that began in a hot, dense state and a rival idea, the steady-state theory, which held that the universe had no beginning and looked broadly the same at all times. A uniform bath of leftover radiation, at exactly the temperature a hot early universe should have cooled to, was very difficult for the steady-state model to explain and fit naturally into the Big Bang picture, and the balance of opinion among cosmologists shifted decisively within a few years.

Six decades later, that same faint microwave hiss, once mistaken for a problem with an antenna, remains one of the most precisely measured signals in all of astrophysics, and one of the more remarkable examples in the history of science of a theoretical prediction, nearly forgotten, being confirmed entirely by accident by researchers who were simply trying to get their equipment to work properly.
