# Why Most Stars Have a Companion

Point even a modest telescope at Alpha Centauri, the nearest star system to the sun, and it resolves into two stars orbiting each other closely, with a third, faint red dwarf further out completing the group. This is not an unusual arrangement. Single stars like the sun, orbited only by planets and not by any stellar companion, are common enough, but among stars generally, and especially among the more massive ones, having at least one companion star is closer to the norm than the exception. Binary and multiple star systems, not solitary suns, may be the more typical outcome of star formation.

This matters for more than just astronomical trivia. Close binary stars interact with each other over their lifetimes in ways that reshape their individual evolution, produce some of the most energetic events observable in the universe, and give astronomers nearly the only direct method available for measuring how much a star actually weighs.

## Counting Companions

The tendency to form in pairs or groups is not uniform across all stars. Massive, hot stars are especially likely to have one or more companions, often in tight orbits, while lower-mass red dwarfs, the most common type of star in the galaxy, are more often found alone. Between those extremes, sunlike stars fall somewhere in the middle, with a substantial fraction, though not an overwhelming majority, belonging to binary or multiple systems.

Why massive stars pair up so readily seems to trace back to how they form: dense, collapsing clumps of gas in a molecular cloud often fragment into two or more pieces before any of them finishes collapsing into a star, and the resulting objects can end up gravitationally bound to each other from the start. Lower-mass stars, forming from smaller, less turbulent clumps, are statistically less likely to fragment this way, though it still happens often enough that low-mass binaries are hardly rare.

## How Astronomers Tell Two Stars Apart from One

Not every binary system is obvious. Some, called visual binaries, are far enough apart and close enough to Earth that a telescope can resolve them as two distinct points of light, as with Alpha Centauri's two brighter members. Many more are too close together, or too far away, to separate visually, and have to be identified indirectly. Spectroscopic binaries reveal themselves through the Doppler shifting of their light: as two stars orbit their common center of mass, one approaches Earth while the other recedes, and the light from each shifts slightly toward the blue or red accordingly, a shift that reverses on a regular cycle as the stars swing around their orbit.

Eclipsing binaries announce themselves differently, through regular dips in total brightness as one star passes in front of the other from Earth's point of view, blocking part or all of its companion's light on a schedule set by the orbital period. And some binaries only reveal themselves through subtle gravitational effects on a visible star's motion through space — a slight wobble that betrays the presence of an unseen companion long before any direct detection becomes possible.

## Living Close Together

When two stars orbit closely enough, their evolution can become entangled in ways that a single star never experiences. Each star in a close binary has a region around it, called its Roche lobe, within which its own gravity dominates; if a star expands enough during its life — as it will when it leaves the main sequence and swells into a giant — it can overflow its Roche lobe and begin spilling gas onto its companion. This mass transfer can dramatically alter both stars' subsequent evolution, speeding up or slowing down their nuclear burning, changing their outward appearance, and sometimes reversing which of the two stars is more massive.

In the closest systems, both stars can even share a common envelope of gas, orbiting within it like two objects embedded in a single fog, a configuration that tends to be short-lived and often ends with the stars merging entirely or the envelope being ejected, leaving a much tighter binary behind.

## The Algol Paradox

One particular puzzle, named for the eclipsing binary star Algol, illustrates just how strange mass transfer can make things. In a normal binary, the more massive star should evolve faster, since higher mass means higher core temperature and pressure and a shorter main-sequence lifetime. Yet in the Algol system, the less massive star of the pair is the one that has already evolved into a giant, while its more massive companion is still comfortably on the main sequence — apparently backward from how stellar evolution should work.

The resolution is mass transfer: the star that is now less massive was originally the more massive of the two, evolved faster as expected, swelled into a giant, and in doing so overflowed its Roche lobe and dumped much of its mass onto its companion. The two stars effectively swapped mass partway through their lives, leaving the original loser now more massive and still youthful-looking, and the original winner stripped down and visibly more evolved. Once astronomers understood mass transfer, what looked like a paradox turned out to be one of the more elegant confirmations of how binary evolution actually works.

## Why Binaries Matter

Beyond their intrinsic strangeness, binary systems give astronomers one of their few direct tools for weighing stars. By observing how two stars orbit each other and applying the same laws that govern planetary orbits, astronomers can calculate the combined mass of the pair directly, and with enough additional information, the mass of each star individually — a measurement that is otherwise remarkably difficult to make for an isolated star.

Binaries are also responsible for some of the more spectacular events in the universe. Mass transfer onto a white dwarf can trigger novae or, in the right circumstances, a Type Ia supernova. Mass transfer onto a neutron star or black hole can produce some of the brightest X-ray sources in the sky, as infalling gas heats to extreme temperatures on its way in. And pairs of neutron stars or black holes that spiral together over great stretches of time eventually merge in events energetic enough to send ripples through spacetime itself, detectable by gravitational-wave observatories on Earth. A universe of only single stars would be missing a great deal of its more interesting behavior.
