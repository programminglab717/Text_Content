# White Dwarfs: The Cores Stars Leave Behind

When astronomers in the middle of the nineteenth century first noticed that Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, wobbled slightly in its motion across the heavens, they suspected an unseen companion tugging on it gravitationally. Decades passed before anyone actually saw that companion through a telescope, and when they finally did, and later managed to measure its properties, the results made no sense by the standards of the time: the object was roughly the mass of the sun, packed into a volume not much bigger than the Earth. Nothing known to nineteenth- and early twentieth-century physics could explain how that much mass could be squeezed into that little space without collapsing further. The object, now called Sirius B, was the first white dwarf studied in any detail, and working out what it actually was took the arrival of quantum mechanics.

White dwarfs are now understood to be one of the most common endpoints for a star's life — the fate awaiting the sun and the great majority of stars in the galaxy — and they remain one of the better illustrations of how strange matter behaves once gravity has compressed it past the point where it behaves like an ordinary gas.

## A Star's Leftover Core

A star like the sun spends most of its life fusing hydrogen into helium, then, after its core hydrogen runs out, briefly fuses helium into carbon and oxygen while its outer envelope swells into a red giant. Stars of this mass never get hot enough at the core to ignite carbon fusion, so once the helium is exhausted, fusion simply stops. The star's outer layers, no longer held by any outward pressure worth mentioning, drift away into space as a slowly expanding, glowing shell called a planetary nebula — a name left over from when early observers thought these objects resembled the disks of planets through small telescopes, though they have nothing to do with actual planets.

What remains at the center, once the outer layers have dispersed, is the exposed core: a dense ball of carbon and oxygen, roughly the mass of the sun compressed into a sphere not much larger than the Earth, glowing white-hot from stored heat with no fusion left to sustain it. That leftover core is the white dwarf.

## Matter Under Extreme Pressure

What holds a white dwarf up against its own gravity is not fusion and not ordinary gas pressure but something called electron degeneracy pressure, a consequence of quantum mechanics rather than heat. Electrons obey a rule called the Pauli exclusion principle, which forbids two of them from occupying the same quantum state in the same place. Squeeze electrons together tightly enough, as gravity does inside a white dwarf, and they are forced into higher and higher energy states simply because the lower ones are already full, whether or not the material is hot. That resistance to further compression is degeneracy pressure, and it is strong enough to hold up roughly a sun's worth of mass against gravity indefinitely.

Degeneracy pressure has a strange property: it depends on density but barely on temperature, which means a white dwarf can cool for billions of years without shrinking. It also means more massive white dwarfs are smaller, not larger, than lighter ones, since more mass requires the electrons to be squeezed into a smaller volume to generate enough pressure to balance it. There is a limit to how much mass degeneracy pressure can support, worked out by the physicist Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar and now named for him, at around one and a half times the mass of the sun. Beyond that mass, electron degeneracy pressure alone cannot hold the star up.

## A Slow Fade

With no fusion occurring, a white dwarf's story from this point on is simply one of cooling. It radiates away its stored heat over an immense stretch of time, dimming and reddening as it goes, supported the entire while by degeneracy pressure rather than heat. Some theoretical models suggest that, given enough time, the carbon and oxygen inside a cooling white dwarf will begin to crystallize into an orderly lattice, releasing additional heat as it does, somewhat like water releasing heat as it freezes, which would slow the cooling process further.

Eventually, in principle, a white dwarf should cool all the way down to become a cold, dark, inert ball of crystallized carbon and oxygen — a black dwarf. No such object is thought to exist yet anywhere in the universe, because the cooling process takes longer than the current age of the universe, which means every white dwarf that has ever formed is, in a sense, still in the middle of fading.

## Finding Them in the Sky

White dwarfs are faint by stellar standards, since they have no fusion to power their light and shine only on stored heat, so most of the ones cataloged so far lie relatively close to the sun, discovered either as companions to brighter stars, whose gravity gives them away, or through dedicated surveys sensitive enough to pick out their faint, hot glow. On a diagram that plots stars by temperature against brightness, white dwarfs sit apart from almost everything else, well below the main sequence where hydrogen-burning stars like the sun spend most of their lives, precisely because they are so much smaller and fainter than an ordinary star of the same surface temperature.

That distinctive position on the diagram makes white dwarfs relatively easy to identify once found, and useful for a purpose beyond their own physics: because their cooling rate is well understood, measuring how faint and cool a white dwarf has become can be used to estimate how long ago it stopped fusing, which in turn helps set age limits on the star clusters or regions of the galaxy where they are found.

## When White Dwarfs Misbehave

A white dwarf left entirely alone simply cools in peace, but a white dwarf in a binary system with a close companion can have a much more eventful afterlife. If the companion star expands or drifts close enough, the white dwarf's gravity can start pulling matter off it, typically hydrogen-rich gas that accumulates on the white dwarf's surface. That accumulated layer can eventually reach a temperature and density high enough to ignite in a sudden burst of fusion, producing a nova: a rapid, dramatic brightening that, unlike a supernova, does not destroy the star and can, in some systems, recur.

In rarer cases, a white dwarf can accumulate enough mass from its companion, or merge with another white dwarf entirely, to approach the Chandrasekhar limit. Crossing that threshold triggers a runaway ignition of the carbon and oxygen throughout the star, unleashing a Type Ia supernova that leaves nothing behind — no remnant core at all, just an expanding cloud of debris. It is a strange fate for an object that, left to its own devices, would simply have cooled quietly for the rest of time.
