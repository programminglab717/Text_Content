# How a Space Telescope Gets Built

Every star you can see with the naked eye is twinkling for a reason that has nothing to do with the star itself. The light left that star in a straight, steady line and only started to waver in the last fraction of a second of its journey, as it passed through pockets of turbulent air of slightly different temperature and density near the bottom of Earth's atmosphere. That shimmer is charming to look at and ruinous for precision astronomy. It blurs fine detail no matter how large or well-made a ground telescope's mirror is, and it is only one of several reasons the most important observatories of the last half-century have been built not to sit on a mountaintop, but to fly.

## Getting Above the Air

Beyond blurring starlight, Earth's atmosphere simply absorbs whole categories of light before they ever reach the ground. Most infrared light, most ultraviolet light, and X-rays are filtered out by gases high in the atmosphere, which is excellent news for anything alive at the surface and a serious problem for astronomers who want to study the universe in those wavelengths, since an enormous amount of astrophysics is only visible there. A telescope in space sidesteps both problems at once. Above the atmosphere there is no turbulence to blur an image, and there is no absorption to block wavelengths that never reach the ground at all. The tradeoff is steep: a ground telescope can be repaired, upgraded, and maintained indefinitely by a technician with a ladder, while a space telescope typically has to work correctly for years or decades with little or no chance of a service call.

## The Trouble With Infrared Light

Different scientific goals call for different kinds of space telescopes, and the design choices cascade from there. An observatory built to study the earliest, most distant galaxies faces a specific problem: the universe has been expanding for the entire time light from those galaxies has been traveling toward us, which stretches that light toward longer, redder wavelengths the farther it has traveled — a phenomenon called redshift. Light from the most distant objects arrives not as visible light at all but as infrared. Detecting it requires an infrared telescope, and infrared telescopes have a peculiar enemy: heat. Any object warmer than deep space glows faintly in infrared itself, including the telescope's own structure, and if the instrument is not kept extremely cold, its own glow floods the detectors and swamps the faint signal it is trying to measure. A telescope built for this kind of observation has to be one of the coldest objects humans have ever flown.

## A Mirror That Folds Like Origami

Collecting enough of that faint, ancient light requires a large primary mirror, and building a large mirror for space runs headlong into a much more mundane constraint: rockets have a fixed width. A mirror much wider than about the diameter of the rocket's payload fairing simply cannot be launched in one piece. The solution adopted for the most advanced infrared observatory flown to date was to build the mirror out of many smaller hexagonal segments, each individually adjustable, arranged into a single large honeycomb-shaped surface too wide to fit in the rocket unless folded. The segments, coated in a thin layer of gold chosen because it reflects infrared light exceptionally well, are hinged so that the outer sections fold back against the rest of the structure for launch, along with a large secondary mirror on a boom that also has to unfold, and a sprawling multilayer sunshield that has to unpack itself into an area larger than a tennis court. None of this can be tested exactly as it will operate before launch, because on the ground the mirror sags very slightly under its own weight in a way it never will in weightlessness, and because a sunshield the size of a tennis court cannot be fully unfolded and tested in an ordinary facility. Engineers rehearsed the deployment sequence on the ground as thoroughly as physically possible, tested every joint, hinge, and motor individually, and then had to trust the sequence to run correctly, unattended, hundreds of thousands of kilometers from the nearest human being.

## Staying Cold Enough to See

The sunshield does the bulk of the cooling work. Its several thin, precisely spaced layers are angled to radiate heat out to the sides rather than letting it conduct straight through, so that the side facing the Sun, Earth, and Moon can sit at a temperature warm enough for onboard electronics to function, while the far side, holding the mirror and instruments, drops to a temperature only slightly above absolute zero, cold enough that the telescope's own structure emits negligible infrared light of its own. Getting that cold passively, using only shielding and radiation to space rather than a mechanical refrigeration system, was essential, because active cooling systems are heavy, consume power, and eventually wear out. A telescope meant to operate for a decade or more needs to get cold and stay cold using nothing but geometry and material choice, since there will be no way to service a broken cooling system once it is on station.

## Parked a Million Miles Away, With No Repair Truck

The earlier generation of space telescopes, including the long-serving observatory that first demonstrated what an orbiting telescope could achieve, orbited close enough to Earth that astronauts could periodically visit them aboard the Space Shuttle, replacing failing instruments, correcting an early flaw in its main mirror, and extending its working life far beyond what its designers had originally planned. That option does not exist for an observatory that needs to be extremely cold and far from Earth's own infrared glow. Instead, the most advanced infrared telescopes are placed in orbit around a gravitationally stable point far beyond the Moon, where the combined pull of the Sun and Earth lets a spacecraft maintain a stable position relative to both while using very little fuel to stay there. It is a useful location precisely because it is so far away, keeping the telescope's view unobstructed and its temperature stable, but that same distance rules out any realistic repair mission. Every mechanism has to work the first time, since a stuck hinge or a failed motor a million and a half kilometers from Earth cannot be fixed by anyone.

## Bringing an Unfolded Telescope Into Focus

Even a perfectly deployed mirror does not produce a usable image right away. Each hexagonal segment starts out pointed in only approximately the right direction, and the many separate mirror pieces have to be nudged, segment by segment, using tiny actuators capable of movements measured in fractions of the wavelength of light, until all of them focus light from the same star onto the exact same point on the detector. Only then does the telescope stop being a collection of individually excellent mirrors and start behaving as one enormous, seamless one. The whole sequence, from launch through unfolding, cooling, and fine alignment, takes months to complete, a slow, deliberate process for an instrument that, once finished, is expected to spend the next decade or more looking farther into the past than any instrument built before it.
