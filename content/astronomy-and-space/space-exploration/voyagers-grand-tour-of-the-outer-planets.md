# Voyager's Grand Tour of the Outer Planets

Twice a century, give or take, the outer planets line up in a way that lets a single spacecraft visit several of them on one trip, using each flyby to bend its path toward the next rather than carrying enough fuel to fly there directly. A young engineer named Gary Flandro noticed in the 1960s that such an alignment was coming due in the late 1970s, and that missing it would mean waiting more than a century for another chance. NASA built two spacecraft to take advantage of it. They were modest by later standards, each roughly the size of a small car with a large dish antenna bolted on and three long booms holding instruments and a nuclear power source away from the main body. They were named Voyager 1 and Voyager 2, and more than four decades later, they are still flying, still returning data, and still farther from home than anything else humanity has built.

## Twins Launched for a Once-in-a-Lifetime Alignment

Both spacecraft left Earth in the late summer of 1977, only weeks apart. Voyager 2 actually launched first, in August, on a slower trajectory; Voyager 1 followed in September on a faster path that overtook its twin before either reached Jupiter. From the start the mission was designed with redundancy and flexibility in mind. Each Voyager carried a nearly identical set of cameras and instruments, and each was capable of covering for the other's objectives if something went wrong along the way. Nothing did, at least not badly enough to end either mission, which in itself says something about the engineering, given how long these spacecraft have now been operating in an environment no one designed spacecraft to survive for this long.

## Jupiter and Saturn, Then a Fork in the Road

Both spacecraft reached Jupiter in 1979, returning images of the planet's turbulent cloud bands and its family of large moons in far greater detail than any previous mission, revealing active volcanoes on Io and hints of a subsurface ocean beneath the icy crust of Europa — discoveries that reshaped how planetary scientists thought about where life might conceivably exist beyond Earth. Both then went on to Saturn, arriving about a year apart, returning the first close, detailed views of the rings' fine structure and of large moons like Titan, whose thick, hazy atmosphere hid its surface from the cameras entirely.

Some of the mission's most important discoveries were nearly missed entirely. While reviewing one of Voyager 1's images of Io to help refine the spacecraft's navigation, an engineer named Linda Morabito noticed an odd crescent-shaped feature rising off the moon's edge, not among the background stars where it should have been if it were an imaging artifact. It turned out to be a volcanic plume, caught by chance in a navigation image, and it made Io the first place beyond Earth where active volcanic eruptions were directly observed. Nobody on the mission had been specifically looking for volcanoes; the discovery came out of a routine engineering check, a reminder of how much of what Voyager found was not so much planned as recognized when it appeared.

At Saturn the mission split. Voyager 1's trajectory was bent deliberately to fly close past Titan for a dedicated look at that atmosphere, a choice mission planners made knowing it would throw the spacecraft's path out of the plane in which the planets orbit, ending any chance of visiting another planet afterward. Voyager 2's path was left clear to continue on toward the outer solar system, using Saturn's gravity to aim it at the next planet in line.

## Voyager 2's Lonely Trip to the Ice Giants

That decision gave Voyager 2 a distinction it still holds alone. It reached Uranus in 1986 and Neptune in 1989, and to this day no other spacecraft has ever visited either planet. The images it sent back defined, for decades afterward, essentially everything the public and most scientists pictured when they thought of those two worlds: Uranus a nearly featureless pale blue-green sphere, tilted almost onto its side; Neptune a deeper, more active blue, marked by a large dark storm system and trailed by the icy moon Triton, whose surface showed signs of ongoing geologic activity despite sitting at a temperature not far above absolute zero. Every gravity assist along the way had been aimed with extraordinary precision, since a small error early in such a long chain of flybys would compound into a large one by the time the spacecraft reached its next target, a planet it would have only one chance to see up close.

## A Record Meant to Outlast Its Makers

Bolted to the outside of each spacecraft is a gold-plated copper phonograph record, commissioned before launch and assembled by a committee that included the astronomer Carl Sagan. The record carries a selection of sounds and images meant to represent Earth: greetings recorded in dozens of languages, music spanning different cultures and eras, natural sounds like wind, surf, and animal calls, and encoded images of human beings, landscapes, and diagrams of basic science. It was never expected that anyone would find it, in any meaningful timeframe. The record was as much a gesture aimed back at Earth as outward toward any hypothetical audience, a way of marking what the mission represented at a moment when reaching the outer planets at all still felt like a stretch. Both records are still attached, riding along on spacecraft that will likely outlast every structure currently standing on the planet they came from.

## Into the Space Between the Stars

Neither Voyager stopped working once its planetary encounters ended. Both continued outward, instruments still measuring the thinning solar wind, the faint magnetic field carried by the Sun, and the density of charged particles around them, waiting for a boundary that theorists had predicted but no spacecraft had ever crossed: the heliopause, where the outward pressure of particles streaming from the Sun finally gives way to the material drifting between stars. Voyager 1 crossed that boundary first, followed years later by Voyager 2 along a different path out of the solar system. Both are now considered to be in interstellar space, the only human-made objects to have reached it, still connected to Earth by a radio link so faint and so delayed that a single signal, traveling at the speed of light, takes the better part of a day to make the trip.

## Still Calling Home

Power is the constraint that will eventually end the mission, not distance or damage. Both spacecraft run on radioisotope thermoelectric generators, which produce electricity from the steady decay of a radioactive fuel source rather than from sunlight, since sunlight this far from the Sun is far too weak to be useful. That power output has been declining gradually for the entire mission, and engineers have kept both spacecraft alive by turning off instruments and heaters one by one, prioritizing whichever measurements still seem most valuable for a spacecraft in a place nothing else has ever been.

The onboard computers making all of this possible are, by any modern standard, remarkably modest, built with the technology available in the early 1970s and never upgraded since, because there has never been a way to reach either spacecraft to do so. The small team of engineers who still operate the mission has had to become specialists in hardware most of the industry moved past generations ago, occasionally consulting original design documentation, and sometimes their own memories, to troubleshoot a spacecraft built when many of today's engineers had not yet been born. That combination of old hardware and new problems has become part of the job: diagnosing a growing list of minor age-related faults using instruments and processors with a small fraction of the capability found in an ordinary modern household appliance, on a spacecraft too far away for any solution other than a carefully worded software patch, radioed across a gap that takes the better part of a day to cross in each direction. Eventually there will not be enough power left to run anything at all, and contact will end for good. Until then, twice-Voyager-launched, twice as old as most of the engineers now monitoring it from Mission Control, the mission keeps doing exactly what it was built to do: keep looking, and keep calling home.
