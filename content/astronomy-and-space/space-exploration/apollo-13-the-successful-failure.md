# Apollo 13: The Successful Failure

Jim Lovell had already flown to the Moon once, on Apollo 8, and watched it slide by beneath him without ever getting to land. Apollo 13 was supposed to fix that. He was set to become the fifth human being to walk on the lunar surface, commanding a mission bound for the Fra Mauro highlands, a rugged region of interest to geologists because it was thought to hold material blasted up from deep inside the Moon by an ancient impact. Two days after launch in the spring of 1970, none of that mattered anymore. The mission was no longer about getting to the Moon. It was about getting three men home alive.

## A Routine Third Landing

By April 1970, landing on the Moon had already happened once, spectacularly, with Apollo 11, and again with Apollo 12. The public's attention had begun to drift, the way it does once a genuine marvel starts to look repeatable. Apollo 13's crew — Lovell in command, Jack Swigert as command module pilot, and Fred Haise as lunar module pilot — had trained for years for a mission that, from the outside, looked like it might not even make the evening news. Swigert was a late addition, brought in only days before launch after the original command module pilot was grounded over concern that he had been exposed to measles. It was, on paper, the most routine kind of extraordinary event: a third trip to the Moon, following a flight plan that by now NASA had largely worked out.

## The Bang

Two days into the flight, with the spacecraft well on its way and nothing eventful to report, ground controllers asked the crew to perform a routine stir of the oxygen tanks in the service module, a housekeeping task meant to keep the supercooled contents evenly mixed. Moments later the crew heard and felt a sharp bang, and warning lights lit up across the cabin. Damaged wiring inside one of the oxygen tanks, insulation stripped away years earlier during a ground test that nobody had recognized as a problem, shorted and ignited, and the tank ruptured. The explosion tore through part of the service module, venting oxygen into space and crippling the fuel cells that supplied the command module's electrical power. Swigert relayed word of the trouble to Mission Control, and within minutes it was clear this was not a routine malfunction. The command module, the only part of the spacecraft designed to survive reentry into Earth's atmosphere, was dying. Its remaining battery power had to be preserved for the only moment that would truly matter: the last few hours before splashdown.

## Improvising a Lifeboat

The lunar module, a spidery vehicle designed to ferry two astronauts down to the Moon's surface and back up to lunar orbit, had never been intended to keep three men alive for the better part of four days. It had been built for two men, for about two days, docked to a healthy command module the whole time. With the command module's systems being shut down to conserve its dwindling battery power for reentry, the crew moved into the lunar module and turned it into precisely the improvised lifeboat it was never designed to be. Its own limited supplies of oxygen, water, and electrical power now had to stretch across a return trip that would take days rather than hours, sustaining a crew fifty percent larger than planned.

Trajectory presented its own puzzle. The spacecraft had already been redirected onto what is called a free-return trajectory, a path shaped so that the Moon's own gravity would swing it back toward Earth without requiring a large engine burn, a precaution built into the original flight plan in case something exactly like this happened. Mission controllers and the crew used the lunar module's descent engine, never intended for this purpose, to speed up the return once the spacecraft had looped around the far side of the Moon, trimming what would otherwise have been a much longer trip home.

## The Carbon Dioxide Problem

Keeping the crew breathing raised a problem nobody had anticipated. The lunar module's air scrubbers, which pulled carbon dioxide out of the cabin atmosphere, used canisters shaped to fit its own systems. The command module carried plenty of spare canisters, but they were a different shape entirely, built for a different socket, and would not fit the lunar module's receptacles. As carbon dioxide levels crept upward inside the cramped cabin, engineers on the ground worked out a way to mate the square command module canisters to the round lunar module fittings using materials the astronauts actually had on board: cardboard, plastic bags, and tape. Talked through the procedure by radio, the crew built the ungainly assembly, nicknamed for its resemblance to a household mailbox, and it worked well enough to keep the air breathable for the rest of the trip.

## The Cold Ride Home

With most systems powered down to save every possible watt, the interior of the spacecraft grew bitterly cold, water condensed on the walls and instrument panels, and the crew had little to do but wait, conserve, and periodically execute the small course corrections needed to keep the trajectory on target. Sleep was difficult, food and water were rationed, and Haise developed a painful kidney infection along the way. As the spacecraft neared Earth, the crew powered the command module back up using a procedure improvised almost entirely during the flight itself, since it had never been designed to be shut down and restarted in space like this. They jettisoned the damaged service module first and, in the light of the spacecraft's windows, got their first look at the harm the explosion had done: an entire panel had been blown off its side, exposing torn wiring and structure. Then, just before reentry, they cast off the lunar module that had kept them alive, a craft that had done everything asked of it and more, left to burn up harmlessly in the atmosphere.

## Splashdown

The command module, Odyssey, reentered the atmosphere and splashed down safely in the South Pacific, where a Navy recovery ship was waiting. All three astronauts came home. The mission that had been meant to put a fourth pair of American bootprints on the Moon instead became a demonstration of something else: how much could be improvised, under pressure, at a distance of hundreds of thousands of kilometers, using little more than radio contact, spare parts, and a lot of very fast thinking on the ground. Apollo 13 never landed. It is remembered anyway, not for what it failed to reach, but for what it managed to survive.
