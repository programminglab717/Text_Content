# A Day in the Life Aboard the Space Station

An astronaut aboard the space station watches the sun rise and set roughly sixteen times over the course of what everyone on board still calls a single day. The station circles Earth at a speed that carries it all the way around the planet in about ninety minutes, which means dawn breaks over the Pacific, arcs across a continent, and gives way to night again before most people on the ground would even be through with lunch. None of that changes the crew's actual schedule. They live on a fixed clock, synchronized to a single time zone shared by mission control centers around the world, because a human body still needs one sunrise and one sunset to organize itself around, even in a place delivering sixteen of each.

## Sixteen Sunrises a Day

The rapid cycling of light and dark is one of the first things new crew members mention finding disorienting, and one of the first problems station design had to solve. Windows have shades for a reason beyond privacy: without them, sunrise every ninety minutes would make anything resembling normal sleep impossible. Circadian rhythms evolved over billions of years around a twenty-four-hour cycle of light and dark, and nothing about traveling to orbit changes what a human body expects. The station's interior lighting is used deliberately to reinforce the crew's assigned schedule, brighter and cooler during the working day, dimmer and warmer in the hours before scheduled sleep, an attempt to give the body the cues it would normally get from an actual sunset that its own orbit destroys sixteen times over.

## Waking Up in a Sleeping Bag Strapped to the Wall

Each crew member has a small personal cabin, not much larger than a closet, just big enough for a sleeping bag tethered to the wall, a laptop, and a few personal items. The tethering matters because without it, a sleeping astronaut would simply drift into the middle of the cabin and bump gently into whatever happened to be floating nearby. Some crew members describe sleeping in microgravity as surprisingly restful once they adjust, since there is no mattress pressing back against a shoulder or hip; others take longer to adjust to the absence of any sensation of lying down at all. On waking, there is no getting out of bed in the usual sense, only unzipping the bag and pushing off toward the module where the day's first tasks are waiting. Showers do not exist on the station in any form resembling the ones on Earth; water does not fall in microgravity, it clings and drifts, so hygiene relies on rinseless soap, wet wipes, and a great deal of practice keeping water where it is supposed to be.

## Breakfast Without a Refrigerator

Food aboard the station is engineered around the same constraint that governs everything else: nothing can be allowed to float away, spill, or create crumbs that drift into equipment. Most meals are dehydrated, thermostabilized, or otherwise shelf-stable, rehydrated with hot or cold water from a dispenser and eaten straight from the package with a spoon, often held to a tray by magnets or velcro so it does not wander off mid-bite. Bread is generally avoided in favor of tortillas, since bread produces crumbs that float and can find their way into sensitive equipment or an astronaut's eye. Fresh fruit and vegetables do make it aboard periodically, carried up on resupply spacecraft, and tend to be eaten quickly and appreciated disproportionately to their size, since there is no persistent cold storage for keeping produce fresh over the long term. Coffee is real coffee, brewed and sipped from a specially designed pouch, because after decades of spaceflight nobody found a good enough reason to make astronauts give it up.

## The Exercise You Cannot Skip

Bones and muscles evolved under the constant load of gravity, and without that load they atrophy, sometimes surprisingly quickly. An astronaut who spent months in orbit doing nothing physical would return to Earth with measurably weaker bones and muscle, a real medical concern rather than a minor inconvenience, since severe enough loss could affect mobility, fracture risk, and cardiovascular health well after the mission ends. The countermeasure is a strict daily exercise regimen, typically around two hours, split between a treadmill fitted with a harness system to hold the runner down against the belt, a stationary bicycle, and a resistance device that uses vacuum cylinders to simulate the feeling of lifting weights in an environment where nothing actually weighs anything. Skipping this routine is not really an option; it is treated as core mission work, scheduled into the day with the same seriousness as any scientific experiment, because in a very real sense the crew's own bodies are among the most important pieces of hardware the mission depends on.

## Working the Job List

Between exercise and sleep, the working day is dense with tasks laid out in a schedule built and adjusted by mission control, itemized to the half hour. Much of it is scientific experiment work: tending biological samples, monitoring materials science experiments that depend on the absence of gravity-driven convection, operating equipment built by research teams on the ground who will never see their experiment in person. A significant portion of any week goes to maintenance, since a station built from modules launched over more than a decade is constantly in need of filters changed, cables rerouted, and equipment repaired, often improvised with tools and spare parts already on hand rather than waiting for a part that might not arrive for months. Crew members also serve as the hands and eyes for researchers on the ground during video-linked sessions, and set aside time for public engagement, educational calls with students, and press interviews, since part of the job has always been explaining what the work is actually for. Weekends are lighter, generally reserved for personal time, contact with family through scheduled video calls, and the simple activity, favored by nearly every astronaut who has ever described it, of looking out the window at the planet going by.

## Where the Water Comes From

Little aboard the station is wasted, and water is treated as the most precious resource of all, since hauling it up from Earth is expensive and resupply flights are infrequent. A dedicated recovery system captures moisture from the cabin air, most of it exhaled breath and sweat from the crew, condenses it, and processes it alongside reclaimed water from the station's toilet, filtering and chemically treating the combined stream until it meets standards for drinking water, often described as cleaner than what comes out of a municipal tap on Earth. The toilet itself works nothing like one on the ground, relying on carefully directed airflow rather than gravity to move waste away from the user and into the collection system, a design that took considerable engineering effort to get right, since anything that goes even slightly wrong in microgravity tends to drift rather than fall. Crew members joke about drinking yesterday's coffee in a more literal sense than intended, but the system has run reliably for years, closing a loop that has to work perfectly in an environment where there is no plumber to call and no municipal backup supply.

## Falling Asleep to the Sound of Fans

The station is never quiet. Fans run constantly, because in microgravity warm exhaled air does not rise away from a sleeping astronaut's face the way it would on Earth, and without forced air circulation carbon dioxide can pool disconcertingly close to where someone is breathing. That steady mechanical hum becomes part of the background of life on board, something many astronauts say they miss, oddly, once they return to the stillness of a bedroom on Earth. At the end of the assigned day, with experiments logged, exercise completed, and the module lights dimmed toward the evening setting, the crew zips back into their sleeping bags for another eight hours of scheduled rest, whether or not the sun happens to be up outside, since by then it almost certainly has risen and set several times already, and will do so several times more before anyone on board wakes up again.
