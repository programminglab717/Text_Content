# The Ice Giants: Uranus and Neptune

Uranus and Neptune get filed together in most descriptions of the solar system, two pale, distant, similarly sized planets sharing a single tidy category: ice giants, as distinct from the larger gas giants Jupiter and Saturn. The label is useful and basically accurate, but it flattens two planets that turn out, on closer inspection, to disagree with each other on some fairly fundamental points, from how they came to be discovered to how their interiors actually behave.

## A Different Kind of Giant Planet

Jupiter and Saturn are built overwhelmingly from hydrogen and helium, the same two elements that dominate the sun, wrapped around comparatively modest rocky cores. Uranus and Neptune are different: while they still carry substantial hydrogen and helium atmospheres, a much larger share of their total mass is made up of heavier volatile compounds, chiefly water, ammonia, and methane, referred to by planetary scientists as ices even though, under the crushing pressure and heat found deep inside these planets, that material behaves less like the ice in a freezer and more like a dense, hot, electrically conductive fluid, possibly including exotic phases such as superionic water that do not occur naturally anywhere on Earth. Both planets are close to four times Earth's diameter, distinctly smaller than Jupiter or Saturn, and similar enough to each other in bulk properties that distinguishing them requires more than a glance.

## Two Very Different Discoveries

Uranus entered the astronomical record almost by accident. William Herschel, conducting a systematic survey of the night sky from his own backyard observatory in 1781, noticed an object that did not look quite like a star and initially assumed he had found a comet. Only after tracking its motion and finding a nearly circular orbit, along with a small but resolvable disk under higher magnification, did it become clear he had found an entirely new planet, the first discovered with a telescope rather than known since antiquity, and an object that at a stroke roughly doubled the known size of the solar system.

Neptune's discovery worked in nearly the opposite direction, starting from mathematics rather than observation. Astronomers tracking Uranus's orbit through the decades after its discovery noticed it consistently drifted slightly from where gravitational calculations, accounting for the pull of all the other known planets, said it should be. Urbain Le Verrier in France, working independently of John Couch Adams in England who reached similar conclusions, calculated the mass and position an unseen additional planet would need to have to produce exactly that deviation. Johann Galle, at the Berlin Observatory, pointed his telescope at the predicted patch of sky in 1846 and found Neptune within a small fraction of a degree of where the calculations placed it, a result often cited as one of the great triumphs of Newtonian mechanics: a planet found on paper before it was ever seen.

## One Planet Tipped Over

Uranus's most distinctive feature is not its composition but its orientation. Its axis of rotation is tilted roughly ninety-eight degrees from the plane of its orbit, meaning the planet essentially rolls around the sun on its side rather than spinning upright the way most of the other planets do. Over its eighty-four-year orbit, this produces seasons unlike anywhere else in the solar system: each pole spends a long stretch of decades pointed nearly straight at the sun in continuous daylight, followed by an equally long stretch of continuous darkness. The leading explanation is that an enormous impact, early in Uranus's history, knocked the planet onto its side, though the exact nature and timing of that collision remains a subject of ongoing modeling and debate. Notably, Uranus's ring system and its family of moons orbit in the same tilted plane as the planet's tipped equator rather than in the original orbital plane, suggesting whatever knocked Uranus over did so before its current set of moons and rings settled into place, or else the whole system reorganized itself around the new tilt afterward.

Neptune, despite its similarity in size and bulk composition, has a far more modest axial tilt, closer to Earth's own, giving it a more conventional seasonal pattern, if seasons that each last for decades can be called conventional, a consequence simply of Neptune's orbit taking about a hundred and sixty-five Earth years to complete.

## Weather on Two Quiet-Looking Worlds

Given how little sunlight reaches either planet this far from the sun, both might be expected to have placid, largely featureless atmospheres. Neptune defies that expectation dramatically. It hosts the fastest winds ever directly measured on any planet in the solar system, and when Voyager 2 flew past in 1989, it photographed a large, dark storm system in Neptune's atmosphere, promptly nicknamed the Great Dark Spot. Unlike Jupiter's centuries-old Great Red Spot, Neptune's storm proved to be short-lived: later observations by the Hubble Space Telescope found it had vanished within just a few years, replaced eventually by different dark spots elsewhere in the atmosphere. Neptune also radiates noticeably more heat outward than it receives from the sun, similar to Jupiter and Saturn, evidence of ongoing internal heat production or leftover formation heat still working its way out.

Uranus, oddly, does not show this same clear excess of internal heat, or shows only a slight one, a genuine anomaly among the giant planets that has no fully satisfying explanation, though the same ancient impact invoked to explain its tilt is sometimes proposed as a factor, perhaps having disrupted the planet's internal heat flow along with its orientation. Consistent with its more sluggish internal engine, Uranus generally shows a calmer, more featureless atmosphere than Neptune, though it is not entirely inert, and ground and space-based telescopes have caught it producing distinct storms of its own, particularly around the time its equator, rather than a pole, faces the sun most directly.

## Rings and an Unusual Moon

Both planets carry ring systems, though nothing resembling the brightness or scale of Saturn's. Uranus's rings were found first, and by an indirect method: astronomers in 1977 watched a distant star's light blink out several times in quick succession, both before and after Uranus itself passed in front of it, revealing a set of narrow, dark rings that no direct image had previously detected. Hints of similarly partial ring material around Neptune, seen as incomplete arcs in ground-based observations, were fully confirmed as complete, if very faint and clumpy, rings when Voyager 2 flew past in 1989, the only spacecraft ever to visit either ice giant.

Neptune's large moon Triton adds one more point of contrast. Triton orbits Neptune backward, opposite to the direction Neptune itself rotates, a strong indication that Triton did not form alongside Neptune but was instead captured later, quite possibly a former resident of the distant Kuiper Belt not unlike Pluto in composition and origin. Despite the deep cold expected of a captured world so far from the sun, Voyager 2 found Triton to be geologically active, its cameras catching active geysers venting nitrogen gas and dark material into the thin atmosphere, evidence of ongoing internal processes on a moon that, by simple distance from the sun, might have been expected to be entirely frozen and inert. Uranus's own moons, by contrast, carry an unusual naming tradition all their own, drawn from the works of Shakespeare and Alexander Pope rather than classical mythology, the only major moons in the solar system named that way.

Both planets have been visited exactly once, briefly, by the same spacecraft passing through decades apart on its way out of the solar system. Nearly everything known about either world in detail traces back to that single flyby each, which is a thin basis for two planets this different from each other, and from everything else nearby.
