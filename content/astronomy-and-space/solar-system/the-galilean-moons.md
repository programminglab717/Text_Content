# The Galilean Moons

In January 1610, looking through a telescope he had built himself, Galileo Galilei noticed four small points of light strung out in a line on either side of Jupiter. Over the following nights he watched them shift position relative to the planet and to each other, and he concluded, correctly, that he was watching four bodies in orbit around Jupiter rather than four fixed background stars. It was a quietly explosive observation: a working example of celestial bodies that plainly did not orbit Earth, at a moment when the Earth-centered model of the universe was still the default assumption. The four moons were later named Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto, after figures from Greek myth associated with Zeus, and moving outward from Jupiter through that same order turns out to be a tour through four almost entirely different worlds.

## Io: The Volcanic One

Io, the innermost of the four, is the most volcanically active body known anywhere in the solar system, more active than Earth by a wide margin despite being a fraction of Earth's size. The reason has nothing to do with leftover heat from formation and everything to do with its neighbors. Io, Europa, and Ganymede move in what is called a Laplace resonance: for every single orbit Ganymede completes, Europa completes almost exactly two, and Io completes almost exactly four. That precise relationship means the three moons periodically line up in the same configurations relative to Jupiter, and their mutual gravitational tugging keeps Io's orbit slightly stretched into an ellipse rather than letting Jupiter's own tidal pull round it into a perfect circle, which is what would otherwise happen fairly quickly.

An elliptical orbit around a planet as massive as Jupiter means Io is squeezed and released, squeezed and released, once per orbit, as the strength of Jupiter's tidal pull varies with distance. That continuous flexing generates friction deep inside the moon, and the friction generates heat, on a scale large enough to keep a substantial portion of Io's interior molten. The surface shows the results directly: hundreds of active volcanic vents, plumes of sulfur and sulfur dioxide gas blasting high above the surface, and lava flows that resurface the moon fast enough that Io has essentially no impact craters left to see, unlike almost every other solid body in the solar system. Its surface is mottled yellow, orange, black, and red, colored by sulfur and its compounds in various states.

## Europa: The Hidden Ocean

Just outside Io orbits Europa, smaller than Earth's Moon and, on the surface, almost the opposite of its neighbor: a smooth shell of water ice, among the smoothest surfaces of any solid body examined, marked mainly by long, straight-edged cracks and ridges rather than craters or mountains. Europa is part of the same orbital resonance as Io, and it experiences the same kind of tidal flexing, though less violently, since it orbits farther from Jupiter. That gentler heating appears to be enough, combined with the insulating effect of a thick ice shell, to maintain a global ocean of liquid salt water underneath the ice, a conclusion supported by subtle variations in Europa's magnetic environment consistent with an electrically conductive fluid layer, along with the moon's overall shape and gravity field. Estimates of that ocean's depth and extent suggest it very likely holds more liquid water than exists in all of Earth's oceans combined, sealed beneath a frozen crust of uncertain but probably considerable thickness.

That combination, a liquid water ocean, plausible contact between that water and a rocky seafloor capable of supplying minerals, and a heat source that does not depend on sunlight, makes Europa one of the most closely watched targets in the search for life elsewhere in the solar system, not because life has been found there, but because the ordinary requirements scientists look for happen to overlap so well.

## Ganymede: The Largest Moon in the Solar System

Ganymede, next outward, is the largest moon orbiting any planet, bigger even than the planet Mercury, though it is considerably less massive than Mercury because so much of its bulk is water ice rather than rock and metal. Ganymede holds a distinction shared by no other moon: it generates its own internal magnetic field, produced by convection in a liquid iron-rich core, entirely separate from the powerful magnetic field of Jupiter that surrounds it. That internal field was inferred from spacecraft measurements and later confirmed through observations of a distinctive pair of auroral belts glowing around Ganymede's poles, a signature that only a body with its own magnetic field would produce. Ganymede's surface itself is split between very old, dark, heavily cratered terrain and younger, brighter regions crossed with parallel grooves and ridges, evidence of a period of internal tectonic activity that reshaped part of the moon long after it first formed. Like Europa, Ganymede shows indirect evidence of a subsurface ocean, in its case sandwiched between layers of ice at different depths within a thick, complex interior.

## Callisto: The Quiet One

Callisto, the outermost of the four, sits beyond the main orbital resonance and so escapes the tidal flexing that heats its three neighbors. It shows the consequences plainly: a heavily and evenly cratered surface that appears to have gone essentially unchanged for most of the solar system's history, among the oldest, most crater-saturated landscapes known anywhere. Nothing about Callisto looks geologically active, which makes it something of a puzzle that it too shows hints, from subtle magnetic measurements, of a possible salty layer of liquid water somewhere within it. Without meaningful tidal heating, what would keep such a layer from freezing solid is unclear; leftover heat from formation, a small amount of radioactive decay in a rocky interior, or the antifreeze effect of dissolved salts and other compounds have all been proposed, and the question remains open.

## A Small Solar System of Its Own

Seen together, the four Galilean moons form a sequence that runs from a volcanic inferno to an ancient, frozen relic in the span of a few planetary diameters, with an ice-covered ocean and a magnetically active giant moon in between. The same tidal forces driven by their mutual resonance that make Io nearly uninhabitable are very likely what make Europa's ocean possible at all, a reminder that a moon's prospects depend as much on its gravitational neighborhood as on its distance from the sun. Galileo saw none of this detail through his own telescope, only four moving points of light. What he understood immediately, though, was enough: whatever those four lights were doing, they were not doing it around Earth.
