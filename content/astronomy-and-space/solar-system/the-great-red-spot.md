# The Great Red Spot

Jupiter's most famous feature is a storm large enough that, at its historical peak, it could have swallowed three planets the size of Earth side by side. It has no coastline to make landfall on, no season in which it is expected to dissipate, and by any reasonable definition it is far older than any hurricane ever tracked on our own planet. Astronomers have watched it, in one form or another, for the better part of two centuries, and it is still turning.

## An Old Storm, Maybe Older Than That

Observers in the seventeenth century, including Giovanni Cassini, recorded a persistent dark marking on Jupiter that they tracked across many nights, and some later writers have been tempted to call this the first sighting of the Great Red Spot. Whether that early spot is truly the same feature still swirling today, or a different storm that happened to occupy a similar latitude before fading away, is not something historians of astronomy can settle with confidence, since record-keeping lapsed for long stretches in between. What is not in doubt is that continuous, unbroken tracking of the storm now called the Great Red Spot goes back to observations made in the middle of the nineteenth century, which makes it, at an absolute minimum, one of the longest-lived weather systems ever documented anywhere.

## What Kind of Storm It Is

The Great Red Spot is an anticyclone, a rotating high-pressure system, sitting in Jupiter's southern hemisphere and turning counterclockwise, consistent with how anticyclones behave in that hemisphere under the Coriolis effect. It is enormous by any standard: nineteenth-century observers estimated its width at a scale that would have comfortably fit three Earths across; more careful modern measurements find it has shrunk substantially since then, to the point where it can now just barely accommodate a single Earth, and the shrinkage appears to be continuing rather than having leveled off. Nobody fully understands why it is shrinking, or whether it will stabilize at some smaller size, fade into an ordinary oval, or vanish entirely the way several of Jupiter's other long-tracked storms have over the past century.

Its color is, if anything, less understood than its size. The storm's reddish-brown hue is almost certainly the product of chemical compounds dredged up from deeper in Jupiter's atmosphere by the storm's own powerful updrafts, exposed to ultraviolet light from the sun at the cloud tops, where photochemical reactions convert them into colored byproducts. Candidates for the specific compounds involved include various forms of sulfur and phosphorus, but there is no settled consensus on exactly which reactions are responsible, and the storm's color has itself varied in intensity over the decades of observation, brightening and dulling for reasons not fully explained.

## The Engine Beneath

Storms on Earth eventually run out of energy, in large part because they cross land, get cut off from the warm ocean water that fuels them, or run into other systems that disrupt their structure. Jupiter offers none of those brakes. It has no solid surface at all in any conventional sense, just atmosphere that thickens gradually with depth until pressure and temperature turn gas into something more exotic, so there is no coastline to weaken a storm through friction or to starve it of moisture. Jupiter also radiates more heat outward from its own interior than it receives from the sun, a residue of the heat generated when the planet gravitationally contracted during formation and, potentially, from helium slowly raining out of the deeper atmosphere even now. That internal heat drives vigorous convection and powers the planet's system of alternating jet streams, bands of wind blowing in opposite directions at different latitudes.

The Great Red Spot sits wedged between two of these jets, one blowing to its north and another to its south in the opposite direction, which act like a pair of gears confining the storm and continually feeding rotational energy into it from both sides. Smaller storms and turbulent eddies that wander too close are sometimes absorbed outright, adding their energy and material to the larger system, a process that may help explain how the Spot has persisted for so long even as it has slowly lost size.

## Visits From Spacecraft

Ground-based telescopes could track the storm's position and rough shape for well over a century, but understanding its structure required getting close. The Pioneer probes returned the first spacecraft images of it in the 1970s, and the two Voyager spacecraft, passing in 1979, captured enough detail to reveal the smaller eddies and filaments swirling within the larger storm, evidence of turbulent structure rather than a simple, smooth rotation. The Galileo spacecraft orbited Jupiter through the late 1990s and into the early 2000s, studying the planet's atmosphere and moons over an extended mission, though the atmospheric probe it dropped into Jupiter happened to descend into an unrelated, unusually dry and clear region rather than the Spot itself.

The most revealing recent data has come from the Juno spacecraft, which has flown directly over the storm at close range on several occasions since arriving at Jupiter. Juno's instruments, designed to sense microwave radiation from well beneath the visible cloud deck, found that the storm's roots extend far deeper than the shallow layer of water clouds long assumed to be the extent of Jupiter's active weather, reaching hundreds of kilometers down into the planet's atmosphere. The Great Red Spot, in other words, is not a thin skin of colored cloud riding on top of the planet. It is a structure with real depth, anchored well below anything sunlight ever reaches.

## A Storm That May Not Be Permanent

Jupiter has produced and lost comparable storms before. In the closing years of the twentieth century, three long-lived white oval storms that had been tracked for decades merged together into a single larger oval, which in the years that followed gradually reddened into something resembling a smaller cousin of the Great Red Spot, nicknamed Red Spot Junior by observers. That storm has had its own volatile history since, at times appearing to weaken and lose its color entirely before recovering it. Set against that record, the senior Spot's long-term shrinkage looks less like an anomaly and more like the ordinary fate of Jovian storms playing out unusually slowly, on a planet patient enough, and violent enough, to keep one weather system spinning for longer than entire human institutions have existed to watch it.
