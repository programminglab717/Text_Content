# The Sun's Interior and Magnetic Cycle

Step from shade into direct sunlight and the warmth seems to arrive instantly, a switch flipped. It hasn't. The photons landing on your skin left the sun's visible surface a little over eight minutes ago, which is the delay most people have heard about. Less appreciated is that the energy carried by those photons began its journey far earlier and much deeper down, in the sun's core, where conditions are strange enough that light itself is almost the wrong way to think about how energy moves.

## A Furnace Built From Weight

The sun's core occupies only about a quarter of its radius, but the sheer weight of everything stacked above it — layer upon layer of plasma reaching out to the visible surface and beyond — compresses that small volume to a density greater than lead and a temperature of around fifteen million degrees. At those conditions, hydrogen nuclei are moving fast enough and packed close enough that quantum tunneling lets some of them fuse despite their mutual electrical repulsion. Four hydrogen nuclei, through a sequence of steps called the proton-proton chain, end up as one helium-4 nucleus, and the resulting nucleus weighs very slightly less than the four particles that made it. That missing mass is not lost; it becomes energy, in the proportions Einstein's equation describes, and the sun performs this transaction on an almost incomprehensible number of nuclei every second.

Fusion happens almost exclusively in the core because the reaction rate depends so steeply on temperature and pressure that it effectively switches off a short distance further out. The layers surrounding the core are not generating energy themselves; they are simply in the business of moving the core's output outward, and they do it astonishingly slowly.

## A Long Walk Outward

Photons produced by fusion do not travel in a straight line to the surface. The plasma in the layer immediately surrounding the core, called the radiative zone, is so dense that a photon travels only a short distance before being absorbed by a nucleus or electron and re-emitted in a random new direction. It is then absorbed again a moment later, and re-emitted again, in another random direction, over and over. This drunkard's walk means that despite moving at the speed of light between absorptions, a unit of energy can take tens of thousands of years — some estimates run well past a hundred thousand — to random-walk its way out of the radiative zone alone. The sunlight falling on your face this afternoon carries energy that started that furious cross-country journey long before agriculture existed.

Closer to the surface, in the outer third or so of the sun's radius, the plasma cools enough that it becomes opaque to radiation in a different way, and the physics of energy transport changes entirely. Instead of photons diffusing outward, the plasma itself starts to move, in the same overturning motion visible in a pot of water heated from below. Hot plasma rises in columns, releases its heat near the top, cools, and sinks back down to be reheated. This convective zone is why the visible sun looks grainy under high magnification: each bright cell, a few thousand kilometers across, is the top of one rising column of plasma, and the surrounding darker lanes are where cooled material is sinking back down. Individual granules last only minutes before being replaced, a visible signature of a churn that never stops.

## The Skin We Can See

What we call the sun's surface, the photosphere, is not a solid boundary but the depth at which the plasma finally becomes transparent enough for photons to escape into space without being reabsorbed. Above it lies the chromosphere, a thin, turbulent layer that flashes briefly into view as a reddish rim during a total solar eclipse, and above that the corona, a tenuous outer atmosphere that extends for millions of kilometers and is, bizarrely, far hotter than the surface beneath it. The photosphere sits at roughly five and a half thousand degrees; the corona runs to a few million. Why the atmosphere gets dramatically hotter as you move away from the heat source remains an active puzzle, with magnetic waves and small, constant flaring across the corona's tangled field lines among the leading suspects.

Sunspots are the most visible sign that something other than simple heat is at work on the sun. They appear dark only by contrast, since they are still extremely hot, but they are cooler than the surrounding photosphere by more than a thousand degrees because intense bundles of magnetic field there suppress the convection that would otherwise carry heat up from below. Sunspots almost always appear in pairs or clusters of opposite magnetic polarity, like the two poles of a bar magnet poking through the surface.

## The Magnetic Heartbeat

The sun does not rotate as a rigid body. Its equator completes a rotation in about twenty-five days, while regions near the poles take closer to thirty-five, a phenomenon called differential rotation. Combined with the churning of the convective zone and the Coriolis effect from the sun's own spin, this uneven rotation stretches, twists, and winds up the sun's internal magnetic field lines, in a process astrophysicists describe using dynamo theory, a framework substantially developed by the physicist Eugene Parker. Field lines dragged around faster at the equator than at the poles get wound tighter over time, like a rubber band twisted around an axle, until they buckle and erupt through the surface as loops, dragging plasma with them and marking their footpoints with sunspot pairs.

This winding and release does not happen at a constant rate. Sunspot activity waxes and wanes on a cycle that averages about eleven years, climbing from a quiet minimum, when the disk may show no spots at all for days at a stretch, to a maximum crowded with active regions, then subsiding again. Astronomers have tracked this rhythm since the nineteenth century, when researchers including Rudolf Wolf established a standardized way of counting sunspots that let cycles be compared across decades. Plot the latitude of new sunspots against time across a cycle and the pattern forms two wing-shaped bands, spots emerging at higher latitudes early in the cycle and drifting toward the equator as it matures, a pattern first documented by the astronomer Walter Maunder and still called the butterfly diagram.

Each eleven-year cycle also carries a hidden reversal. The magnetic polarity of sunspot pairs — which pole leads in the northern hemisphere versus the southern — flips from one cycle to the next, so the sun's large-scale magnetic field only returns to its original configuration after two sunspot cycles, a full magnetic cycle of roughly twenty-two years. This connection between sunspots and magnetism was established by George Ellery Hale in the early twentieth century, and the twenty-two-year period is sometimes called the Hale cycle in his honor.

## When the Sun Reaches Outward

None of this stays contained near the surface. The corona is so hot that the sun's gravity cannot hold onto all of it, and a continuous stream of charged particles, the solar wind, flows outward through the solar system, inflating a vast magnetic bubble called the heliosphere that extends well past the orbit of Pluto. Superimposed on that steady wind are more violent events tied directly to the magnetic cycle. Solar flares are sudden, intense bursts of radiation released when tangled magnetic field lines near an active sunspot region snap and reconnect into a simpler configuration, releasing their stored energy in minutes. Coronal mass ejections are larger and slower: enormous bubbles of plasma and magnetic field hurled outward from the corona, taking a day or more to cross the distance to Earth.

When a coronal mass ejection strikes Earth's magnetic field, it can compress and disturb it enough to drive currents through the upper atmosphere and, on the ground, through long conductors like power lines and pipelines — the mechanism behind the geomagnetic disturbances that in the most extreme historical cases, like the Carrington event of 1859, set telegraph equipment sparking. The same interaction that dumps energy into the atmosphere lights it up as aurora, charged particles funneled along magnetic field lines toward the poles, colliding with oxygen and nitrogen and exciting them to glow. Solar activity of this kind tracks the same eleven-year rhythm as the sunspots that announce it, rising and falling with a cycle that began, from the sun's point of view, in the crush and heat of the core, tens of thousands of years before the light it eventually produced ever reached your face.
