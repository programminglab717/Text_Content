# The Water History of Mars

From orbit, a fan of pale sediment spreads across the floor of Jezero Crater, spilling through a gap in the crater wall like a delta easing into a bay. It is a delta, in fact, or was one, sometime in the deep past: a place where a river carrying sand and mud slowed as it entered standing water and dropped its load in the same layered, branching pattern that any river delta on Earth produces. Nothing has flowed there in a very long time. Mars today is desert in a sense no Earth desert can match, a world with less water vapor in its entire atmosphere than sits over a moderately dry patch of the Sahara. Yet its surface is covered in the fingerprints of water that once moved, pooled, and shaped rock, and reconstructing when and how that water disappeared has become one of the central projects of Mars exploration.

## Reading a Landscape Like a Text

The oldest terrain on Mars, dated by the density of impact craters scarring it, preserves branching networks of narrow valleys that look, from orbit, remarkably like the drainage patterns carved by rainfall and runoff on Earth. These valley networks are concentrated in Mars's most ancient highlands and are best explained by a climate, early in the planet's history, warm and thick enough to support at least episodic rain or snowmelt feeding surface streams. Younger terrain tells a different story. Rather than delicate branching valleys, it shows enormous outflow channels, some large enough to swallow the biggest rivers on Earth many times over, carved not by gentle persistent flow but by sudden catastrophic floods, most likely triggered when subsurface ice or pressurized groundwater broke through the surface all at once, perhaps released by an impact or volcanic activity fracturing the ground above an aquifer. Between the delicate old valleys and the violent later floods lies a transition from a Mars with a working, if modest, water cycle to a Mars where liquid water existed mainly underground, occasionally erupting rather than routinely flowing.

Deltas like the one at Jezero, along with related lake-bed deposits found in other craters, sit chronologically in between, evidence of standing bodies of water persisting long enough, in at least some basins, to build up the same kind of layered sediment fans a lake produces on Earth over a period of years. Rovers sent specifically to examine such sites have found rock textures consistent with quiet sedimentary settling rather than the violence of a flash flood, meaning at least some Martian lakes were placid enough, for some stretch of time, to have supported a stable shoreline.

## Minerals That Needed Water

Rock chemistry offers a second, independent line of evidence, and it tracks a similar arc from wetter to drier. Instruments aboard orbiting spacecraft, using spectrometers tuned to detect the fingerprints of specific minerals in reflected sunlight, have mapped the oldest Martian terrain and found it rich in clay minerals, compounds that only form where rock is exposed to liquid water for extended periods under roughly neutral chemical conditions. Younger rock layers, by contrast, are dominated by sulfate minerals, which tend to form from water that is more acidic and more transient, evaporating and leaving mineral crusts behind rather than sitting in a stable lake or aquifer indefinitely. Rovers exploring these younger sulfate-rich layers have also found small spherical mineral concretions, nicknamed blueberries where first identified, made of iron oxide in a form that itself requires liquid water moving through porous rock to form. Read together, the mineral record and the landscape record tell the same story from two different angles: a Mars that started with widespread, relatively gentle, long-lived water, and moved toward a drier, harsher, more sporadic relationship with liquid water before losing it at the surface almost entirely.

## Where the Water and the Air Went

Explaining that transition requires looking at Mars's interior as much as its surface. Mars is considerably smaller than Earth, which means it cooled faster after formation, and evidence embedded in its oldest crust, in the form of a patchwork of local magnetism, suggests Mars once had a global magnetic field generated by a churning molten core, a field that had already shut down before much of the planet's visible surface finished forming. Earth's magnetic field does something easy to take for granted: it deflects the solar wind, the constant stream of charged particles flowing outward from the sun, around the planet rather than letting it strike the atmosphere directly. Without that shield, the solar wind can interact directly with a planet's upper atmosphere, and over very long timescales it strips gas away into space. Spacecraft built specifically to measure this process at Mars have confirmed that atmospheric loss of exactly this kind continues today, a slow bleed that has presumably been going on since not long after the dynamo shut off.

As the atmosphere thinned, its pressure at the surface dropped, and pressure turns out to matter enormously for water. Below a certain atmospheric pressure, liquid water cannot exist stably on a planet's surface at all; it either freezes solid or boils away as vapor, skipping the liquid phase depending on temperature, the same physical rule that makes water boil at a lower temperature on a high mountain on Earth taken to its logical extreme. As Martian air pressure fell, the range of conditions under which liquid water could persist on the surface shrank and eventually closed, forcing whatever water remained into ice, underground reservoirs, or the atmosphere, where much of it likely met a fate similar to what happened at Venus: split apart by solar ultraviolet light, with the lightest fragment, hydrogen, escaping to space for good.

## What Remains

Mars did not lose all its water. Both polar regions carry substantial caps of water ice, buried for part of the year under a thinner seasonal frost of frozen carbon dioxide that comes and goes with the Martian seasons. Ground-penetrating radar carried by orbiting spacecraft has found what appear to be debris-covered glaciers preserved at moderate latitudes, insulated from sublimation by a protective layer of rock and dust on top. A lander that touched down on Mars's arctic plains found water ice just beneath the surface soil, confirmed when its robotic arm scraped away a thin layer of dirt and exposed a hard, bright layer beneath. Even features once proposed as evidence of present-day flowing brine on crater walls and slopes, while still debated, show that the question of exactly how much mobile water Mars retains, in exactly what form, has not been fully settled.

## An Ocean That May Have Been

Some of the boldest reconstructions of early Mars go further than isolated lakes and propose a genuine ocean, covering a large fraction of the planet's northern hemisphere during at least part of its early history. The idea starts from simple topography: Mars's northern lowlands sit dramatically lower and smoother than the heavily cratered southern highlands, a hemispheric divide with no fully settled explanation of its own, and a basin that low and that broad is exactly the kind of place a planet-scale body of water would have pooled had enough of it existed. Some researchers have pointed to faint features ringing the lowlands at roughly consistent elevations as possible ancient shorelines, and have noted that several of the largest outflow channels on Mars, carved by catastrophic floods, run downhill directly into this same basin, as if draining toward a sea that was there to receive them.

The hypothesis remains genuinely contested. Proposed shoreline features do not sit at a perfectly consistent elevation all the way around the basin the way a true ancient coastline should, a discrepancy that could reflect billions of years of subsequent warping of the Martian crust, or could just as easily mean the features being measured were never shorelines in the first place, but something produced by entirely different processes such as volcanism or wind. Without a lander returning samples from deep within the lowlands themselves, the question of whether Mars once had a true ocean, rather than scattered regional lakes and periodic floods, remains open.

## Why the Record Matters

The mineral record's turning point, from long-lived neutral water to short-lived acidic water, matters beyond geology, because it marks roughly the window during which Mars would have offered the kind of stable, chemically hospitable environment that life, if it ever arose there, would have needed. Lake sediments like those at Jezero are exactly the setting where such evidence, if it exists, would be best preserved: fine-grained mud settling slowly to a lakebed can entomb organic material and hold onto subtle chemical signatures far better than rock exposed to billions of years of surface radiation. That is why recent rover missions have drilled and cached rock cores from precisely these deposits, banking on a future mission to carry them back to laboratories on Earth. The delta at Jezero has waited billions of years already. It can wait a while longer for someone to come and read what it has to say.
