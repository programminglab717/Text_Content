# The Pythagorean Theorem and Its Many Proofs

Take a rope, tie twelve evenly spaced knots into it to make twelve equal segments, and pull it taut into a triangle with sides of three, four, and five knot-lengths. The corner between the sides of three and four will be a perfect right angle. Ancient surveyors and builders relied on exactly this trick, long before anyone had written down a general theory of why it worked, to lay out square corners for fields, foundations, and temples. The relationship they were exploiting — that three squared plus four squared equals five squared — is a single case of the most famous theorem in elementary mathematics, and the fact that it can be discovered by hand, with a rope, before it is ever proved on paper, says something about why it has attracted more distinct proofs than perhaps any other result in the subject.

The theorem itself needs no more than a sentence: in a right triangle, the square built on the hypotenuse has exactly the same area as the two squares built on the other two sides added together. It is named for Pythagoras, a Greek philosopher and mathematician of the sixth century BCE, though the underlying relationship was almost certainly known, in the form of specific numerical examples like the 3-4-5 triangle, to Babylonian and Egyptian mathematicians well before him. What later Greek mathematics contributed, and what earns the theorem its lasting fame, was a general proof — an argument that the relationship holds for every right triangle, not just the ones you can measure by hand and check.

## The Dissection Proof

The most intuitive way to see why the theorem is true does not use algebra at all. It uses scissors, at least conceptually. Draw a right triangle, and on each of its three sides build a square, growing outward. The claim is that the area of the big square on the hypotenuse exactly equals the combined area of the two smaller squares on the legs. One elegant way to see this is to take four identical copies of the original right triangle and arrange them inside a single large square whose side length equals the hypotenuse, positioned so their right-angle corners point inward, leaving an empty square hole in the middle whose side length is the difference between the triangle's two legs. Now rearrange those same four triangles a different way, sliding two of them, so that instead of one square hole in the middle you get two separate square holes, one matching each leg of the original triangle. Because you never added or removed any triangle and never changed the size of the outer square, the empty area left over must be the same both times. In the first arrangement that empty area was one square, on the hypotenuse's difference; in the second it was two squares, matching the two legs. A little more care with exactly how the squares are built (using the hypotenuse itself, rather than the difference of the legs, as the relevant square) turns this into a fully rigorous proof, and versions of this dissection appear independently in ancient Chinese mathematical texts, where a diagram called the "hsuan-thu" lays out essentially this same rearrangement.

An old story, probably more legend than history but often repeated for good reason, has an Indian mathematician presenting a version of this proof with a diagram and the single word "Behold" — the picture doing all the persuading a formal argument would otherwise require in paragraphs.

## Euclid's Own Approach

Euclid's *Elements* proves the theorem differently, using an argument about areas of triangles and parallelograms rather than a direct cut-and-rearrange trick. The proof, which appears late in Book I as one of Euclid's more elaborate constructions, drops a perpendicular from the right angle down to the hypotenuse, splitting the big square on the hypotenuse into two rectangles. Euclid then shows, through a careful chain of triangle-area arguments, that one of these two rectangles has exactly the same area as the square on one leg, and the other rectangle matches the square on the other leg. Generations of geometry students nicknamed the accompanying diagram "the bride's chair" for its vaguely armchair-like silhouette, and generations of geometry students also found it one of the harder proofs in the *Elements* to hold in the mind all at once, since it requires tracking several overlapping figures simultaneously rather than a single clean rearrangement.

## Proof by Similar Triangles

A cleaner modern approach uses the same perpendicular dropped from the right angle to the hypotenuse, but reasons about it differently. That single perpendicular line splits the original right triangle into two smaller triangles, and it turns out both of these smaller triangles are similar to the original triangle and to each other — meaning they have the same three angles, just at different sizes. Similar triangles have sides in fixed proportion, and writing out those proportions for the two smaller triangles and combining them algebraically produces the Pythagorean relationship directly, without any need to think about squares or areas at all. This proof is shorter than either of the previous two, though it trades the visual immediacy of a dissection for the more abstract idea of similarity, and it is usually the version taught first in modern courses because it generalizes so easily to related results, including the law of cosines, which extends the same idea to triangles that are not right triangles.

## A Proof from an Unlikely Source

One of the more charming proofs of the theorem is credited to James Garfield, a nineteenth-century member of the United States House of Representatives who later served briefly as president, who reportedly worked it out independently as a kind of mathematical hobby before his political career eclipsed such pursuits. Garfield's version arranges two copies of the same right triangle to form a trapezoid, with the two right-angle legs forming the trapezoid's two parallel sides and the two hypotenuses meeting at a single point along what would otherwise be the trapezoid's slanted top edge. Computing the trapezoid's area two different ways — once using the standard trapezoid area formula, and once by adding up the areas of the three triangles the construction creates — and setting the two calculations equal to each other produces the theorem after a short bit of algebra. It is not the shortest proof available, but it has the appeal of using a shape, the trapezoid, that nobody would have expected to be relevant, and of coming from a source, a sitting or future head of government, that nobody expects to be doing original geometry.

## Why So Many Proofs Exist at All

Compilations of Pythagorean theorem proofs, assembled by enthusiasts over the past century or so, run into the hundreds of genuinely distinct arguments, drawing on algebra, geometry, trigonometry, and even mechanical reasoning about calculus-style arguments involving areas that change as a shape is stretched. Few if any other theorems in mathematics have attracted anything close to this volume of independent verification. Part of the reason is surely the theorem's simplicity: it can be stated to a child, tested with a tape measure, and yet it touches deep structural facts about area and similarity, which means it can be reached from many different directions, algebraic, geometric, and otherwise, by anyone patient enough to look.

There is also a kind of institutional momentum to it. Because the theorem is the first serious, memorable result most students encounter that they can actually verify with their own hands, generations of mathematically curious people, professional and amateur alike, have felt drawn to find "their own" proof of it as a kind of rite of passage, the geometric equivalent of writing a first short story. The theorem's converse is just as useful as the theorem itself: if you measure a triangle's three sides and find that the two shorter squares add up to the longer one, the triangle must contain a right angle, full stop, no matter how it was drawn. That converse is precisely what the rope-and-knots surveyors were relying on, whether or not anyone in the field could have proved it, and it is why the theorem earns its keep every time someone needs a square corner without a protractor in sight — a bridge builder squaring a foundation, a carpenter checking a frame, a gardener staking out a rectangular bed, all leaning, usually without thinking about it, on a relationship first nailed down in general form some twenty-five centuries ago.
