# Calculus and the Idea of a Limit

Look at a car's speedometer at any random moment and it reports a single number, a speed, as if the car had a speed "right now," at that exact instant. But speed is distance divided by time, and an instant has no duration at all — zero elapsed time, zero distance traveled in it, which by the ordinary rules of division should produce nothing but zero divided by zero, a meaningless expression rather than a meaningful number. And yet the speedometer is not lying, and the number it reports is not nonsense. Making sense of exactly what that number means, and why it is not the contradiction it first appears to be, took mathematicians a couple of thousand years, and the concept that finally cracked it is called a limit.

## An Old Paradox About Motion

The ancient Greek philosopher Zeno of Elea is remembered for a set of puzzles about motion that were designed to sound like proofs that motion is impossible, or at least that our ordinary reasoning about it is confused. In one version, an arrow in flight is, at any single instant, occupying one fixed position in space, indistinguishable in that frozen instant from an arrow simply sitting still. If the arrow is motionless at every individual instant of its flight, Zeno's puzzle asks, how does it ever manage to move at all across the whole flight? In another version, a swift runner chasing a slower one who has a head start must first reach the point where the slower runner started, by which time the slower runner has moved a bit further ahead; the pursuer must then close that new, smaller gap, by which time the other has again moved a little further; and this seems to repeat forever, an endless sequence of gaps to close, suggesting the faster runner can never actually catch up, which obviously contradicts everyday experience of one runner overtaking another.

Zeno's paradoxes are not really arguments that motion is impossible; they are arguments that something is wrong with the tools available at the time for reasoning about infinitely many small steps happening within a finite stretch of time or space. The Greek mathematician Eudoxus, and later Archimedes, developed an early technique called the method of exhaustion, using sequences of ever-closer approximations, often polygons squeezing in on a curved shape from both sides, to reason rigorously about quantities that could not be measured directly in one step. It worked, and it produced real, provable results about areas and volumes, but it was applied case by case, freshly reinvented for each new shape, rather than distilled into one general reusable idea.

## What a Limit Actually Claims

The general idea, once mathematicians finally isolated it explicitly, turns out to be fairly intuitive once stated plainly, even though pinning it down with full rigor proved difficult. A limit describes what value a quantity settles toward as some other quantity changes and gets closer and closer to a particular target, without necessarily ever exactly reaching that target. Consider average speed measured over shorter and shorter time intervals, all starting at the same moment: average speed over the next hour, then the next minute, then the next second, then the next thousandth of a second, each average computed the ordinary way, distance covered divided by time elapsed. As the time interval shrinks toward zero, without ever actually becoming zero, these average speeds typically settle down, getting closer and closer to some particular number and staying close to it, rather than bouncing around unpredictably or racing off toward infinity. That settled-toward number is defined to be the instantaneous speed at the starting moment — not distance divided by zero time, which remains meaningless, but the value that ordinary, perfectly well-defined average speeds are converging toward as the time interval used to compute them shrinks toward nothing. The speedometer, in effect, is reporting a limit, even though nobody driving a car ever needs to think of it that way.

This same idea, applied to the geometric problem of measuring area rather than the physical problem of measuring speed, resolves an equally old difficulty. The area under a curved graph cannot be found with the simple length-times-width formula that works for a rectangle, since a curve has no fixed height. But slice the region into many thin vertical strips, each one narrow enough that the curve barely changes height across it, treat each strip as an approximate rectangle, and add up all those approximate rectangular areas. This total is not exactly right, but as the strips get thinner and thinner, and more and more numerous, the total area of all the approximate rectangles settles toward a particular number, again without any individual strip actually having zero width. That settled-toward value is defined to be the exact area under the curve. This is precisely the modern, general version of the squeezing technique Archimedes used centuries earlier to trap the value of pi between the perimeters of inscribed and circumscribed polygons, except now recognized as one instance of a completely general technique applicable to any curve at all, not a clever trick invented fresh for each new shape.

## Two Ideas, One Concept Underneath

These two problems, finding an instantaneous rate of change and finding an exact area, look at first like they have nothing to do with each other, one about motion and one about shape. It was the significant insight of calculus's development, associated chiefly with Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz working independently in the late seventeenth century, that the two problems are intimately connected, each one in a precise sense the reverse of the other, and that both rest on exactly the same underlying idea of a limit, a quantity approached ever more closely by a sequence of ordinary, computable approximations. Newton and Leibniz each built extensive, practically usable systems of rules and notation for working with these ideas, rules that let mathematicians and scientists compute rates of change and areas for an enormous range of curves and physical situations without redoing the whole approximating process by hand each time. What neither of them supplied, and what mathematicians of their era generally were not especially troubled by lacking, was a fully airtight logical definition of what a limit precisely is, one that did not lean on slightly hand-wavy language about quantities "approaching" other quantities or becoming "infinitely small."

## A Second Paradox the Same Idea Resolves

Limits quietly dissolve a smaller, more arithmetic-flavored puzzle too, one that still trips people up long after they have finished a calculus course: whether the repeating decimal consisting of an endless string of nines is actually equal to one, or merely very close to it. Treated as a limit, the question stops being mysterious. The decimal is shorthand for an infinite sum, nine tenths plus nine hundredths plus nine thousandths and so on forever, and the partial sums you get by stopping after some fixed number of terms — nine tenths, ninety-nine hundredths, nine hundred ninety-nine thousandths — climb steadily toward one and can be made to land as close to one as anyone likes, provided enough terms are included, while never once exceeding it. That settling behavior is exactly what a limit describes, and the limit of this particular infinite sum is, without any hedging or approximation, exactly one, not almost one. The discomfort many people feel here is really discomfort with the idea that an infinite process can have a perfectly exact, finite outcome at all, the same discomfort Zeno's runner paradox exploits, just wearing decimal notation instead of a footrace.

## Making It Rigorous

That gap sat unresolved for roughly a century and a half, papered over in practice by the fact that Newton and Leibniz's methods kept producing correct, verifiable answers even without a fully rigorous foundation underneath them. It was closed in the nineteenth century, largely through the work of mathematicians including Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Karl Weierstrass, who developed a precise, thoroughly unambiguous way of defining what it means for a sequence of values to approach a limit, phrased in terms of guaranteeing that the values can be made to stay as close as anyone likes to the limiting value, provided you go far enough along the sequence or close enough to the target point, with no appeal to vague language about infinitely small quantities required at all. This rigorous formulation finally let mathematicians answer, with total confidence, the exact question that had made Zeno's paradoxes bite in the first place: whether infinitely many steps, each one individually shrinking, can add up to a finite, ordinary result. They can, routinely, and the runner's chase in Zeno's puzzle is simply an infinite sum of shrinking time intervals that adds up to a perfectly ordinary finite time, the moment the faster runner actually does catch up, exactly as everyday experience always insisted must happen.

The idea of a limit, once made rigorous, turned out to be the single foundation stone underneath the entire structure of calculus, and by extension a large fraction of the mathematics used throughout the physical sciences, engineering, and economics ever since. A speedometer needle, a planet's changing velocity along its orbit, the area swept out by a curve, the total accumulated effect of a continuously varying force — all of it rests, when traced back far enough, on the same basic move Zeno's paradoxes could not quite get past: trusting that an infinite sequence of ever-finer approximations really can settle, exactly and without contradiction, on a single ordinary number.
