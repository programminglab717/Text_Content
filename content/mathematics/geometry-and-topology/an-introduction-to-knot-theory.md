# An Introduction to Knot Theory

Take a shoelace, tie an ordinary overhand knot in it, and then join the two loose ends together with a bit of tape so the whole thing forms one closed loop with the knot trapped inside it. You now have, in your hands, an actual mathematical knot, and you have also just done the one thing that separates a mathematician's knot from everyone else's: closing the loop. A knot in the everyday sense, the kind in a shoelace or a ship's rigging, always has two free ends you could, in principle, thread back through and undo. A mathematical knot has no free ends at all. It is a closed loop sitting in three-dimensional space, and the entire question knot theory asks is deceptively simple to state and often ferociously hard to answer: given two such loops, is there any way to smoothly deform one, stretching and moving it around without ever cutting the loop or letting it pass through itself, until it looks exactly like the other?

## The Simplest Cases

The easiest possible knot is not really a knot at all in any meaningful sense: a plain closed loop with no crossings, like a rubber band lying flat, called the unknot. Even the unknot can be drawn in deceptively tangled-looking ways, looped over itself repeatedly on paper, and part of the challenge of the subject is that a diagram which looks hopelessly complicated on the page can sometimes be wiggled apart, one crossing at a time, back into a plain circle, while a different diagram that looks almost as messy genuinely cannot be undone no matter how it is manipulated.

The simplest genuine knot, the one every introduction to the subject reaches for, is the trefoil, which looks roughly like a pretzel with three lobes, or the decorative knot used in some heraldic and religious symbols going back many centuries before anyone studied it mathematically. The trefoil comes in two mirror-image versions, left-handed and right-handed, and one of the earlier surprises in the field was the proof that these two versions genuinely cannot be deformed into each other, no matter how cleverly you try, because the twisting in the two versions runs in permanently opposite senses that no amount of smooth manipulation in three-dimensional space can reverse. Reflecting an object in a mirror is not a move knot theory allows, and it turns out to matter.

## Untangling on Paper

Working with actual physical loops of rope quickly becomes impractical for serious study, so knot theorists work instead with diagrams: a knot flattened onto a page, drawn as a curve that crosses over and under itself at various points, with small breaks in the line indicating which strand passes underneath at each crossing. The immediate problem with diagrams is that the same knot can be drawn in wildly different-looking ways, with different numbers of crossings, depending on how it happens to be laid out on the page, so a naive comparison of two diagrams tells you very little about whether the underlying knots are actually the same.

The mathematician Kurt Reidemeister solved the key piece of this problem in the early twentieth century by identifying three specific local moves — now called Reidemeister moves — that can be performed on a small piece of a knot diagram without changing the underlying knot they represent. One move lets you twist or untwist a simple loop in a strand; another lets you slide one strand entirely over or under another where they briefly overlap; the third lets you slide a strand across a crossing between two other strands. Reidemeister proved that these three moves are, in a precise sense, all you ever need: two diagrams represent the same knot if and only if one can be transformed into the other using some sequence of these three moves, however long that sequence might have to be. This turned an vague, intuitive question about wiggling rope in space into a well-defined, if often still very difficult, combinatorial problem about manipulating diagrams according to fixed rules.

## Telling Knots Apart

Knowing the rules for manipulating diagrams does not, by itself, make it easy to tell two knots apart, because proving two diagrams are different knots means proving that no sequence of Reidemeister moves, however long, connects them, and checking every possible sequence is not a realistic option. Knot theorists instead look for invariants, calculable properties of a diagram guaranteed to come out the same no matter which diagram of a given knot you started with, which means two knots with different invariant values must genuinely be different knots, even without checking every possible deformation directly.

One of the simplest invariants to actually compute by hand is tricolorability: the question of whether every strand in a knot diagram can be colored using exactly three colors, following two rules — at least two colors must actually be used somewhere in the diagram, and at every crossing, the three strands meeting there must be either all the same color or all three different colors. The unknot's simplest diagram, a plain loop with no crossings, cannot be tricolored this way, because there is only one strand and the rule requires at least two colors to appear. The trefoil, by contrast, can always be tricolored, its three strands taking three different colors around the whole loop. Since tricolorability is provably unaffected by any Reidemeister move, and the unknot is not tricolorable while the trefoil is, this single test proves conclusively that the trefoil is not the unknot, a fact that feels obvious by eye but had to actually be proved rather than merely assumed. More powerful invariants, including numerical polynomials attached to a knot diagram through more elaborate crossing-by-crossing calculations, extend this same basic strategy to distinguish knots far too similar-looking for a simple coloring test to tell apart.

## A False Start in Physics, a Real Start in Mathematics

Knot theory owes an unusual debt to a wrong idea. In the late nineteenth century, the physicist Lord Kelvin proposed that atoms might actually be knotted vortices, tiny stable whirlpools twisted into different knot shapes within a pervasive medium then believed to fill all of space, with each chemical element corresponding to a different type of knot. The theory was entirely wrong as physics; the medium it depended on does not exist, and atoms are not knots. But mathematicians and physicists took the idea seriously enough at the time that the Scottish physicist Peter Guthrie Tait undertook the painstaking work of cataloguing every distinct knot up to a fairly large number of crossings, by hand, producing knot tables that remained a standard reference for decades. When the vortex-atom theory was eventually abandoned, Tait's tables did not disappear with it; they had become a genuine mathematical resource in their own right, and knot theory carried on as a subject of pure mathematics long after the physical motivation that spurred its first serious cataloguing effort had been discarded entirely.

## Knots That Matter Today

The subject eventually found its way back into science, though not through Kelvin's vortices. DNA inside a cell is a long, thin, physically real strand, and it frequently becomes tangled or knotted during ordinary cellular processes like replication, in ways that can be described and classified using the same crossing-diagram framework knot theorists had developed for entirely abstract reasons. Cells contain specialized enzymes whose job is essentially to reach in and perform local surgery on the DNA strand, cutting and rejoining it in ways that reduce its knottedness, and biologists studying how these enzymes work have borrowed knot theory's vocabulary and invariants directly to describe what state the DNA is in before and after the enzyme acts. Chemists studying molecules that are mirror images of each other, a property called chirality with real consequences for how a molecule behaves and interacts with other chiral molecules, have found direct use for the same "left-handed versus right-handed trefoil" distinction that once seemed like a piece of pure mathematical trivia. A subject born from a mistaken theory of atoms turned out, a full century later, to describe something real, just several scales of the natural world away from where anyone first went looking for it.
