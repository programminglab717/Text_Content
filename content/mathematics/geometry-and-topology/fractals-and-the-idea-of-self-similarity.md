# Fractals and the Idea of Self-Similarity

Break off a single floret from a head of Romanesco broccoli and look at it next to the whole vegetable. The floret is, in almost every visible respect, a smaller copy of the thing it came from — the same spiraling arrangement of bumps, the same cone-like swirl, repeated at a scale you can hold between two fingers. Break a smaller piece off that floret and, for another step or two, the resemblance holds again. Nature did not consult a mathematician before growing this way, and yet what the vegetable is doing has a precise name in mathematics: self-similarity, the property of looking roughly the same at different scales of magnification. Ferns do it in the way each frond mirrors the shape of the whole leaf. River networks do it in the way a tributary's branching pattern echoes the branching of the whole watershed. Once you know to look for it, the pattern turns out to be almost everywhere, and mathematicians spent a surprisingly long time treating it as an embarrassment before deciding to treat it as a subject.

## Monsters in the Attic

For much of the nineteenth century, mathematicians working in the young field of real analysis kept stumbling onto curves that behaved in ways nobody wanted to believe were legitimate. The mathematician Karl Weierstrass constructed a function, continuous everywhere, meaning it never jumps or breaks, but differentiable nowhere, meaning it has no well-defined slope or tangent line at any single point on its entire length — a curve that is somehow smooth enough to draw without lifting your pen, yet so jagged at every conceivable magnification that it has no direction anywhere. Contemporaries reportedly found the object monstrous, an affront to the intuitive picture of curves as basically smooth things with occasional rough patches, and for decades such constructions were treated as pathological exceptions, curiosities to be filed away rather than a real phenomenon worth studying on its own terms.

A similar attitude greeted a shape now called the Koch snowflake, built by an endlessly repeating rule: take a straight line segment, replace its middle third with two sides of an equilateral triangle pointing outward, and then apply that exact same replacement to every straight segment in the resulting figure, forever. After one step you have a shape with four segments instead of one. After the next step, sixteen. Keep going indefinitely and you get a curve with a finite, calculable area sitting underneath it, but an infinitely long perimeter, since every application of the rule increases the total boundary length by the same fixed proportion, forever, with no ceiling. A shape enclosing a finite area with an infinite boundary sounds like a contradiction until you actually work through the construction, at which point it becomes merely strange, and then, if you sit with it, oddly beautiful: a boundary so relentlessly jagged that no matter how far you zoom into any portion of it, you find exactly the same jagged triangular bumps repeating, an early and exact mathematical instance of self-similarity built entirely from a single, simple instruction applied over and over.

A related construction, called the Sierpinski triangle, makes the self-similarity even more visually direct. Start with a single solid triangle, find the midpoints of its three sides, and remove the smaller upside-down triangle those midpoints outline, leaving three solid triangular corners behind, each half the size of the original. Repeat the exact same removal on each of those three smaller triangles, and then on each of the nine still-smaller triangles that produces, forever. What remains after infinitely many removals is a shape riddled with holes at every scale, containing, remarkably, zero area in the limit, since each step removes a fixed fraction of whatever area remained, while its boundary, all those triangular edges, keeps growing without bound, another instance of the same finite-area-infinite-boundary strangeness the Koch snowflake produces by building outward instead of carving inward. Zoom into any surviving corner of a Sierpinski triangle and you find a smaller but otherwise identical copy of the whole pattern, holes within holes within holes, which makes it a favorite teaching example precisely because the self-similarity is something you can watch happen, one removal at a time, rather than something you have to take on faith.

## The Coastline That Has No Fixed Length

The English mathematician and meteorologist Lewis Fry Richardson, investigating an unrelated question about whether the length of a shared national border affects the likelihood of conflict between the two countries it separates, ran into a puzzle that seems almost absurd on first hearing: the measured length of a coastline depends heavily on the length of the ruler you use to measure it, and it does not settle down to a fixed value as the ruler gets shorter. Measure a rugged coastline with a hundred-mile ruler, laying straight segments end to end and following the coast's general outline, and you get one figure. Measure the same coastline with a one-mile ruler, now able to follow inlets, headlands, and bends the longer ruler skipped straight across, and the total length increases substantially, because you are now capturing detail the cruder measurement smoothed over. Shrink the ruler further, to capture the wiggles of individual rocks and tide pools, and the measured length increases again, with no sign of approaching a fixed final answer the way the perimeter of a circle or a square would if you kept refining your ruler.

This observation, sometimes called the coastline paradox, is really just Richardson noticing a real-world approximation to Koch-snowflake-style self-similarity: a natural coastline tends to have roughly similar jaggedness at many different scales, so that zooming in reveals new wiggles rather than smoothing out into straight lines, much as the mathematical snowflake reveals new triangular bumps at every level of magnification, without limit.

## Mandelbrot Names the Family

It was the mathematician Benoit Mandelbrot, working through the middle decades of the twentieth century, who connected Richardson's coastline observation, Weierstrass's monstrous curve, the Koch snowflake, and a scattering of other previously isolated mathematical curiosities into a single unified family, and gave that family a name: fractals. Mandelbrot's key conceptual move was to take seriously an idea that sounds almost like wordplay but turns out to be mathematically precise — that these shapes have a dimension that is not a whole number. An ordinary smooth curve has a dimension of one; a filled-in flat region has a dimension of two. A sufficiently jagged curve, one that packs so much wiggling detail into itself that it starts to fill up space more thoroughly than a plain line does but not as thoroughly as a solid area, can have a dimension somewhere in between, a number like one-and-a-fraction, quantifying exactly how much rougher the curve is than a straight line without being as space-filling as a flat plane. A real coastline's fractal dimension, estimated from Richardson-style measurements at different scales, typically comes out a bit above one, a number capturing, in a single figure, just how jagged that particular stretch of coast tends to be.

Mandelbrot also popularized what remains the most recognizable image in the entire subject: a shape now called the Mandelbrot set, generated not by a geometric cutting rule like the snowflake but by repeatedly applying a simple arithmetic rule to points on a plane and checking whether the results stay bounded or run off to infinity. The boundary of the resulting shape is infinitely detailed, and zooming into almost any portion of that boundary reveals smaller versions of the whole set's distinctive bulbous silhouette, along with entirely new decorative structures never seen at the previous scale, a computer-generated echo of the Romanesco broccoli's hand-sized self-similarity, except that the zooming here has no natural stopping point at all.

## Roughness, Rehabilitated

The deeper shift Mandelbrot pushed for was less about any single curve and more about attitude. Classical geometry, going back to the Greeks, is a geometry of smooth things: circles, spheres, straight lines, gently curving surfaces you could describe with a modest handful of numbers. Mountains are not smooth. Coastlines are not smooth. Clouds, blood vessels, lightning bolts, and the branching patterns of lungs and trees are not smooth. For a long time, the gap between clean mathematical idealization and the actual roughness of the physical world was treated as simply outside geometry's job description, a matter for other disciplines to handle empirically as best they could. Fractal geometry offered, for the first time, a mathematically rigorous vocabulary for roughness itself, treating jaggedness not as noise obscuring some ideal smooth shape underneath, but as the essential, describable feature of many real objects.

That vocabulary went on to prove useful well beyond decorative computer graphics, showing up in the design of compact antennas that use fractal-like folding to pack a long effective length into a small physical space, in models of how blood vessels branch to efficiently supply tissue, and in techniques for compressing digital images by exploiting the same kind of repeating self-similar structure Mandelbrot had spent decades arguing was hiding in plain sight, in a broccoli floret and a coastline alike, waiting for the right kind of attention.
