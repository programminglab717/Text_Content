# Pi and the Long Effort to Measure a Circle

Wrap a piece of string around a drinking glass, then straighten the string out and lay it against the glass's diameter. You will find that the string is a little more than three diameters long, always, no matter how wide or narrow the glass is. Try it with a dinner plate, a wagon wheel, or a coin, and the ratio comes out the same each time: a little more than three, consistently, stubbornly, for every circle that has ever existed. That constant ratio is pi, and the fact that it is the same number for every circle, from a bicycle tire to the orbit of a planet, is either completely obvious or completely astonishing depending on how long you let yourself think about it.

Ancient societies noticed the ratio long before anyone could explain why it held. Babylonian and Egyptian mathematicians, working independently many centuries before the Greek geometers usually credited with formalizing the subject, arrived at rough fractional approximations good enough for the construction and land-surveying problems they actually faced. Neither civilization treated the constant with anything like the reverence it would later receive; it was a practical number, useful for estimating how much grain a circular granary would hold or how long a curved wall would need to be, accurate to within a percent or two, which was more than sufficient for the job at hand.

## Archimedes and the Squeeze

The first person to attack pi as a genuine mathematical problem, rather than a useful approximation, was Archimedes of Syracuse, in the third century BCE. His method is one of the cleaner ideas in the history of mathematics, and it does not require anything beyond careful reasoning about polygons. Take a circle, and inscribe a regular hexagon inside it, touching the circle at six points. The hexagon's perimeter is easy to compute exactly, and because the hexagon sits entirely inside the circle, its perimeter must be a little less than the circle's circumference. Then circumscribe a second hexagon around the outside of the same circle, touching it at six points from the outside; this hexagon's perimeter must be a little more than the circumference. Pi is trapped between the two.

Archimedes then did the genuinely clever part: he doubled the number of sides, from hexagons to twelve-sided polygons, then twenty-four, then forty-eight, then ninety-six, recalculating the inscribed and circumscribed perimeters at each stage using a relationship he derived for how a polygon's side length changes when you double its number of sides. Each doubling squeezed the upper and lower bounds on pi closer together. By the time he reached ninety-six-sided polygons, done entirely with geometric reasoning and arithmetic by hand, no calculating devices of any kind, he had pinned pi down to between two fractions extremely close together, establishing for the first time that the constant was somewhere in a narrow, provable range rather than simply "about three and a bit." The method is called the method of exhaustion, and it is, in spirit, an early ancestor of the limiting processes that would eventually become calculus two thousand years later: the true value of pi is approached, squeezed from both sides, by a sequence of computable approximations that never quite reach it but get arbitrarily close.

## The Baton Passes East

After Archimedes, the project of computing pi to greater precision migrated across several mathematical traditions, each pushing the polygon method, or variations on it, further than the last. Chinese mathematicians, including Liu Hui in the third century CE and later Zu Chongzhi, extended the inscribed-polygon approach to many more sides than Archimedes had used, reaching a level of precision that would stand as a record for centuries. Indian mathematicians working in the centuries that followed, particularly those associated with the Kerala school in the medieval period, took a fundamentally different approach, discovering infinite series — sums with infinitely many terms that add up to a finite value — that could compute pi directly, without any reference to polygons at all. These series converge slowly, meaning you need to add a great many terms to get a good approximation, but they represented a genuinely new way of thinking about the constant, treating it as the limit of an infinite additive process rather than the limit of a geometric squeezing process.

That series-based approach reached Europe independently, centuries later, when mathematicians associated with the development of calculus, including Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, rediscovered similar infinite series for pi in the seventeenth century, connecting the constant explicitly to the emerging machinery of calculus. Other mathematicians of the same general era, including John Wallis, found different infinite expressions for pi built from products rather than sums. None of these series were efficient by modern standards — some require an enormous number of terms to gain even a few correct digits — but their existence mattered more than their speed. They showed pi was reachable through the everyday tools of analysis, not just through geometric constructions with a compass and straightedge.

## What Kind of Number Pi Is

For a long time, an open question sat underneath all this computation: was pi perhaps a fraction after all, just a very inconvenient one, hiding somewhere past the digits anyone had bothered to compute? This was settled in the eighteenth century, when Johann Lambert proved that pi is irrational, meaning it cannot be written as a ratio of two whole numbers no matter how large those numbers are allowed to be. Its decimal expansion does not terminate and does not eventually repeat in a cycle, ever, which is a much stronger statement than simply having "a lot" of digits.

An even stronger result followed in the nineteenth century, when Ferdinand von Lindemann proved that pi is transcendental, meaning it is not the solution to any polynomial equation with whole-number coefficients, of any degree. This sounds like a technical distinction, but it settled a genuinely ancient practical question: whether a circle could be "squared" using only a compass and straightedge, meaning constructing a square with exactly the same area as a given circle, a challenge going back to Greek geometry that had resisted solution for over two thousand years. Lindemann's result proved this construction is permanently impossible, not merely undiscovered, because compass-and-straightedge constructions can only ever produce numbers with a much more restricted algebraic character than a transcendental number like pi can have.

## Why We Keep Computing More Digits

No physical calculation anyone will ever perform needs more than a modest number of decimal digits of pi. Even calculations spanning astronomical distances, computed to a precision far beyond anything an instrument could measure, saturate after a few dozen digits; the extra precision simply has nowhere left to matter. And yet the effort to compute pi to ever more digits never really stopped, moving from Archimedes' hand-done polygon arithmetic to slide rules and mechanical calculators and eventually to electronic computers capable of grinding through algorithms that converge far faster than the old infinite series, adding many correct digits with each iteration rather than one.

Part of the appeal is simply that pi makes an excellent stress test: computing it to extreme precision exercises a computer's arithmetic, its memory, and its software in ways that reveal hardware flaws and software bugs that more mundane calculations would never expose. Part of it is aesthetic, even competitive, in the way that record-setting pursuits often are, independent of any practical payoff. And part of it is that the digits of pi, so far as anyone has ever been able to tell, show no discernible pattern at all, marching on with the same apparent statistical randomness whether you look at the first hundred or deep into digits no one had ever computed before. A number defined by something as simple and universal as the ratio between a circle's circumference and its width contains, buried in its decimal expansion, what looks for all the world like pure chaos — a strange contrast that keeps drawing people back to a constant the ancient Egyptians already had a decent working approximation for.
