# Tilings and Tessellations

A honeycomb is a geometry lesson that happens to also store honey. Every cell is a hexagon, and the hexagons meet edge to edge across the whole comb without a single gap or overlap, a pattern bees arrive at through evolved building behavior rather than instruction, and a pattern mathematicians can prove is close to the most efficient way to divide a flat surface into equal-sized compartments using the least total wall length. That efficiency argument is one small piece of a much larger subject: the study of how shapes can cover a plane completely, edge to edge, with nothing left uncovered and nothing doubled up. Mathematicians call this tiling or tessellation, and the rules governing which shapes can do it, and how many different ways they can do it, turn out to be surprisingly tight for something that looks, on a bathroom floor or a quilt pattern, like a matter of pure decorative taste.

## The Rule Hiding in Every Corner

Picture any point where several tiles meet at a single vertex. For the tiling to work with no gaps and no overlaps, the interior angles of all the tiles meeting at that point have to add up to exactly a full turn: three hundred sixty degrees, no more and no less. This single requirement does an enormous amount of work in explaining what tessellations are possible.

Consider tiling with a single regular shape, meaning a shape with all sides and all angles equal, repeated over and over. A regular hexagon's interior angle is a hundred twenty degrees, and three of them meeting at a point add up to exactly three hundred sixty, which is why hexagons tile perfectly, as any honeycomb demonstrates. A square's interior angle is ninety degrees, and four of them meet perfectly at a point, which is why square tiles have covered floors for as long as floors have been tiled. An equilateral triangle's angle is sixty degrees, and six of them meet at a point with no gap, which is why triangular tiles work too. Those three shapes — the triangle, the square, and the hexagon — are the only regular polygons that tile the plane by themselves. Try it with a regular pentagon, whose interior angle is a hundred eight degrees, and three of them at a point only add up to three hundred twenty-four degrees, leaving an unavoidable gap that a fourth pentagon is too wide to fill. Regular pentagons simply cannot tessellate on their own, a fact that surprises people who assume any sufficiently symmetric shape ought to be able to do it.

## Mixing Shapes Together

Restricting a tiling to a single repeated regular shape is only the simplest case. Allow more than one type of regular polygon in the same tiling, as long as the arrangement of shapes around every vertex is identical throughout the pattern, and you get what are called semi-regular or Archimedean tilings. One familiar example alternates triangles and squares in a band pattern; another combines squares, hexagons, and dodecagons around each vertex point, a combination frequently visible in decorative pavement and historic tilework. There are exactly eight of these semi-regular tilings possible in the flat plane, a fact established through the same angle-counting logic that rules out the pentagon, just applied to combinations of different polygons rather than a single repeated one, checking every combination of regular polygon angles that can sum to exactly three hundred sixty degrees around a point while still allowing a consistent, repeatable pattern to extend indefinitely.

Once you drop the requirement that every vertex look identical, and allow irregular or non-convex shapes, the number of possible tessellations becomes vastly larger and much harder to classify completely. Any triangle at all, regular or wildly scalene, will tile the plane, because two copies of any triangle can always be joined along their longest side to form a parallelogram, and parallelograms tile trivially by stacking in rows and columns. Any quadrilateral at all, even one with no parallel sides and no equal angles, will also tile the plane, through a similar though less obvious rearrangement trick involving rotating alternating copies of the shape around the midpoints of its sides. Pentagons are more restrictive and became a genuine research puzzle: mathematicians spent much of the twentieth century searching for every family of convex pentagon that can tile the plane, a hunt that produced periodic new discoveries, including some found by amateur mathematicians working outside any university setting, well into recent decades.

## Patterns That Never Repeat

For a long time, mathematicians assumed that any set of tile shapes capable of tiling the plane at all could also tile it periodically, meaning the whole pattern could be shifted sideways by some fixed distance and land exactly back on top of itself, the way wallpaper repeats. This assumption turned out to be false. In the twentieth century, mathematicians discovered sets of tiles, most famously a pair of shapes now called Penrose tiles, that can tile the entire infinite plane without gaps or overlaps, but only in patterns that never repeat no matter how far you extend them. A Penrose tiling looks, at any given patch, like it might be part of some larger repeating wallpaper, and the local patterns do recur throughout the tiling with a kind of statistical regularity, but no amount of purely sideways shifting will ever line the whole infinite pattern up with itself again. These aperiodic tilings turned out to have an unexpected echo in physical materials: certain metallic alloys, called quasicrystals, arrange their atoms in three-dimensional patterns with the same never-quite-repeating structure, a discovery that surprised chemists who had assumed, on the basis of long-established crystallography, that atomic structures were only ever periodic.

## Symmetry, Classified

Step back from the individual tiles and look instead at the symmetries of an entire tiling — all the ways you could shift, rotate, or reflect the pattern and have it land back exactly on itself. Mathematicians proved, in work completed around the end of the nineteenth century, that despite the apparently unlimited variety of decorative patterns humans have invented across different cultures and centuries, there are exactly seventeen distinct symmetry types possible for a periodic pattern repeating across a flat plane, called the wallpaper groups. Every periodic tiling or repeating decorative pattern ever created, whether in Islamic geometric tilework, medieval European floor patterns, or a modern printed fabric, falls into exactly one of these seventeen categories, distinguished by which combinations of rotation, reflection, and sliding symmetry the pattern admits. Islamic artisans, working over many centuries across a wide geographic range and largely without the formal group-theory vocabulary later mathematicians would use to classify their work, are frequently credited with having independently produced examples falling into most or all of the seventeen types, an achievement more often demonstrated through careful modern analysis of surviving tilework than through any historical record of the artisans' own reasoning.

The artist M. C. Escher took a different route into the same territory, spending years studying tessellation patterns, partly inspired by Islamic tilework he encountered and sketched, and then bending the underlying mathematical rules toward recognizable imagery: interlocking birds, fish, lizards, and human figures that tile the plane exactly as cleanly as squares or hexagons do, using the same angle-sum logic and the same symmetry operations, but disguised inside outlines that read as living creatures rather than abstract polygons. Escher was not a trained mathematician, and he arrived at much of his technique through hands-on experimentation and correspondence with mathematicians rather than formal study, but his prints remain probably the most widely recognized public face of a subject whose formal rules were worked out, angle by angle and symmetry by symmetry, by people thinking about how many degrees fit around a point.
