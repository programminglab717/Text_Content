# Correlation Is Not Causation

Every summer, ice cream sales climb, and every summer, so do drownings. Plot one against the other across the months of the year and the line connecting them looks almost too clean to be a coincidence, the kind of relationship a beginning statistics student might be tempted to write up as a discovery: eating ice cream, it would seem, is dangerous. Nobody actually believes this, of course, and the reason nobody believes it is exactly the lesson the example is built to teach. Ice cream and drowning rise together not because one causes the other but because a third factor, warm weather, drives both independently. Hot days send people to buy ice cream and, separately, send people to swim, wade, and boat in numbers that raise the drowning count. The correlation is completely real. The causal story implied by it is completely wrong.

This is one of the oldest cautionary tales in statistics, repeated in classrooms so often that it has become a cliché, and clichés earn that status by being useful often enough that people keep reaching for them. The trouble is that outside the classroom, the confusion this example warns against is not always so easy to spot, because most real correlations do not come with anything as obvious as a shared summer heat wave sitting in plain view.

## The Third Variable Hiding in Plain Sight

The technical name for the ice cream and drowning problem is confounding, and the shared summer heat is what statisticians call a confounding variable: something that influences both of the factors being compared, creating a relationship between them that has nothing to do with either one causing the other. A classic and slightly comic version of the same trap involves towns with more storks nesting nearby also tending to have higher birth rates, an old illustration used to poke fun at the idea that storks deliver babies. The actual explanation is unremarkable once you look for it: storks tend to nest in rural areas with more open land, and rural areas, for a variety of social and economic reasons unrelated to birds, have historically tended to have somewhat higher birth rates than dense urban centers. Neither stork nor birth rate causes the other. Both simply track something else, the character of the surrounding landscape, that happens to move them in the same direction.

Confounding variables are rarely as easy to spot as weather or geography, and that is precisely what makes them dangerous. A study might find that people who take a particular vitamin supplement have better health outcomes than people who do not, and it is tempting to credit the vitamin. But people who choose to take supplements in the first place often differ from those who do not in dozens of other ways — income, education, exercise habits, access to medical care — any of which could plausibly be doing the real work, with the supplement itself contributing little or nothing. Untangling which of these overlapping factors actually deserves the credit is often the hardest and most important part of any serious research into cause and effect.

## Which Way Does the Arrow Even Point

Confounding is not the only trap. Sometimes a correlation is real, and even causal, but running in the opposite direction from the one that seems obvious at first glance. City fire departments have long noticed that fires attended by more firefighters and more trucks tend to cause more property damage than fires attended by fewer. Read carelessly, this looks like a case for sending fewer trucks. Read correctly, it is obvious that bigger, more dangerous fires are the reason more trucks get sent in the first place, and those same bigger fires are also the reason more damage results. The number of trucks does not cause the damage; the size of the fire causes both the number of trucks dispatched and the extent of the damage. The correlation between trucks and damage is completely real and completely useless as an argument for changing firefighting policy, because the arrow of causation runs through a variable — the size of the fire — that a naive reading of the statistics leaves out entirely.

Reversed causation shows up constantly in questions about human behavior and confidence. Confident students tend to perform better on exams, which invites the plausible-sounding advice that building a student's confidence will raise their grades. It is at least as plausible, and arguably more likely, that the arrow runs the other way: performing well builds confidence, rather than confidence producing performance, and the two feed back into each other in ways that a single correlation cannot separate.

## How Science Tries to Escape the Trap

The most reliable way researchers have found to break out of these ambiguities is the randomized controlled experiment. Instead of merely observing who happens to take a supplement, drink coffee, or attend a particular school, researchers assign people to different conditions at random and compare the outcomes. Randomization is the crucial move, because it scatters all the confounding variables — income, genetics, motivation, and everything else researchers did not even think to measure — roughly evenly across both groups, so that any difference in outcome that remains can be attributed to the one thing that was deliberately varied. This is why medical trials that can be run this way are trusted so much more than simple observational comparisons, and why fields like nutrition, where randomizing what people eat over years or decades is rarely practical, struggle so persistently with contradictory and unreliable findings.

Not every question can be put through a randomized experiment, for reasons of cost, ethics, or plain impossibility — nobody can randomly assign people to smoke for thirty years to test whether it causes cancer. In cases like this, researchers lean on other strategies: looking for a dose-response relationship, where more exposure produces proportionally more of the effect; checking whether the same relationship holds up across many different populations and study designs; and asking whether a plausible biological or physical mechanism connects the two. The connection between smoking and lung cancer was established this way, over a period of years, through the patient accumulation of many different kinds of evidence pointing in the same direction, rather than through any single definitive experiment, since the definitive experiment was never something researchers could ethically run.

## A Clue, Not a Verdict

None of this makes correlation useless. It is very often the first thread anyone pulls on the way to discovering a genuine causal relationship, and plenty of causal relationships that are now well established were first noticed as puzzling correlations that researchers refused to dismiss. The mistake is not in noticing a correlation. The mistake is treating a correlation as though it had already answered the harder question of cause and effect, when in reality it has only pointed toward a question still waiting to be asked properly.
