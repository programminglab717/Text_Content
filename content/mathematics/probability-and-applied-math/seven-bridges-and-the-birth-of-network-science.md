# Seven Bridges and the Birth of Network Science

The old Prussian city of Königsberg was built across both banks of a river and around two islands sitting in its middle, and by the early eighteenth century the whole arrangement was stitched together by seven bridges. Locals apparently enjoyed a Sunday pastime of strolling through the city trying to solve a simple-sounding puzzle: could you plan a walk that crossed every one of the seven bridges exactly once, neither skipping a bridge nor doubling back over one you had already crossed? People tried, failed, tried again from a different starting point, and failed again. Nobody could quite manage it, and nobody could quite explain why not, until the problem reached the Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler.

## A Sunday Puzzle Reaches a Mathematician

Euler, one of the most prolific mathematicians in history, might easily have dismissed the Königsberg bridge puzzle as a trivial diversion beneath serious attention, and in fact he initially described it in roughly those terms in his own writing, before going on to solve it so thoroughly that he accidentally founded an entire branch of mathematics in the process. His key move was to notice that the specific geography of Königsberg, the exact shapes of its islands and the precise lengths of its bridges, was completely irrelevant to the puzzle. All that mattered was which landmasses were connected to which other landmasses, and by how many bridges. Euler stripped the map down to its bare essentials: four points, representing the two riverbanks and two islands, connected by seven lines, representing the seven bridges. Distances, shapes, and directions vanished from the problem entirely, leaving only a pattern of connections.

## Ignoring the Map, Counting the Doors

With the problem reduced to points and connecting lines, Euler noticed something elegant about what it would take to walk a route crossing every line exactly once. Picture arriving at any landmass, other than your starting or ending point, partway through such a walk. You arrive by one bridge and must leave by a different one, since you can only cross each bridge once. Every time you pass through that landmass again later in the walk, you use up another pair of bridges: one in, one out. This means that any landmass you are not starting or ending your walk at must have an even number of bridges connected to it, so that all of its bridges can be paired off neatly into arrivals and departures. Euler checked the four landmasses of Königsberg against this requirement and found that all four had an odd number of bridges touching them. Not one landmass qualified as a valid midpoint, let alone all but two of them, which is what a successful walk would have required. The walk was not merely difficult or unlucky to find. It was, Euler proved, mathematically impossible, for any starting point and any route whatsoever.

This solution, published in the 1730s, is now generally regarded as the founding result of graph theory, the branch of mathematics that studies networks of points, called vertices or nodes, connected by lines, called edges. It is also considered one of the earliest results in topology, the branch of mathematics concerned with properties that survive stretching, bending, and distorting a shape, since Euler's proof did not care in the slightest what the islands actually looked like or how far apart the bridges actually were, only how everything connected to everything else.

## From Bridges to Everything

The abstraction Euler performed, stripping away irrelevant geographic detail to focus purely on the pattern of connections, turned out to be almost absurdly general. Once you accept that a network can be represented as nothing more than a set of points and the connections between them, an enormous range of real-world systems reveal themselves to be graphs in disguise. A social network is a graph in which people are nodes and friendships or acquaintances are edges. The internet is a graph in which computers or web pages are nodes and physical connections or hyperlinks are edges. A map of airline routes is a graph in which airports are nodes and direct flights are edges. Even a human brain can be modeled as a graph, with neurons as nodes and synaptic connections as edges. In every one of these cases, questions that would once have seemed hopelessly specific to bridges and islands turn out to have direct, useful analogues: which nodes are most central, how many steps separate any two nodes, and whether the whole network can be traversed efficiently, or would collapse into disconnected pieces if a few key connections were removed.

## Small Worlds and Famous Numbers

One of the most famous questions asked about large real-world networks concerns exactly how many steps typically separate any two nodes within them. In the 1960s, the social psychologist Stanley Milgram ran an experiment sending letters from ordinary people in the American Midwest toward a specific target person in Boston, asking each recipient to forward the letter to someone they knew personally who seemed more likely than themselves to know the target, or to know someone who did. The chains of forwarded letters that successfully reached their destination tended to pass through a surprisingly small number of intermediaries, and the experiment helped popularize the now-famous idea of six degrees of separation: the notion that any two people, however distant their circumstances, are typically connected through a strikingly short chain of mutual acquaintances. Later researchers have debated the exact numbers and methods of Milgram's original study, but the broader phenomenon it pointed toward, often called the small-world property, has held up remarkably well across all kinds of large networks, from social circles to the structure of the internet itself, and reflects a general truth about networks that combine tight local clustering with a handful of long-range connections bridging otherwise distant parts of the graph.

Mathematicians have their own playful version of the same idea, known as the Erdős number, named after the extraordinarily prolific and well-traveled mathematician Paul Erdős, who published papers with an enormous number of collaborators over his lifetime. A mathematician's Erdős number measures their collaborative distance from Erdős himself: Erdős's own direct co-authors have an Erdős number of one, people who have co-authored a paper with one of those co-authors have an Erdős number of two, and so on. The concept is scientifically trivial and socially irresistible, tracked half-seriously across the mathematical community in much the same spirit as the popular parlor game that connects Hollywood actors to Kevin Bacon through shared film credits, itself just another small, informal graph traversal exercise dressed up as trivia.

## Hubs, and Why Some Networks Break More Gracefully Than Others

Not every node in a large real-world network is equally well connected, and the pattern of who gets to be a hub turns out to matter enormously for how the whole network behaves under stress. Researchers studying networks ranging from airline routes to the architecture of the World Wide Web, including the physicist Albert-László Barabási, found that many real-world networks are dominated by a relatively small number of extremely well-connected hub nodes, alongside a much larger number of sparsely connected ordinary ones, rather than every node having a roughly similar number of connections. This lopsided structure, often called scale-free, has a curious double-edged consequence. A network built this way tends to be remarkably resistant to random failures, since randomly disabling nodes will overwhelmingly hit one of the many sparsely connected ordinary nodes and barely dent the overall network. But that same structure is unusually vulnerable to a targeted attack on its hubs, since knocking out even a small number of the most connected nodes can fragment the entire network far more severely than removing any equivalent number of ordinary ones. Airlines, epidemiologists, and internet engineers all now think about this tradeoff explicitly, whether the concern is a handful of major hub airports whose closure could disrupt continent-wide travel, or a handful of highly connected individuals whose exposure to an infectious disease could accelerate an outbreak far more than an equivalent number of otherwise ordinary contacts.

## The Graphs Running Underneath Modern Life

Graph theory's most consequential modern application may be one most people use every day without thinking about it. Early internet search engines ranked pages largely by matching keywords, a method easily gamed and often unhelpful. The approach that displaced it, developed by the founders of Google, treated the entire web as a graph, with pages as nodes and hyperlinks as edges, and ranked each page partly according to how many other important pages linked to it, an idea directly descended from the same basic toolkit Euler built to settle an argument about Prussian bridges. Epidemiologists model the spread of infectious disease through a population as a graph problem, tracing how an infection moves along the edges of a contact network to identify which nodes, if isolated or vaccinated, would do the most to slow the whole system down. None of it would exist in its current form without a mathematician deciding, one Sunday afternoon several centuries ago, that a walking puzzle about seven bridges was worth taking seriously after all.
