# Why Polls Go Wrong

In the autumn of 1936, a well-regarded American magazine called the Literary Digest mailed out an enormous number of straw-poll postcards asking readers who they intended to vote for in the upcoming presidential election, pulling names and addresses from telephone directories and lists of registered automobile owners. Millions of responses came back, an almost absurdly large sample by the standards of any political poll before or since, and the magazine confidently predicted a comfortable win for the Republican challenger, Alf Landon, over the incumbent, Franklin Roosevelt. Roosevelt won in a landslide, carrying every state in the country except Maine and Vermont. The Literary Digest, humiliated by the scale of its error, folded not long afterward. Meanwhile, a far smaller survey conducted by a pollster named George Gallup, using a fraction of the respondents, correctly called the race.

The lesson buried in that disaster has outlived the magazine that generated it, and it remains the single most important idea in the entire practice of sampling: a poll is only as good as its ability to represent the population it claims to describe, and the size of a sample matters far less than how honestly it was drawn.

## Bigger Is Not Better

The Literary Digest's fatal flaw was not carelessness in counting responses; it was carelessness in deciding whom to ask in the first place. In the middle of the Great Depression, owning a telephone or an automobile was a marker of relative prosperity, and prosperous Americans leaned more heavily Republican than the electorate as a whole. By drawing its enormous sample from phone books and car registrations, the magazine had unknowingly built a poll that systematically overrepresented wealthier voters and underrepresented everyone else, no matter how many millions of postcards it mailed out. A larger sample does not fix a biased sampling method; it just produces a more confidently wrong answer, because the same skew gets repeated at scale rather than being diluted by it. Gallup's smaller survey worked better not because it was cleverer in some mysterious way, but because it was built around a more deliberate effort to reach a cross-section of the population resembling the actual electorate, rather than simply reaching whoever happened to be easiest to contact through existing lists.

This distinction, between a sample's size and its representativeness, is the single hardest thing for a casual reader of polls to keep straight, because instinct suggests that more data should always mean more truth. A poll of ten million self-selected respondents drawn from a biased list can be far less trustworthy than a poll of a thousand people drawn carefully at random, because the smaller poll's randomness gives every kind of voter a fair chance of being included, while the larger poll's bias guarantees that certain kinds of voters are systematically missing no matter how long the mailing list grows.

## Dewey Defeats Truman

A different kind of sampling failure struck American pollsters again in 1948, memorably enough that a photograph of the winning president holding up a newspaper with the wrong headline has remained a fixture of political folklore ever since. Most major polling organizations that year predicted a comfortable victory for the Republican candidate, Thomas Dewey, over the incumbent, Harry Truman. The Chicago Tribune, working against an early press deadline and trusting the pollsters, ran the headline "Dewey Defeats Truman" before the actual results were in. Truman won.

This failure had less to do with who was surveyed and more to do with when. Several pollsters had stopped collecting data days or even weeks before the election, on the assumption that most voters had already made up their minds and further polling would be a waste of effort. That assumption missed a late shift among undecided voters breaking toward Truman in the campaign's final days, a shift no one caught because no one was still measuring it. The episode became a lasting lesson about the danger of assuming public opinion is more settled than it actually is, and it pushed the polling industry toward continuing to survey much closer to election day itself.

## Building a Sample That Actually Represents Something

The corrective to both failures is the deceptively simple idea of random sampling: giving every member of a population a known, ideally equal, chance of being selected, rather than relying on whoever is easiest to reach. True random sampling is harder to achieve in practice than it sounds, which is why professional survey organizations invest so much effort in techniques like stratification, deliberately ensuring that important subgroups — by region, age, or other characteristics known to correlate with the opinions being measured — are represented in the sample roughly in proportion to their share of the real population, correcting for the fact that some groups are naturally easier to reach by phone, mail, or online panel than others.

The margin of error reported alongside a well-conducted poll is a direct mathematical consequence of the sample's size, following the same logic that shows up throughout probability: larger random samples give a more precise estimate, but with steadily diminishing returns, so that doubling a sample's size does not double its precision, and there comes a point past which spending more money on additional respondents buys very little extra accuracy compared to spending that same effort making sure the respondents you already have are truly representative. A margin of error, properly understood, describes only the uncertainty introduced by random sampling itself; it says nothing about additional error introduced by a poorly worded question, a skewed method of contacting people, or a systematic tendency for one kind of respondent to decline to answer, and it is exactly these unmeasured sources of error that tend to produce the most spectacular polling failures.

## The Question Behind the Question

Sampling is only half the challenge of building a trustworthy survey; the other half is designing the questions themselves, since even a perfectly random sample can produce misleading results if the questions nudge respondents toward a particular answer. Researchers have long known that asking whether the government should "provide assistance to the poor" draws a noticeably different response than asking whether it should expand "welfare," even though both phrases point at roughly the same policy, simply because the two words carry different connotations for many respondents. The order in which questions are asked matters too: a question about the economy asked right after a string of questions about a controversial leader can prime respondents to answer differently than the identical question asked cold. Survey designers have also long wrestled with social desirability bias, the tendency of some respondents to shade their answers toward whatever seems more socially acceptable rather than what they actually believe or intend to do, particularly on sensitive topics. Whether this effect meaningfully explains any particular historical polling miss remains genuinely debated among researchers, but the underlying concern is well established enough that professional pollsters spend as much effort testing and refining their exact wording as they spend worrying about who picks up the phone.

## Why Modern Polls Still Struggle

The core mathematics of sampling has not changed since Gallup's era, but the practical difficulty of gathering a truly representative sample has grown substantially harder. Response rates to telephone surveys have declined enormously as caller identification and general skepticism toward unknown numbers have become the norm, meaning that the people who do agree to answer a pollster's questions may differ systematically from the much larger number who decline, in ways that are difficult to measure and correct for. Online panels of volunteer respondents introduce their own version of the original Literary Digest problem, since people who sign up to answer surveys for small rewards are not a random cross-section of the public, and pollsters have to apply increasingly elaborate statistical adjustments to compensate.

Polling organizations have also had to grapple with the challenge of identifying likely voters rather than simply surveying all adults, since only a fraction of any adult population actually turns out to vote, and guessing which respondents will follow through introduces a whole additional layer of uncertainty on top of ordinary sampling error. None of this means polling is broken or useless; it means that the fundamental lesson of 1936 has only become more demanding to satisfy over time, in a world with more ways than ever to reach people and, somehow, just as many ways to end up hearing from the wrong ones.
