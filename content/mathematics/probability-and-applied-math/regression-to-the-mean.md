# Regression to the Mean

A rookie player puts together a spectacular first season, breaking records, drawing comparisons to the greatest players of a generation, landing on the cover of every sports magazine that covers the league. The next season, the same player performs merely well, nowhere near the dazzling heights of the first year. Commentators reach immediately for a story: the pressure got to him, or he stopped working as hard once the money and attention arrived, or the league figured out how to defend against him. Sometimes those stories are even true. But there is a much simpler explanation available, one that requires no change in effort, skill, or pressure at all, and it applies just as well to students, salespeople, medical patients, and stock pickers as it does to rookie athletes. It is called regression to the mean, and it is one of the most persistently misunderstood ideas in statistics, mostly because the human mind is so eager to find a story where none is actually needed.

## Galton's Sweet Peas

The concept traces back to Francis Galton, a Victorian polymath and cousin of Charles Darwin, who spent much of his career trying to quantify heredity. In one of his early investigations, Galton studied the seeds produced by sweet pea plants, comparing the size of parent seeds to the size of the seeds those parents produced. He expected, reasonably enough, that especially large parent seeds would produce especially large offspring seeds, and especially small parents would produce especially small offspring. What he found instead was that the offspring of unusually large parent seeds tended to be smaller than their parents, drifting back toward the average size for the whole population, while the offspring of unusually small parents tended to be larger than their parents, also drifting back toward that same average. Extreme parents produced less extreme children, on average, in both directions at once.

Galton later found the identical pattern studying human height, comparing the heights of fathers to the heights of their adult sons. Unusually tall fathers tended to have sons who were tall, but typically not quite as tall as they were. Unusually short fathers tended to have sons who were short, but typically not quite as short. Galton called the pattern regression toward mediocrity, a phrase that has since softened into the more neutral regression to the mean, though the underlying observation is exactly the same one he made studying his sweet peas: extreme measurements, in almost any domain governed partly by chance, tend to be followed by measurements closer to the average.

## Why Extremes Do Not Stay Extreme

The mechanism behind the pattern becomes clear once you accept that most measured outcomes are a mix of two ingredients: some underlying, relatively stable quality, and some amount of random noise layered on top of it. A rookie athlete's spectacular season reflects genuine talent, certainly, but it also reflects a share of good fortune — favorable matchups, a few bounces that went the right way, a stretch free of injury. A truly exceptional season is, almost by definition, a season where the underlying talent and the random noise both happened to point in the same favorable direction at once. The next season, the athlete's underlying talent is still there, largely unchanged, but the random noise starts fresh, and it is very unlikely to land favorably twice in a row to the same extreme degree. The result looks like decline, and gets described as one, when it is often nothing more than the luck component reverting to something closer to its average contribution, while the skill component stays exactly where it always was.

This is why regression to the mean is strongest precisely where measurement is noisiest, and weakest where a trait is measured with great precision and little randomness involved. Height, once someone reaches adulthood, is a fairly stable trait with a large genetic component, so Galton still found some regression toward the average, but not an overwhelming amount. A single season of athletic performance, or a single exam score, or a single quarter's sales figures, tends to carry much more noise relative to the underlying stable quality it is meant to measure, so the pull back toward average shows up far more dramatically.

## A Trap for the Unwary

Because regression to the mean produces a pattern that looks exactly like cause and effect, it has fooled careful observers across a wide range of fields, including some who should have known better. Flight instructors training pilots noticed that pilots who were praised for an unusually smooth landing tended to perform worse on their very next attempt, while pilots who were criticized for a rough landing tended to perform better next time. The instructors drew what seemed like an obvious conclusion: praise makes pilots complacent, and criticism sharpens their focus, so punishment works better than encouragement. This observation, examined more carefully by researchers studying human judgment in the mid-twentieth century, turns out to be a textbook case of regression to the mean rather than any real effect of praise or blame. An unusually smooth landing is, in part, a matter of favorable random conditions on that particular approach, and the next landing was always likely to be less extreme regardless of what the instructor said afterward. Punishment appeared to work, and praise appeared to backfire, purely because extreme performances of either kind are naturally followed by more ordinary ones.

A similar trap awaits in medicine, particularly around treatments of uncertain effectiveness. Patients tend to seek treatment exactly when their symptoms are at their worst, which is also, almost by definition, close to the moment when random fluctuation is most likely to carry those symptoms back toward a more typical level regardless of what a doctor does. A patient who tries a remedy during an unusually bad flare-up of a chronic condition and then feels better afterward may credit the remedy entirely, when at least part of the improvement, and sometimes the entire improvement, would have happened anyway. This is one of the quieter reasons ineffective remedies can accumulate such sincere, heartfelt testimonials from people who are not lying and not foolish, simply describing an improvement that genuinely happened, without realizing how much of it their body was always going to deliver on its own.

## The Fund Manager's Lucky Streak

Financial markets offer one of the more expensive illustrations of the same trap. Investment funds that post the best returns over some trailing period, five years or ten, tend to attract a rush of new money from investors eager to buy into whatever produced those standout results. A good deal of research into fund performance suggests that the outperformance driving those glowing track records is only partly attributable to genuine manager skill; a meaningful share is ordinary noise, the investment equivalent of a favorable run of cards, and noise, again, does not repeat reliably. Funds that topped the rankings in one period regress, on average, toward more ordinary performance in the next, leaving the investors who chased the hot streak disappointed, not because they were deceived by anyone, but because they mistook a partly lucky outcome for a fully repeatable skill. It is worth noting, as something of a historical footnote, that Galton himself went on to originate the statistical concept of correlation as a direct byproduct of studying this exact phenomenon, trying to measure precisely how strongly one generation's traits predicted the next; the mathematical tool later formalized by Karl Pearson as the correlation coefficient grew directly out of Galton's attempts to quantify regression to the mean with real numerical precision.

## A Kind of Statistical Immune System

Understanding regression to the mean does not mean dismissing every instance of improvement or decline as an illusion; sometimes the rookie really has changed, the pilot really has learned something, and the remedy really does work. What the idea offers is something more like a habit of caution: a reminder to ask, whenever an extreme result is followed by a more ordinary one, whether anything actually needs to be explained at all, or whether the pattern is simply what randomness looks like when it is layered on top of something stable. It is a small piece of mathematics with an outsized reach, quietly explaining why sequels disappoint, why last year's hottest stock picker often cools off, and why the most confident story about someone's rise and fall is so often simpler, and less interesting, than it first appears.
