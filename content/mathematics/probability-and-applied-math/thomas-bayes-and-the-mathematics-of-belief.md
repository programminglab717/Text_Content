# Thomas Bayes and the Mathematics of Belief

Somewhere among the papers left behind by a nonconformist minister in the English town of Tunbridge Wells was an essay on a problem that, on its face, sounds like a curiosity for gamblers: if you know how likely a cause is to produce a certain effect, what can you say about the cause after you have only seen the effect? Thomas Bayes, who died in the middle of the eighteenth century, never published the essay himself. It was his friend Richard Price, a fellow minister with a serious interest in mathematics, who found the manuscript among Bayes's effects, judged it important, tidied it up, and communicated it to the Royal Society a few years later. Bayes could not have known that the essay would eventually give its author's name to one of the most consequential ideas in the history of statistics, an idea now embedded in spam filters, medical diagnosis, spacecraft navigation, and the machine learning systems that guess what a person means to type before they finish typing it.

## Reasoning Backwards

Most probability problems of Bayes's era ran in one direction: given a known cause, what is the likely distribution of effects? If you know a die is fair, you can calculate the odds of rolling three sixes in a row without much trouble. What Bayes was after was the reverse, and much harder, question: given an observed effect, what can be said about the cause that produced it? If you draw a series of colored balls from an urn whose exact mixture of colors you do not know, what does the sequence of draws tell you about the mixture inside? This is sometimes called inverse probability, and it is a fundamentally different kind of question from the forward problems that dominated gambling mathematics, because it asks the mathematics to reason backward from evidence to explanation, which is exactly the shape of the reasoning doctors, judges, and scientists actually need most of the time.

Bayes's essay provided a rule for doing this: a way of taking a starting assumption about how likely different causes are, updating that assumption in light of new evidence, and arriving at a revised, more informed assumption. The elegance of the rule is that it does not throw away what you believed before the new evidence arrived. It combines the old belief with the new evidence in a precise, quantitative way, weighting each according to how much support it actually provides.

## Laplace Builds a Machine From It

Bayes's own essay was narrow in scope and mathematically incomplete in places, and it might have remained a historical footnote if not for the French mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace, who arrived at very similar ideas independently and pushed them dramatically further. Laplace was one of the most ambitious scientific minds of his era, and he applied what is now called Bayesian reasoning to an enormous range of problems: estimating the mass of Saturn from incomplete astronomical observations, judging the reliability of testimony in courtroom proceedings, and analyzing the ratio of male to female births recorded in Paris. In each case, the method followed the same pattern Bayes had sketched: begin with an assumption, however rough, about what is likely to be true; gather evidence; and use the evidence to reshape the assumption into something sharper and better justified. Laplace's mathematical machinery, more than Bayes's own essay, is what turned inverse probability into a working tool that scientists could actually use, which is part of why some historians argue that the theorem might just as fairly be named after him.

## A Doctor, a Test, and a Number That Surprises Almost Everyone

The clearest way to feel why this idea matters is through a scenario that has nothing to do with urns or billiard balls. Imagine a medical test for a rare condition, a test that is quite accurate, correctly flagging the condition in the overwhelming majority of people who truly have it, and correctly clearing the overwhelming majority of people who do not. Now imagine a patient tests positive. It is tempting to assume the patient is now almost certainly ill, because the test is "accurate." But if the condition is rare enough in the general population, the mathematics can work out so that most people who test positive do not actually have it, simply because there are so many more healthy people taking the test than sick ones, and even a small false-positive rate applied to a huge pool of healthy people can generate more false alarms than the smaller pool of genuinely sick people generates correct alarms.

This is the base rate at work, and Bayes's rule is precisely the tool for combining it correctly with the test's accuracy. Ignoring the base rate — treating a positive test result as though it existed in a vacuum, disconnected from how common the condition actually is — is one of the most common reasoning errors in medicine, law, and everyday judgment, and it is exactly the error Bayes's essay quietly corrects. The theorem does not just say that new evidence matters; it specifies, with precision, exactly how much it should move a reasonable person's prior belief, given how strong the evidence really is and how surprising the claim was to begin with.

## Exile and Revival

For much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, Bayesian reasoning fell out of favor among many professional statisticians, who worried that it required starting with a subjective assumption — the prior belief — before any data had been collected at all, and that different people with different priors could reach different conclusions from the same evidence. A rival school, often called frequentist statistics, tried to build a framework that avoided specifying prior beliefs altogether, relying instead on the long-run frequency of outcomes under repeated sampling. For a long stretch of the twentieth century, frequentist methods dominated scientific publishing, taught in nearly every introductory statistics course, while Bayesian methods survived mostly at the margins, kept alive by a smaller community of dedicated practitioners in fields like actuarial work and some corners of physics.

The rise of cheap, powerful computing changed the balance considerably. Many Bayesian calculations that were mathematically hopeless to carry out by hand became entirely tractable once computers could simulate their way to an answer, and the field saw a substantial revival beginning in the latter part of the twentieth century. Today, Bayesian updating sits underneath a great deal of everyday technology without most people noticing it. A spam filter maintains a running belief about how likely an email is to be junk, and updates that belief word by word as it scans the message, treating each suspicious phrase as evidence in exactly the sense Bayes described. Search engines, recommendation systems, and self-driving cars all lean on the same basic move: start with an assumption, encounter evidence, and revise the assumption in proportion to how strongly the evidence actually supports the revision. It is a strikingly modest piece of eighteenth-century reasoning to find running quietly behind so much of contemporary life, discovered by a minister who never saw his own essay published and could not have imagined the uses it would eventually be put to.
