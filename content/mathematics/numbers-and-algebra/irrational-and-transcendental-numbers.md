# Irrational and Transcendental Numbers

Draw a square with sides exactly one unit long and measure its diagonal. The length is there, plainly, a specific physical distance you could mark on a ruler if your ruler were fine enough — and yet no fraction of whole numbers, however large you allow the numerator and denominator to grow, will ever express it exactly. According to a story handed down from antiquity, probably more legend than verified history but persistent for good reason, this discovery unsettled a school of thinkers built around the belief that whole numbers and their ratios were the foundation of all order in the universe, and one of their own, a mathematician sometimes named as Hippasus, was said to have drowned, whether by accident, punishment, or myth-making embellishment nobody can now say, shortly after revealing it. True or not, the story captures something real: the discovery that some lengths simply refuse to be ratios was, for the people who first ran into it, a small crisis.

## A Crack in the Pythagorean Universe

The proof that the diagonal of a unit square cannot be a fraction is short enough to carry around in your head, and it works by assuming the opposite and watching the assumption collapse. Suppose the diagonal's length could be written as a fraction of two whole numbers, reduced as far as it will go, with no common factor left between them. Squaring that fraction has to give exactly 2, since that is what the geometry demands. A little algebra shows that the numerator of the fraction must then be even, which forces the denominator to be even as well, once you trace the consequences through — but a fraction reduced as far as possible was never supposed to have both a numerator and a denominator divisible by 2 at the same time. The assumption devours itself. No such fraction can exist, and the diagonal's length, which today gets called an irrational number, sits on the number line at a precise, fixed location that no ratio of whole numbers will ever land on exactly.

## What Makes a Number Irrational

An irrational number, defined plainly, is any real number that cannot be written as one whole number divided by another. Its decimal expansion never terminates and never settles into a repeating cycle, which distinguishes it sharply from fractions like one-third, whose decimal expansion repeats a pattern of digits forever but at least repeats. Once mathematicians had one confirmed irrational number, more followed quickly: the square root of any whole number that is not itself a perfect square turns out to be irrational, by an argument that closely mirrors the one for the square root of 2. For a long time, "irrational" and "hard to pin down exactly" were treated as roughly the same idea, but a further, sharper distinction was waiting underneath.

## Beyond Algebra: The Transcendentals

Call a number algebraic if it is a solution to some polynomial equation built from whole-number coefficients — the square root of 2, despite being irrational, is algebraic, because it solves the tidy equation x squared equals 2. For a long time, mathematicians wondered whether every irrational number might secretly be algebraic in this sense, the solution to some polynomial equation if you only looked hard enough or allowed a high enough degree. The answer turned out to be no. Numbers that are not the solution to any polynomial equation with whole-number coefficients, of any degree whatsoever, are called transcendental, because they transcend the reach of ordinary algebra entirely. Proving that any specific number belongs to this category is considerably harder than proving mere irrationality, since you have to rule out an infinite family of possible polynomial equations all at once, rather than just ruling out fractions.

The constant e, the base of natural logarithms and one of the most important numbers in all of mathematics, was shown to be transcendental by Charles Hermite in the 1870s, the first naturally occurring mathematical constant to be proven so. Pi turned out to be transcendental too, established shortly afterward through an argument that built cleverly on Hermite's methods, a fact with its own separate and celebrated history tied to an old geometric puzzle about circles and squares.

## Building a Number Designed to Escape

Rather than wait for a naturally occurring constant to be proven transcendental, the mathematician Joseph Liouville took a more direct approach earlier in the nineteenth century: he built a number specifically engineered to be transcendental, and proved a general theorem showing it had to be. His insight rested on how well algebraic irrational numbers can be approximated by fractions. It turns out there is a strict limit to how astonishingly close a simple fraction can get to an algebraic irrational number, a limit that gets harder to beat as the fraction's denominator grows, tied to the degree of the polynomial the number solves. Liouville constructed a decimal number, built from a very specific and sparse pattern of digits placed far apart from each other, that could be approximated by fractions far more closely than that limit allows for any algebraic number. Since no algebraic number could ever be approximated that well, his constructed number could not be algebraic, and therefore had to be transcendental — the first number ever proven transcendental by design rather than discovered and later analyzed.

## Almost Everything Is Transcendental

Here is the strangest twist in the story: even though naming a specific transcendental number is hard, and proving a given number is transcendental can be extraordinarily difficult, transcendental numbers vastly, overwhelmingly outnumber algebraic ones. The algebraic numbers, despite including every fraction, every root of a whole number, and every solution to every polynomial equation with whole-number coefficients you could ever write down, form only a thin, countable scattering along the number line. The real numbers as a whole are a strictly larger kind of infinity, and once you remove the countable algebraic numbers from that larger infinity, what remains, the transcendental numbers, is essentially all of it. In a very precise sense, a real number picked entirely at random would almost certainly be transcendental; algebraic numbers, familiar and countable as they are, are the rare exception rather than the rule. And yet, of that overwhelming majority, mathematicians have only ever managed to prove transcendence for a relatively small named list, a strange asymmetry between how common these numbers are and how hard each individual one is to pin down and certify.
