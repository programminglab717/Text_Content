# Why Imaginary Numbers Matter

Call something "imaginary" and you have more or less told people not to take it seriously. That is exactly the fate that befell a class of numbers first encountered by Renaissance algebraists working on formulas for solving cubic equations, equations involving a quantity cubed rather than merely squared. Their formulas worked, arriving at perfectly ordinary, sensible answers, but only if the mathematician was willing to pass through an intermediate step involving the square root of a negative number, a quantity that seemed to describe nothing at all, since no real number multiplied by itself produces a negative result. Mathematicians of the sixteenth century found this deeply uncomfortable, tolerated it only because it kept producing correct final answers, and left it saddled with a name, imaginary, that mathematics has never quite managed to shake off even after the numbers turned out to be indispensable.

## A Troubling Detour in an Old Formula

The basic move was simple to write down and hard to accept. Define a new quantity, usually written as i, with the defining property that squaring it gives negative one. Ordinary numbers combined with multiples of this new quantity, in the form of an ordinary number plus some multiple of i, became known as complex numbers. Early on, i was treated as little more than bookkeeping, a symbol algebraists pushed through a calculation and hoped would cancel out by the time they reached the final, real-numbered answer, which it very often did. Only slowly did mathematicians begin asking a more productive question: rather than treating i as a regrettable detour, what if it were a legitimate number in its own right, describing something real about the structure of numbers, just not something you could point to on the ordinary number line? Even so, the discomfort ran deep and lasted a long time; prominent mathematicians of later generations continued to refer to such numbers as impossible or fictitious even while quietly using them to get correct results, a strange kind of working relationship with an object nobody yet trusted.

## A Number That Also Points Somewhere

The turning point came from a shift in how complex numbers were pictured. Rather than squeezing a complex number onto the familiar single line used for ordinary numbers, several mathematicians working around the turn of the nineteenth century, including Caspar Wessel, Jean-Robert Argand, and later Carl Friedrich Gauss, proposed plotting them on a plane: the ordinary, "real" part of the number measured along a horizontal axis, and the "imaginary" part measured along a vertical axis. A complex number stopped being a suspicious algebraic byproduct and became, instead, a point, or equivalently an arrow, with both a length and a direction. This single reframing dissolved most of the old discomfort. There was nothing incoherent about a number encoding two pieces of information, a size and a direction, at once; ordinary velocity does exactly that, and nobody calls velocity imaginary. Once mathematicians had this picture in hand, adding two complex numbers became as simple as adding two arrows tip to tail, and the entire subject acquired a geometric intuition it had previously lacked completely.

## Rotation in Disguise

Once complex numbers had a plane to live on, multiplication by i revealed itself as something almost mechanical rather than mysterious: it rotates whatever arrow you multiply it by a quarter turn, exactly ninety degrees, around the origin of the plane. Multiply by i twice, a total half turn, and you land pointing in exactly the opposite direction, which is another way of saying you have flipped the sign of the original number — precisely what squaring i to get negative one was quietly encoding all along, translated out of pure algebra and into a picture you could actually watch happen. This connection between multiplying by i and rotating by a quarter turn extends further: any complex number can be understood as an instruction combining a stretch and a rotation together, and multiplying two complex numbers means combining their stretches and adding their rotation angles. One of the most celebrated results in all of mathematics, a formula credited to Leonhard Euler, ties this rotational behavior directly to the exponential function: raising the constant e to an imaginary exponent does not produce runaway growth the way raising it to an ordinary real exponent would; instead it produces pure rotation, tracing out a perfect circle in the complex plane and linking, in one compact relationship, exponential growth, rotation, and the trigonometry of circles, three ideas that look entirely unrelated until complex numbers show they were never separate at all.

## Completing Algebra

Complex numbers also solved a nagging structural problem in algebra that had nothing to do with cubic equations specifically. A basic polynomial equation, built from a variable raised to various whole-number powers, is supposed to have a number of solutions matching its highest power, but working only with real numbers, plenty of polynomial equations of that kind come up short, having fewer real solutions than their degree would suggest, or none at all. Allow complex numbers into the picture, and this shortfall vanishes entirely: every polynomial equation with a degree of one or higher has exactly as many complex solutions as its degree, counted properly, no exceptions, ever. This result, proven rigorously by Gauss and now called the fundamental theorem of algebra, meant that complex numbers were not a strange extra add-on bolted awkwardly onto the number system, but the missing piece that made algebra behave consistently for the first time, closing a gap that real numbers alone could never close on their own. Mathematicians sometimes describe the complex numbers as algebraically closed for exactly this reason: whatever polynomial equation you write using them, the solutions you need are already guaranteed to live inside the same system, with no need to invent yet another still-stranger kind of number to accommodate some future equation.

## Where Complex Numbers Go to Work

The payoff for taking complex numbers seriously eventually showed up far outside pure algebra. Electrical engineers describing circuits that run on alternating current found that resistance alone could not fully capture how a circuit responds, since coils and capacitors introduce a timing shift between voltage and current in addition to simply opposing the flow of charge; representing both effects together as a single complex number, called impedance, lets engineers combine and analyze entire circuits using the same straightforward arithmetic they would use for plain resistors, with the rotation built into complex multiplication automatically tracking the timing shifts. Engineers and scientists analyzing signals of every kind, from sound waves to radio transmissions, found that representing a signal as a sum of rotating complex exponentials, rather than ordinary sine and cosine waves treated separately, made otherwise unwieldy calculations collapse into much simpler ones. Quantum mechanics went further still, describing the fundamental state of a physical system using a wavefunction that is complex-valued by necessity, not merely for calculational convenience; nobody has found a way to describe the theory's predictions using real numbers alone, and the interference patterns that give quantum mechanics its strangest character emerge directly from how these complex quantities combine.

None of this was visible to the sixteenth-century algebraists who first stumbled into the square root of a negative number while chasing an entirely different problem, and stuck with an insulting name for a real, load-bearing piece of arithmetic almost by accident of history.
