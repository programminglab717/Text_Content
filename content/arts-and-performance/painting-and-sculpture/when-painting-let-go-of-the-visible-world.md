# When Painting Let Go of the Visible World

In December 1915, in a Petrograd gallery, Kazimir Malevich hung a black square
on a white ground high in the corner of the room, where the two walls met the
ceiling. Russian visitors would have understood the placement instantly. That
corner is where an icon goes in a Russian house. Malevich was not being coy
about the size of his claim.

The painting itself is unremarkable to describe: a quadrilateral of black paint,
not quite square, not quite centered, on a white field. The surface has since
crazed into a web of fine cracks, and underneath it — modern imaging has
confirmed what the cracks hinted — there are earlier compositions. It is one of
the most consequential objects made in the twentieth century, and the strangest
thing about it is that Malevich was not alone. Within about five years, in at
least four countries, painters who mostly did not know each other arrived at the
same destination by different roads.

## Not One Origin

The tidy version of the story has Kandinsky inventing abstraction in Munich
around 1910 and everyone else following. The tidy version is wrong in several
directions.

In Paris, František Kupka — a Czech who had supported himself as a spiritualist
medium before turning to painting full time — was exhibiting canvases of pure
colored arcs by 1912 with titles borrowed from music. Robert and Sonia Delaunay
were pushing color contrast until the subject dissolved out of it, and
Apollinaire gave their work a name, Orphism, that put the musical analogy right
in the label. In Moscow, Mikhail Larionov and Natalia Goncharova were building
compositions out of intersecting rays. In the Netherlands, Piet Mondrian spent
the war years reducing a pier jutting into the sea to a lattice of short
horizontal and vertical strokes, and then let go of the pier.

And in Stockholm, years before any of them, Hilma af Klint had already made
large non-representational canvases — spirals, nested circles, forms that look
startlingly like the diagrams of a science that had not yet been written. She
began around 1906, working with a group of women who conducted séances and
believed the pictures were dictated to them. She also concluded that the world
was not ready, and directed that the paintings be kept from public view for
decades after her death. They were. Her absence from the story for most of a
century is a reminder that art history records what got exhibited, not what got
made.

## The Pressure from Below

The explanation everyone reaches for is photography: the camera took over the
job of recording appearances, so painting had to find something else to do. It
is not wrong, but it is far too neat. Photography had been around for most of a
century by 1910, and the immediate effect on painting was not liberation but a
booming market for painted portraits among people who also had themselves
photographed. Painters had absorbed the camera long since — some used it
directly, and the strange cropping of Degas owes it something.

The sharper pressure came from inside the discipline. Impressionism had already
made the painted surface a subject in its own right: once you are looking at a
haystack rendered in unblended dabs of violet and orange, half your attention is
on the dabs. Kandinsky told the story of coming into his studio at dusk and
seeing a canvas of "indescribable beauty" leaning against the wall, which turned
out to be one of his own paintings on its side, unrecognizable as its subject.
He also described standing in front of a Monet haystack in Moscow and being
unable to identify what it depicted — and finding, to his unease, that it worked
on him anyway.

That is the real discovery, and it is an empirical one. If a picture still
moves you when you cannot tell what it shows, then whatever is doing the moving
is not the subject. Color, interval, weight, rhythm, the pull between a mass at
one edge and a void at the other: these were doing work all along, underneath
the ostensible content. The question that produced abstraction was not "what can
painting do now that the camera exists," but "what happens if we remove the
subject and keep only the part that was actually operating."

Cubism supplied the crowbar. Braque and Picasso spent the years before the war
taking a guitar or a seated figure apart into shifting facets, and by about 1911
their canvases had become near-illegible thickets of ochre and grey where only a
clef, a moustache, a rope of chair caning told you that anything was being
depicted. They stopped there, deliberately; both men insisted on keeping a
thread back to the visible object. But they had demonstrated in public that a
painting could survive the near-total loss of its subject and remain gripping.
Others declined to keep the thread.

## The Argument from Music

Nearly everyone who made the leap reached for music to justify it, and the
reason is obvious once stated. Here was an art everyone agreed was profound,
which represented nothing whatsoever. A sonata is not a picture of anything. It
moves people to tears by means of pure relation — tension and release, interval,
tempo, timbre. Walter Pater had written in the nineteenth century that all art
constantly aspires to the condition of music, and by 1910 painters were taking
the line as a set of instructions rather than a bon mot.

So the titles went musical. Kandinsky called his canvases Improvisations and
Compositions and grouped them by number like symphonies. Kupka reached for the
fugue. The analogy gave abstraction a defense against the obvious objection —
that a painting of nothing is about nothing — and it gave painters a working
vocabulary. If a yellow could be shrill and a blue could be deep, then color had
a behavior independent of what it described, and a canvas could be orchestrated.

Kandinsky, who seems to have experienced some genuine cross-sensory blending,
pushed this further than most, assigning specific instruments to specific hues
and writing an entire book, published in Munich around 1911, to lay out the
psychology of form and color. Much of the system does not survive contact with
scrutiny. Its influence was enormous anyway, because it was the first sustained
attempt to say what a non-representational painting could be for.

## The Occult Engine

Modern audiences tend to find the next part embarrassing and skip it, which
distorts the history badly. A remarkable proportion of the first abstract
painters were involved with Theosophy, spiritualism, or Rudolf Steiner's
anthroposophy. Kandinsky read Theosophical literature closely. Mondrian was a
member of the Theosophical Society for most of his adult life. Af Klint's
paintings were, in her own account, guided by entities she met in séance. Kupka
had worked as a medium.

These movements offered exactly what a painter abandoning appearances needed: a
detailed doctrine holding that the visible world is a crust over a realer
structure, that this structure is geometric and vibratory, and — crucially —
that it can be depicted. Theosophical writers had already published books of
colored diagrams purporting to show the shapes that emotions and thoughts take
in a subtler medium. A painter who believed this was not abandoning
representation at all. He was representing something else, something he thought
more real than a bowl of fruit.

Mondrian's case is the clearest. His grids of black lines and primary rectangles
are usually taught as design, as the pure formal endpoint of a reduction. He
described them as an attempt to render a universal equilibrium between opposed
forces — vertical against horizontal, matter against spirit — and he cared
intensely about whether a given canvas achieved it. He forbade green. He
rejected diagonals so firmly that he broke with his closest collaborator,
Theo van Doesburg, over their introduction. That is not the behavior of a man
arranging shapes he finds pleasant.

## Zero, and the Problem of Wallpaper

Malevich went the other way. Suprematism, as he named it in a pamphlet issued
alongside the 1915 exhibition, was not a route to a hidden world but an arrival
at zero — the elimination of every object so that pure feeling could be given
form. The black square was a floor, a point below which painting could not be
taken. Others promptly tried anyway. A few years later in Moscow, Alexander
Rodchenko exhibited three canvases of flat red, flat yellow, and flat blue and
declared painting finished, its remaining task to be taken up by design,
photography, and industry.

It was not finished, of course. But the anxiety underneath that gesture had been
there from the start, and it is worth naming, because it is the strongest
objection abstraction has ever faced. If a painting shows nothing, what
distinguishes it from decoration? Islamic tile, Celtic interlace, and any
competent textile had been arranging non-representational form beautifully for
centuries without anyone calling it a revolution. Kandinsky worried in print
that his pictures might amount to nothing more than ornament — that they would
come out looking like a necktie.

The answer the first abstractionists gave was that their paintings had content,
just not depicted content: spiritual, emotional, structural. The answer later
painters gave was different and more austere — that the work is about the
conditions of its own making, the scale of the field, the behavior of the
material, the encounter with a viewer standing in front of it. Neither answer
has ever fully closed the question. Which is probably why a black quadrilateral
painted in a Russian winter can still start an argument in a gallery.
