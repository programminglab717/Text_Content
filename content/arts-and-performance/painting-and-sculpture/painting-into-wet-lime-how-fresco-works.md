# Painting Into Wet Lime: How Fresco Works

Stand close to an Italian wall painting from the fourteenth or fifteenth
century, preferably in raking light from a window off to one side, and you will
start to see seams. They run in irregular closed shapes across the surface,
skirting a shoulder here, following the edge of a cloak there, occasionally
cutting straight across a patch of sky where the painter had nothing to hide
behind. Each shape is one day's work. The whole wall is a patchwork of them,
assembled in sequence over weeks, and once you have learned to see the seams you
cannot unsee them. They are the clearest evidence that fresco is not really a
style of painting. It is a schedule imposed by a chemical reaction.

## The Wall Is the Binder

Ordinary paint is pigment plus something sticky: egg yolk, gum arabic, drying
oil, acrylic emulsion. Buon fresco has no binder at all. The pigment is ground
in plain water and brushed onto plaster that is still damp, and the plaster
itself locks it in place.

The cycle starts in a kiln. Limestone, heated hard enough and long enough, gives
off carbon dioxide and becomes quicklime. Quicklime slaked with water becomes
calcium hydroxide, a caustic white putty that traditional workshops stored in
pits for months and often for years, because long-aged lime putty is smoother,
less prone to blistering, and easier to trowel. Mixed with clean sand, that putty
becomes plaster. And as plaster sits exposed to air, it slowly runs the kiln
reaction in reverse: calcium hydroxide takes carbon dioxide back out of the
atmosphere and reverts to calcium carbonate, which is to say back to limestone.
The reversion happens as a mesh of interlocking crystals growing at and just
below the surface. Pigment particles sitting in the wet skin get caught in that
mesh and become part of the stone.

This is why a genuine fresco is so durable. The color is not stuck to the wall;
it is inside the top fraction of a millimeter of it, mineral embedded in
mineral, with nothing organic to yellow, go brittle, or feed mold. It is also
why the technique is so unforgiving. Carbonation only draws pigment down while
there is enough moisture to carry it. When the surface dries, the window shuts,
and anything brushed on afterward is merely sitting on top.

## Arriccio, Sinopia, Cartoon

A fresco wall is built up in coats. First a coarse render of lime and gritty
sand is thrown onto the masonry and keyed so it grips, then left to cure. Onto
this rough layer — the arriccio — the painter set out the composition at full
size, brushing it in freehand with a red earth pigment from Sinope on the Black
Sea, which is where the underdrawing gets its name: the sinopia. These drawings
were invisible once the final coat went on, and nobody expected them to be seen
again, which makes them unusually direct. They are working drawings, confident
and fast, with figures blocked in and abandoned and redrawn.

From the early fifteenth century the sinopia was gradually displaced by the
cartoon: a full-scale drawing on joined sheets of paper, worked up in the
studio, where mistakes cost paper rather than plaster. Transfer was done in one
of two ways. The outlines could be pricked with a needle and the paper held
against the fresh surface while a muslin bag of charcoal dust was pounced over
it, leaving dotted lines. Or the cartoon could be laid on the wet plaster and
its contours traced hard with a blunt stylus, pressing a groove into the
surface. Both traces survive, and both are visible today in oblique light.
Architecture was often incised directly with straightedge and compass, which is
how we know so much about the geometry underneath some celebrated pictures:
Masaccio's Trinity in Santa Maria Novella still carries the incised orthogonals
of its coffered vault, converging on a small hole where a nail or string was
anchored at the vanishing point.

## The Giornata

On painting day the plasterer applies the intonaco — a fine, smooth coat of
aged lime and washed sand, sometimes with marble dust — but only over the area
the painter expects to finish before it sets. That area is the giornata. In
practice a working day gave perhaps six to eight hours of usable dampness,
longer in a cold damp church, much shorter in summer.

The craft of dividing a wall into giornate is a real skill. Seams were cut along
edges that would disguise them: the line of a jaw, the fold of a sleeve, the
boundary between a figure and the ground behind it. Work proceeded from the top
down, so that runs and splashes fell on plaster not yet painted, and a head — the
passage that mattered most and could least afford a join through it — was almost
always given its own day. If the day went badly, the remedy was not to repaint
but to chop the intonaco out with a hammer, replaster, and start again the next
morning. Correction in fresco means demolition. The early campaign on the
Sistine ceiling reportedly had to be cut away after the plaster, made with a
Roman recipe that held its water too long, bloomed with mold.

Then there is the problem of judging color you cannot yet see. Fresh fresco
paint looks dark and saturated while wet and dries considerably lighter and
chalkier as the lime crystallizes over it. Painters had to work toward an
appearance that would not exist until the next day. Because the paint is thin
and largely transparent, modeling is built by hatching layer over layer, letting
the white of the lime supply the light, rather than by blending opaque tones the
way one can in oil.

## What Lime Will Not Tolerate

The other constraint is chemical. Wet lime is strongly alkaline and it destroys
a large part of the historical palette. What survives it is essentially the
geology of the earth: yellow and red ochres, siennas, umbers, green earth,
carbon black, and lime white made from aged putty itself. Lead white blackens.
Verdigris and orpiment break down. Organic lakes fade or are eaten outright.
Vermilion is unreliable and can darken.

Most conspicuously, lime destroys the blues. Azurite loses its color in an
alkaline environment, and natural ultramarine, ground from lapis lazuli, was far
too expensive to risk. So blue in a medieval or Renaissance wall painting was
nearly always applied after the plaster had set, bound in glue or egg — a
secco, on the dry. It is a good binder on a bad footing, and the consequence is
visible in any chapel of the period: the skies and the Virgin's mantle are
exactly the passages that have flaked, leaving a ghostly gray underpaint behind.
Giotto's Arena Chapel gives the effect in its purest form, brilliant lapis
skies overhead with losses scattered through them like static. Gold leaf,
tooled haloes, and final touches of expensive color went on the same way.

Painting a secco is not cheating, and whole traditions north of the Alps used
almost nothing else, working in lime wash or glue tempera on dry plaster
because it is faster, cheaper, and admits any pigment you like. It simply does
not last. Nearly every surviving wall painting is a mixture: a true fresco
core, with secco passages that have partly disappeared.

## Salt, Sulfur, and the Rescue That Backfired

The enemy of fresco is water. Moisture moving through masonry dissolves salts
from mortar, brick, and groundwater, carries them to the surface, and leaves
them there as it evaporates. If evaporation happens a millimeter behind the
paint film, the growing crystals push the surface off the wall in blisters and
powder. Airborne sulfur compounds do something worse: they convert calcium
carbonate into gypsum, which occupies more volume and, unlike limestone,
dissolves in water. A sulfated fresco surface turns soft and crusty and can be
rubbed away with a thumb.

Faced with walls rotting from behind, restorers in the nineteenth and twentieth
centuries developed detachment. In stacco, the intonaco is cut free and lifted
with its plaster. In strappo, canvas is glued to the painted face and peeled
off, tearing away only the thin colored skin. Strappo is violent and effective,
and in mid-twentieth-century Italy it became close to routine, accelerating
sharply after the Arno flooded Florence in 1966 and left hundreds of damaged
works needing emergency treatment. One unexpected dividend was scholarly: pull
the painting off and the sinopia underneath is exposed, and a whole literature
grew up around drawings no one had been meant to see.

The enthusiasm has since cooled. A detached fresco loses the depth and slight
translucency that came from color sitting inside plaster, it loses its wall, and
it usually loses the architecture that determined how it was composed in the
first place. Conservators now prefer to fix the water and leave the painting
where the plasterer put it, seam by seam, morning by morning.
