# How to Read a Still Life

Look for the lemon. In a certain kind of seventeenth-century Dutch picture there
is almost always a lemon, half-peeled, the rind spiraling down over the edge of
the table in one continuous curl so that it hangs in the air with the light
coming through it. The flesh is cut and glistening. Nobody is eating it. The
curl is a piece of showing off — a painter demonstrating that he can render
translucency, a cast shadow, and a shape that twists in three dimensions — and
it is also a small statement about money, because in northern Europe a lemon
came a very long way in a ship.

Still life rewards this kind of attention more reliably than any other genre,
because nothing in it is accidental. A landscape painter can plausibly claim he
painted what was in front of him. A still life painter put every single object
on that table himself.

## The Bottom of the Ladder

Academies ranked the genres, and still life came last. History painting sat at
the top — multiple figures, classical or scriptural subjects, the human body in
action, invention required. Portraiture came next, then scenes of daily life,
then landscape, and then, at the bottom, the painting of inanimate things, on
the reasoning that it demanded only patient copying and no imagination at all.
The French name for it, nature morte, carries that condescension in the words.

The hierarchy is worth knowing because it explains the career shape of almost
every still life painter before the twentieth century. They painted these
pictures because there was a market for them, and the market was in places where
the academic hierarchy carried less weight than a merchant's wall did — above
all in the Dutch Republic, a Protestant society with no large church commissions,
a great deal of new commercial money, and houses whose rooms wanted filling.
Specialization followed the market. Painters became known for fish, for flowers,
for dead game, for the glint on silver, and they repeated their specialty for
decades because it sold.

## The Table Is Not a Table

Start with the staging, because it is the most artificial thing in the picture
and the easiest to miss.

The objects nearly always sit on a shallow ledge or table edge that runs close
to parallel with the bottom of the canvas, seen from just above eye level — high
enough to show the tops of the vessels, low enough that the far edge of the
table is a horizon. Something almost always overhangs the front edge: the lemon
peel, a knife handle pointing out at the viewer, the corner of a crumpled
tablecloth. That overhang is doing a specific job. It breaks the plane of the
picture, insists the objects occupy space continuous with yours, and gives the
eye a way in.

The light comes from one side, usually the upper left, raking across the objects
at a steep enough angle to throw long shadows and separate everything from
everything else. It is theatrical lighting, not room lighting. In the most
austere Spanish work the staging becomes explicit: Juan Sánchez Cotán, a painter
who later became a Carthusian lay brother, set a quince and a cabbage hanging on
strings in a black window recess with a melon and a cut slice arranged on the
sill, everything spaced along a precise curve against absolute darkness. It is
closer to a devotional diagram than a meal.

## Things That Cannot Be in the Same Room

Here is a test you can run on any Dutch flower painting. Identify the blooms.
You will usually find tulips, roses, irises, carnations, anemones, sometimes a
crown imperial standing at the top, occasionally a sprig of apple blossom.

Those flowers do not bloom at the same time.

The bouquets are impossible. They were assembled from studies made over a year
or more, kept in the studio as a reference library and recombined to order,
which is why the same distinctive striped tulip turns up in several pictures by
the same hand. Knowing this changes the picture from a record of a vase to a
catalogue of desire — an arrangement nobody could buy, painted for people who
were intensely interested in the market for bulbs. Tulips had been the object of
a speculative mania in the 1630s, and the most coveted varieties were the ones
with flamed and feathered petals, an effect now known to be caused by a virus
carried between plants by aphids. Collectors were paying fortunes for a sick
flower.

The same logic runs through the luxury still lifes, the pronkstilleven, where
the display is frankly of imported goods: Chinese blue-and-white porcelain, a
Turkish carpet used as a tablecloth, a nautilus shell mounted in silver gilt, a
lidded salt, pepper wrapped in a twist of paper, a Venetian glass. These are
inventories of global trade, painted in the country running most of it. A viewer
in Amsterdam would have read each object's provenance the way a modern viewer
reads a logo.

## Hard Surfaces

A great deal of still life is technical showing off, and it is more enjoyable
once you know what is difficult.

Glass is difficult, because it has no color of its own and is depicted almost
entirely through what happens at its edges: the bright line where the rim
catches light, the darkening where the wall thickens, the distorted view of the
tablecloth through the bowl, the rippled highlight on a prunted German wine
glass. Half-full glass is harder still, since the liquid refracts whatever is
behind it and displaces the line of the far rim.

Pewter and silver are difficult in a different way. They have no local color
either — they are made entirely of reflections — so painting a pewter plate
means painting a compressed, curved image of the whole room. Painters exploited
this. Clara Peeters, working in Antwerp early in the seventeenth century, put
minute reflections into the polished surfaces of her vessels, and in several of
them a tiny figure appears that is generally taken to be the painter herself, at
her easel, reflected in a pewter lid a few centimeters across.

Then there are the textures that demonstrate patience: the bloom on a grape, the
matte dust on a plum, the crumb structure of a broken bread roll, the individual
barbs on a dead partridge's wing, the wet surface of an oyster in its shell with
the shell's nacre ghosting underneath. In the best of these pictures, the
painter has also thought about tempo — a few passages worked to a glassy finish,
others left summary, so the eye is led rather than exhausted.

## Decay on Schedule

Now count the things in the picture that are ending.

A very large group of Dutch still lifes, concentrated especially around the
university town of Leiden, are built on the theme of vanitas: everything here
passes. The vocabulary is fixed enough to be read almost word by word. A skull,
obviously. A candle snuffed out with its smoke still rising. An hourglass. A
watch, which is the mechanical successor to the hourglass and carries the same
message with a modern accent. Soap bubbles. A knocked-over roemer. Books and
sheet music, standing for learning and pleasure that the owner will not take
with him. Coins and jewels for the same reason. A flower already dropping its
petals.

Even the cheerful pictures carry the theme in smaller print. The half-eaten pie,
the knife left across the plate, the wine glass tipped on its side, the crumpled
napkin — every one of them says that somebody has been here and gone. And in the
flower pieces, look closely at the leaves. There will be a hole chewed by a
caterpillar, a browning edge, a fly standing on a petal. Painters put insects in
partly as a trompe-l'oeil joke, daring the viewer to brush the fly away, and
partly because a picture of perfect blooms without any corruption in it would
have been a lie about the world.

How seriously the original owners took the sermon is a real question. A painting
that warns you against the vanity of worldly possessions, executed in expensive
pigments, hung in your dining room, and depicting your own porcelain, is a
morally flexible object. It is likely that both readings were available at once,
and that this was part of the appeal.

## When the Symbols Ran Out

The genre survived the collapse of its symbolic language by becoming about
perception instead.

Chardin, in eighteenth-century Paris, painted a copper pot, a few eggs, a slab
of ray fish, a glass of water, and he painted them in a granular, slightly dry
touch that never resolves into illusion if you stand close. Step back and the
surface snaps into a rendering of light so convincing it is almost dull. There
is no skull, no lemon peel, no message. The subject is the quiet weight of
ordinary things and the fact that looking at them carefully is an experience
worth having.

Cézanne took the table apart. His apples sit on cloths that seem to be seen from
slightly above while the far edge of the table is seen from slightly to the
side, and the two accounts do not meet — a deliberate refusal to fix the viewer
in one spot, which is one of the doors through which twentieth-century painting
walked. He reportedly said he intended to astonish Paris with an apple, and the
remark is funny mostly because he was serious.

And Giorgio Morandi spent most of a working life in Bologna painting the same
few bottles, tins, and boxes, arranged on the same table, in a narrow range of
chalky greys, ochres and dusty pinks. He is said to have dulled the objects
themselves so they would not shine. Nothing in the pictures is symbolic and
nothing is dramatic. What changes from canvas to canvas is a few centimeters of
spacing, and once your eye adjusts, a few centimeters is an enormous amount.
