# Brunelleschi's Mirror and the Rules of Perspective

The most consequential painting of the early Renaissance is lost, and it was
about the size of a dinner plate. Filippo Brunelleschi, a goldsmith and
clockmaker who would later build the dome of Florence cathedral, painted a small
panel showing the octagonal Baptistery as seen from just inside the cathedral
door. Then he drilled a hole through it. The hole was narrow on the painted face
and widened toward the back, like a funnel, so that an eye pressed against it
looked out through a single point.

The demonstration went like this. You stood where Brunelleschi had stood, held
the panel with its painted face turned away from you, and looked through the
hole from behind. In your other hand you held a mirror at arm's length. The
mirror showed you the painting. Then you lowered the mirror, and there was the
actual Baptistery, in the same place, at the same size, with the same buildings
running off to either side. He had burnished the sky area in silver so that real
clouds would drift across it. The picture and the building were, from that one
spot, indistinguishable.

Nobody had proved anything like this before, and it is worth being precise about
what was proved. Not that painters should make things look real — they had been
trying to for a very long time. What the panel showed was that a flat surface
could be made to deliver the same bundle of light rays to an eye as a
three-dimensional scene does, and that this was a matter of geometry, which
meant it could be constructed rather than judged.

## What Was Already Known

Nothing here came out of empty air. Greek geometers had modeled vision as a cone
of rays with the eye at its apex, and Euclid's treatise on optics proves the
sort of propositions a painter needs: that equal magnitudes at unequal distances
subtend unequal angles, that parallel lines appear to draw together. Islamic
optics went much further. Ibn al-Haytham's great book, translated into Latin in
the thirteenth century and studied for three hundred years afterward, reversed
the direction of the rays — light travels from objects to the eye, not the other
way — and built a physical account of image formation on top of it. By the
fourteenth century perspectiva was a university subject, taught out of Latin
manuals, entirely familiar to educated Italians.

Painters, meanwhile, had been foreshortening for centuries. Roman wall painters
did it fluently. Giotto built solid boxy architecture that his figures could
plausibly stand inside. In Sienese painting the floor tiles converge toward the
back of a room, and the convergence is not random. But it was aimed by eye.
Orthogonals in a fourteenth-century interior tend to head for a region rather
than a point, and a picture with two buildings in it often runs two independent
systems that do not agree. Every part is plausible; the whole does not lock.

What was missing was a single rule, applying to everything in the picture at
once, anchored to one position for the viewer's eye. That is what Brunelleschi's
panel asserted and what the next generation wrote down.

## The Construction

Leon Battista Alberti published the method in a short treatise on painting in
1435, issuing an Italian version the following year with a dedication to
Brunelleschi. His famous formulation is that the picture surface should be
treated as an open window through which the subject is seen — more usefully, as
a plane slicing through the cone of rays running from the scene to the eye. The
painter's job is to record where each ray crosses the glass.

The practical recipe is almost embarrassingly simple. Decide how tall the viewer
is; draw a horizontal line at that height, which is the horizon. Choose a point
on it directly opposite the viewer's eye. Divide the bottom edge of the picture
into equal units — Alberti used a fraction of a human height, so that the scale
of the floor and the scale of the figures are the same scale — and run a line
from each division to that single point. Those are the orthogonals, the lines
that in the real world run straight away from you.

The hard part is the transversals: how far apart should the lines running across
the floor be? Guessing produces floors that look like they are tipping up. The
correct spacing depends on one more quantity, the distance from the eye to the
picture plane, and it can be found by drawing a small side elevation beside the
picture, or by marking a distance point on the horizon and running diagonals to
it. Either way the tiles get shallower toward the back by a specific, calculable
amount. And the construction carries its own check: if the foreshortening is
right, a line drawn through the corners of a diagonal run of tiles comes out
perfectly straight. If you have fudged it, the line bends, and it bends
visibly. A painter could now be wrong in a way that could be demonstrated on the
drawing board.

Geometry handles the floor and the buildings. For an irregular object — a body,
a horse, a heap of drapery — projecting every point by construction is possible
but murderous, so the period also produced instruments. Alberti described a
veil: a thin cloth stretched on a frame and ruled into squares with thread,
set between painter and subject, with a fixed sight to keep the eye in one
position. Whatever falls in a given square of the veil goes into the matching
square of a gridded sheet, and the foreshortening takes care of itself. Durer,
who went to Italy specifically to find out how the Italians were doing this, cut
woodcuts of several such devices: the gridded frame, a sighting rig with a taut
string standing in for a ray of light, a glass pane drawn on directly. They are
mechanical transcriptions of exactly the same idea Brunelleschi's drilled hole
enforced — hold the eye still, and record where the rays cross the plane.

## What It Did to Pictures

Masaccio's Trinity, painted on a wall in Santa Maria Novella within about a
decade of Alberti's treatise, is the earliest large statement of the new system.
A coffered barrel vault recedes into the wall, its coffers shrinking in exact
sequence, and the vanishing point sits low — at the eye level of a person
standing on the church floor. The painting is not a window onto a landscape; it
is a chapel that does not exist, cut convincingly into masonry that does.

The deeper change is that space now comes first. In a medieval picture, figures
are placed and then a setting is arranged around them, and scale follows
importance: the saint is larger because he matters more. Under the new system
the architecture is built first, and everything in it gets the size its position
allows. A donor kneeling in the foreground is bigger than the priest behind him
for one reason only, and the reason has nothing to do with theology.

Some painters took to it with unusual appetite. Paolo Uccello, according to
Vasari, would sit up all night over his vanishing points, telling his wife what
a sweet thing perspective was; his drawings of the faceted headdress called a
mazzocchio are exercises in projecting a complicated solid for no purpose beyond
the projecting of it. Piero della Francesca, who was a serious mathematician as
well as one of the great painters, wrote a whole treatise on painterly
perspective, working out point by laborious point how to project a column, a
cube set at an angle, a human head reduced to a grid of coordinates. The
procedure he describes is essentially what a computer does now to render a
scene, carried out by hand with a straightedge.

## The Fine Print

The system has costs, and they were understood early. It describes what a
single, motionless, one-eyed viewer sees from one exact spot. Stand somewhere
else and the picture is strictly incorrect, though the brain is generous about
this and compensates without being asked — which is fortunate, since almost
nobody views paintings from the prescribed point.

More awkward is what happens at the edges. A correct projection onto a flat
plane stretches things that lie well off to the side: a row of identical columns
comes out fattest at the margins, and a sphere projects as an ellipse. The
mathematics is not wrong — a real column at the edge of your visual field does
subtend that angle — but the result looks distorted, because in life you turn
your head and it never lands on your retina that way. Painters handle this by
cheating, drawing the outer columns as they ought to look rather than as the
construction demands, and the cheat goes unnoticed.

Push the same geometry the other way and you get anamorphosis: a form projected
for a viewpoint so extreme that from the front it is an unreadable smear, and
from the side it snaps into focus. Holbein put a skull across the bottom of a
double portrait that way. It is the same rule, used as a trick, and it makes
plain how arbitrary the ordinary case is.

## A Convention That Feels Like Fact

Because the camera obeys the same geometry, linear perspective now looks less
like a convention than like a description of how seeing works, and it takes some
effort to remember that it is a choice. Chinese landscape painters, who had
excellent mathematics available, kept to parallel projection: receding lines in a
handscroll stay parallel, so a pavilion holds the same proportions wherever it
appears, and no point in the picture claims to be where the viewer is standing.
That is not a failed attempt at the vanishing point. A handscroll is opened a
forearm's width at a time and read across; it is built for a moving eye, and a
single fixed station point would be useless in it.

European painting eventually made the same discovery from the other side. When
Cézanne tilted a tabletop to show more of the fruit, or when a Cubist canvas
reported a bottle from three angles at once, the complaint that these pictures
were incorrect missed what they were rejecting. Five centuries of painting had
quietly asked the audience to do what Brunelleschi asked: stand in one place,
close one eye, hold still.
