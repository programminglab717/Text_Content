# The Workshop Behind a Japanese Woodblock Print

Tilt a good nineteenth-century Japanese print under a raking light and the
surface stops being flat. The paper is faintly quilted where a printer's pad
drove it down into the carved block. The black outlines sit slightly sunken.
Areas of flat color have a soft nap, because the pigment soaked into the fibers
rather than sitting on top of them the way oil ink does. Nothing about the sheet
announces that four separate trades made it, and the artist whose name appears
in the corner probably never touched a block, a knife, or a sheet of damp paper.

That division of labor is not a footnote to ukiyo-e. It is the reason the prints
look the way they do.

## Four Trades, One Sheet

The person who organized everything was the publisher, the hanmoto. He put up
the capital, judged what would sell, commissioned the design, owned the blocks
once they were cut, and took the risk if the edition sat unsold in the shop. A
publisher with a good eye could make a career — Tsutaya Jūzaburō, who issued
some of Utamaro's finest work and the whole strange, brief output of Sharaku,
was as consequential to the art form as any designer.

The artist supplied a drawing in ink on thin paper: the hanshita-e. It was not a
precious object. It was a working document, and it was destroyed in the course
of being used, because the carver pasted it face down onto the block and cut
straight through it. Whatever the artist had drawn survived only as the ridges
of wood left standing after everything around them was gouged away.

The carver, the horishi, was the most exacting of the four. Working with a small
knife held like a dagger and drawn toward the body, he cut on both sides of
every line the artist had drawn, then cleared the waste between the lines with
chisels and gouges. The finest work — the wisps of hair at a courtesan's temple,
where individual strands narrow to a width close to that of a hair itself — was
usually reserved for a senior hand in the shop, while apprentices cleared
background. Hair carving was a specialty in its own right.

The printer, the surishi, had the least glamorous job and arguably the most
influence over the finished object. He mixed the pigments, judged how wet the
paper should be, decided how hard to press, and produced the gradations that
give the best prints their atmosphere. Two printers working from identical
blocks could produce sheets that do not look like the same image.

## Cherry, Knife, and the Key Block

The wood was mountain cherry, cut plankwise — along the grain, not across it.
This matters. European wood engravers of the same era worked on the polished
end grain of boxwood, a surface with essentially no directional structure, which
is why their work could hold dense crosshatched tone. Cherry planks have a grain
that the knife must constantly negotiate, and a carver cutting a long curve is
always crossing from easy cutting to difficult and back. Japanese carvers
responded by favoring clean contour lines over hatching, and by turning the
block rather than the knife when a curve demanded it.

The first block cut was the key block, the one carrying every outline. From it,
the shop pulled a set of proofs in plain black ink. The artist — or the
publisher, or both — marked on these proofs which areas were to take which
color. Each marked proof was then pasted onto a fresh block, and the carver cut
away everything that was not that color. A print with a dozen colors needed a
corresponding stack of blocks, though both faces of a plank were commonly used
and two small color areas that never touched could share one surface.

## The Notch and the Bar

Printing a color exactly inside an outline requires the paper to land in the
same place every time, and the solution the Japanese trade arrived at is so
simple it is easy to overlook. Cut into the margin of every block, in identical
positions, were two shallow registration marks called kentō: an L-shaped notch
at one corner and a straight bar along the adjacent edge. The printer pushed the
sheet into the corner of the L, laid it down against the bar, and let it fall.

No pins, no frames, no mechanism. The whole color system of ukiyo-e rests on a
carpenter's notch. It is accurate to a fraction of a millimeter in skilled
hands, and when you find a print where the reds sit a hair outside the black
contours, you are usually looking at a late impression pulled by a tired shop
from worn blocks, or at paper that shifted because its moisture was wrong.

## Damp Paper, Rice Paste, and the Baren

Japanese printers used no press. Paper was handmade washi, most often from the
long inner-bark fibers of paper mulberry, and it was dampened and interleaved
the night before so that every sheet in the stack carried the same amount of
water. Damp paper takes pigment into its fiber; dry paper lets it sit on the
surface and skid.

The colorants were water-based — mineral and vegetable pigments, and a stick of
sumi ink ground for the black — and on their own they would have soaked
straight through. So the printer brushed a dab of rice paste onto the block
along with the color, and worked the two together with a stiff brush until the
paste gave the pigment enough body to sit where it was put. The proportion of
paste to pigment was a judgment made fresh for every color and every day's
humidity, and it is the single variable that most separates a luminous
impression from a muddy one.

The pressure came from the baren: a disc of coiled cord, traditionally twisted
from bamboo sheath, packed into a backing and wrapped in a smooth bamboo skin.
The printer laid the sheet on the block, held it at the registration corner, and
rubbed the back of the paper in circles or sweeps, bearing down harder where he
wanted saturation. Because the pressure was applied by hand and not by a
machine, it could vary within a single pull — which is what makes bokashi
possible. To grade a sky from deep blue at the top to nothing at the horizon,
the printer wiped pigment onto only part of the block and feathered it out with
a damp brush before each impression. Every sheet had to be graded individually.
No two are quite alike, and the gradient in Hiroshige's skies exists nowhere on
the block at all.

## What the Market Wanted

These were commercial goods, not editions. There were no numbers, no signatures
in pencil, no artificial scarcity. A popular design was printed as long as it
sold and the blocks held out; when a line wore shallow it might be recut,
sometimes crudely. A single sheet cost roughly what a townsman might spend on a
meal out. That price point shaped everything — the subjects were what the city
of Edo wanted to look at, which meant kabuki actors in their signature roles,
the famous beauties of the pleasure districts, sumo wrestlers, ghost stories,
travel views, erotica, and later on the foreigners who began appearing in the
treaty ports.

It also meant official attention. The Tokugawa authorities periodically issued
edicts against luxury and against depictions they considered subversive or
merely expensive, at times limiting the number of blocks that could be used in a
single print. Publishers absorbed these rules the way commercial printers
usually do: by complying loudly and finding a workaround. Censor seals stamped
into the margins of surviving prints are, incidentally, a gift to modern
scholars, since their changing designs allow sheets to be dated within a narrow
window.

Technology moved through the trade quickly when it was cheap enough. The
synthetic pigment known in Japan as bero-ai — Prussian blue, imported through
the Dutch trade — became affordable in the early nineteenth century, and its
arrival is visible in the landscape series that followed. It was stable, deep,
and it did not fade the way the traditional blue from dayflower petals did. A
whole fashion for prints worked almost entirely in blue followed. The wave off
Kanagawa, and the Fuji views it belongs to, are unimaginable in the older
palette.

## After the Trade Collapsed

The system did not survive contact with industrial printing. Lithography,
photography, and mechanized presses undercut the woodblock shops within a
generation of the Meiji restoration, and the carvers and printers whose skills
had taken a lifetime to acquire found the demand for them gone. By a historical
irony that has been noted often, prints were already flowing to Europe by then,
where Degas, Whistler, Van Gogh, and Mary Cassatt were studying the flat color
fields, the cropped compositions, and the high viewpoints, and taking from them
permission to abandon a great deal of Western pictorial convention.

Two revivals followed in the twentieth century. One, shin-hanga, kept the old
four-part workshop intact and pointed it at subjects — misty landscapes,
domestic interiors — pitched partly at foreign collectors. The other,
sōsaku-hanga, rejected the division of labor outright and insisted that the
artist design, carve, and print the block himself, on the grounds that the marks
of the maker's own hand were the point. Both positions are still argued. What
neither could restore was the thing that made the original trade possible: a
city full of people who bought pictures the way they bought food, and a set of
workshops that could make them, one damp sheet at a time, for the price of a
good dinner.
