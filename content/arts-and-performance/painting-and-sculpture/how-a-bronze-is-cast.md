# How a Bronze Is Cast

By Benvenuto Cellini's own telling, the night he cast his Perseus nearly
destroyed him. He was ill and feverish and had gone to bed convinced he would
die; his workshop roof caught fire; a storm came in off the Arno; the furnace
crusted over and the metal in it went sluggish and refused to run. He got up,
came down, drove his assistants, had the fire fed harder, and when the alloy
still would not flow he sent men to gather every pewter plate, bowl, and
porringer in the house and threw the household's tableware into the melt. The
metal loosened. The mold filled. The figure came out short of bronze only in the
toes of one foot, which he repaired afterward.

Cellini wrote his autobiography to be admired, and the story has the shape of a
tale polished across many retellings. But the technical details are sound, and
that is what makes it worth repeating. Everything in it is a real hazard of the
process: metal that stiffens because the furnace lost temperature, tin added to
a copper-heavy melt to make it run thinner, and a mold that fills right to the
last extremity or does not. Casting a large bronze is an operation with one
irreversible moment in it, and the whole elaborate apparatus around that moment
exists to make the pour survive contact with physics.

## A Trade of Metallurgists

Bronze is copper alloyed mainly with tin, usually somewhere around a tenth tin
by weight for statuary, often with some lead added. Each ingredient is doing a
job. Pure copper is difficult to cast well: it melts high, absorbs gas from the
furnace atmosphere, and comes out of the mold porous and stiff. Tin lowers the
melting point, makes the liquid metal flow further into thin sections before it
freezes, and hardens the result. Lead, which does not really dissolve into the
alloy so much as disperse through it, makes the melt run thinner still and the
finished metal easier to work with a chisel afterward. Roman founders used a
good deal of it. So did later European workshops, and the mixture varies by
period and region enough that an alloy analysis can help place an undated
object.

A founder therefore has to be a metallurgist before he is anything else, and
before reliable thermometers existed he judged everything by eye: the color of
the melt, the way a skin formed on its surface, the behavior of the metal on a
test rod. The same trade cast cannon and church bells. A man who could fill a
bell mold could fill a statue, and in many cities the two jobs were done by the
same family.

## Wax, Direct and Indirect

The technique that dominates figurative casting is lost wax, cire perdue, and it
comes in two versions that differ in one consequential respect.

In the direct method, the sculptor builds the model in wax — over a rough core
of clay and plaster if the piece is to be hollow — and works that wax as the
final surface. Every detail in the bronze is a detail he cut into that wax. The
wax is then encased, melted out, and replaced by metal, which means the original
is annihilated in the process of reproducing it. One model, one cast, no second
chance. Small ancient bronzes were frequently made this way, and so is a great
deal of modern work where the maker wants the immediacy.

In the indirect method, the sculptor's model — in clay, plaster, or anything
else — is first captured in a mold made of interlocking sections, so that it can
be taken off the model without destroying either. Molten wax is then brushed or
slushed into that mold and built up to a shell perhaps a centimeter thick. When
the mold is opened, what comes out is a hollow wax replica of the model. The
original survives, which means more waxes can be made and a piece can be cast in
an edition, and it also means a large figure can be divided into arms, legs,
head, and torso, each cast separately and joined later.

That second advantage is why the indirect method was such a leap. Greek founders
were casting life-size figures in separately made sections by the fifth century
BC, joining them with molten metal run into prepared seams. It let them make
figures with limbs extended into open space, which a single monolithic pour
struggles with badly. The two warriors recovered from the sea off Riace in
Calabria in 1972 show how far the technique had been pushed: hollow-cast bodies
with copper inlaid at the lips and nipples, silver teeth, eyes built up from
separate materials, and a surface finish that had to be chased by hand across
every square centimeter.

Not every tradition went this route. The ritual vessels of Shang and Zhou China
were made by an entirely different and equally sophisticated method, in which
ceramic molds were taken off a model in sections, the ornament was carved and
sharpened into the interiors of those sections, and the whole assembly was
reconstructed around a core. The extraordinary crispness of those bronzes comes
from detail cut into the mold rather than into a model.

## The Core and the Gate

A solid bronze figure would be ruinously heavy, ruinously expensive, and prone
to cracking as its interior cooled long after its surface. So the wax is a
shell, and the space inside it is filled with a refractory core — traditionally
a clay mixture, often with sand, ground-up fired clay, and organic matter such
as dung or chopped straw worked in to let it breathe and to give it some give
when it is heated.

This creates an immediate problem. Once the wax melts away, the core is floating
inside a cavity with nothing holding it in position, and if it shifts by even a
few millimeters the metal will be too thin on one side and may not fill at all.
The solution is old and unchanged: chaplets, small metal pins pushed through the
wax so that they bridge the core to the outer mold. They hold the gap open
during burnout and pour. They also end up embedded in the finished casting,
which is why an X-ray of an ancient bronze often shows a scatter of pins through
the wall.

Around the outside of the wax, the founder builds the delivery system. A funnel
and a vertical downsprue carry metal from the pouring cup. Runners branch off it
to gates that enter the figure at discreet points. Risers rise from the highest
parts of the form, along with fine vents. These are not afterthoughts. Metal
must arrive at the bottom of the cavity and rise steadily, pushing air out ahead
of it; if it splashes and folds, the fold becomes a permanent flaw. The risers
keep a reservoir of still-liquid metal feeding the casting as it shrinks,
because bronze contracts as it solidifies and a section that has nothing left to
draw on will pull a cavity inside itself.

Then the whole assembly — wax, core, chaplets, sprues — is invested: coated in
successive layers of fine refractory slurry that pick up every fingerprint, then
backed with coarser material until the mold has real structural mass.

## Burnout, Pour, and the Ways It Goes Wrong

The invested mold goes into a kiln, upside down, and is held there for hours or
days. Most of the wax runs out early. The rest burns off as the temperature
rises, and the mold fires to a hard ceramic while every trace of water is driven
out of it. Residual moisture is genuinely dangerous: a pocket of water turned
instantly to steam by incoming metal will throw molten bronze out of the mold.

Large molds are then buried in a pit and packed with sand, partly for support
and partly because a mold that cracks under the hydrostatic pressure of a column
of liquid metal will otherwise empty itself across the foundry floor. Ideally
the mold is still hot when the pour begins, so that the metal does not chill as
it enters.

The pour itself takes seconds. The crucible comes out of the furnace, the dross
is skimmed off the surface, and the metal goes in as one continuous stream — a
stream that must not break, because a pause lets a skin form. The failures have
names. A misrun is a casting where the metal froze before it reached the end of
the cavity; that is what threatened Cellini's Perseus. A cold shut is worse and
more insidious: two fronts of metal meet, each already skinned over, and fail to
fuse, leaving a seam that looks like a fine line and is in fact a crack running
through the wall.

Then it cools, for hours or days depending on the mass, and the founder waits,
because there is nothing else to do.

## Chasing and Skin

What comes out of a broken-open mold is not a sculpture. It is a rough casting
wearing a tree of sprues and risers, coated in burnt investment, with chaplet
stubs, flashing along every mold seam, and possibly a defect or two.

Chasing is the long work that follows. Sprues are cut off and the stubs ground
back. Seams are filed down. Porosity is drilled out and plugged or welded.
Separately cast sections are joined and the joins disguised. And then detail is
put back in with punches and chisels, because grinding a seam flat also removes
whatever texture crossed it. A chaser who is good enough can make a join
invisible; a chaser who is not can flatten a sculptor's modeling into something
lifeless. On a large figure this stage can take longer than everything before it.

The final step is the patina, which is a controlled corrosion. Chemicals are
brushed or sprayed onto the metal, usually while it is heated with a torch, and
the compounds that form give the color: sulfides for browns and near-blacks,
nitrates for reds or for the blues and greens people associate with weathered
bronze. It is a skin a few molecules deep, and it is sealed with wax. Ancient
bronzes wear a different kind of patina, one nobody applied — centuries of
genuine oxidation, sometimes including the unstable chloride corrosion that
conservators call bronze disease and that will eat a buried object from inside
if it is not arrested.

## Why So Little Survives

Here is the melancholy arithmetic of the medium. We know from ancient writers
that Greek cities held thousands of bronze statues. A few dozen large ones
survive, and a strikingly high proportion of those came out of the sea, where
they had been sitting in shipwrecks, safely beyond reach.

The reason is that bronze is recyclable and valuable, and a statue is a stock of
metal standing in the open. Through late antiquity and the Middle Ages, figures
were melted down for coinage, for tools, for bells, and above all for artillery.
The process worked in both directions during wartime: bells became cannon,
cannon became bells. Michelangelo cast a colossal bronze of a pope for Bologna,
and within a few years political enemies had taken it down and melted it for
gun-metal — a fate that consumed an unknown but large fraction of everything
ever cast.

Marble breaks and weathers but is no use to anyone as raw material. Bronze is
always worth what it weighs. That is why the horses above the door of San Marco
in Venice are still there: they were plunder, hauled from Constantinople and
mounted where everyone could see them, which for a few tonnes of ancient alloy
turned out to be safer than standing quietly in a city that owned them.
