# The Baroque and the Decisive Instant

In the Villa Borghese there is a block of marble in which a woman is turning
into a tree. Bernini carved Apollo and Daphne in his early twenties, and he
chose to fix the group not before the transformation and not after it, but
precisely during: bark has closed over one thigh and is climbing, the fingers of
one hand have gone to twigs, leaves are coming out of the hair, and the god's
hand is still pressed against a rib cage that is already becoming wood. The
leaves at the top are cut so thin that light passes through them. Walk around
the group and the story runs forward, because Bernini worked out what you would
see from each angle and in what order.

Almost everything that distinguishes seventeenth-century European art from the
century before it is visible in that decision. The Renaissance preferred the
state; the Baroque preferred the instant. And not just any instant — the one
that is about to tip.

The name itself was an insult, applied long afterward by critics who found the
period undisciplined. It probably comes from the Portuguese word for a
misshapen pearl, possibly by way of a scholastic term for a tortuous piece of
reasoning, and in either case it meant irregular, overwrought, wanting in
proportion. The label has outlived the complaint, and it is worth keeping,
provided it means a set of choices rather than a temperament.

## The Moment Chosen

Compare three sculptures of David. Donatello's is a boy standing over a severed
head: the event has happened. Michelangelo's is a young man at rest, weight on
one leg, stone in hand, watching: the event has not happened. Bernini's David is
mid-twist, torso wrenched around, sling loaded and about to come through, lower
lip caught between his teeth, and his body claims the space behind him and to
the side. You cannot stand in front of it without being in the way.

Painting made the same turn. Caravaggio's Calling of Saint Matthew takes place
in what looks like a back room where men are counting money, into which a
diagonal shaft of light arrives along with two figures, one of whom lifts a hand
and points. Matthew has not yet risen. He is pointing at his own chest with an
expression of ordinary confusion. The picture catches the half second in which a
life changes and before the man in it knows. Artemisia Gentileschi painted
Judith with the sword already in the neck and both women braced with their
weight into the work — not the triumph afterward, which is what the previous
century would have painted, but the physical middle of an act of butchery.

The formal consequences follow. Compositions built on a stable frontal plane
give way to diagonals that drive into depth and run out past the frame. Figures
spill over the edges of their architecture. Light stops being an even studio
illumination and becomes directional, hard, often from a single source outside
the picture, throwing most of the canvas into a darkness out of which a knee or
a face emerges. Contour softens; drama arrives through value rather than line.
And where High Renaissance pictures tend to be self-contained, Baroque ones
reach for the viewer — a figure looks out, an arm comes toward you, a step is
about to be taken in your direction.

## Art That Wants Something From You

This was not an aesthetic drifting on its own. The Catholic Church, having spent
the sixteenth century losing northern Europe, ended the Council of Trent with a
decree on sacred images that amounted to a design brief: images teach the
unlettered, they must be intelligible and decorous, they must be free of
doctrinal error, and they must move the faithful toward devotion. Move is the
operative verb. The Church was not asking for beauty. It was asking for
persuasion.

The Jesuits supplied the method. The Spiritual Exercises of Ignatius of Loyola
train the meditant to perform a composition of place: to construct the scene in
the imagination with all five senses engaged, to see the road, smell the stable,
hear the voices, and to insert oneself bodily into the event. Baroque religious
art does that work on the viewer's behalf. Caravaggio's pilgrims have filthy
feet and his apostles have the faces of men you could find in a tavern, and the
point of the dirt is not realism for its own sake. It puts the miracle in a room
with a floor like yours.

Subject matter shifts accordingly toward states that are felt in the body:
martyrdom, conversion, ecstasy, doubt. Bernini's Saint Teresa, collapsed in her
habit with her mouth open and one bare foot hanging, is depicting an experience
the saint described in language that is frankly physical, and he declined to
soften it.

## Dissolving the Seams

Bernini's chapel for the Cornaro family in Santa Maria della Vittoria is the
period's argument in one room. The sculptural group sits in an aedicule of
colored marble, lit from above by a window you cannot see, which feeds real
daylight down onto gilded bronze rays so that the illumination is partly carved
and partly actual. On the side walls, in what read unmistakably as theatre
boxes, members of the Cornaro family are carved in relief, leaning forward,
talking, watching the vision. They are the audience, and having been given an
audience to watch, you become an audience watching an audience. The ceiling
opens into painted sky. Where sculpture ends and architecture begins, and where
either ends and painting begins, is not answerable, and that is the intention.

Ceiling painting pushed illusion further still. Andrea Pozzo covered the nave
vault of the Jesuit church of Sant'Ignazio in Rome with painted architecture
that continues the real architecture upward through several storeys before
opening onto a sky full of ascending figures. It works from one spot, and the
church helpfully marks it with a disc set into the floor. Stand on the disc and
the building rises without a seam. Take five paces and the painted columns begin
to lean and buckle. The same church has a dome that is not a dome: a large flat
canvas painted with coffering and a lantern, installed when the intended cupola
proved unaffordable, and from the correct distance entirely convincing.

## Two Temperaments in Rome

The period's two great architects wanted opposite things from the same
vocabulary. Bernini was a public man, a favorite of popes, a designer of
fountains and colonnades and stage machinery, and his architecture organizes
crowds: the embrace of the piazza at Saint Peter's, the route that takes you
through a dark colonnade into blazing light.

Francesco Borromini worked smaller, stranger, and from geometry. San Carlo alle
Quattro Fontane sits on a scrap of land at a crossroads, and its plan is
generated from interlocking triangles that produce an undulating wall — concave,
convex, concave — so the building seems to breathe. Its oval dome is coffered
with crosses, hexagons and octagons that shrink as they rise, exaggerating the
height of a space barely large enough for a congregation. At Sant'Ivo he built a
plan on a six-pointed star and topped it with a spiral. Contemporaries who
believed the classical orders were rules found this licentious and said so.
Borromini was a difficult, solitary man, in and out of favor, and he killed
himself in the end.

## The Other Baroque

North of the Alps the same decades produced something the Roman model does not
explain. The Dutch Republic had no papal commissions, a Calvinist church that
wanted bare walls, and no resident court of consequence. Painting went instead
onto the open market — sold at fairs, through dealers, out of the studio, to
merchants, brewers, and farmers, and hung in ordinary houses. Painters
specialized ruthlessly, one man spending a career on winter scenes, another on
church interiors, another on tabletops of half-peeled lemons and Chinese
porcelain, and most of them struggled financially. Rembrandt, the most
celebrated painter in Amsterdam, went through an insolvency and sold his
collections; Vermeer left his widow with debts and a very small number of
paintings.

Rembrandt took the Baroque instant and applied it to secular and private
subjects — a conspiracy, a lesson in anatomy, a militia company caught mid-muster
rather than lined up, and above all his own face, painted for forty years until
the paint itself thickened into something troweled and scraped. Vermeer went the
other way entirely and drained the instant of event: a woman pouring milk, a
woman holding a balance, light coming through a window onto a plaster wall, all
motion suspended.

A day's ride south, in Catholic Antwerp, Rubens ran the opposite kind of
operation: a large house with a purpose-built studio, a staff of assistants and
specialists — one for animals, one for flowers, one for architecture — and a
production system in which the master painted a small, blazingly fluent oil
sketch and the workshop enlarged it, with the master returning at the end to
pull the whole thing together. It let him accept commissions at a scale nobody
else could handle, including an enormous allegorical cycle glorifying the life
of a French queen who had rather little to be glorified about. He was also a
classical scholar and a working diplomat, carrying negotiations between courts
under cover of artistic business, and he was knighted by two kings. Nothing in
his career resembles the Romantic idea of a painter, and his figures, heaped
and twisting and spilling sideways out of their compositions, are the most
purely Baroque bodies anyone painted.

In Madrid, Velázquez built the period's most self-aware picture. Las Meninas
shows the painter at work on a canvas whose face we cannot see, the small
princess and her attendants turning toward us, a mirror at the back reflecting
the king and queen, who must therefore be standing where we are standing. The
subject of the painting is the act of looking, and the viewer has been assigned
a role in it without being asked.

That, finally, is what the word ought to mean. Not excess, not curls and gilding,
but an art that refuses to sit quietly on the far side of the frame and instead
reaches out to place you — on a specific paving stone, at a specific instant,
inside an event that has not yet finished happening. Step off the disc in the
floor of Sant'Ignazio and the columns bend. The whole period is that disc.
