# The Long Invention of Oil Paint

Giorgio Vasari tells the story like a fable. A painter in Bruges, learned in the
sort of chemistry that shaded into alchemy, finished a panel, varnished it, and
set it outside to dry in the sun. The heat opened the joints of the wood and
the picture split. Furious, the painter resolved to find a varnish that would
dry in the shade, experimented with oils, and hit on linseed and nut oil, which
turned out to be not merely varnishes but the finest painting medium anyone had
ever used. The painter is Jan van Eyck. The story was written down more than a
century after his death, by a Tuscan with a professional interest in tidy
beginnings, and it is wrong in roughly the way origin stories usually are: not
about the substance, but about the moment.

## Older Than the Story

Oil as a paint medium was not new in the fifteenth century, or even close to
new. Theophilus, the pseudonymous monk-craftsman whose handbook of medieval
technique dates from around the twelfth century, describes grinding pigments in
linseed oil perfectly matter-of-factly. He also describes the drawback: each
coat has to be dried in the sun before the next goes on, and for anything as
detailed as a figure this is, he says, tediously slow. Oil was known, and it was
mostly used where slowness did not matter — on shields, saddles, carved
statuary, architectural woodwork, anything that needed a tough weatherproof
skin rather than a face.

Go back further and it turns up outside Europe entirely. When scientists in the
2000s analyzed paint samples from the Buddhist cave paintings in the Bamiyan
valley in Afghanistan, decorated in the middle of the first millennium, they
found layers bound in drying oils, centuries before any European treatise
mentions the practice. Whatever van Eyck did, it was not to discover that oil
will carry pigment.

The real question is narrower and more interesting. Vegetable oil is a
difficult, sluggish, yellowing material that takes days to set and weeks to
harden. Getting from that to a medium a painter can control at the speed of
thought required a long accumulation of workshop fixes, most of them anonymous,
and it happened in the Low Countries in the decades around 1400.

## Making a Stubborn Material Behave

Drying oil does not dry in the ordinary sense. Nothing evaporates. The oil takes
oxygen out of the air and its molecules cross-link into a continuous solid
network, so a film of linseed oil actually gains weight as it sets. The process
starts at the surface and works inward, which is the source of most of oil
painting's virtues and nearly all of its pathologies.

Left raw, the oil is thin, slow, and prone to going brown. The fixes that
emerged are all essentially ways of starting the reaction before the paint
reaches the panel. Oil could be washed with water or sand to remove the
mucilage that encouraged yellowing. It could be left in shallow trays under
glass for weeks, where sunlight both bleached it and thickened it into a
honeyed, fast-drying syrup. It could be cooked, sometimes with lead compounds
dissolved into it, producing heat-bodied oil that flowed from the brush and
gripped the layer beneath. Metallic driers — lead first, manganese and cobalt
much later — cut setting time from days to a working overnight. Resins could be
dissolved in to add gloss, body, and a certain reluctance in the paint that let
a brushstroke stand up instead of levelling out.

None of this is a single invention. It is a craft tradition converging on a
recipe, and the Netherlandish workshops of the early fifteenth century were
simply the ones who got the recipe good enough that a painter could stop
fighting the material and start exploiting it.

## What the Medium Made Possible

What they exploited was mainly patience and transparency, which egg tempera —
the standard fine-painting medium of the preceding centuries — denies you on
both counts. Tempera sets in seconds. You cannot blend it; you build tone by
laying down thousands of small hatched strokes, and it lightens as it dries, so
the color you brush on is not the color you get. It is a superb medium for
line, pattern, and clear local color, which is why medieval panel painting looks
the way it does.

Oil stays open for hours. Two tones laid side by side can be stroked together
into an edge so soft it has no location. And because the paint is a colored
varnish rather than a colored paste, thin layers of it are genuinely see-through.
The Netherlandish system used that directly: an oak panel coated with chalk
bound in animal glue and smoothed to the finish of an eggshell, an underdrawing
brushed on in black, then paint applied in thin films, opaque and loaded with lead white in the
lights, transparent in the shadows. Light entering the picture
passes through the colored layers, reflects off the white ground, and comes back
out through them again. The result is a depth and saturation that cannot be
obtained by mixing, because the color is being seen in transmission, the way a
stained-glass window is seen and not the way a painted wall is.

Once the technique matured, the descriptive ambition went up accordingly. A
brush loaded with oil paint can draw a line finer than a hair and keep it wet
long enough to soften one end of it. The famous passages of early Netherlandish
painting are all demonstrations of this: the convex mirror in the Arnolfini
portrait with its cargo of reflected room, the single brass chandelier, the
beading on a hem, the specific tired texture of an old man's skin. Nothing
about the subject matter demanded it. The medium simply made it possible, and
patrons paid for the difference.

Slowness turned out to be a feature as well. A tempera painting has to be
resolved before it is begun, because every stroke is permanent within a minute
of landing. An oil painting can be argued with. A figure can be shifted a hand's
width, a background can be repainted three times, a commission can sit against
the studio wall for a year while the painter thinks about it. Composition
migrated out of the preparatory drawing and onto the picture itself, and the
workshop economics followed: apprentices could lay in draperies and skies in
dead color while the master reserved the heads and the final glazes for himself,
and the pictures could be produced in quantity for a merchant class in Bruges
and Ghent that wanted portraits and small devotional panels rather than chapels.

## South Across the Alps

Italy came to oil late and then changed it. The standard account credits
Antonello da Messina, who painted in a recognizably Netherlandish manner and
who was in Venice in the mid-1470s, with carrying the method north into the
lagoon; in truth Flemish panels were already circulating in Italian collections
and Italian painters had been picking at the technique for a generation, first
using oil for glazes over a tempera underpainting and gradually abandoning the
egg altogether.

What Venice contributed was the support. Fresco rots in salt air and large
panels warp, so Venetian painters moved to canvas, which was cheap, locally
abundant from the sail trade, light enough to hang on damp masonry, and — a
point not lost on an export economy — could be unstretched and rolled into a
tube for shipping. Canvas also has a tooth. A dry brush dragged across it skips,
catching the weave and leaving broken color, and the surface of the picture
stops being a window pane and starts being a fabric with paint sitting on it.

Titian spent sixty years exploring what that permitted, and by his last decades
he was working in a way no fifteenth-century painter would have recognized as
finishing a picture at all: opaque underpainting scrubbed in, glazes flooded
over, thick strokes dragged across the top, passages pushed around with his
fingers. Seen close, the surface is incoherent. Seen at distance it resolves. He
had discovered that oil paint can describe a thing and simultaneously advertise
that it is paint, and most European painting for the next three hundred years
lives somewhere on the line he opened between those two options.

The ground changed with it. Fifteenth-century panels were prepared brilliant
white, because the whole optical trick depended on light coming back through the
color. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century painters increasingly toned their
canvases instead, to a warm gray or a dull reddish brown, and then worked in
both directions from that middle value — pulling darks over it with transparent
washes, cutting lights into it with thick opaque paint. It is a faster way to
get a picture standing up, it makes shadow luminous almost for free, and it is
the reason so much Baroque painting emerges out of a dusky middle tone. It also
had a long cost: those darkened grounds keep darkening, and in many pictures the
shadows have closed up into an obscurity that was never intended.

## The Bill

The medium charged for all of this, and it still does. Linseed oil yellows,
particularly in darkness, so that pictures kept in shuttered rooms come out
with a warm bloom nobody painted; walnut and poppy oil yellow less but make
weaker, slower films. Paint layers that dry at different rates tear each other
apart — a fast-drying layer over a slow one skins over and then cracks into wide
crocodile patterns as the paint beneath it moves, which is why the old rule
demands fat over lean, oil-rich layers always on top of leaner ones.

Then there are the pigments. Red lakes made from madder and cochineal fade in
light, so that draperies painted a deep crimson now read as pale brown. The
green glazes of copper dissolved in resin, brilliant when fresh, have gone brown
across whole centuries of landscape painting, which means a good deal of the
sober foliage in old pictures was not sober at all. Bitumen, fashionable in the
eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries as a warm transparent brown, never
properly dries; it keeps creeping, and pictures that used it heavily have
wrinkled and slid into wreckage.

And time does something stranger. Oil films grow slowly more transparent as
they age, so layers a painter meant to bury begin to surface. Arms appear where
an arm was moved. A hat turns out to have been painted over a different hat.
Ships emerge in bare seas. The medium that let painters revise as freely as
writers turns out to keep the drafts, and hands them back a few centuries later
to anyone standing close enough to look.
