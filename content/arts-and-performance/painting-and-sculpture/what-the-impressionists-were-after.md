# What the Impressionists Were After

In the spring of 1874 a group of painters who could not reliably get into the
official Salon rented rooms on the boulevard des Capucines from the photographer
Nadar and hung their own show. There was no jury. Works were hung two or three
deep, as was normal, and the participants had agreed to no selection committee
and no prizes, which was not normal at all. Among the pictures was a small,
rapidly brushed harbor scene by Claude Monet, a dull orange sun over Le Havre
with boats indicated by a few dark strokes, which he had called *Impression,
Sunrise* largely because it was plainly not a finished view of anything. A
critic named Louis Leroy wrote a mocking review built around the word, imagining
a bewildered academician wandering the rooms. The name stuck, the way hostile
names often do, and within a decade the painters were using it themselves.

A century and a half of reproduction has since sanded the movement down to
pleasantness: water lilies on umbrellas, dappled light, tea towels. It is worth
recovering what was actually at stake, because the pictures were understood at
the time as aggressive, and the aggression was technical before it was anything
else.

## The System They Were Refusing

French painting in the middle of the nineteenth century ran through a single
institution. The Salon was the only exhibition that mattered, admission was
decided by a jury drawn from the Academy, and a painter's income, students, and
state commissions followed from whether the jury said yes. The jury had a
hierarchy of subjects — history and myth at the top, portraits below,
landscape and still life near the bottom — and a hierarchy of finish. A
submitted picture was expected to have its brushwork resolved into a smooth
surface, its shadows warm and brown, its composition built up over months from
drawings. The public understood the code. A visible stroke meant a sketch, and a
sketch was a private document, not something you framed.

Rejection rates in some years were severe enough to produce public complaint,
and in 1863 the state relented far enough to mount a Salon des Refusés for the
excluded work. Crowds came largely to laugh, and stayed to argue about Manet's
picnic scene with its nude woman sitting among clothed men and looking straight
out at the viewer. Manet was never an Impressionist in the strict sense and
never exhibited with the group, but he showed the younger painters two things:
that a modern subject could carry a large painting, and that a jury could be
survived.

## New Paint, New Places

The technical enablers arrived at roughly the same time and were genuinely
decisive. Paint in collapsible metal tubes, introduced in the middle of the
century, replaced pig bladders that had to be punctured and could not be
resealed. A painter could now carry two dozen colors into a field, use a
thumbnail's worth of one, and find it still usable a week later. Portable
easels, prepared canvases bought ready-stretched, and the commercial color
trade's habit of grinding pigment finely and packing it stiff all pushed in the
same direction.

The colors themselves were new. The nineteenth-century chemical industry
delivered a run of bright, reliable pigments that had never existed: synthetic
ultramarine at a fraction of the price of lapis, cobalt blue, cerulean,
viridian, chrome and cadmium yellows, emerald green. The old palette had been
weakest exactly where the Impressionists wanted strength — in intense, light,
saturated blues and greens — and suddenly it was not.

And the railways had rebuilt the geography of leisure around Paris. Argenteuil,
Chatou, Bougival, Pontoise, the beaches of Normandy: an hour or two from the
Gare Saint-Lazare, with a return train in the evening. These places are the
subject matter of the movement, and they were subject matter of a specific kind
— not untouched nature, but the new Sunday landscape of regattas, riverside
restaurants, bridges, and factory chimneys sitting in the background of the view
without apology.

## Painting the Light Instead of the Thing

The method follows from a decision about shadow. Academic practice built shadows
out of earth colors and bitumen, so that a well-behaved picture had a warm brown
substructure running under everything. Monet and Renoir threw it out. If you
look at a shadow on a sunlit path it is not brown; it is blue and violet,
because the surface in shadow is lit not by the sun but by the sky, and because
the eye, saturated with the sunlit color around it, supplies a complementary
tinge of its own. So shadows got painted blue, and the critics said the
pictures looked diseased.

From there the rest unwinds. Painting on a white or pale ground rather than a
dark one keeps everything luminous. Mixing pigments on the palette dulls them,
so instead of stirring green and yellow into a single tone, you put a stroke of
each beside the other and let them combine in the viewer's eye at the proper
distance — brighter, and restless in a way a mixed tone never is. Working wet
into wet, outdoors, in one or two sessions, rules out the layered construction
of a studio picture; the whole surface has to be brought along together, which
is why an Impressionist canvas tends to have the same degree of finish in every
square inch, with no hierarchy of detail. And the stroke stays visible. That was
the offense. A painting that shows its own making refuses to pretend to be a
window, and the refusal was read as incompetence or insolence, depending on the
critic.

None of this made them a school with a doctrine. Degas, who exhibited with the
group in nearly every show and did more than anyone to organize them, loathed
the word Impressionist, worked indoors from memory and drawings, and thought
painting outdoors was a stunt; his interest was in bodies caught mid-movement,
in odd croppings borrowed from photography and Japanese prints, in the pastel
surfaces he built up over years. Pissarro, the oldest, was the connective
tissue, the one who taught Cézanne and later Gauguin and who tried pointillism
in his sixties out of curiosity. Berthe Morisot painted domestic interiors with
a touch so loose that her canvases look unfinished even by the group's
standards. Mary Cassatt, an American in Paris, brought in a hard draughtsmanship
and later made prints in flat color that owe everything to Japanese woodcuts.
Renoir eventually decided the whole thing had been a wrong turn, went to Italy,
looked at Raphael, and spent the 1880s trying to get line and contour back.

## Series, Money, and the End of the Alliance

Monet's late strategy pushed the logic to its conclusion. If the subject of the
painting is really the light, then the object can be held constant and the light
varied: the same grainstacks at morning and evening and under snow, the same row
of poplars, the same cathedral facade at Rouen painted again and again from a
rented room opposite, with several canvases going at once and each brought out
only when its particular hour returned. The series turned the method into an
argument. What changes between the canvases is not the thing. It is everything
else.

The money question was never solved by the exhibitions, which mostly lost money.
It was solved by dealers, principally Paul Durand-Ruel, who bought in bulk,
carried inventory for years at real financial risk, and eventually found the
market that Paris would not supply by shipping a large exhibition to New York in
the 1880s. American collectors bought heavily, which is why so many of the best
Impressionist paintings hang in Boston, Philadelphia, and Chicago rather than in
France.

By the eighth and final group exhibition in 1886 the alliance had dissolved.
Monet and Renoir were not in it. Seurat was, with the enormous Sunday scene on
the Grande Jatte, its figures built from tiny dots of unmixed color and frozen
into profile stillness — a picture that takes the color theory further than any
Impressionist had and simultaneously abandons everything spontaneous about the
method. The younger painters who mattered most over the following decade had all
passed through Impressionism and were, by then, working their way out of it:
Cézanne toward structure, Van Gogh toward the stroke as pure feeling, Gauguin
toward flat color and invented scenes.

What survived was not a style but a permission. After the 1870s a painter could
put a picture in public that declared itself paint, took its subject from
ordinary life, and asked the viewer to do part of the assembling. That is a
smaller claim than genius and a much larger one than sunlight on water.
