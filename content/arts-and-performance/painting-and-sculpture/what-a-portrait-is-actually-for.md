# What a Portrait Is Actually For

In a museum case somewhere there is a thin wooden panel, painted in pigment
suspended in hot beeswax, showing a young man with heavy brows, a short beard,
and enormous dark eyes. He lived in Roman Egypt. The panel was cut down and
bound into the linen wrappings over his face when he was buried, so that the
portrait lay where the face lay. He has been dead for eighteen centuries and he
is still, unmistakably, looking at you.

The Fayum portraits are the earliest paintings most people encounter that feel
like photographs of individuals rather than depictions of types, and they make
the basic problem of portraiture visible right at the start. The panel was
commissioned by people who knew this man and would have noticed if it were
wrong. It was also made to serve him in a condition where accuracy of the nose
was arguably beside the point. A portrait is asked to do two things at once, and
the two things are not always friends.

## The Record and the Presence

The first job is documentary. Somebody needs to know what this person looked
like — a distant fiancé, a parliament, a posterity, a court. Roman republican
sculpture took this job with a seriousness that can still be unnerving. The
portrait busts of that period give us jowls, warts, receding gums, the deep
brackets around an old man's mouth, and they do it because in that culture a
face worn by decades of public service was the whole argument. Aristocratic
families kept wax masks of their ancestors and carried them in funeral
processions. The face was a legal and political document.

The second job is something else: to put a person in the room. Not a
measurement of a person, a presence. This is what the Fayum panels achieve with
those oversized eyes, and it is what a passport photograph, which has better
documentary accuracy than any painting ever made, conspicuously does not.

These two demands come apart constantly. A portrait can be a flawless likeness
and completely inert. It can also depart from the sitter's actual proportions in
half a dozen measurable ways and strike everyone who knew them as exact. Good
portraitists have always known this and have manipulated it without
embarrassment — enlarging the eyes slightly, dropping the light so that it
catches one cheekbone and lets the other side go, shifting the head a few
degrees off the axis of the shoulders so the figure seems to have just turned
toward you.

## The Turn of the Head

That turn has a history. Early Renaissance portraits in Italy were overwhelmingly
in strict profile, heads presented flat against the picture plane like a face on
a coin, and the resemblance to coinage is not accidental: Roman medals were
being dug up and collected, and a profile carried an imperial authority that
patrons wanted. A profile is also safe. It flattens the face into a contour, a
line that can be checked against a real nose, and it commits to nothing about
the interior of the person because it cannot meet your eye.

The three-quarter view came south from the Netherlands over the course of the
fifteenth century and changed the genre permanently. Once the head turns, the
far eye recedes, the nose casts a shadow across the cheek, and the skull
acquires volume. More importantly, the sitter can now look out. That single
degree of freedom converts a picture of somebody into an encounter with
somebody, and it introduces the question every portrait since has had to answer:
where is this person looking, and what do they think of us?

Jan van Eyck pushed the machinery about as far as it could go almost
immediately. The double portrait of the Arnolfini couple puts two figures in a
Bruges room and then, on the back wall, hangs a convex mirror that shows the
room from behind — including two small figures standing in the doorway where the
viewer would be. Above the mirror he wrote, in an elaborate legal hand, that Jan
van Eyck was here. The painting is a record of two people, and it is also a
statement that somebody stood in that room and saw them, and that seeing is part
of what is being recorded.

## The Negotiation

Almost every portrait before the modern period was paid for by its subject or by
someone with an interest in them, which makes it a negotiated object and
sometimes a hostage situation. The painter needs the commission and the next one
it might bring. The sitter wants to be recognizable and also wants to look like
the best available account of themselves. Neither party can fully control the
result.

Hans Holbein spent his English years threading this needle at a court where
failure had real consequences. His drawings of Henry VIII's circle are
extraordinarily unsparing — sagging chins, bad skin, small suspicious eyes — and
yet they flattered by a different route, by treating every sitter as a
personage worth this much scrutiny. The traditional account of his portrait of
Anne of Cleves, painted so the king could assess a bride he had not met, holds
that Henry was displeased on meeting her and blamed the picture. Whether the
portrait was too kind or the king simply disliked her, the episode shows what
was at stake in the genre when it worked as reconnaissance.

Velázquez, painting Pope Innocent X in Rome, produced a picture of a
hard-mouthed, fleshy, pink-faced man with a lawyer's gaze whose intelligence is
also unmistakable and slightly frightening. The pope is said to have remarked
that it was too true. The remark may be apocryphal, but it identifies exactly
the pleasure and the danger of the thing: a portrait that succeeds has caught
something its sitter did not authorize.

Occasionally the negotiation collapsed in public. When John Singer Sargent
exhibited his portrait of a celebrated Parisian beauty in the 1880s — pale
powdered skin, black dress, head turned away in profile, one jeweled shoulder
strap slipped down her arm — the Salon reacted with genuine scandal, the
sitter's family demanded its withdrawal, and Sargent eventually repainted the
strap into its proper position and left the country. The painting had said
something true about how its subject presented herself, and saying it out loud
turned out to be the offense.

## What the Camera Took

Photography arrived in 1839 and within two decades had made portraits cheap.
Small mounted photographic cards were collected and exchanged by the million,
and the middle-class customer who would previously have sat for a modest painted
likeness simply stopped. The bottom fell out of the trade, and a great many
competent provincial portrait painters became photographers or gave up.

What survived at the top end survived by doing what the camera could not. A
photographic exposure captures a specific instant, and a face in a specific
instant is frequently not what that face is like. A painted portrait is
assembled over many hours from many glances, which lets the painter average
across a person — take the way the mouth sets when they are thinking, combine it
with the tilt of the head they use when listening, and arrive at a
configuration the sitter may never have held for a full second. That composite
is often what family members mean when they say a picture is exactly them.

Painting also kept the body and the room. A portrait can spend as much attention
on a hand, a coat, a chair, and a doorway as on a face, and it can weight them
deliberately. Alice Neel painted people in her apartment with hands and feet
enlarged and slightly clawed, rendering exhaustion and nerve directly into
anatomy. Lucian Freud built faces out of thick pigment over dozens of sittings
until the paint acquired the density of flesh, which is an argument that a
person is a physical fact before they are anything else.

## The Loophole

There is one arrangement where the negotiation vanishes entirely: when the
painter is the sitter. Nobody has to be flattered, nobody can withdraw the
commission, and the model works for free at any hour.

Rembrandt used this loophole for forty years, producing dozens of self-portraits
in paint and etching across the whole span of his working life — a young man in
a feathered cap pulling faces to study expression, a prosperous burgher in fine
clothes at the height of his success, and then, after bankruptcy and loss, an
old man in a plain cap looking out with an expression that refuses to resolve
into any single emotion you can name. Read in sequence they are unlike anything
else in art, because the variable being tracked is not just a face aging but a
man's changing estimate of himself.

That is the thing portraiture can do and almost nothing else can. Not the
recording of features, which any camera does better, and not the assertion of
status, which architecture and clothing manage on their own. It is the
production of a second-order fact: here is what it was like to be looked at
closely by someone who was paying attention, and here, underneath, is what the
person looking thought they saw.
