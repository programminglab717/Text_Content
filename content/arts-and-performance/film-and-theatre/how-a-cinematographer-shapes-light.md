# How a Cinematographer Shapes Light

Put a bare bulb three feet from someone's face and you get a hard, mean picture:
a bright cheek, a shadow of the nose with an edge you could cut yourself on, and
an eye socket that has gone to black. Hang a four-by-four frame of diffusion
between the bulb and the face and almost everything changes. The shadow edge
softens into a gradient, the eye comes back, the skin stops looking like it has
been shot with a flash. Nothing about the bulb changed. What changed is how big
the light is from the face's point of view, and that single variable does more
work on a film set than any other.

Hardness is a question of angular size, not power. The sun is enormous and still
produces knife-edged shadows, because at ninety-odd million miles it occupies a
tiny slice of the sky. Put a cloud deck in front of it and the whole sky becomes
the source, the shadows vanish, and the light wraps around a face from every
direction at once. Everything a crew does with bounce boards, silks, muslin,
bedsheets, and white-painted walls is an attempt to buy that cloud deck on
demand — to take a small, cheap, controllable source and make it behave as
though it were the size of a window.

## Distance, Direction, Ratio

Three decisions follow from the choice of source, and they get made in that
order on most sets.

Distance sets falloff. Illumination drops with the square of the distance, so a
lamp placed close to an actor is dramatically brighter on the near cheek than on
the far one, and an actor who leans six inches toward it changes exposure
visibly. Pull the same lamp back and increase its output, and the light across
the subject flattens out; now the actor can cross the room without the operator
riding the iris. Contrasty, tightly controlled portraits want the lamp near.
Scenes with a lot of movement want it far, which is why so much of the light on
a big set comes from units parked high and well back, or from a huge soft source
overhead that the actors simply walk around beneath.

Direction sets shape. As a key light swings from beside the camera around toward
the side, the nose shadow grows from a stub into a loop, then reaches the
shadow-side cheek and closes a small triangle of light beneath the eye, then
travels further until the face splits into one lit half and one dark. A source
raised above eye level reads as ordinary because sun and ceiling fixtures come
from above; a source dropped below the chin reads as wrong, which is why it has
been the standard equipment of ghost stories since the silent era. Backlight
does something different again, separating hair and shoulders from whatever is
behind them and giving a flat image the illusion of depth.

Ratio sets mood. Once there is a key, the question is how much fill goes into
the shadows, and the answer is usually expressed in stops. A shadow one stop
under the key is a gentle, unemphatic portrait. Three or four stops under is
drama. Eight is a silhouette. The distinction between what the trade calls high
key and low key is nothing more mysterious than that number, but it drives how
a film feels from the first frame. Gordon Willis built *The Godfather* around
an unusually dark end of the scale, pushing light down from above so that Marlon
Brando's eyes sit in shadow for whole scenes, and studio executives worried
loudly that audiences would not be able to see the actor. That was the point.

## Color, and Why It Has to Be Managed

Light has a color even when it looks white, and the eye adapts to it so
completely that most people never notice until a camera refuses to. Household
tungsten burns warm, around thirty-two hundred kelvin. Midday sun with blue sky
runs closer to fifty-six hundred. Put both in one frame and whichever one the
camera is balanced for will look neutral while the other goes orange or blue.

Crews spend a surprising share of their day on this. Sheets of gel convert one
source to the other — blue to push tungsten toward daylight, orange to drag
daylight down toward tungsten — so that a lamp inside a room can match the
window it sits beside, or deliberately refuse to. Older fluorescent tubes threw
a green cast that had to be corrected with magenta filtration. Modern LED
fixtures are tunable and miserly with power, but the cheap ones have spiky,
gap-toothed spectra, and a skin tone lit by them can be measurably correct and
still look faintly ill, because certain wavelengths simply are not there to be
reflected.

Managed well, color becomes a storytelling instrument rather than a nuisance.
Letting interiors sit warm and exteriors sit cold, or the reverse, cues the
audience about safety and exposure without a word of dialogue. Letting a single
uncorrected source glow hard blue in the corner of a warm room draws the eye
exactly where the director wants it.

## What the Lens Contributes

The other half of the job happens in front of the film or the sensor. Focal
length does not just change how much of a scene fits in frame; it changes how
space itself is rendered. A long lens compresses distance, stacking a crowd into
a wall of faces and making a running figure appear to make no progress. A wide
lens exaggerates it, so a hand extended toward camera balloons and a room
stretches away. Bring a wide lens close to a face and the nose dominates and the
ears recede — unflattering, unsettling, and extremely useful when a character is
coming apart.

Aperture governs both exposure and depth of field. Open the iris and only a thin
slab of the scene is sharp, isolating a face against a wash of unresolved
background. Stop down and the world resolves from the foreground to the horizon.
Cinematographers mark their lenses in T-stops rather than f-stops, because a
T-stop accounts for light actually lost inside the glass; two lenses of the same
nominal geometry can transmit measurably different amounts, and on a set where
shots from three lenses will be cut together, matching the real transmission
matters more than honoring the arithmetic.

Gregg Toland's work on *Citizen Kane* is the textbook case of using depth
deliberately. Wide lenses, small apertures, brutal quantities of light, and
newly available faster stock let him hold a face in the foreground and an event
at the back of the room in focus at the same time, so that a shot could contain
a whole scene's worth of relationships without a cut. Décor in the deep
background was not filler. It was a second line of dialogue.

## Exposure as a Choice, Not a Setting

There is no correct exposure, only a decision about where to place the important
tones. Photochemical negative tolerates generous overexposure and punishes
underexposure, so film crews traditionally rated stock conservatively and gave
the shadows more light than the meter strictly asked for, knowing that detail
buried in the toe of the curve is gone for good. Digital sensors behave the
other way around. Highlights clip abruptly and irrecoverably, while shadows,
though noisy, can be dragged back in a grade. The practical result is that
digital crews tend to protect the highlights and lift the darkness later, which
is a different instinct from the one a generation of cinematographers trained on
negative had to unlearn.

The instruments changed with it. An incident meter held at the actor's cheek,
pointed back toward the lens, tells you how much light is falling on the subject
and nothing about the scene's contrast; a spot meter reads individual surfaces
and lets you calculate the spread from brightest to darkest. Both are still in
use. But on a digital set they now sit beside a waveform monitor and a false
color display, which shade the image according to exposure level and show at a
glance exactly which part of a forehead is about to go white.

## Working With What the Day Gives

Plenty of the most admired photography comes from crews who declined to fight
conditions. Néstor Almendros shot much of *Days of Heaven* in the short window
after sunset when the sky is bright and the sun is gone, accepting the brutal
constraint that each day gave perhaps twenty usable minutes in exchange for a
soft, shadowless, rapidly dying light that no lamp truck could counterfeit.
Raoul Coutard, shooting for the French New Wave, bounced small lamps off
ceilings and pushed fast black-and-white stock so a crew could work in real
apartments with the furniture where the tenants left it. Sven Nykvist spent a
career chasing the plain, even, slightly cold light of northern windows and
worked hard to keep audiences from being able to tell he had lit anything.

None of these approaches is an accident of taste. Each is a solution to a
question that gets asked on every production, which is how the light in front of
the lens should make the audience feel about the person standing in it.
