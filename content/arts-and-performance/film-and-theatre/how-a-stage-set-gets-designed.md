# How a Stage Set Gets Designed

On a table in a rehearsal room there is a box about the size of a microwave
oven, open at one end, built of white card and foamboard. Inside it is a room,
or a hillside, or a corridor with three doors, rendered at one twenty-fifth
scale. A director picks up a paper figure roughly six centimeters tall, sets it
by the upstage door, and says: if she comes in there, can the audience on this
side still see her face? The designer slides the figure a hand's width
downstage. Everyone leans over the box. This is the central instrument of
scenic design, and for all the software now involved it has not been improved
upon, because a model lets several people look at the same imaginary space at
once and disagree about it productively.

The white card model comes early and deliberately lacks colour and texture. Its
job is to settle geometry: where the walls are, how high, how far apart, what
the actors can walk on and what they must walk around. Colour arrives later
because colour is seductive and will paper over a floor plan that does not
work.

Long before the card gets cut, the design begins with an unglamorous reading of
the text for its physical demands. How many separate entrances does the action
require, and can any of them be shared? Does anyone have to overhear a
conversation while concealed on stage? Does a body have to be removed, and by
whom, and how heavy will that actor be? How many seconds does the script give
between the end of one scene and the start of the next, and is that enough time
to move a table in the dark? A designer with a pencil and a copy of the play
usually finds three or four constraints that quietly determine everything else,
and finding them in week one is far cheaper than finding them in the technical
rehearsal.

## The Ground Plan Is the Contract

Underneath the model is a drawing that matters more than any rendering: the
ground plan, a scaled overhead view of the stage with the set on it, the
proscenium line, the centre line, and the setting line marked. Everyone builds
from it. The carpenter cuts to it, the stage manager tapes the rehearsal room
floor from it, the lighting designer hangs from it, and the director blocks
against it months before anybody sees a real wall.

Drawn onto that plan, in thin radiating lines, are the sightlines. They come
from the two or three worst seats in the house — extreme side stalls, the front
row of the upper circle — and they define what is genuinely visible. Sightlines
are unforgiving arithmetic. A wall that reads handsomely in the model may block
a third of the audience from the only chair in the play. A balcony that looks
elegant in elevation may cut off the heads of everyone above row F. Much of the
early design conversation is a negotiation between what a space should look like
and what it will still look like from the cheap seats, and the cheap seats
always win, because a paying customer who cannot see the actor has been sold
nothing.

The related discipline is masking: the flats, legs, borders, and black drapes
that hide the wings, the crew, the ladders, and the back wall. Get masking wrong
and an audience member three rows off centre spends act two watching a
stagehand eat a sandwich.

## Saying Everything Before Anyone Speaks

A set is read very fast. The house lights go down, the curtain goes out or the
lights come up, and within a few seconds the audience has decided what century
they are in, roughly how much money these people have, whether the world is
plausible or frankly theatrical, and — most importantly — what kind of thing is
allowed to happen here. That last judgment is the one designers work hardest
on. A room with a cracked ceiling and an actual working tap makes a promise of
naturalism; if a character later flies, the audience feels cheated. A bare
raked platform with a single chair makes a different promise, and permits almost
anything.

Peter Brook opened his book on the subject by observing that a bare stage
becomes theatre the moment someone walks across it and someone else watches.
That is a useful corrective to the instinct to build. Detail costs attention as
well as money. Every additional object on stage is another thing the audience
must decide to ignore, and a set that specifies everything leaves the audience
nothing to do.

But the opposite extreme has its own trap. Abstraction is not automatically
serious. An empty space with a slope and a lot of haze can be as lazy as a
fully dressed drawing room, and it frequently tells the actors less. The best
sets tend to give the performers something to act against: a raked floor that
makes standing still difficult, a door that sticks, a table too large for the
number of people at it, a wall that does not quite reach the ceiling.

## Making Things That Are Light, Strong, and Wrong Up Close

Most of what appears on stage is built by a small shop under time pressure, out
of timber, ply, steel, and cloth. The traditional unit is the flat: a rectangular
frame, historically covered in muslin or canvas stretched and shrunk tight with
size, now often faced in thin plywood when it must take abuse. A soft-covered
flat is astonishingly light, which matters enormously when four of them must be
walked offstage in a ten-second blackout. A hard-faced flat takes a slammed
door without shuddering, which matters when a farce depends on eleven slams a
minute.

Everything is a compromise between weight and rigidity, and everything must be
fire-retardant, which is why scenic shops are full of treated fabrics and why
naked polystyrene is unwelcome. Joins between flats are hidden with a strip of
cloth — the Dutchman — glued over the seam and painted through, so that a wall
assembled from four pieces reads as one surface.

Scenic paint is its own trade, worked flat on the floor with long-handled
brushes and sponges, and almost none of its effects survive close inspection. A
marble pilaster is a sequence of scumbles, spatters, and dragged veins that look
like mud from a metre away and like stone from row H. Wood grain, brick,
plaster decay, damp, soot: all of them are painted illusions calibrated for a
viewing distance of ten to thirty metres. The first time a visitor walks onto a
finished set they are usually disappointed, and that disappointment is the
evidence that the job was done properly.

## The Stage as a Machine

Behind and above the set is the apparatus that moves it. In a traditional
house, a counterweight flying system lets a line set carrying half a tonne of
scenery be flown out above the sightline by one or two people, balanced by
stacked steel weights on a cradle. Cloths, borders, chandeliers, and entire
walls live up there. On the deck, tracked wagons roll rooms on and off; a
revolve turns a scene through ninety degrees and reveals the next; traps in the
floor swallow actors and furniture.

An increasing amount of this is now automated, with computer-controlled winches
and encoders that can repeat a move within a few millimetres, night after night.
The result is that scene changes have become performances in their own right,
choreographed and cued with the precision of a dance number, sometimes with the
cast doing the moving in character. It is also the reason technical rehearsals
take days. Every one of those moves must be timed against light, sound, and
bodies, and any one of them can crush a foot.

## Designed to Be Lit

A set is only half finished when it is built, because its final appearance is
made of light. This is why designers talk about surfaces in terms of how they
take light rather than how they look on a paint chart. Matte, broken, textured
surfaces hold light and reveal angle; gloss throws it back at the audience in
hot spots. A wall painted a flat mid-grey can be made warm, sickly, dawn-blue
or nearly black by a lighting designer with the right gels and angles, which is
precisely why so many stage walls are a flat mid-grey.

The collaboration runs deeper than colour. If the set has a solid ceiling, the
overhead lighting positions disappear and the whole rig must come from the
sides and front, which changes every face on stage. If the walls are tall and
close, actors will be lit against them and will cast hard shadows. Designers who
have spent a season watching their own work get lit start drawing sets with
deliberate gaps: a wall that stops short, a slot between flats, a doorway wide
enough to admit a shaft of light from an angle nobody would ever mistake for
the sun.

Then come the ordinary humiliations of the real world. The set has to fit
through the get-in door, which in a Victorian building may be a metre wide. On
tour it has to pack into a truck and be erected in six hours in a venue with a
shallower stage and a lower grid. In repertory it has to be struck overnight and
stored in a space the size of a garage. The designs that survive these
conditions are not the cleverest ones. They are the ones drawn by someone who
already knew where the truck would be parked.
