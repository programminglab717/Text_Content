# Montage and the Argument Over the Cut

Sometime in the early 1920s, in a Moscow film school so short of stock that
students practiced by cutting and recutting the same worn reels, Lev Kuleshov
spliced a length of old footage showing an actor's face — placid, unhurried,
gazing slightly off-camera — against three unrelated images: a bowl of soup, a
child in a coffin, a woman reclining on a divan. Viewers, by his later account,
admired the actor's range. They saw hunger, then grief, then desire, in a face
that had not moved a muscle between one version and the next.

The original strips are long gone and the details wobble from one retelling to
the next, so the experiment survives as parable rather than evidence. The parable
holds up. A great deal of what a film means is not photographed. It is
manufactured at the splice, inside the viewer, out of a relationship between two
images that were shot weeks apart in different rooms and have nothing in common
except adjacency. Everything that followed in editing theory is an argument
about what to do with that fact.

## Collision Versus Linkage

The Soviet filmmakers who came out of Kuleshov's workshop disagreed vigorously
about the answer, which is how the field got its first real theory. Sergei
Eisenstein treated the cut as a collision. Two shots placed together should not
blend into a smooth flow but strike sparks, the way two ideas grind against each
other in a dialectic, producing a third thing present in neither. He catalogued
kinds of montage — by shot length, by internal rhythm, by tonal weight — and
reached hardest for what he called intellectual montage, in which images cut
together to state a proposition the camera never filmed. Workers gunned down
intercut with cattle slaughtered. A politician intercut with a mechanical
peacock spreading its tail.

The Odessa Steps sequence in *Battleship Potemkin* remains the demonstration
piece, partly because it cheats time so brazenly. The descent takes far longer
on screen than any real staircase would allow, because Eisenstein overlaps
actions, repeats fragments, and returns to the same pram from angles that could
not follow one another in life. The effect is not confusion. It is pressure.
The cutting keeps re-starting the fall, and the sequence acquires a duration
that belongs to dread rather than to geography.

Vsevolod Pudovkin, working from the same school, argued almost the opposite.
For him shots were bricks, and the editor's job was linkage — laying them so
each supported the next, building an emotional line the audience could climb.
Where Eisenstein wanted friction, Pudovkin wanted accumulation. Dziga Vertov,
meanwhile, rejected staged fiction altogether and used editing to assemble a
mechanical eye that could be in twenty places at once, cutting between a woman
waking and a city's shutters opening as though they were the same gesture.

## The Invisible System

While the Soviets were theorizing the cut, American studios were busy hiding it.
The continuity system that hardened in Hollywood during the 1910s and 1920s
solved a practical problem: how to shatter a scene into dozens of separate
camera setups without the audience losing its footing. The solutions became so
standardized that they now feel like natural law rather than convention.

Keep the camera on one side of an imaginary line drawn through the action, so
that characters facing each other keep facing each other and screen direction
stays stable. Cut on movement rather than stillness, so the eye is busy
completing a gesture and has no attention left for the splice. Match the
eyelines, so that a character looking off-frame right is answered by a shot of
whatever lies to frame right. Change angle by enough that the new shot reads as
a new view rather than a hiccup, and change image size at the same time. None of
these rules describe how vision works. They describe a grammar audiences learned
so thoroughly that violations now register as style — a jolt, a lie, a dream.

The system's real achievement is that it makes the constructed space feel
continuous even though it never existed. A two-shot filmed on a set in
California, a close-up filmed there three weeks later, and an insert of a hand
filmed by a second unit can be assembled into a single unbroken minute that the
audience experiences as happening in one room in one span of time. Editors call
this coverage, and it is less glamorous than dialectic, but it is the substrate
on which almost all narrative film sits.

## Bazin's Objection

The most serious challenge to montage came not from a rival editor but from a
critic. André Bazin, writing in postwar France, argued that cutting a scene into
pieces and reassembling it was an act of authorship that robbed the viewer of a
choice. When a director shows you a hand, then a face, then a door, the film has
already decided what matters. Bazin admired instead the long take and deep
focus — sequences held unbroken, with foreground and far background both sharp,
so the audience must scan the frame and locate significance for itself. He found
this ambiguity morally serious. Reality does not come pre-sorted, and a cinema
honest about reality should not pre-sort it either.

He pointed to Jean Renoir staging action in depth across a frame, to Orson
Welles and Gregg Toland holding a boy visible through a window at the back of a
shot while adults in the foreground sign his life away, to the Italian
neorealists letting scenes run past the moment when a tidier film would have
cut. The objection never displaced montage, and it was never meant to — Bazin
wrote about editing constantly and had no interest in banning it. But it broke
the assumption that the cut is automatically where cinema becomes art, and it
gave later directors a defensible alternative when they wanted to let a shot
breathe.

## What Editors Actually Weigh

Working editors tend to talk about this in less theoretical terms. Walter Murch
proposed a ranked list of what a cut should serve, and put emotion at the top by
a wide margin — over half the decision, in his rough accounting — with story
next, then rhythm, then the smaller mechanical concerns like where the viewer's
eye happens to be resting and whether the change preserves the geography of the
scene. His argument was that if a cut delivers the right feeling, an audience
will forgive a spatial contradiction it would otherwise notice; if it delivers
the wrong feeling, perfect continuity will not save it.

That hierarchy explains a lot of celebrated cutting. Dede Allen brought jagged,
overlapping edits to American features in the late 1960s, trimming frames off
reaction shots and letting sound run across picture changes, in part because the
French New Wave had already proved audiences could keep up. Jean-Luc Godard's
jump cuts in *Breathless* were partly a length problem solved by hacking chunks
out of the middle of shots, and the ragged result turned out to communicate
restlessness better than any smooth alternative. Thelma Schoonmaker's work for
Martin Scorsese runs the other way in places, holding on a face well past
comfort so that the audience starts to feel it is intruding.

The tools shaped the thinking more than anyone likes to admit. For most of the
century an editor stood at a Moviola or sat at a flatbed, physically cutting
film and hanging trims in a bin, and every change cost time. That expense
encouraged planning, and it made an editor live with a version long enough to
know whether it worked. Digital systems removed the cost. An assembly can now be
restructured in an afternoon, alternate versions kept in parallel, and a scene
tried forty ways. Something was gained in freedom and something lost in
commitment, and editors still argue about the exchange rate.

What has not changed is the basic transaction Kuleshov stumbled onto. Two images
in sequence are never merely two images. The viewer, who cannot help it, reaches
for a reason they are together, and whatever reason gets supplied becomes the
film. An editor is not assembling footage so much as governing that reflex —
deciding when to feed it, when to starve it, and when to let it produce a
meaning nobody photographed.
