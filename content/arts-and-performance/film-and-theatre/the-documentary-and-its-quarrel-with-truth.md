# The Documentary and Its Quarrel With Truth

The igloo in *Nanook of the North* is too big and it has no roof. Robert
Flaherty needed interior shots of a family waking, dressing, and eating, and no
camera available in 1922 could expose an image inside an actual snow house. So
he had one built oversized, then had half of it cut away and left open to the
Arctic sky, and filmed the Inuit family shivering through their domestic scenes
in the open air while pretending to be indoors. The hunting was arranged too.
The men used harpoons on the walrus because Flaherty asked them to, though they
owned rifles and had for years. The man billed as Nanook was named
Allakariallak.

The film is still routinely described as the first feature documentary, and it
deserves the title, and it is also staged from end to end. That contradiction is
not a scandal at the edge of the form. It is the form's central problem, and
every tradition in nonfiction filmmaking since amounts to a different proposal
for what to do about it.

John Grierson, who put the word documentary into English usage in a review of
Flaherty's next film, gave the definition that concedes the difficulty outright:
the creative treatment of actuality. Both halves are load-bearing. Something
real is in front of the lens, and somebody with intentions is behind it,
choosing where to stand, when to roll, and what to keep.

## The Record and the Rhythm

The earliest films were documentary by default, because nothing else had been
invented. The Lumière operators pointed a camera at workers leaving a factory
gate, a train pulling into a station, a baby being fed, and let it run until the
film ran out. These are not arguments about anything. They are a new machine
demonstrating that it works, and their power lay in the sheer fact of movement
reproduced.

Within a generation, filmmakers had discovered that real footage could be
organized by something other than storytelling. The city symphonies of the 1920s
took a metropolis from dawn to night and assembled it by rhythm and visual
rhyme — shutters opening, wheels turning, machines and crowds cut to the same
pulse. Walter Ruttmann did it with Berlin. Dziga Vertov did it with more
mischief in *Man with a Movie Camera*, a film that keeps showing you its own
cameraman climbing towers and its own editor at her bench splicing the very
shots you are watching. Vertov's argument was that the camera sees better than
the eye and that the audience should be reminded of the machinery rather than
lulled into forgetting it. Nearly every reflexive documentary since has been
working in his debt.

## Documentary as a Civic Instrument

Grierson's British movement in the 1930s pointed the form somewhere else
entirely. He worked inside government film units, funded by post offices and
gas boards and ministries, and he was blunt that he was not making art for its
own sake — he wanted documentary used as a hammer rather than a mirror, shaping
citizens rather than merely reflecting them. That the money came from sponsors
with interests was understood and largely unembarrassing.

The best of those films outran their briefs. *Night Mail*, nominally about a
train carrying post from London to Scotland, ends with verse written by W. H.
Auden cut to the rhythm of the wheels and scored by Benjamin Britten, and the
result is closer to a poem than a public information film. *Housing Problems*
did something quieter and arguably more radical: it put slum residents in front
of the camera to describe their own damp walls and vermin in their own words, at
a moment when the convention was an educated voice explaining the poor to
everyone else.

The war that followed pushed the civic tradition in both directions at once. On
one side sat the compilation film, assembled largely from existing footage,
including footage shot by the enemy and recut so that the same images argued the
opposite case — a demonstration, if anyone needed it, that a shot carries no
fixed meaning and takes whatever meaning its surroundings assign. On the other
side sat Humphrey Jennings, working inside the British propaganda apparatus and
producing films that barely argue at all. His portraits of wartime Britain
proceed by association — a dance band heard over a factory floor, a piano recital
in a gallery stripped of its paintings, harvesters working under a sky full of
aircraft — and they persuade precisely because they never tell you what to
think. Propaganda and poetry turn out to be perfectly capable of sharing a
budget line.

This tradition also produced the mode most people picture when they hear the
word documentary — an authoritative narrator over illustrative images, telling
you what the pictures mean. It is efficient and it is honest about being
argumentative. It is also the mode that shades most easily into propaganda. The
Nuremberg rallies filmed by Leni Riefenstahl in the mid-1930s present the
sharpest version of the problem, because the event itself was designed to be
photographed: the architecture, the formations, the camera pits dug in advance.
Nothing in the film is faked, and the whole thing is a fabrication.

## Getting Out of the Way

The next tradition began with hardware. Through the 1950s, synchronized sound
required a camera tethered by cable to a heavy recorder, which meant a tripod,
which meant that spontaneous life was effectively unfilmable with sound. Then
came lightweight 16mm cameras a person could shoulder, portable tape machines
from Nagra that a second crew member could carry, and crystal-controlled motors
that let picture and sound run in step without any cable between them. Two
people could now walk into a room and record what happened there.

The American filmmakers who seized this — Robert Drew's group, Richard Leacock,
D. A. Pennebaker, the Maysles brothers — built an ethic on top of the equipment
and called it direct cinema. No narration. No interviews. No lights. No asking
anyone to do anything twice. Follow a candidate through a primary campaign or a
Bible salesman through a bad week in Florida, shoot far more than you can use,
and find the film in the edit. The claim was that if you waited long enough and
stayed quiet enough, people would forget you and behave.

In France the ambition ran the other way. Jean Rouch and Edgar Morin borrowed
Vertov's phrase for film-truth and made *Chronicle of a Summer* by doing
everything direct cinema forbade. They stopped Parisians in the street and
demanded to know whether they were happy. They appeared on camera. They gathered
their subjects to watch the rushes and then filmed that screening, including the
participants complaining that other participants had been performing. Their
premise was that a camera never produces ordinary behavior, so the honest move
is to use it as a provocation and record what the provocation reveals.

Frederick Wiseman has spent a career in the gap between these positions. His
films take an institution — a state hospital, a high school, a welfare office, a
museum — and offer no narration, no interviews, no titles telling you who anyone
is. They also emerge from months of editing in which hundreds of hours become
two, and he has never claimed the result is unmediated. He has called his films
reality fictions, and the phrase is exact. His first, shot inside a Massachusetts
hospital for the criminally insane, was restricted by court order for decades on
privacy grounds, which is the clearest illustration available of the form's
unresolved ethical question: the subjects consented to a filmmaker, and what
they got was a permanent public record of their worst condition.

## Arguing in the First Person

Two later traditions gave up on effacement entirely. The essay film thinks out
loud. Chris Marker assembled footage from Tokyo and Guinea-Bissau and Iceland
into meditations narrated as letters from a traveler who may not exist. Agnès
Varda, carrying a small digital camera in her own hands, made a film about
people who glean leftover produce from harvested fields and kept turning the
lens on her own aging hands and her own hair, treating her presence as part of
the subject rather than an intrusion on it.

The other move was to admit that reconstruction is sometimes the only way to
examine an event nobody filmed. Errol Morris built *The Thin Blue Line* around a
murder in Texas and staged the shooting repeatedly, each version matching a
different witness's account, so the reenactments contradict one another and the
accumulating impossibility becomes the argument. Philip Glass's score refuses
the neutral tone that documentaries traditionally adopt. The film is
unapologetically constructed, and it helped free a man from a wrongful
conviction, which complicates any simple rule that stylization is dishonest.

Claude Lanzmann drew the opposite line with equal conviction. *Shoah* runs
nine-plus hours on the Nazi extermination camps and uses no archival footage at
all — only survivors, bystanders, and perpetrators speaking in the present, and
long, unhurried shots of the fields and rail lines as they look now. His
position was that existing footage was shot by the murderers for their own
purposes, and that reenactment would be obscene, so the only admissible evidence
was a living voice and the ground itself.

Critics have sorted these approaches into modes — expository, observational,
participatory, reflexive, poetic, performative — and the taxonomy is useful as
long as it is not mistaken for a hierarchy. None of the modes escapes the
original problem. A camera in a room changes the room. An editor choosing among
forty hours is making an argument whether or not a narrator states it. The
question a documentary answers is never quite whether it is true. It is whether
the choices it made are ones it is willing to show you.
