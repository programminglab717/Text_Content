# When the Movies Learned to Talk

The Vitaphone disc was sixteen inches across, spun at thirty-three and a third
revolutions per minute, and played from the inside outward — the needle starting
near the label and traveling toward the rim. A shaft linked the turntable to the
projector so that the record and the reel advanced together. It worked, in the
sense that thousands of theaters ran it for several years. It also meant that if
the film broke in the booth and the projectionist trimmed the tear, every frame
he removed threw the rest of that reel permanently out of sync, and the actor on
screen finished the scene speaking somebody else's lines slightly ahead of his
own mouth.

That fragility is a useful thing to hold in mind, because the arrival of
recorded sound is usually told as a story of sudden triumph, and it was nothing
of the kind. It was a messy, expensive, technically compromised scramble that
made films temporarily worse before it made them different, and the industry
went through it in roughly three years.

## Silence Was Never Silent

Start with the label. Films before 1927 were not shown in silence. A
neighborhood house had a pianist; a first-run palace had an orchestra of
substantial size, a Wurlitzer, and a librarian's worth of cue sheets matching
published mood music to the action on screen. Effects men worked wind machines
and coconut shells. In Japan the benshi stood beside the screen and narrated,
performed the dialogue, and in many cases drew a larger audience than the film
did. Sound was live, local, and different every night, which is exactly what the
studios eventually decided they could not tolerate.

Attempts to mechanize it went back decades. Edison's people linked phonographs
to projectors; Gaumont in France did the same. All of them ran into two walls.
Synchronization drifted, and the phonograph was far too quiet to fill a hall of
a thousand people. The second problem was the real one, and it was not solved in
the film business at all. It was solved by the vacuum tube. Once electrical
amplification and a decent loudspeaker existed — the same technology that gave
the world broadcast radio in the early 1920s — sound pictures became possible on
a commercial scale, and it was only a question of who would move first.

Lee de Forest, whose own work on the vacuum tube had helped make that possible,
tried. His Phonofilm process recorded sound photographically on the edge of the
film strip, which is the approach that eventually won, and through the mid-1920s
he made hundreds of shorts of singers, vaudevillians, and politicians. The major
studios were not interested. They had enormous investments in silent production
and a profitable international business built on a product that needed no
translation beyond swapping the intertitles.

## Warner Bros. Forces the Issue

Warner Bros., smaller and hungrier than the studios it was competing with, took
the gamble. *Don Juan* in 1926 carried a recorded orchestral score and sound
effects but not a word of dialogue, and the economic argument was explicit:
every theater in the country could now have a symphony behind its picture for
the price of a disc. *The Jazz Singer* followed in 1927 and is remembered as the
first talking picture, which it was not. It is a silent film with intertitles,
interrupted by songs, and a few minutes in which Al Jolson talks — some of it
reportedly ad-libbed around the recording, which is precisely why it landed. A
scripted speech would have sounded like a demonstration. A man interrupting
himself sounded like a person.

*Lights of New York* the following year was the first feature with dialogue all
the way through, and it is a stiff, stagey thing that shows every limitation of
the equipment. It also made money, and after that the argument was over. Within
about two years the studios had converted production and the exhibitors had
wired their houses, at an expense that shook out much of the independent end of
the business. Fox's Movietone system, which put an optical track on the film
itself, displaced the disc almost immediately for the obvious reason: a print
that carries its own sound cannot fall out of step with itself.

## The Camera Goes Back in a Box

Then came the technical humiliation. Silent cameras clattered, and the
microphones of the period were omnidirectional and indiscriminate, so the camera
had to be sealed away in a soundproofed booth with a glass window. The booth was
immobile and unventilated — crews called it the icebox, with the sarcasm that
implies — and a technique that had spent thirty years learning to move suddenly
could not. Microphones could not move either. They were hidden in flower
arrangements and desk telephones, and actors had to deliver lines toward them,
which produced the peculiar blocking of 1929 films in which everybody clusters
around a table for no visible reason.

Editing suffered worse. With sound recorded in one continuous take, cutting the
picture meant cutting the sound, so directors ran several cameras at once from
several booths to get their angles simultaneously. The result was coverage
matched in sound but lit for the convenience of three lenses at once, which is
to say lit badly.

The repairs came faster than the problems deserved. Padded housings called
blimps quietened the camera enough to bring it out of the booth within a couple
of years. Microphones went onto booms and became directional. Rouben Mamoulian,
shooting *Applause* in 1929, insisted on two microphones on separate channels so
that a whispered line and a sung one could be recorded at once and balanced
afterward, which sounds obvious and was not. Once sound could be re-recorded and
laid back against picture after the fact, dialogue could be replaced, effects
built separately, and the cut restored to the editor. By the early 1930s the
camera was mobile again and the grammar of the silent era had been recovered
with a soundtrack attached.

## Casualties and Adaptations

The people hurt worst were not the actors. They were the musicians. A large
share of working instrumentalists in the country had been employed in movie
theaters, and the pits emptied within a few seasons. The musicians' union
campaigned bitterly against what it called canned music, with advertisements
depicting a robot conductor, and lost completely.

The actors' story is more complicated than the version everybody knows. Careers
did end, and studios did import stage-trained performers from New York by the
trainful and hire voice coaches for the ones they kept. But most major silent
stars who wanted to continue did so. John Gilbert's collapse is the case always
cited, and the popular explanation — that his voice was absurdly high — does not
survive listening to the films; the likelier causes involve dialogue written for
a silent idiom, a contract dispute with his studio, and bad luck. What genuinely
changed was the style of performance. Silent acting had built a vocabulary of
gesture that could carry a scene without a word, and once words arrived that
vocabulary read as overdone almost overnight.

Sound also broke the export business. A silent film crossed borders by swapping
title cards; a talkie did not. For a few years studios shot multiple-language
versions of the same picture, using the same sets and different casts working in
shifts, with Paramount running a factory near Paris devoted to the practice.
Laurel and Hardy went further and performed their own foreign versions
phonetically, reading off boards, with results that European audiences seem to
have found endearing. It was ruinously expensive, and by the middle of the 1930s
dubbing and subtitling had settled the question.

## What the New Instrument Could Do

A few people saw past naturalism almost immediately. Eisenstein, Pudovkin, and
Alexandrov published a statement in 1928 — before most of them had made a sound
film — warning that using sound simply to let images talk would kill montage,
and arguing for sound used in counterpoint with the picture rather than in
service of it. René Clair, initially hostile, made films in which a song
crossing a courtyard does the work three scenes of dialogue would have. Alfred
Hitchcock, converting *Blackmail* to sound midway through production in 1929,
staged a breakfast table where a neighbor chatters about a murder and the
soundtrack blurs everything except the repeated word *knife*, which comes
through hard each time because the woman who cannot stop hearing it is the one
we are listening with.

Chaplin held out the longest, and not from stubbornness alone. The Tramp was a
figure built entirely out of movement, and giving him a specific voice would
have given him a specific nationality and class. *City Lights* in 1931 carried a
synchronized score and effects but no speech. *Modern Times*, five years later,
finally let him be heard — singing a song in invented gibberish, which preserved
the joke by refusing the language. Only with *The Great Dictator* did he make a
talking picture, and by then the silent film had been gone for a decade.

What the industry gained, past the novelty, was a second channel that could
contradict the first. Offscreen sound implied offscreen space, so a room could
extend past the frame. A whisper could carry to the back of a theater, which
meant intimacy no longer required a gesture large enough to be seen. Performers
stopped indicating and started underplaying. The camera, once it got out of the
box, went right back to moving — but everything in front of it had changed
scale.
