# Acting Methods from Stanislavski Onward

An actor in a rehearsal room says a line. The teacher asks what she wants from
the other person. She answers. She says the line again. The teacher asks why she
wants it now and not yesterday. She says it again, and this time something in
the room changes, and everyone watching feels it without being able to say
which of the words was different. Almost every system of actor training
invented in the last century and a half is an attempt to make that change
happen on purpose rather than by luck — and, more demandingly, to make it happen
again on Wednesday.

That is the real problem, and it is easy to lose sight of. Anyone can be moving
once. Theatre requires being moving on schedule, eight times a week, opposite a
colleague with a head cold, in front of an audience that includes someone
unwrapping a sweet. Film requires it in forty-second fragments shot out of
order across three months. The competing schools are not rival theories of
human emotion so much as rival engineering solutions to the problem of
repeatability.

## Before the System

Nineteenth-century acting had a technique, but it was a public one. Performers
worked from a shared vocabulary of gesture and vocal attitude that audiences
could read as fluently as a language: a hand to the brow, a step back with the
palm raised, a sustained tone dropping at the line's end. François Delsarte
codified an enormous amount of this into charts correlating posture, gesture,
and inner state, and his system spread through elocution teaching on both sides
of the Atlantic. Great actors built their reputations on a handful of famous
"points" — set-piece moments audiences came specifically to see, delivered much
the same way for thirty years.

Diderot had already put the sharpest question to this tradition in his essay on
the paradox of the actor. The performer who genuinely weeps, he argued, will
weep unevenly and unreliably; the one who has studied exactly what weeping looks
like and reproduces it at will, feeling nothing, is the one who devastates the
house every night. The claim is deliberately provocative and it has never gone
away. Every subsequent method is in some sense an answer to it.

## Stanislavski's Long Revision

Konstantin Stanislavski, co-founder of the Moscow Art Theatre with Vladimir
Nemirovich-Danchenko, spent roughly forty years building and then rebuilding
what he simply called the system. He was reacting against exactly the
declamatory habit described above, which by his account produced performances
that were skilful and dead.

His early apparatus is the part most people have heard of. Given circumstances:
everything true of the character's situation that the text supplies or implies.
The magic *if*: not "I am this person" but "if I were in these circumstances,
what would I do?" — a question an actor can actually answer. Units and
objectives: breaking a scene into stretches, each with a concrete want, phrased
as an action rather than a mood. The through-line: what the character is
pursuing across the whole play. Subtext: the current of intention running
beneath the spoken words. And emotion memory, the deliberate recall of a
personally experienced feeling to fuel a fictional one.

What is less widely known is that Stanislavski grew steadily more suspicious of
that last item. Emotion summoned directly proved unreliable and, in his
observation, sometimes damaging to the actors who leaned on it. In the last
decade of his life he shifted the weight of the system onto what he called the
method of physical actions: work outward from precise, achievable physical
behavior in pursuit of a clear objective, and let feeling arrive as a
by-product. He was still revising when he died in 1938, which is why almost
every faction in the later arguments could cite him honestly.

## The American Argument

The system reached New York in stages. Richard Boleslavsky and Maria
Ouspenskaya, both Moscow Art Theatre alumni, taught in the 1920s at the
American Laboratory Theatre, and their students carried the vocabulary into the
Group Theatre, the intensely serious collective active through the 1930s under
Harold Clurman, Cheryl Crawford, and Lee Strasberg.

Strasberg built his teaching on the early Stanislavski. Relaxation exercises to
strip out physical tension, sense memory to rebuild the specific textures of a
remembered experience, and affective memory to generate genuine, personally
sourced emotion on cue. The results, at their best, had a rawness American
audiences had not seen before.

The break came when Stella Adler, frustrated, went to Paris in the mid-1930s
and worked directly with Stanislavski for several weeks. She returned reporting
that he himself had moved away from emotional recall toward action and
imagination, and that the Group had been drilling a superseded draft. Strasberg
did not accept the correction. The split that followed produced the three
lineages that dominated American training for the rest of the century.

Strasberg took the Method proper to the Actors Studio, which he led from the
early 1950s, and pushed inward: relaxation, private moment, emotional
substitution drawn from the actor's own history. Adler taught the opposite
emphasis — that an actor's own biography is a small and shabby resource
compared with a trained imagination, and that the work is to research and
inhabit the given circumstances so thoroughly that the feeling follows.
Sanford Meisner, at the Neighborhood Playhouse, went sideways: he defined acting
as behaving truthfully within imagined circumstances, and built a curriculum
around getting the actor's attention entirely off themselves. His repetition
exercise — two actors trading an observation back and forth until the words
stop mattering and the impulses take over — is designed to make listening
involuntary. Uta Hagen, working in a different corner of the same city, wrote
practical exercises around objects and simple physical tasks, and a set of
questions an actor could answer about any scene: who am I, what has just
happened, what do I want, what is in my way.

## The Other Half of the Inheritance

The psychological tradition is only one branch. Vsevolod Meyerhold, who had
acted for Stanislavski, rejected interiority almost entirely and trained his
actors as athletes, in a system he called biomechanics: sequences of balance,
rebound, and refusal, designed to produce expressive physical form the way a
gymnast produces a vault. He was arrested in 1939 and executed the following
year, and his work went underground for a generation.

Bertolt Brecht wanted something different again. If an audience lost itself
inside a character's feelings, he argued, it would stop thinking about why that
character was in that situation. His actors were to show a person rather than
merge with one, holding a visible gap between performer and role, and to find
the *gestus* of a scene — the single physical attitude that reveals the social
relation underneath it. He used the image of a witness at a street accident
demonstrating what happened: vivid, committed, and plainly not pretending to be
the driver.

Michael Chekhov, nephew of the playwright and one of the Moscow Art Theatre's
most admired actors, developed a technique of psychological gesture: a large,
non-naturalistic movement — pulling, crushing, reaching — practised until it
produces its corresponding inner quality, then shrunk down and hidden inside
ordinary behavior. Jerzy Grotowski, in Poland, stripped theatre of scenery,
lighting effects, and costume to concentrate everything on the trained
performer, and put his actors through a physical and vocal discipline aimed at
removing blocks rather than adding skills. Jacques Lecoq, in Paris, taught
through neutral mask, movement analysis, and eventually clown, on the principle
that the body finds the character before the intellect does.

## What Actually Gets Taught Now

Walk into a conservatoire today and you will not find a single doctrine. You
will find a timetable. Voice work descends from teachers like Cicely Berry and
Kristin Linklater, aimed at freeing breath and releasing habitual constriction
so that a large space can be filled without shouting. Movement borrows from
Laban's analysis of effort, from Alexander technique for undoing postural habit,
from Lecoq for play and mask. Text work, especially in the British tradition,
still starts from the outside — with scansion, with the shape of the verse,
with what the shoes and the corset do to a spine — on the assumption that
feeling will come to meet a well-made form.

The old quarrel between inside-out and outside-in has quietly settled into a
working truce, partly because film demanded it. Screen acting punishes anything
indicated or pushed, and rewards thought that is genuinely occurring in front of
the lens; but it also demands that the thought occur within a mark taped on the
floor and a focal plane a few centimetres deep. Most working actors now carry a
mixed toolkit and reach for whatever gets them through the afternoon: an
objective from Stanislavski, a physical task from Hagen, a repetition warm-up
from Meisner, a gesture borrowed from Chekhov, and the plain outside-in craft of
knowing how to stand still and be watched.

What survives across all of it is the shift Stanislavski made and never quite
finished: the recognition that an actor cannot order themselves to feel, but can
always order themselves to do. Wanting something specific from the person
opposite, and going after it, turns out to be the one instruction that works at
eight o'clock on a Wednesday.
