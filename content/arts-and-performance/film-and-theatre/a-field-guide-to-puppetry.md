# A Field Guide to Puppetry

Three people in grey crouch around a horse built of cane, aluminium and gauze.
One is inside the chest working the heart and the front legs, one is at the
hindquarters, one stands at the head with a handle that turns the ears and tips
the muzzle. Nothing about the construction is hidden — you can see straight
through the ribs to the operator's arms. Then the horse takes a breath. The
barrel of its chest lifts and falls, the ears swivel toward a noise offstage,
and within about a minute nobody in the audience is watching the three people
any more, even though all three remain in plain sight for the next two hours.

That is the central fact of puppetry, and it is stranger than it first appears.
The audience is not fooled. They know perfectly well what they are looking at.
They choose to attend to the horse instead, and once they have made that choice
they experience its distress and its exhaustion as if it were a living thing.
Puppetry is less a trick played on an audience than a contract offered to one.

## The Four Things a Puppet Must Do

Most contemporary training reduces the manipulation of a figure to a handful of
disciplines, and they are surprisingly consistent across styles.

Breath comes first. A living body is never entirely still; it rises and settles
on a rhythm, and that rhythm changes with exertion and emotion. A puppet that
does not breathe is furniture. Puppeteers spend their first weeks doing nothing
else, moving a figure a centimetre up and a centimetre down until it reads as
alive from the back of a room.

Focus is second. A puppet looks where its head points, and an audience follows
that line without being asked. Because the puppet's eyes do not move, the whole
head must do the work of a glance, which means aiming it accurately and holding
it. When two puppets look at each other and then both look at a door, the door
becomes the most important object on stage.

Weight is third. A puppet has almost no mass, so its apparent weight must be
manufactured: a hand set down on a table must press, a body lowered into a chair
must settle, a foot must meet the floor and stop there. Most amateur puppetry
fails here, because the figure floats.

The fourth is the fixed point — the discipline of keeping some part of the
figure genuinely still while another part moves. Movement only reads against
stillness. A puppet in which everything wobbles at once looks like a bundle of
cloth being shaken, which of course is exactly what it is.

## Strings, Rods, Hands, Shadows

The physical types are ancient and they persist, mostly because each solves a
different problem.

Marionettes hang from above on strings run to a control bar. A simple figure
might use nine strings; a virtuoso one carries many more, with separate controls
for shoulders, elbows, hips, knees, and the spine. Gravity does much of the
work, which gives a marionette its characteristic hanging softness, and makes
the walk the hardest thing in the craft — the puppet must be swung, caught, and
weighted onto alternating feet without ever appearing to be lowered onto them.
The European court and fairground traditions ran on marionettes, and the form
carried into the twentieth century through companies like the Salzburg
Marionette Theatre and the Czech school, which produced enduring comic
characters and a national attachment to puppet stages that survived several
changes of regime. Sicilian *opera dei pupi* substitutes stiff metal rods for
most of the strings — one to the head, one to the sword arm — trading finesse
for the brute decisiveness needed to stage armoured knights hacking at each
other for an hour.

Rod puppets are operated from below, on a central rod supporting the body and
thinner rods to the hands. The mechanics are direct and the movement is
correspondingly crisp, which suits both the carved, painted figures of the
Sundanese *wayang golek* and a great deal of modern stage work.

Hand or glove puppets put the operator's fingers inside the figure. The tradition
that reached Britain as Punch and Judy descends from the Italian commedia
character Pulcinella, and it kept the booth — a narrow fit-up the operator
stands inside, hidden, holding both characters above their head for the whole
show. The Punch performer also uses a swazzle, a small reed device held in the
mouth, which produces the rasping voice and has the useful side effect of making
the most offensive lines difficult to make out.

Shadow puppetry replaces the object with its silhouette. In Javanese and
Balinese *wayang kulit*, figures are cut from buffalo hide, pierced with
patterns so that the shadow is lace rather than a solid block, gilded and
painted on a side almost nobody sees directly, and worked against a stretched
cloth screen lit from behind. A single performer, the *dalang*, manipulates
every figure, speaks every voice, narrates, and cues the gamelan orchestra, in
performances that traditionally run through the night. The repertoire comes from
the Ramayana and Mahabharata, and the characters are so codified that an
audience identifies each by the shape of its nose and the tilt of its head.
Related shadow traditions run through China and, in a bawdier register, through
Turkey, where the sparring pair of Karagöz and Hacivat carried satire that no
living actor could have safely delivered.

## The Puppeteer in Plain Sight

Japanese *bunraku* made the most radical formal choice of any tradition, and
made it centuries ago: it put the operators on stage and dressed most of them in
black without pretending they are invisible. A figure roughly half life-size
takes three people. The principal operator supports the head and works the right
arm, standing on raised wooden clogs to bring the puppet up to height, and in
many companies performs unhooded, face visible. A second operator works the left
arm, a third the feet. All three must breathe together, and the apprenticeship
to the feet alone is measured in years. At the side of the stage a chanter
delivers every character's speech and the narration, accompanied by a single
shamisen player. Nothing is concealed and the emotional effect is overwhelming
— the standard observation of first-time audiences is that they start out
watching the operators and end up unable to look anywhere but the puppet's
hands.

Heinrich von Kleist got at something like this in his short essay on the
marionette theatre, written in 1810. His argument, delivered through a dancer he
claims to have met, is that the puppet possesses a grace that a human performer
cannot achieve, because it has no self-consciousness and no vanity; its limbs
simply obey the physics of a moving centre of gravity, and it never strikes an
attitude for effect. The essay is a paradox rather than a manual, but any
puppeteer recognises the observation. A wooden arm falling under gravity is more
convincingly a falling arm than most actors can manage.

## What a Puppet Can Do That a Body Cannot

The practical case for puppetry has always rested on the things it makes easy.
Scale, first: a figure can be six inches or thirty feet, and the French company
Royal de Luxe has walked giants several storeys high through European cities,
hauled by teams on ropes and gantries, closing streets for days. Transformation:
a puppet can age, come apart, burn, become another creature, or die in a way no
insured performer can. Non-human bodies: a bird, an insect, a god, a god with
the head of an animal. And an unsentimental relationship with death, since the
figure can genuinely stop — the moment a puppet goes limp and becomes wood again
is one of the most reliably devastating effects in the theatre, and it works
precisely because the audience has been doing the animating all along and is
suddenly asked to stop.

There is a political tradition too. The Bread and Puppet Theater, founded by
Peter Schumann and long based in rural Vermont, built enormous papier-mâché
figures for street processions and protest, on the reasoning that a twenty-foot
mourning woman carried through a city is harder to argue with and harder to
forget than a speech.

The craft also migrated onto screens. Television puppetry developed its own
grammar, with performers working below the set line, arms held overhead for
hours, watching monitors to see what the camera sees rather than the puppet
itself — an inversion that takes months to learn and changes the acting
entirely, since the performance exists only in the frame. Animatronics and
creature effects grew out of the same shops. So, arguably, did a good deal of
digital character animation, which faces the identical problem: how to make a
constructed thing appear to want something, using breath, focus, weight, and a
still point to move against.

At the end of a show the puppet is usually set down or hung on its rack. It goes
from being a person to being a bundle of cane and cloth in about a second, and
the people who have just spent two hours giving it a life walk off and get
changed. Performers describe this transition in oddly consistent terms across
very different traditions — a slight reluctance, a habit of arranging the figure
carefully rather than dropping it, an unwillingness to leave it face down.
Nobody believes it is alive. Everybody puts it to bed properly anyway.
