# From the Amphitheatre to the Black Box

The guide at Epidaurus does the same demonstration every time. She walks to the
centre of the circular floor, tears a sheet of paper, and lets a coin fall onto
the stone, and the fifty-fourth row hears both. Acousticians have since argued
that much of the effect comes from the seating itself: the rows of limestone
benches act as a corrugated filter, damping the low-frequency rumble of wind and
crowd while reflecting the higher frequencies of a human voice back up the
slope. Whether or not the trick is quite as magical as the tour suggests, the
building does what a theatre has to do. Fourteen thousand people can hear one
person, and nearly all of them can see.

Every theatre built since has been answering the same two questions in different
ways. Where do the watchers sit, and what is the relationship between them and
the watched? The answers have swung between extremes — open and enclosed,
shared light and separated dark, surrounding and facing — and the history is
less a march of improvements than a long argument that keeps changing sides.

## The Hillside and the Hut

The Greek theatre of the fifth century BC took its shape from the ground. A bowl
of hillside was cut into tiers of seating, the *theatron* or seeing-place, which
wrapped somewhat more than a semicircle around a flat circular floor. That
floor, the *orchestra*, was the dancing place, and it belonged to the chorus —
the singing, moving collective that carried much of the dramatic weight. Two
open ramps at the sides, the *parodoi*, let performers and audience in.

Behind the orchestra stood the *skene*, originally a practical hut for changing
masks and costume, which gradually acquired a painted face and became the
backdrop. The word survives in "scene" and "scenery", which is a fair record of
what happened to it. The Greeks also built machines: a crane, the *mechane*, for
lowering a god into the action, and a rolling platform, the *ekkyklema*, for
pushing the results of offstage violence into view, since the convention was
that killing happened where the audience could not see it.

Crucially, the whole event took place outdoors in daylight during a civic
religious festival. Performers and audience shared the same light and the same
weather. Nobody was hidden.

Rome inherited the form and rebuilt it as architecture rather than landscape.
Roman theatres were freestanding masonry structures that did not need a hillside,
with the seating bowl and the stage house bonded into a single enclosed building.
The orchestra shrank to a semicircle and became seating for magistrates and
guests. Behind the now-raised stage rose the *scaenae frons*, a permanent
multi-storey wall of columns, niches, statues and doorways — a fixed
architectural backdrop rather than a changeable one. Awnings could be winched
across to shade the crowd. The Roman theatre was a public building of the same
family as the basilica and the baths, and when the empire's building programme
stopped, that whole category of structure stopped with it.

## When the Theatre Had No Building

For most of the European Middle Ages there was no theatre architecture, because
performance travelled to the audience instead. Drama re-emerged inside the
liturgy, spread to the church porch and then the churchyard, and eventually into
the street as the trade guilds took it over.

Two arrangements did the work. In the pageant-wagon cycles of English towns, an
episode was staged on a wheeled cart that pulled up at a street corner, played,
and moved on to the next stop while the following wagon arrived. In the
place-and-scaffold plan, a field or town square was ringed with fixed stations —
mansions representing Heaven, Herod's court, the Mouth of Hell — with the open
ground between them, the *platea*, serving as neutral space that became whatever
the action required. Audiences stood among the scenery and moved with it. The
scenic logic was simultaneous rather than sequential: everywhere in the story
existed at once, and you looked where the action was.

## The Wooden O and the Prince's Chair

Two very different buildings appeared within about thirty years of each other,
and between them they set the terms for everything since.

The English public playhouse — the Theatre of 1576, the Rose, the Globe of 1599
— was descended from inn yards and from the polygonal rings built for bear
baiting. A tall frame of timber galleries enclosed an open yard where the
cheapest customers stood. A large platform stage thrust out into that yard so
the audience surrounded it on three sides. Behind it, the tiring house wall
provided two doors, a curtained discovery space, and a gallery above that could
be a battlement, a balcony, or a musicians' loft. A canopy — the heavens —
covered part of the stage and contained a trap for descents; another trap in the
platform floor opened into what everyone called hell. Performances ran in the
afternoon, in daylight, with no way to dim anything, so the audience watched the
play and each other simultaneously, and the text carried the scenery: a
character announced the wood, the night, the storm.

The Italian court took the opposite path. Renaissance designers, intoxicated by
linear perspective, built stage pictures that resolved correctly from one ideal
viewpoint — which was, conveniently, the seat of the prince. The Teatro Olimpico
in Vicenza, completed in the 1580s, still has its startling forced-perspective
streets receding behind the openings of a classical screen. The Teatro Farnese
in Parma, a generation later, is usually cited among the earliest surviving
houses with a full proscenium arch.

That arch changed everything. It framed the stage as a picture, concealed the
machinery at the sides and above, and made possible the great baroque scene-change
systems — banks of painted wings sliding in grooves, or mounted on chariots
running on rails below the floor, all of them connected to a single winch so
that an entire landscape could transform in seconds in full view of the
audience. The stage was raked, sloping up away from the crowd, to help the
perspective; the words upstage and downstage are a fossil of that slope.

## Boxes, Darkness, and the Fourth Wall

The commercial descendant of the court theatre was the horseshoe auditorium:
tiers of boxes curving around a deep pit, which became the standard opera and
playhouse form for two centuries. Its sightlines onto the stage were frequently
terrible and its sightlines onto other members of the audience were excellent,
which was largely the point. The house was lit throughout the performance.
People arrived late, talked, and looked around.

Gas lighting, and then electricity, made it possible to take the light off the
audience, and the nineteenth century gradually did. Richard Wagner's Bayreuth
Festspielhaus of 1876 pushed the logic to its conclusion: no boxes, a single
fan-shaped rake of seats all facing forward with roughly equal sightlines, the
orchestra sunk out of view in a covered pit, and the auditorium in darkness. The
audience was to disappear and attend.

Naturalism supplied the matching stage. The box set — three real walls, a
ceiling, working doors, actual furniture — turned the proscenium opening into
the invisible fourth wall of a room, through which the audience watched people
who behaved as though unobserved. It was a genuinely radical proposition and it
became, within a few decades, simply what a theatre was.

## Breaking the Frame

The twentieth century spent much of its energy attacking that arrangement.
Jacques Copeau stripped his Paris stage back to a bare permanent architectural
platform. Walter Gropius designed a Total Theatre for Erwin Piscator whose
seating bank would rotate to convert the room between end-stage, thrust, and
arena; it was never built, but the idea circulated widely.

The thrust stage came back in earnest after the Second World War. Tyrone Guthrie
and the designer Tanya Moiseiwitsch built an open stage under a tent at Stratford
in Ontario in 1953, surrounded on three sides, and repeated the principle in
Minneapolis a decade later; Chichester in England followed the same model. The
argument was that actors surrounded by an audience play to people rather than to
a frame, and that scenery becomes unnecessary when the audience opposite you is
visible behind the actor's head. In Scarborough, Stephen Joseph pushed further
and put the audience the whole way round.

The black box came out of the same impulse but refused to commit to any of the
answers. It is, deliberately, an unremarkable room: rectangular, a few hundred
seats at most, walls and ceiling painted matte black to vanish, an exposed
technical grid overhead, a flat floor, and seating on demountable rostra that can
be assembled end-on, in traverse, in the round, or not at all. Everything is
adjustable and nothing is permanent. It became the default studio space
attached to almost every new theatre and drama department from the 1960s onward,
partly for ideological reasons and partly because it is cheap, forgiving, and
generates rehearsal-room intimacy without any architecture having to be paid
for.

It is worth noticing that none of these forms actually replaced its predecessor.
A company today may work in a black box in the spring, a nineteenth-century
proscenium house in the autumn, and a reconstructed Elizabethan thrust in
between, and the actors adjust their scale and their address accordingly. What
has not moved at all is the physical constant the Greeks were already working
around: a face is legible at perhaps twenty metres and a gesture at not much
more than thirty. Every seating plan ever drawn has been a negotiation with that
limit, and the reason the modern black box seats two hundred rather than
fourteen thousand is that it has chosen the face over the crowd.
