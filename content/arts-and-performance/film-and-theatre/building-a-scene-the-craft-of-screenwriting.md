# Building a Scene: The Craft of Screenwriting

Open a produced screenplay at random and the first thing you notice is the
white space. Perhaps a quarter of the page carries text; the rest is margin and
air. That layout is not a matter of taste. It descends from a rough industrial
equivalence — one page of correctly formatted script runs about a minute on
screen — and the equivalence only survives if the writer refuses to fill the
page. A screenplay has to be read fast, often by someone reading a dozen of
them over a weekend, and it has to convert cleanly into a schedule, a budget,
and eventually a shot list. Nearly every convention on the page is serving one
of those two masters.

So the form is severe. Scene headings in capitals give interior or exterior,
location, and day or night, which are the three facts a production manager
needs before anything else. Action runs in the present tense in short blocks,
because a block longer than four or five lines starts reading like a novel and
stops reading like a film. Character names sit centered above their lines.
Little else is permitted. A spec script avoids camera directions, avoids
explaining what a character feels, avoids adjectives no lens can photograph.
The writer who types *she is devastated* has written a private note. The writer
who types *she keeps folding the same towel* has written something an actor can
do and a camera can see.

## What a Scene Is Actually For

The working unit of a screenplay is not the line or the act. It is the scene,
and the most useful definition of a scene is brutally functional: a scene is a
stretch of continuous time in one place at the end of which the situation
differs from the situation at the start. The difference can be in information —
someone now knows what they did not know. It can be in relationship — two
people who were allies are now wary of each other. It can be in position — a
door that was open has closed, an option has been spent, a promise has been
made that cannot be unmade.

There is a plain test for this. Write one short clause describing the state of
affairs entering the scene and another describing it on the way out. *He
suspects her / He is certain.* *She could still walk away / She cannot.* If the
two clauses are identical, the scene is decoration, however well written, and
it will be cut — if not by the writer then by the editor, six months and
considerable money later.

The engine that produces the change is almost always characters at
cross-purposes. This is not the same as argument. Two people who love each
other, sitting quietly, can drive a scene beautifully if one has come to confess
and the other has come to leave. Each character in a scene wants something
specific from the other, wants it now, and is pursuing it with a strategy. When
those strategies collide, the scene generates heat without anyone raising their
voice.

Most scenes also have a turn: a single moment where the pressure changes
direction, where the person who was in control is suddenly not. Skilled writers
place that turn late and get out immediately afterward. The old workshop advice
to enter a scene as late as possible and leave as early as possible is really
advice about this. Cut the arrival, the hello, the removal of the coat, and the
goodbye. Start on the second question. End on the line that lands.

## Writing a Mind From the Outside

Prose can go inside a skull and report from there. Film cannot, except by
cheating — voiceover, dream sequence, a character conveniently talking to a
therapist. A screenwriter's hardest and most characteristic work is inventing
external equivalents for internal states.

The tools are behavior, choice, and object. Behavior means what someone does
when they think no one is watching, or what they do with their hands while
saying something else. Choice means the fork the character actually takes, since
a person is defined much more precisely by what they pick under pressure than by
what they claim to value. Object means the physical thing that carries meaning
and can accumulate more of it: a wedding ring, a service revolver, a child's
drawing pinned to a fridge. An object introduced casually in the first twenty
minutes and returned to in the last twenty acquires a charge that no dialogue
can match, because the audience supplies the connection themselves and feels
clever for it.

Chekhov's remark about the rifle on the wall is usually quoted as a rule about
guns. It is better read as a rule about economy. Everything a camera lingers on
is a promise, and an audience keeps an unconscious ledger of promises made.

Exposition is the same problem wearing a different hat. The audience must learn
things, and characters who explain those things to each other sound like
characters explaining things to each other. The reliable dodges: hand the
information to someone who does not want to hear it; make another character
determined to conceal it; deliver it while both parties are busy doing something
physically urgent; or fragment it so that the audience assembles the picture
from pieces. That last one works because attention given voluntarily is worth
several times attention demanded.

## Dialogue and the Thing Underneath It

People almost never state what they want. They approach it sideways, test the
ground, and retreat. Dialogue on screen is largely a record of what characters
say instead of the thing, which is why subtext is not an ornament but the
substance of the craft.

Practically, that means writing each character a distinct strategy rather than a
distinct verbal tic. A vocabulary quirk is a costume. A strategy — this one
flatters, this one interrogates, this one answers a slightly different question
than the one asked — is a person. Evasion is enormously useful. A character who
changes the subject has told the audience exactly where the wound is, without
either party naming it.

The other lesson is that spoken language has a rhythm written language does not.
People interrupt. They trail off. They use contractions constantly and complete
sentences rarely. A line that reads smoothly on the page can be unspeakable in
the mouth, and the only reliable detector is reading it aloud, badly, in a room
by yourself.

Silence counts as dialogue. A line left unanswered puts weight on the person who
did not answer. So does a reply that arrives one beat late.

## Sequences and the Long Shape

Above the scene sits the sequence: a run of perhaps eight to fifteen minutes
with its own small tension and its own resolution, the resolution of which
creates the next problem. The unit is partly a fossil of the projection booth,
since a reel of thirty-five millimeter film ran roughly ten minutes and early
narrative cinema was constructed in reel-sized chunks. The habit outlasted the
technology because it matches something real about attention. A feature is
essentially eight or so of these strung end to end.

The familiar three-act description sits above that, and it is worth being
careful about what it claims. Acts are not a rulebook handed down from
antiquity; they are a way of noticing that the audience's central question
changes twice during a well-built film. For a while the question is *what is
going on and what does this person want.* Then it becomes *can they get it, now
that they have committed.* Then it becomes *what will they sacrifice.* Templates
that specify a page number for each turn are describing a statistical habit of
the studio feature, not a law. Plenty of durable films ignore the schedule
entirely; almost none ignore the underlying fact that the question has to keep
changing.

## The Draft Nobody Sees

First drafts exist to be replaced, and experienced writers build revision
routines that attack the page from odd angles. Read only the dialogue, skipping
all action, and see whether the scenes still track. Read only the action, and
see whether the film would be comprehensible with the sound off — a surprising
number of good films nearly are. Delete the first and last line of every scene
and check how many you want back. Count how many consecutive scenes take place
in rooms with two people sitting down.

And then let it go, because the screenplay is the one literary form designed to
be consumed by its own production. The director will cut the line the writer
was proudest of. An actor will find a silence better than the speech. The editor
will discover that scene fourteen works perfectly well if you start it in the
middle and never establish the room at all. A script is not a finished object
but a proposal precise enough to be argued with, and the measure of a good one
is how much better the argument gets.
