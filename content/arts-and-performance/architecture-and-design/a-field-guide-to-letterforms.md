# A Field Guide to Letterforms

Hold a well-set page at arm's length and let your eyes go soft until the words
stop resolving. What remains is a grey field with a texture — even or blotchy,
open or tight, warm or cold. Printers have called this the colour of the page
for centuries, and judging it is the first skill in reading type, because
everything that has gone right or wrong in the setting shows up there before you
can consciously name it.

Bring the page back into focus and the field becomes a lot of small decisions.
Type is one of the few designed objects most people look at for hours a day
without ever looking at, and the vocabulary for noticing it is small enough to
learn in an afternoon.

## The Parts

Start with a lowercase n. The vertical is a stem; the arch that leaves it is a
shoulder; the enclosed white inside a b or o is a counter, and the partially
enclosed white in a c or an e is an aperture. The invisible line the letters sit
on is the baseline. The height of that n — of the lowercase without ascenders —
is the x-height, and it is the single most consequential proportion in a typeface.
A large x-height relative to cap height makes text look bigger at the same
nominal point size, fills the line, and holds up at small sizes; taken far, it
flattens the distinctions between letters and makes long text monotonous. A small
x-height with long ascenders and descenders gives an elegant, airy, slightly
old-fashioned page that goes illegible on a phone.

Look at the strokes. In most serif faces they vary in thickness, thick on some
parts of the curve and thin on others, and the imaginary line connecting the two
thinnest points of an o is the axis or stress. If that axis leans to the left,
the face descends from handwriting with a broad-nibbed pen. If it is dead
vertical, it descends from engraving. The ratio between the thickest and thinnest
stroke is the contrast, and it runs from almost none in a slab serif to violent
in a Didone.

Then the serifs themselves, the small finishing strokes at the ends of stems.
They can be bracketed, joining the stem on a curve; or unbracketed, meeting it at
a right angle like a shelf; thin as a hair, or as heavy as the stem. Where a
letter ends without a serif — the tail of an a, the top of a c — that ending is a
terminal, and its shape, teardrop or beak or sheared flat, is often the quickest
way to recognise a face you have seen before.

## The Tool Inside the Letter

Nearly every family of shapes in the Latin alphabet is a fossil of the
instrument that made it. Trace the history through the tools and the
classifications explain themselves.

Roman capitals were brush-drawn onto stone before being cut, which is why their
serifs flare and their proportions vary letter by letter — a rigidly geometric E
next to a wide O. The lowercase arrived a thousand years later out of scribal
hands using a broad-nibbed pen held at a consistent angle. Hold such a pen at
roughly thirty degrees and the thick and thin strokes fall out automatically,
with the stress tilted to the left. When Venetian printers in the 1470s cut
metal type after the humanist scribes' hand, they froze that pen angle into
metal. Those humanist and old-style faces still have the slanted stress, modest
contrast, and slightly irregular warmth of a written letter, and they remain the
most comfortable things to read at length.

Two centuries of refinement gradually straightened the axis and sharpened the
contrast, driven by smoother paper, better presses, and burins rather than pens.
Baskerville's types in mid-eighteenth-century Birmingham were thought glaring by
his contemporaries — the whites were too bright, the thins too fine. By the end
of that century Bodoni in Parma and the Didots in Paris had pushed all the way to
vertical stress, hairline unbracketed serifs, and a brilliant, brittle page that
looks magnificent large and disintegrates small. That is the Didone, and it is
the reason fashion magazines still look like fashion magazines.

The nineteenth century industrialised display. Posters needed letters visible
across a street, so foundries produced fat faces, slab serifs with serifs as
heavy as the stems, and, early in the century, the first printed types with no
serifs at all — awkward, blocky things shown almost apologetically in English
specimen books. The sans serif took a hundred years to become respectable. It
did so in two different ways: through the grotesques of German foundries,
refined into the neutral, tightly fitted faces of the 1950s that dominated
corporate printing for the rest of the century; and through the humanist sans,
which keeps the proportions and skeleton of a roman letter while dropping the
serifs, an approach used for London's Underground lettering before the First
World War and widely copied since.

A practical way to tell those two apart: look at a lowercase a and a c. If the
aperture is nearly closed, the terminals cut off horizontally, and the letters
feel like variations on a circle and a rectangle, you are in grotesque
territory. If the apertures open out and the terminals angle away, it is
humanist, and it will almost certainly be easier to read in a paragraph and
easier to distinguish at distance — which is why signage systems, airport
lettering and road signs keep converging on that model.

## Spacing, Which Is the Real Work

Drawing the letters is the visible part of type design and not the hard part. The
hard part is the space around and between them, because reading depends on
consistent rhythm far more than on the beauty of any individual character. A
typeface is not a collection of shapes; it is a system of relationships in which
no letter is ever seen alone.

Each glyph carries side bearings, blank strips left and right, tuned so that the
white between any two letters looks equal to the white between any other two.
Looks equal, not measures equal: the gap between two straight stems, as in an
n next to an i, must be measurably narrower than the gap between two round
letters, or the round pair will appear to drift apart. Certain combinations
defeat side bearings entirely — a capital A beside a V, a T beside an o, a
capital F over a comma — and those get kerning pairs, individual exceptions
stored in the font. A serious text face may carry thousands of them.

At the paragraph level the two levers are measure and leading. Measure is line
length, and the comfortable range for continuous reading is roughly forty-five to
seventy-five characters; much longer and the eye loses the return to the next
line, much shorter and the rhythm breaks into fragments and the word spacing goes
ragged. Leading is the vertical distance between baselines, and it has to grow as
the measure grows, because a longer line needs a more clearly defined channel to
return along. Tight leading on a wide measure is the most common reason a page
that uses a good typeface still feels unreadable.

Word spacing deserves more suspicion than it gets. Justified text achieves its
flush right edge by stretching and squeezing the spaces between words, and
without hyphenation and a decent line-breaking algorithm the result is rivers of
white running down the column. Ragged-right setting keeps the word space
constant, which is usually the better trade for anything set narrow.

## Size, and the Loss of the Optical Scale

Metal type was cut separately at every size, and the punchcutter changed the
design as the size shrank: larger x-height, thicker hairlines, more generous
spacing, blunter details, wider counters, so the letters would survive ink spread
and small dimensions. The same family at twelve point and seventy-two point were
visibly different drawings, and nobody thought of that as inconsistency.

Photocomposition and then the first digital fonts threw this away, because a
single outline could be scaled to any size instantly and cheaply. The result was
text faces that looked spindly and cramped small and display settings that looked
clumsy large. The knowledge did not disappear — designers who knew the problem
shipped separate text and display cuts, and faces drawn for hostile printing
conditions used exaggerated ink traps, notches carved into the joints that fill
in on press and vanish. Variable font technology has now restored an optical size
axis, so a single file can subtly thicken its hairlines and open its spacing as
the rendered size drops. It is one of the few genuine advances of the last
decade, and it is mostly a recovery of what foundries did by hand.

Screens added their own distortions. For years, type had to be rendered onto a
grid coarse enough that a stem could land between two pixels and blur, which
produced hinting — instructions inside the font telling the rasteriser to snap
particular features to the pixel grid — and a generation of faces drawn to
survive it. High-resolution displays have quietly made most of that obsolete, and
the remaining screen-specific problems are dull ones: fonts that fail to load,
fallback stacks that shift the layout, and text set in a light weight and a pale
grey by someone who has never read anything outdoors.

Beatrice Warde argued in 1930 that printing should be like a crystal goblet —
transparent, showing the wine and not itself. It is a good argument for a novel
and a bad one for a poster, which is the whole reason the discipline has
something to argue about.
