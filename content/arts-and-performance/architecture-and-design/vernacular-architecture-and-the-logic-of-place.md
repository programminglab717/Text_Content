# Vernacular Architecture and the Logic of Place

From the roof of any old house in Yazd, on the edge of the Iranian central
desert, you can see dozens of tall square chimneys standing above the mud-brick
city, each one open on several sides and divided internally into shafts. They
are not chimneys. A badgir catches whatever breeze is moving at roof level,
turns it down into the house, and pushes the hot air inside up and out the
downwind shaft. Some are built over a basement cistern or the cool damp air of
an underground water channel, so the incoming air is chilled by evaporation
before it reaches anybody. Nobody designed this in the sense an architect
designs; it accumulated, over many centuries, in a place where getting it wrong
meant a summer that could kill you.

That accumulation is what the word vernacular is trying to name. Not a style,
not a period, and not quite a tradition either — rather a way of producing
buildings in which the design is already settled before anyone starts, carried
in the hands of local builders, adjusted incrementally, and validated by the
brutally direct test of whether the last one leaked.

## A Design Process Without Designers

Bernard Rudofsky put the idea in front of a wide audience in 1964 with an
exhibition of photographs at the Museum of Modern Art called Architecture
Without Architects: cliff villages, granaries, salt pans, terraces, roofs made
of things nobody would have thought of as roofing material. His argument was
partly a rebuke to the profession, and the rebuke was overstated, but the
photographs made a point that survives it. Buildings produced without drawings,
without a named author, and without any theory at all were routinely better
adapted to their sites than what was being built alongside them.

The mechanism is selection rather than invention. A village has a type — the
house everyone builds — and each new house is mostly a copy of the last one
with small adjustments. Adjustments that fail are visible. A roof pitched too
shallowly for the local rain rots its thatch within a few seasons and the
builder hears about it; a wall of the wrong thickness makes a house nobody
wants to sleep in during August. Over enough generations the type converges on
something well fitted to the climate, the available materials, the size of
households, and the way work is organized. It converges slowly, and it does not
transfer: the type that works in one valley may be wrong two valleys over.

## Climate, Argued in Mud and Timber

The most legible thing about vernacular buildings is how directly they encode
weather. In hot dry climates with large swings between day and night, the
answer is mass. Thick walls of mud brick, adobe, or stone absorb the day's heat
so slowly that it arrives inside around the time the sun goes down, by which
point the outside air is cooling and the heat can be flushed out. Rooms are
arranged around a courtyard that traps cool night air at the bottom like water
in a basin. Openings are small, high, and shaded. In Cairo and across the Gulf
the wooden lattice screen called a mashrabiya shades a window while letting air
through, and often held porous water jars in the airflow to be cooled by
evaporation. In Shibam in Yemen the same mud brick goes up five, six, eight
storeys, a whole city of towers packed close so that the streets between them
stay in shadow most of the day.

In hot wet climates mass is exactly wrong, because nights are not cool enough
to discharge it and the real enemy is humidity. So the buildings go light and
open: raised on posts to catch the breeze and stay clear of flooding and
vermin, walls of woven panel or louvre rather than masonry, and a steep roof
with deep overhangs and a ventilated ridge that lets hot air escape at the top
while rain runs off fast. The great saddle-backed roofs of Sumatra and Sulawesi
are the same logic carried to the point of spectacle.

Cold climates invert the priorities again. Insulation matters more than mass,
so turf, sod, thatch, and stacked logs do the work; the building crouches low,
buries part of itself in a slope, and shrinks its openings. A Hebridean
blackhouse had double drystone walls with an earth core, rounded corners to
shed the Atlantic wind, a roof of thatch weighted down under stones and ropes,
and livestock at the lower end of the same room whose body heat was part of the
arrangement. The peat fire burned in the middle of the floor with no chimney,
because the smoke seeping through the thatch kept the insects down and the
soot-laden thatch was stripped every spring and spread on the fields.

Roof pitch is a dialect of its own, and the covering sets the grammar. Thatch
and wooden shingle need a steep slope so water runs off before it can soak in;
overlapping clay tiles will sit lower; a flat roof is only honest in places
where heavy rain is rare and snow rarer still. You can read a rough band of
annual rainfall off the skyline of an old village without asking anyone.

The same reasoning operates above the scale of the single house. Settlements in
hot dry regions pack tight so that buildings shade one another and the lanes
between them stay narrow and dim through the afternoon; cold-climate villages
spread out to keep sun on every wall that faces it. Hillside villages terrace,
so one household's roof is the next household's yard. Where flooding is
seasonal, the whole settlement sits on the levee or on posts, and the fields
are allowed to drown.

## What You Can Carry

Before railways, the material a building was made of was decided almost
entirely by what lay within a day's cart journey. This is why traditional
building maps so precisely onto geology. Chalk and clay country produced cob
and thatch; flint country produced walls of knapped nodules set in thick lime
mortar; limestone belts produced quarried block and stone-slate roofs; forested
regions produced log and timber frame; the East African coast built in cut
coral rag because that is what the shoreline offered. Where there was neither
good stone nor timber, people built magnificently in earth — rammed between
shuttering, moulded into bricks and dried, or piled in courses and shaped by
hand, as in the ribbed conical houses of the Musgum in Cameroon. The Hakka of
Fujian took rammed earth further than almost anyone, building circular
communal houses several storeys high with walls thick enough at the base to
stop a bandit raid, housing an entire clan around a shared courtyard.

The economics of local material also enforce a discipline that modern building
has largely lost: nothing is wasted, everything is repairable with what is at
hand, and the building is expected to be maintained continuously rather than
built once and forgotten. Thatch gets patched. Lime render gets renewed. Earth
walls get a fresh coat each year. A vernacular building is less an object than
an ongoing activity, which is one reason so many of them vanish once the people
who knew the activity stop doing it.

## The Memory of Earthquakes

Some of the most impressive vernacular knowledge is encoded in ways that are
invisible until the ground moves. Across a long belt from the Balkans through
Anatolia and Iran into the Himalayas, traditional builders learned to lace
masonry with horizontal timbers, so that a wall is not a monolithic brittle
plate but a series of bands tied together by wood. In Kashmir, dhajji dewari
construction fills a braced timber frame with small panels of brick or stone in
mud mortar; when the frame racks in an earthquake the panels crack and dissipate
energy while the timber holds the geometry together. Similar systems appear in
northern Pakistan, in Ottoman timber-framed townhouses, and in the cane-and-mud
infill walls of Andean and Central American building. After major earthquakes
in these regions, surveyors have repeatedly found old timber-laced buildings
standing among collapsed modern masonry put up without reinforcement — not
because the old builders understood ductility as a concept, but because the
houses that survived the last big one were the ones people copied.

## Against Romanticizing It

It is easy to overcorrect here, and the literature often does. Many vernacular
houses were dark, smoky, damp, crowded, and full of things that bite. The
blackhouse with the open fire also gave its occupants chronic eye and lung
problems. Traditional earth walls need constant labour, usually unpaid and
usually women's. The houses were sized for households that no longer exist and
for economies that no longer function. When people in such places get access to
cement, corrugated steel sheet, and glass, they very often abandon the old
methods immediately and without regret, and outsiders lamenting this should be
clear about who is being asked to carry on living in a picturesque building.

The more useful reading is that vernacular building is not a museum category
but a process, and that the process is still running — it just runs now on
concrete block and steel sheet and whatever is cheap at the builders' merchant.
What deserves study is not the mud wall as such but the way the type was
allowed to learn: slowly, from feedback, with the builder living among the
results. Modern construction has almost none of that loop. A contractor who
gets a detail wrong is long gone before the damp appears, and the person who
specified it will never hear.

That is the transferable lesson, and it is being relearned in odd corners —
rammed earth appearing in building codes, passive cooling redrawn as engineering
diagrams, deep overhangs and cross-ventilation reappearing in buildings that
would rather not run air conditioning all summer. The badgirs of Yazd are
studied now with anemometers and computational models, and what the models keep
finding is that the shape is already close to right.
