# The Second Life of Buildings

Walk south from the Capitoline Hill in Rome and you pass a long curve of
travertine arcades, Doric columns below and Ionic above, unmistakably the
outside of a Roman theatre. Look up. There are apartments in it. Shutters,
window boxes, a bicycle on a balcony, somebody's washing. The Theatre of
Marcellus was dedicated under Augustus, stripped for building stone in the
Middle Ages, fortified by one baronial family, rebuilt as a palace for another
by Baldassare Peruzzi in the sixteenth century, and eventually subdivided into
flats that people still pay rent on. Nobody planned that sequence. It is simply
what happens to a large, well-built, conveniently located structure given
enough time.

Architects call this adaptive reuse, which makes it sound like a specialist
technique, a line of business alongside new construction. Historically it is
closer to the default condition of the built world. Buildings outlive the
purposes that produced them, almost always, and the question has rarely been
whether to reuse them but how much violence the next use is permitted to do.

## The Long Normal

Diocletian built a fortified retirement palace on the Dalmatian coast around the
turn of the fourth century. Three hundred years later, refugees from the sacked
city of Salona moved inside the walls because the walls were there, and they
stayed. Streets grew along the palace's axial corridors. Houses were built into
the colonnades and out of the emperor's mausoleum, which became the cathedral.
The result is the old town of Split, a functioning city whose street plan is the
floor plan of one man's house.

The pattern repeats wherever stone was expensive and labour was cheap. The great
mosque of Córdoba was raised on the site and partly from the spoils of a
Visigothic church, expanded four times, and then had a Renaissance cathedral
inserted bodily into the middle of its hypostyle hall after the reconquest —
an intervention so brutal that Charles V is said to have regretted permitting it,
though the story may be tidier than the truth. Hagia Sophia has been a cathedral,
a mosque, a museum, and a mosque again, each conversion leaving its plaster and
its fittings. Medieval European cities are full of houses whose ground floors
are Roman, whose middles are fourteenth century, and whose roofs date from after
the last fire.

What changed in the twentieth century was not the practice but the attitude.
Modernism's great projects imagined clearing the ground first. Congestion,
darkness, and old drains were to be swept away and replaced with light, air, and
rational plans, and for several decades the demolition of solid nineteenth-century
fabric was an unremarkable act of hygiene. The reaction, when it came, was
partly aesthetic and partly economic. Artists in Lower Manhattan started moving
illegally into cast-iron manufacturing lofts in the 1960s because the rents were
nothing and the spaces were enormous, twelve-foot ceilings and windows on two
sides. They painted "A.I.R." on the doors so firefighters would know the
buildings were occupied. Within twenty years the loft had become a real-estate
category and the same buildings were unaffordable. Rehabilitation had been
discovered as a source of value rather than a salvage operation.

The set pieces followed. A Paris railway terminus built for the 1900 exposition
and abandoned for mainline traffic because its platforms were too short became
the Musée d'Orsay, its barrel-vaulted train shed lit from above and filled with
nineteenth-century painting. Giles Gilbert Scott's Bankside Power Station,
directly across the river from St Paul's and derelict for years, became Tate
Modern, with the turbine hall left as a single enormous room that turned out to
be the most useful thing in London. Four vast brick gasholders in Vienna's
Simmering district were hollowed out and filled with apartments, a mall, a
student hall, and a concert venue, each drum handled by a different architect.
Fiat's Lingotto factory in Turin, famous for the banked test track on its roof,
became exhibition halls, a hotel, and a concert space, with the track kept. None
of these were cheap. All of them traded on something new construction cannot
manufacture: the sense of a building that already had a life.

## What the Old Building Will and Won't Do

Underneath the romance there is a fairly unglamorous survey. Every existing
structure arrives with a set of fixed properties, and the art of the thing is
matching a new programme to them rather than fighting them.

Structure is usually the good news. A New England textile mill with heavy timber
posts and plank floors was designed to carry looms and the vibration of a line
shaft; residential live loads are trivial by comparison. A grain silo or a power
station is over-engineered by any normal standard. The mismatch is more often
dimensional. Mills and warehouses are deep — sixty, eighty, a hundred feet from
window wall to window wall — and daylight does not reach far past fifteen feet
from a window with any useful intensity. That is why so many warehouse
conversions involve cutting an atrium or a light well down through the middle,
sacrificing floor area to make the rest of it lettable. A deep plan that worked
for machinery will produce apartments with a bedroom in permanent darkness.

Floor-to-floor height decides whether services can be hidden. New offices assume
a plenum for ducts and a raised floor for cables; a nineteenth-century building
with nine-foot storeys offers neither, so the ductwork gets exposed and painted,
which the market has now learned to read as character. Window openings are fixed,
and enlarging them in a load-bearing masonry wall means lintels, needling, and
an argument with whoever protects the facade. Floor plates rarely line up
between adjoining buildings, so combining two on a street produces a section
full of ramps and steps, which collides with accessibility requirements.

Then the genuinely hard constraints. Egress: old buildings very often have one
stair where current codes want two, or want a protected one. Fire compartmentation
in a timber structure with open floor penetrations is a real problem, and the
usual answers — sprinklers, encapsulation, a new concrete or steel core —
are expensive and visible. Lateral stability in seismic regions means unreinforced
masonry has to be tied to its floors and often braced, the single largest cost
in many West Coast conversions. Contamination is routine: asbestos in pipe
lagging and floor tile, lead paint, and in industrial buildings, whatever soaked
into the slab. Surveys are never complete, which is why reuse projects carry
larger contingencies than new build and why the first four weeks of demolition
determine whether the budget survives.

Regulators have caught up unevenly. Many jurisdictions now allow existing
buildings to be judged against a proportional standard — improve what you touch,
meet the intent rather than the letter — because applying new-build rules
wholesale to a hundred-year-old structure guarantees either demolition or
falsified paperwork. Where that flexibility exists, reuse gets built. Where it
doesn't, the bulldozer usually wins on paper before anyone has argued about
architecture.

## The Arithmetic of Not Demolishing

For most of the last century the environmental case for keeping a building was
made in terms of heating bills, and old buildings lost. Single glazing, no
insulation, cold bridges everywhere. Then the accounting changed. As operational
energy fell — better glazing, better plant, decarbonising grids — the carbon
embodied in materials and construction stopped being a rounding error and became
the larger half of a new building's lifetime emissions. Concrete and steel are
the expensive items: cement manufacture releases carbon dioxide from the
limestone itself, not just from the fuel, and primary steel is made by burning
carbon to strip oxygen from ore.

An existing structure has already paid that bill. Keeping frame, foundations,
and floors avoids the single biggest charge in a new project's carbon ledger, and
avoids the demolition waste as well. Carl Elefante's line that the greenest
building is the one that is already built has become the slogan of the argument,
and while it is too neat — a leaky building heated with oil for another forty
years is not green — the underlying comparison holds more often than intuition
suggests. A deep retrofit that strips a building back to its frame, insulates it
properly, and replaces its systems will usually beat demolition and replacement
on total emissions over any horizon short enough to matter.

## How Much to Show

The interesting arguments in reuse are not technical. They are about legibility:
how much of the old building the new work is allowed to conceal, imitate, or
contradict.

Carlo Scarpa's remodelling of the Castelvecchio in Verona is the reference point
for one answer. Scarpa stripped away nineteenth-century additions, left the
resulting scars visible, and inserted new elements — steel, concrete, brass,
carefully unmatched stone — that never pretend to be old and never touch the old
fabric casually. Every junction is a detail about the difference between the two.
David Chipperfield's reconstruction of the Neues Museum in Berlin took the
principle to a building that had spent sixty years as a war ruin: bullet-scarred
plaster and missing frescoes were stabilised and left, with new brick and
pre-cast concrete completing the volumes in plain, unornamented form. The effect
is neither restoration nor replacement. It is repair that admits to being repair.

The opposite temptation — to make the join invisible, to build a new wing in
matching stone and matching mouldings — is not wrong so much as it is a
different claim about time. It says the building has no history, only a style.
Most planning authorities, faced with a facade in a conservation area, quietly
prefer it. Most architects, given the choice, would rather do what Zumthor did in
Cologne, where the Kolumba museum rises in long pale brick directly out of the
ruins of a bombed Gothic church, a grey perforated screen wall letting daylight
fall over the excavated foundations below. Old and new are structurally
continuous and visually distinct, which is the harder trick and, when it works,
the one that makes the older building look more present rather than less.
