# Why Shoebox Concert Halls Sound Right

In the late 1890s a young Harvard physicist named Wallace Clement Sabine was
handed a problem nobody expected him to solve. The lecture room in the
university's new art museum was unusable: a speaker's words hung in the air
long enough to smear into the next sentence. Sabine spent nights hauling seat
cushions from a nearby theatre into the room, one batch at a time, and timing
with a stopwatch and an organ pipe how long a tone took to fade to inaudibility.
The relationship that emerged was simple and durable. Reverberation time rises
with the volume of the room and falls with the total absorption in it. Double
the cubic metres and the sound lingers twice as long; double the soft material
and it dies twice as fast.

When Boston's new symphony hall was being designed a few years later, Sabine was
consulted, and the building became the first auditorium in the world shaped by a
quantitative prediction rather than by copying something that had worked. What
he told the architects, in effect, was to copy something that had worked — the
old Leipzig Gewandhaus — but at a calculated size, and he could say in advance
what the result would sound like. It opened in 1900, and more than a century
later musicians still rank it among the two or three best rooms on earth.

## Time

Reverberation is the part of the puzzle everyone knows about, and it is the
easiest to get approximately right. A large symphonic repertoire wants a
reverberation time in the occupied hall of somewhere around two seconds — long
enough for the sound to bloom and for a chord to hang after the players stop,
short enough that a fast passage does not turn to porridge. Chamber music prefers
something shorter, opera shorter still, because in opera the words have to
survive. Speech wants under a second. A Gothic cathedral runs to five or six,
which is why plainchant moves slowly and why organ music written for such rooms
changes harmony at a pace the building can tolerate.

Because absorption is what sets the decay, and because a seated human being is a
large piece of absorptive material — clothing, hair, skin, lungs — the audience
is acoustically the most important variable object in the room. A hall tuned to
sound glorious when full will sound harsh and endless when half empty, which is
intolerable for rehearsals and bad for matinées. The standard solution is to
build the seats to absorb roughly as much as the person who sits in them:
upholstered backs, perforated undersides, fabric that lets sound into the
cushion. A good hall sounds nearly the same at half capacity as at full, and
that is not luck.

Reverberation time alone, though, turns out to be a poor predictor of whether
musicians like a room. Two halls can measure identically and sound entirely
different, because what the ear actually judges is the shape of the decay in its
first fraction of a second — how quickly the early reflections arrive after the
direct sound, from which directions, and how soon the tail becomes a smooth
statistical wash rather than a set of distinguishable echoes. The gap between
the direct sound and the first strong reflection is small in a narrow room and
large in a wide one, and listeners consistently prefer it small. Clarity and
reverberance pull against each other: early energy gives you definition and the
ability to hear counterpoint, late energy gives you warmth and envelopment, and
every hall is a position taken on that trade.

## Direction

The insight that reorganised the field in the 1960s, argued most forcefully by
the New Zealand acoustician Harold Marshall, is that where reflections come from
matters as much as when. Sound arriving from directly overhead reaches both ears
with nearly identical timing and content. Sound arriving from the side reaches
the two ears differently, and that difference — technically, a low correlation
between the signals at the left and right ears — is what the brain reads as
spatial impression: the sense that the orchestra is broad, and that you are
inside the sound rather than in front of it.

This is the whole argument for the shoebox. A narrow rectangular room with high
parallel side walls delivers strong, early, lateral reflections to every seat,
because the walls are close and the geometry is unavoidable. The Musikverein in
Vienna, the Concertgebouw in Amsterdam and Boston's Symphony Hall are all
essentially the same box, built within thirty years of each other by people
working from precedent and intuition, and all three are lateral-reflection
machines.

The shapes that fail tend to fail for the same reason in reverse. The wide
fan-shaped auditorium, popular in the mid-twentieth century because it put more
seats closer to the stage, sends the side walls away at an angle so that the
early reflections either arrive late and weak or miss the audience entirely.
Concave surfaces — domes, curved rear walls, barrel ceilings — focus sound into
hot spots and starve everywhere else; the Royal Albert Hall's famous echo, the
one that let you hear everything twice, came from its dome, and was finally
tamed by hanging a swarm of fibreglass diffusing discs beneath it. New York's
Philharmonic Hall at Lincoln Center opened in 1962 to complaints about thin bass
and weak strings, was modified repeatedly, and was eventually gutted to the
structure and rebuilt as a rectangle.

The one convincing alternative to the box is the vineyard, invented by Hans
Scharoun for the Berlin Philharmonie in the early 1960s with Lothar Cremer
advising on the acoustics. The audience surrounds the orchestra in terraced
blocks at varying heights, and the retaining walls of those terraces become the
local side walls for the seats they hold. Each block gets its own lateral
reflections from the vineyard wall in front of it. It is much harder to design
than a shoebox — the geometry has to be checked block by block, and the seats
behind the orchestra hear an orchestra playing away from them — but it puts far
more of the audience close to the players, and the best modern examples, several
of them shaped by the Japanese acoustician Yasuhisa Toyota, work very well.

Surface texture matters too, and it is the reason nineteenth-century ornament
has an acoustic afterlife. Coffered ceilings, statues in niches, heavy cornices,
the carved fronts of shallow balconies: all of them scatter sound rather than
reflecting it as a mirror would, spreading energy in time and direction and
preventing the flutter that two bare parallel walls produce. Modern halls have
to manufacture diffusion deliberately, with faceted panels, shaped reliefs or
irregular plaster, because plain flat gypsum is acoustically crude.

There are also stubborn seat-specific effects. Sound travelling at a grazing
angle across rows of seats toward the stalls loses a chunk of its energy in the
low-mid range — the seat-dip effect — which is one reason the raked or terraced
floor, lifting each row above the one in front, does more than improve
sightlines. Balcony overhangs that are too deep leave the seats beneath them
without access to the reverberant volume of the room at all, so the sound goes
dry and small under the shelf. The rule of thumb is to keep the overhang
shallower than the opening height beneath it.

## The Players' Room

A concert hall has two audiences, and the smaller one is on stage. Musicians
need to hear themselves, hear each other, and feel that the room responds. An
ensemble that cannot hear across the platform will drift apart in ensemble and
push in dynamics, and players describe an unresponsive stage as exhausting to
play in — they work harder for less sound.

So the platform is treated as a room of its own. Side walls and a rear enclosure
keep energy from escaping into the flies. A canopy or an array of reflectors
above the stage returns early reflections to the players quickly, and its height
is a real design decision: too low and the orchestra hears a hard slap and the
hall loses the upper volume; too high and the return arrives too late to help.
Risers separate the sections vertically so brass and percussion are not firing
directly into the backs of the violas' heads, which is both an ensemble question
and an occupational health one. The floor is usually timber over a void, and
players insist they feel the bass through it, which acousticians are politely
unwilling to either confirm or deny.

## Tuning a Room After It Exists

Predictions are good now — scale models with ultrasonic sources, ray-tracing and
wave-based simulation, auralisation that lets a client listen to the unbuilt hall
through headphones — but rooms still surprise. Air itself absorbs high
frequencies, and how much depends on humidity, so a hall can sound different in
January and July. Every soft surface added afterward, including a heavy stage
curtain someone leaves closed, moves the numbers.

Which is why the better modern halls are built adjustable. Retractable banners
or roller absorbers are stored in slots above the ceiling or behind grilles and
deployed to shorten reverberation for amplified events. More ambitiously, some
halls have reverberation chambers: large empty volumes wrapped around the main
room behind heavy concrete doors, which open to couple extra cubic metres to the
hall and lengthen the decay without changing anything the audience can see. The
principle goes back to Sabine's formula, which the whole field has been arguing
with, refining and failing to replace for well over a century. Volume over
absorption. Everything else is where you put it.
