# How Japanese Timber Buildings Hold Together

A carpenter draws a plane down a length of cypress and lifts off a shaving long
enough to reach across the workshop and thin enough to read newsprint through.
There is no sandpaper anywhere in the room. The surface left behind is not
abraded but sheared, the wood fibres cut clean rather than torn, and it has a
faint sheen that sanded timber never achieves and that sheds water better
because the cut cells are closed rather than fuzzed open. Planing contests are
held in Japan where the shavings are measured in microns and passed around like
silk. This is not showmanship for its own sake. A surface finished that way
will still be sound in two hundred years.

Everything else about Japanese timber building follows from a similar refusal to
treat wood as a generic commodity — and from the fact that the country is wet,
seismic, forested, and was for centuries short of iron.

## Buying the Mountain

The first decision is made in the forest. Traditional temple carpenters, the
miyadaiku, were taught to select standing trees rather than order sawn stock,
because a tree's history is written into it and that history determines where
the timber can go. A pine that grew on an exposed slope leaning into the
prevailing wind has compressed fibres on one side and tension on the other; put
it in a building and it will keep trying to move the way it always moved. So
the rule is to set it where it grew: the north face of the trunk toward the
north side of the building, a twisting timber paired with one that twists the
other way so the two lock each other still. Nishioka Tsunekazu, who led the
great postwar dismantling and repair of Hōryū-ji and the rebuilding of
Yakushi-ji's pagoda, taught this as a matter of reading a wood's habit rather
than fighting it.

The preferred species is hinoki, Japanese cypress — straight-grained, fragrant,
dimensionally stable, and naturally resistant to rot and insects. Sugi, the
Japanese cedar, supplies lighter work and cladding, sometimes charred black on
its face to give a weather-resistant skin. Zelkova goes where a hard, figured
timber is wanted. Temple carpenters hold that hinoki continues to stiffen for
a couple of centuries after felling before it begins its very slow decline,
which is the kind of claim that sounds like folklore until you consider that
the oldest wooden buildings on earth are made of it and are still standing at
Hōryū-ji, roughly thirteen centuries on.

## A Frame That Is Not Fastened Down

A traditional building is a cage of posts and beams. The walls carry nothing:
they are earth plaster on a bamboo lattice, or sliding panels, or nothing at
all. The roof is enormously heavy, and its weight runs down through the posts
to a foundation of individual stones sitting on compacted ground.

The posts are not bolted to those stones. They simply stand on them. Where the
stone has an irregular top, the carpenter scribes its profile onto the column
base and carves the end grain to match, so that the post sits in full contact
without any fixing at all. During an earthquake the whole frame can shift and
rock on its stones, and the friction of that sliding bleeds off energy that a
rigidly anchored building would have to absorb by breaking something.

Inside the frame the horizontal members do the rest. Penetrating ties called
nuki pass straight through mortises cut in the posts and are locked with
hardwood wedges driven from the side — a technique that arrived with the
Chinese-influenced style used to rebuild Tōdai-ji in the late twelfth century
and spread through Japanese carpentry thereafter. A frame tied this way is not
stiff. It is deliberately ductile: when the ground shakes, the joints rotate
slightly, the wedges crush the wood fibres around them, and the energy goes
into that localized crushing instead of into a catastrophic failure. The
building sways, complains, and comes back.

The five-storey pagoda is the extreme case. Its stories are stacked rather than
continuously framed, each one resting on the one below with a central pillar,
the shinbashira, running up the middle — in many cases resting on or even
suspended above the foundation rather than carrying load. The usual explanation
for the pagodas' remarkable seismic record is that the loosely stacked stories
move out of phase with one another, each damping its neighbours, while the
central pillar limits how far the whole thing can lean. Engineers still argue
over the details, and the idea has been borrowed for modern towers.

## Cutting the Joints

With almost no metal fasteners, the joints have to do everything, and Japanese
carpentry developed hundreds of them. They divide into two families: tsugite,
which splice two timbers end to end to make a longer one, and shiguchi, which
connect members meeting at an angle.

A good splice is an exercise in getting wood to resist tension, which is the
thing wood joints are worst at. The kanawa-tsugi, one of the standard temple
splices, interlocks two hooked halves and then locks them with a hardwood key
driven into a slot through the middle, so that pulling on either end only
tightens the assembly. Other joints use gooseneck or dovetail profiles that
resist withdrawal by geometry, stub tenons pinned with bamboo or oak pegs, and
tapered wedges that are driven after assembly to take up any slack. Almost all
of them are designed so that the finished joint hides its own workings; from
the outside you see two timbers meeting in a hairline and nothing else.

The consequence is that the order of assembly is fixed. A traditional frame
goes together in one sequence and only one, each member trapping the last, and
if a piece is cut wrong it cannot be quietly adjusted on site — the error
propagates through every joint that depends on it. That is why the layout stage
carries such weight. The carpenter works with a sumitsubo, an inked line reel
that snaps a perfectly straight mark along a rough log, and a sashigane, a steel
square whose back face carries a second scale graduated by the square root of
two. That scale turns diagonal problems into direct readings: the largest square
beam obtainable from a round log, the true length of a hip rafter, the cuts
needed where a valley meets a ridge.

Proportion is systematized too. The kiwari rules, written down in carpenters'
manuals from the medieval period onward and closely held within lineages,
derive every dimension in a building from the width of its columns — bracket
depth, beam spacing, rafter pitch, eave projection. The bay, or ken, became a
planning module, and the floor mat sized to fit it fixed room dimensions so
thoroughly that Japanese rooms are still counted in mats, even though the exact
size of the mat differed between the Kansai and Kantō traditions and still
does.

## The Roof Does the Arguing

Look at a temple from outside and what registers is the roof: its bulk, the
depth of its overhang, and the slow upward curve at the eaves. Deep eaves are a
practical necessity in a climate of heavy summer rain, keeping water off earth
walls and unpainted timber and keeping the high summer sun off the veranda
while letting low winter sun underneath.

Cantilevering a roof that far out is hard. The Chinese-derived answer, visible
on any large temple, is the bracket complex: stacked blocks and arms stepping
outward from the column head in two or three tiers, each one carrying the load
a little further from the post. These assemblies are structural and also the
main decorative display of the building, and their configuration is one of the
clearest ways to date a hall.

Japanese carpenters then added a trick of their own. From the Heian period
onward they separated the roof you see from the roof that works. Above the
visible rafters — which stay slender, evenly spaced, and set at the elegant
shallow pitch the eye wants — sits a concealed structure of steeper rafters and
heavy levers called hanegi, hidden in the roof cavity, which pivot over an
internal support and use the weight of the ridge to hold the eaves up. The
visible geometry became a matter of taste rather than statics. The gentle
concave sweep of a temple eave, which looks like an aesthetic indulgence, is
produced by a hidden cantilever system doing arithmetic.

## Continuity by Replacement

The survival of these buildings depends on an assumption almost no Western
building tradition shares: that the structure will be taken apart. Major temple
halls undergo kaitai shūri, a complete dismantling repair, every century or so.
Every member is numbered and removed, decayed timbers are scarfed with new wood
or replaced outright, the roof is relaid, and the building goes back together.
Because it was assembled with joints rather than glue or nails, it can be.

Ise takes the principle to its limit. The shrine is rebuilt entirely every
twenty years on an alternate plot beside the current one, in unpainted hinoki
with thatched roofs, to a design that has been transmitted by repetition since
the seventh century. The point is not the building but the transmission: each
cycle trains a generation of carpenters who will lead the next, and the
knowledge is stored in hands rather than drawings. Philosophers enjoy asking
whether the shrine is old or new. The carpenters do not appear to find the
question interesting.

What is genuinely at risk is the supply chain and the craft. Timbers of the
size the great halls need take several hundred years to grow, and the forests
that can supply them were reserved and replanted centuries ago by people who
knew they would never see the trees cut. Postwar building regulations, written
around braced and bolted construction, long made permitting an unbraced
traditional frame difficult. The apprenticeships are long and the work is
scarce. The joints survive because a few hundred people still cut them, and
because every so often another roof has to come off.
