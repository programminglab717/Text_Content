# The Long Argument About Brutalism

Stand close to the outside of the National Theatre in London and you can read
the grain of the pine boards that held the wet concrete in place half a century
ago. The knots are there, the butt joints between planks, the faint ridges
where cement paste crept into the gaps. Somebody chose that timber, ordered it
sawn in a particular width, and arranged the boards so the pattern would run
horizontally across the terraces like bedding planes in a cliff. Nothing about
it is accidental, and nothing about it is finish in the usual sense. The
building is showing you how it was made.

That is the part of Brutalism most of its critics never engaged with, and it is
the reason the style has proved so hard to argue about honestly. For thirty
years the word functioned in British and American public discourse as a synonym
for ugly, cheap, and brutal — the false etymology did enormous damage — while
the buildings themselves were mostly expensive, extremely deliberate, and built
for institutions that believed they were doing something generous.

## What the Word Was Supposed to Mean

It comes from béton brut, raw concrete, the term Le Corbusier used for the
shuttered surfaces of the Unité d'Habitation in Marseille. By the mid-1950s the
phrase New Brutalism was circulating among young British architects, and Reyner
Banham gave it a definition that had almost nothing to do with concrete. He
proposed three tests: the building should be memorable as an image, it should
exhibit its structure plainly, and it should value its materials as found —
meaning the brick looks like brick, the steel like steel, the services like
pipes, with nothing plastered over to make the thing look easier than it was.

Banham's own founding example, Alison and Peter Smithson's school at
Hunstanton, is steel and brick with the plumbing on display and barely any
concrete in sight. The movement's connection with poured concrete came later
and for practical reasons: concrete was the cheapest way to make large
complicated shapes in postwar Europe, it required no imported steel during
years when steel was rationed, and it could be worked by relatively unskilled
labour. It also happened to photograph magnificently.

The Marseille block itself repays description, because so much of it was copied
and so little of what mattered. Le Corbusier lifted more than three hundred
apartments onto massive tapered legs, threaded them together with internal
corridors he called streets, and gave the roof over to a running track, a
nursery, a gymnasium, and a row of ventilation stacks shaped like ship funnels.
Each flat runs through the full depth of the slab, so every household takes
light from both sides, and deep concrete fins shade the glass from high summer
sun. The dimensions came from his Modulor, a proportional scale derived from a
notional human figure with one arm raised. What housing authorities elsewhere
took from all this was the slab, the legs, and the concrete. The double-height
flats, the shops halfway up, the roof, and the budget did not generally survive
the translation.

The ethical claim underneath was that a building should not lie about itself.
This is a moral position dressed as an aesthetic one, and it was aimed squarely
at the soft, tasteful, gently modern architecture that had become respectable —
buildings with thin stone facings and polite proportions that concealed a
structural frame nobody was supposed to think about. The Smithsons' generation
found that dishonest and a bit cowardly. Whether a wall made of board-marked
concrete is more truthful than a wall made of limestone veneer is a question
that has never been settled, but the seriousness of the intent is not in doubt.

## The Public Sector's House Style

What turned an argument among architects into a global idiom was who was
building in those decades. From the late 1950s through the mid-1970s, the
institutions putting up large buildings across much of the world were public:
universities expanding to take in a generation that had never gone before, city
governments replacing bombed or slum-cleared districts, national broadcasters,
libraries, hospitals, arts centres, transit authorities. These clients had
budgets, ambition, and a working assumption that a civic building should look
unmistakably civic.

The results were frequently extraordinary. The Barbican in London, whose
concrete was hand-tooled with picks after curing to break the surface open and
expose the aggregate — a labour-intensive process that makes the estate feel
carved rather than poured. Ernő Goldfinger's towers at Trellick and Balfron,
with their lift and service shafts pulled out into separate slabs linked by
bridges, an arrangement that keeps the noisy machinery away from the flats.
Boston's City Hall, an upside-down ziggurat that puts the mayor's office in a
projecting box you can identify from the plaza. Paul Rudolph's art and
architecture building at Yale, with concrete corrugations bush-hammered by hand
into something with the texture of split rock. In Brazil the language went
lighter and more tropical in the hands of Lina Bo Bardi and the Paulista
school; in Japan Kenzo Tange used it to reinterpret temple carpentry at urban
scale; in Yugoslavia it produced a whole national architecture of concrete
civic optimism that most of Western Europe only noticed decades later.

Housing is where the record is most mixed, and most misrepresented. Park Hill
in Sheffield and Robin Hood Gardens in east London were both built on the
"streets in the sky" idea — wide access decks meant to reproduce the sociability
of terraced streets at height. The intention was not utopian abstraction; the
Smithsons in particular had studied how working-class streets actually
functioned and were trying to keep what they found. The decks did not work as
hoped, for reasons that had as much to do with who was housed there and what
maintenance budgets survived the next decade as with the width of a walkway.

## How It Lost

Concrete weathers badly in wet northern climates, and the architects of the
period did not solve the problem. Rainwater running off a horizontal surface
without a proper drip detail streaks the wall below in dark tears. Algae take
hold on north faces. Worse, over time carbon dioxide penetrates the concrete
and neutralizes the alkalinity that protects the steel reinforcement inside;
once the rebar starts rusting it expands, and the expansion cracks off the
cover concrete in sheets. A building designed to look raw and permanent begins
to look like it is decomposing, and that impression is not entirely unfair,
because it is.

The energy performance was worse. Single glazing, uninsulated concrete walls
acting as enormous thermal bridges, and heating systems designed when fuel was
cheap: after the oil shocks these buildings became expensive to run at exactly
the moment public budgets contracted. Maintenance was deferred, then deferred
again, and a style whose whole premise was that surfaces should be left
untreated turned out to need constant attention.

Then the political argument arrived. Oscar Newman's work on defensible space,
and later Alice Coleman's in Britain, blamed the physical design of high-rise
estates — the anonymous walkways, the unclaimed ground, the lack of eyes on
communal space — for crime and neglect. The thesis was overstated and the
statistics contested, but it gave politicians who wanted to stop funding public
housing a technical reason to do so. By the 1980s the concrete estate had
become shorthand for everything the postwar settlement was supposed to have got
wrong, and the fastest way for a council to look decisive was to knock one
down.

## The Second Life

The revival began, as these things do, with photographs. Around 2010 a certain
kind of image started circulating: a concrete stair tower shot against grey
sky, high contrast, no people. Books of them sold. The hashtag campaigns
followed, and with them a genuine and effective preservation movement —
Preston's bus station, a vast curved concrete gallery of a building that a
council wanted gone, was listed and then restored after years of public
campaigning.

It is worth being sceptical about parts of this. The photographic enthusiasm is
almost entirely formal: it likes the shapes and the shadows, and it is largely
indifferent to the social programme that produced them, which is precisely the
inversion Banham warned about when he asked whether Brutalism was an ethic or
an aesthetic. A movement founded on materials as found has been recuperated as
a mood. Meanwhile a number of the best buildings were lost during the years
when admiring them was eccentric: Birmingham's central library, an inverted
concrete pyramid over a public square, came down while the revival was already
underway. Robin Hood Gardens was demolished despite repeated campaigns, and a
three-storey slice of it was carted off to a museum, which is a peculiar sort
of compromise.

The strongest case for keeping the survivors is not nostalgia and not
photogenic massing. It is that the carbon has already been spent. Cement
manufacture is responsible for a substantial share of global emissions, and a
1960s concrete frame represents an enormous quantity of it, paid for sixty
years ago. Demolishing such a building releases nothing and wastes everything;
replacing it means paying the carbon cost again for a structure that will not
obviously last as long. Park Hill has been stripped back to its frame and
refilled with new flats and coloured panels, and whatever one thinks of the
result, the skeleton it kept was the expensive part.

These buildings were never as loved as their architects assumed they would be,
and pretending otherwise does the argument no favours. But they were blamed for
a great deal they did not cause — for housing policy, for maintenance budgets,
for the way institutions treated the people they put inside them. A concrete
wall does not decide who gets housed on the fourteenth floor. It just stands
there afterwards, streaked, taking the blame, with the grain of somebody's pine
boards still legible on its surface.
