# How to Read a Housing Plan

Put two apartment plans side by side with the same floor area — say eight
hundred square feet, two bedrooms — and one of them will feel like a home and
the other like an airport hotel. The square footage is identical. The rent may
be identical. The difference lives in things the listing never mentions: which
direction the windows face, how deep the plan is from the window wall to the
back, how much of that eight hundred feet is hallway, whether the front door
opens straight into the sofa, and where the washing machine goes.

Housing design is a small discipline with enormous consequences, and it is
unusually legible. Once you know what to look at, a floor plan tells you most of
what it will be like to live in the place. Here is roughly the order in which
the decisions get made, and what each one costs.

## Where the Space Actually Goes

Developers talk about efficiency, meaning the ratio of saleable or rentable
floor area to the total area of the building. Everything that isn't inside
somebody's front door — stairs, lift shafts, corridors, lobbies, refuse rooms,
plant, risers, structure — is overhead, and it has to be paid for by the
apartments. A slab block with long double-loaded corridors might get eighty-five
per cent of its gross area into units. A tower with two stairs, two lifts, and a
generous lobby on every floor might get seventy. That fifteen-point difference
is not an abstraction; it is the reason so much new housing is arranged along
interior corridors with doors on both sides, which is also the reason so much
new housing has apartments facing in only one direction.

The second great constraint is the structural grid, and in any building with
parking underneath it, the grid is set by cars. A standard parking bay is about
two and a half metres wide, so columns land at roughly eight metres to let three
cars fit between them. Those columns then march upward through the housing
whether or not eight metres is a sensible dimension for a bedroom. Podium
buildings all over the world have their room sizes quietly dictated by the turning
circle of a mid-size sedan. Where the parking is omitted, plans loosen up
immediately, and it shows.

## The Access Diagram

How you get to your own front door determines more about a building than any
other single decision.

The double-loaded corridor is the cheapest arrangement per unit and the worst
for daylight. Apartments occupy both sides of an internal hall; each one has
windows on one wall only; the corridor itself has no daylight at either end
unless the architect fights for it. Single-aspect units can be made to work if
they are shallow and face a decent direction, but a single-aspect apartment
facing north in a cold climate, or facing west in a hot one, is a permanent
problem that no amount of specification fixes. Several planning authorities have
responded by restricting north-facing single-aspect units outright, which
developers dislike and residents never complain about.

Gallery access — the deck or balcony running along the outside, familiar from
inter-war municipal housing in Britain and the Netherlands — gets you daylight in
the circulation and a cross-ventilated flat, at the price of a window that looks
onto a walkway. It works when the gallery is wide enough to be occupied rather
than merely traversed, and when the rooms facing it are the ones you do not mind
being seen in: kitchen, entry, a study.

The point-access block is the arrangement most of continental Europe prefers. A
single stair and lift core serves two to four apartments per floor, each of
which then has windows on at least two sides, often three. There is no corridor
at all. Circulation overhead is low even though the core is generous, because
the core is short. You meet your neighbours. Until recently, most North American
codes made this illegal above about three storeys by requiring two separated
exit stairs from every floor, which forces the plan to stretch into a bar with a
corridor connecting the stairs. The rules are now being revisited — several
cities and states have moved to permit single-stair buildings up to five or six
storeys with sprinklers, robust construction and limits on units per floor,
following the practice of countries where this has been ordinary for a century.
The interest in the reform is not fire policy for its own sake. It is that a
building with one stair can have cross-ventilated corner apartments on a small
lot, and that is the difference between a decent flat and a corridor cell.

## Depth, Light and the Window Head

Daylight does not travel far. As a working rule, useful daylight reaches into a
room roughly two to two and a half times the height of the top of the window
above the floor. A window head at two metres gives you four or five metres of
properly lit room and then a gloomy tail. This is why the height of the window
head matters more than the area of the glass, and why the fashionable move of
dropping a sill to the floor while keeping the head low produces a room that is
glary at the front and dark at the back. Tall windows with high heads and deep
reveals beat large low ones almost every time.

Plan depth follows directly. A single-aspect apartment much deeper than about
nine metres will have a dead zone; developers solve this by putting the kitchen
in it, which is how the open-plan kitchen-living room with the kitchen at the
dark end became standard. A dual-aspect apartment can be deeper because the
light comes from both ends, and it can be ventilated by opening two windows and
letting air move through, which matters enormously in a warming climate where
mechanical cooling is the fallback. The physics of a cross-ventilated flat on a
summer night is worth more than any amount of insulation.

Then the balcony. Outdoor space attached to a dwelling is one of the few
amenities people reliably use and reliably pay for, and the pandemic made the
point unarguable. But a balcony cantilevered off a concrete slab is a fin of
conductive material sticking out through the insulation line — a thermal bridge
that in a cold climate can undo a meaningful share of the wall's performance,
and that will condense and mould at the junction if detailed carelessly. The
fixes are structural thermal breaks, separate self-supporting balcony frames, or
insetting the balcony into the plan, which costs you floor area. Any of them is
better than ignoring it.

## Rooms, Doors and Furniture

The most consequential change in domestic plans over the last century was not
the disappearance of the parlour but the arrival of the corridor, and it arrived
much earlier than people assume. Robin Evans made the argument memorably: a
sixteenth-century house was a matrix of rooms opening into one another, so that
moving through the house meant passing through other people's occupations. The
corridor plan separates circulation from occupation, and buys privacy at the cost
of connection. Every plan since has been negotiating the trade. A modern
apartment with a tight entry hall and doors to everything is very private and
faintly institutional; a loft with no internal doors is sociable and impossible
to share with anyone who keeps different hours.

The other thing worth studying is whether the rooms actually hold furniture. This
sounds trivial and is the single most common failure in built housing. A bedroom
needs a bed with access on the sides people need, a wardrobe with door swing, and
a window that isn't blocked by either. A living space needs a wall long enough
for a sofa that isn't interrupted by a door swing, a radiator, and a route to the
balcony. Margarete Schütte-Lihotzky's Frankfurt kitchen of the mid-1920s, designed
for Ernst May's social housing programme, was an early and rigorous attempt to
work from the body outward — timing the movements of cooking, then sizing the
room to them. It was tiny, and it worked, because every dimension had a reason.
Plenty of larger kitchens since have not, because they were drawn as a shape and
fitted out afterwards.

Storage is the unfashionable item that determines whether people stay. Coats,
bicycles, prams, suitcases, vacuum cleaners, sports equipment, the boxes nobody
opens. Housing standards that specify a minimum built-in storage volume are
routinely value-engineered out, and the missing cubic metres then colonise the
hallway and the second bedroom. A family leaves an apartment because the child
has nowhere to put anything, and records the reason as needing more space.

Finally, sound. Neighbour noise is the most frequent serious complaint in
multi-family housing, and almost all of it is avoidable. Airborne sound through
a party wall is handled by mass and by separation — two independent leaves with
a cavity beat one heavy wall of the same weight. Impact sound, footfall through
a floor, is harder, and is the reason timber and light-gauge steel structures
need floating floor systems or resilient channels while a thick concrete slab
with a good topping mostly solves itself. What defeats both is flanking:
structure that carries vibration around the separating element, a continuous
joist, a rigid connection at a junction, a service penetration nobody sealed.
Acoustic performance is destroyed on site, not on the drawing, which means the
detail is only as good as the person installing it at four o'clock on a Friday.
