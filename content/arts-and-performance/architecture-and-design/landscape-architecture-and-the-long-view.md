# Landscape Architecture and the Long View

The cleverest thing in Central Park is something you are not meant to notice.
Four roads cross the park from east to west, carrying crosstown traffic that in
1858 meant carts and coal wagons and now means buses and delivery vans. None of
them interrupts a single path. They run in sunken cuts below the level of the
park, bridged over by the walks and drives, so that a person crossing the Ramble
never meets a vehicle and never quite works out why. That idea was in the
competition entry from the start, and it is the reason the park still works a
hundred and seventy years later while the traffic around it has changed beyond
recognition.

Frederick Law Olmsted was not trained for this. He had been a farmer on Staten
Island, a merchant seaman, and a journalist who travelled the American South
writing unsparing dispatches about the economics of slavery. He got the
superintendent's job at the unbuilt park partly through connections, then won
the design competition with the English-born architect Calvert Vaux, and spent
the next decades inventing a profession as he went — including its name. The
title landscape architect was not in use as a job description before Olmsted and
Vaux insisted on it, and the insistence was deliberate: what they did was not
gardening, and not engineering, though it required both.

## What the Ground Was Actually Like

The most persistent misunderstanding about Central Park is that it preserves a
piece of Manhattan. It preserves nothing. The site was swamp, rock outcrop,
squatters' settlements, a reservoir, bone-boiling works, and the established
village of Seneca Land whose Black and Irish residents were evicted to make room.
The ground that reads today as pastoral — the Sheep Meadow's easy sweep, the
rocky wildness of the Ramble, the lake with its hidden ends — was made. Millions
of cubic yards of material were moved. Drainage tile was laid under the meadows
so that soil which naturally held water would grow grass. Gunpowder went into the
schist. Topsoil was imported by the barge-load, and hundreds of thousands of
plants went in. It is one of the largest earthworks of its century, disguised as
the countryside.

That disguise is the discipline's founding trick and its recurring political
problem. A building announces itself as designed. A landscape that succeeds
usually looks like it was always there, which makes it very hard to fund, very
easy to neglect, and almost impossible to defend when someone wants to put a
parking lot on it.

Olmsted's later work in Boston made the engineering explicit for once. The Back
Bay Fens, the first link in what became the Emerald Necklace, was not conceived
as scenery. Stony Brook and Muddy River were drowning the neighbourhood in
floodwater mixed with sewage, and the design rebuilt the tidal creek as a
brackish basin planted to tolerate salt and inundation, holding storm flows and
releasing them slowly. It looked like a marsh because it was one, doing a
marsh's job. A century before anyone said green infrastructure, the model was
already built and photographed.

## The Medium Is Dirt and Water

Ask a landscape architect what the real material is and you will not hear about
plants. You will hear about grading.

Every outdoor surface has to shed water somewhere, and where it goes is designed
in centimetres. A paved plaza needs a cross slope of around two per cent — enough
to drain, little enough to feel level, and just gentle enough to stay within
accessibility limits. A path that rises more than about one in twenty stops
counting as a walk and becomes a ramp, with everything that implies: handrails,
landings, a much longer alignment. So the drawings that matter are not the
picturesque views but the grading plans, dense with spot elevations at every
corner and contours redrawn by hand until the water runs where it should, the
slopes stay walkable, and the existing trees keep their original soil level.
That last constraint bites: raise the grade half a metre over a mature oak's
roots and you have killed it, slowly, over about five years, and nobody will
connect the two events.

Below the surface the problems get worse, because the requirements of pavement
and the requirements of roots are opposites. Paving needs a subgrade compacted
until it is nearly impermeable, and roots need air, water and room. A street tree
planted in a small pit surrounded by compacted structural fill is growing in a
sealed container the size of a bathtub; it will be stunted by year five and dead
or dying by year twelve, and the city will replace it with another one in the
same hole. A tree meant to reach a real canopy needs soil measured in tens of
cubic metres, connected under the pavement — by structural soils that can bear
load through a stone skeleton while roots occupy the gaps, by suspended paving
on cells or piers, or simply by linking pits into continuous trenches. It is
invisible, unphotogenic, and the difference between a boulevard and a row of
dying sticks.

## Time as a Material

Architects hand over finished buildings. Landscape architects hand over the
opening position of a process that will run for fifty years without them.

Nothing else in design has this property. A wall does not get taller. A planting
does — and thickens, and shades out the things beneath it, and drops seed, and
loses individuals to drought and disease, and is grazed and trampled and mown.
The design is therefore not a fixed state but a trajectory, and every plan
carries an implicit theory of how the site will change. Draw a woodland edge as
a static line on paper and it will not stay there; woody plants march outward
into any grassland nobody manages, and within two decades your meadow is scrub
unless something eats it, burns it or cuts it.

Which makes maintenance a design decision rather than an afterthought, and the
one most often lost in the handover. The difference between a wildflower meadow
and a neglected verge is not the seed mix. It is the cut: once a year, late, with
the arisings removed so nutrients decline and the aggressive grasses lose their
advantage. Mow the same ground monthly and you get lawn. Mow it never and you get
bramble. The same plant palette produces three entirely different landscapes
depending on a maintenance schedule that is usually decided by whoever holds the
grounds contract, years after the designer has gone.

The naturalistic planting that has dominated the last few decades is essentially
an argument about this. Designers working in that tradition — Piet Oudolf's
perennial plantings being the best known — select for structure that persists
after flowering, so that seed heads and stems carry the scheme through winter,
and for combinations that behave as a plant community rather than as blocks of
individuals waiting to be deadheaded. It looks casual and is anything but; the
drawings specify plants per square metre and expect a particular succession to
happen over the first several seasons, with some species retreating and others
filling in. Gilles Clément made the point more radically with what he called the
garden in movement, where the gardener's job is to steer plants that are already
moving themselves, editing rather than composing.

## Afterlives

The most interesting sites of the last forty years have been the ruined ones.

When the Ruhr's steel industry collapsed, the region was left with vast
contaminated works that were too expensive to demolish and too toxic to build
on. At Duisburg-Nord, Peter Latz's team did neither. The blast furnaces were left
standing and made climbable. Ore bunkers became enclosed gardens and rock-climbing
walls. A gasholder was flooded for scuba training. Contaminated ground was capped
rather than excavated, and plants were allowed to colonise the slag — including
the odd tramp species that had arrived decades earlier in ore shipments from
elsewhere. The polluted watercourse was separated from clean rainwater, which is
collected and cleaned on site through the old cooling channels. It is a park, a
monument, and a remediation project that is still running.

The same logic scaled up at Fresh Kills on Staten Island, once the largest
landfill in the world and now slowly becoming a park several times the size of
Central Park, its mounds capped and vented and seeded, methane collected, the
transformation staged across decades because nothing about settling garbage can
be hurried. And it scales sideways into the flood-management work now going on in
Chinese cities under the sponge city label — wetlands, terraced riverbanks and
permeable ground designed to absorb monsoon rain rather than rush it into
concrete channels, which is the Back Bay Fens argument made again at the scale of
a river basin.

There is an obvious reason this work has moved from the margin to the centre.
Cities are getting hotter and wetter in bursts, and the instruments that address
that are not buildings. Shade from a mature canopy can drop the felt temperature
on a street by several degrees, mostly through evapotranspiration, which is a
cooling mechanism no paint or canopy structure reproduces. Ground that absorbs
rain reduces the surge that overwhelms combined sewers. Both are cheap compared
with the engineering alternatives, and both take twenty years to work properly,
which is precisely why they are so hard to get funded and so easy to cut.
