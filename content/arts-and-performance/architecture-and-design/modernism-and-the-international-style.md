# Modernism and the International Style

In the summer of 1927 a hillside above Stuttgart filled with visitors who had
come to look at houses. Twenty-one buildings had gone up on the site, designed
by architects from five countries under the direction of Mies van der Rohe, and
what the crowds found were flat roofs, white walls, windows in long horizontal
bands, and interiors with almost nothing on them. A local postcard publisher
issued an image of the estate retouched to look like an Arab village, camels
included, which tells you how the buildings landed. Within a few years the same
formal language would be visible in Rotterdam, Tel Aviv, Moscow, Rio de
Janeiro, and Los Angeles — and it would have a name that none of the architects
had chosen.

## The Frame Comes First

Nothing about the look was possible before the structure. For most of building
history a wall did two jobs at once: it held the floor up and it kept the
weather out. Steel framing, worked out in Chicago's tall office buildings in
the 1880s and 1890s, and reinforced concrete, industrialized across Europe
around the turn of the century, separated those jobs permanently. Once a grid
of columns carried the load, the exterior wall became a skin that could be
glass, thin render, metal panel, or nothing at all.

Le Corbusier drew the consequence in 1914 as a diagram he called Dom-Ino: six
slender concrete posts, three flat slabs, a stair, and no walls whatsoever. It
is an unremarkable piece of engineering and an extraordinarily aggressive
picture, because every design decision it leaves open had previously been
closed. A decade later he turned it into the Five Points — lift the building
off the ground on pilotis, free the plan, free the facade, run the window
horizontally across the whole front, put a garden on the roof — and built the
Villa Savoye outside Paris as the demonstration piece.

The polemics ran ahead of the buildings. Adolf Loos had argued before the First
World War that ornament on a modern object was a kind of waste, moral and
economic both. Le Corbusier's Vers une architecture put grain elevators and
ocean liners and biplanes on facing pages with Greek temples, insisting that
engineers had already found the beauty architects were still looking for, and
delivered the line about a house being a machine for living in — a phrase that
has been used against him ever since by people who did not read the sentences
around it.

## Weimar, and Housing for Workers

The German version of the argument was less about ships and more about
shortages. After 1918 the housing deficit in German cities was acute,
municipal governments under the new republic had money and mandate, and
architects found themselves designing not villas but whole estates. Ernst May
built tens of thousands of dwellings around Frankfurt on the principle that
standardization was what made decency affordable: repeating plans, repeating
components, and a compact fitted kitchen worked out by Margarete
Schütte-Lihotzky from time-and-motion studies of a housewife's movements. It
was the first kitchen designed as a machine, and it was installed in thousands
of flats.

The Bauhaus supplied the theory and most of the publicity. Gropius founded it
in Weimar in 1919 as a craft school with a mystical manifesto about the
cathedral of the future, and within six years it had become something closer to
a design laboratory for industrial production, housed in Dessau in a building
Gropius designed with a glass corner that turns the structure inside out. Its
politics made it a target from the beginning; it moved, changed directors,
moved again, and shut down in 1933 under pressure from a government that
considered flat roofs un-German. The closure scattered its staff and students
across the world, which is the single most effective distribution mechanism the
movement ever had.

## An Exhibition Gives It a Name

In 1932 the young Museum of Modern Art in New York mounted a show of recent
European and American architecture, curated by Henry-Russell Hitchcock and
Philip Johnson. The accompanying book called what it found the International
Style, and defined it with three propositions: architecture should be conceived
as enclosed volume rather than solid mass, it should be ordered by regularity
rather than by axial symmetry, and it should refuse applied ornament.

That formulation was enormously useful and slightly dishonest. It took a set of
movements with sharply different politics — Dutch abstraction, Soviet
constructivism, German social housing, French formal experiment — and read them
as a coherent visual style, which made them legible to American architects and
clients who had no interest at all in the welfare-state programme underneath.
Mies, who disliked the word style, never accepted the label. But the curators
were right that the buildings looked alike, and the name stuck because a name
was wanted.

What followed was an exodus. Gropius went to Harvard and rebuilt its
architecture school around the new approach; Marcel Breuer went with him. Mies
went to Chicago, took over the architecture programme at what became the
Illinois Institute of Technology, and spent two decades refining a steel-and-
glass vocabulary on its campus. Their students filled American offices for the
next thirty years. The transformation was so complete that by the 1950s a
student could graduate from a good school having never been taught to draw a
cornice.

## Glass Boxes and Corporations

The postwar skyscraper is where the style met capital and settled down. Lever
House on Park Avenue, finished in 1952 by Gordon Bunshaft at Skidmore, Owings
& Merrill, was the demonstration: a slender green glass slab standing free on a
podium, set back from the street behind an open plaza, with a curtain wall hung
entirely outside the structure. Six years later Mies and Johnson completed the
Seagram Building across the avenue — bronze and amber glass, pulled back from
the property line to leave a granite plaza, with I-beam mullions running the
full height of the facade to express a steel frame that fire code required to
be hidden in concrete. That last detail is either the purest statement of the
movement's logic or a flat contradiction of it, and arguments about which have
been keeping critics employed ever since.

The technology made it easy. Air conditioning meant deep floor plates no longer
needed operable windows; fluorescent lighting meant they no longer needed
daylight; sealed glass curtain walls could be assembled from repeating units by
crews who never had to cut a stone. A developer could get more rentable floor
area faster and cheaper than with any load-bearing alternative. By 1965 the
formula had been repeated in every major city on earth, usually by people with
none of Bunshaft's care about proportion or Mies's obsession with the depth of
a shadow line.

## Where It Broke

Criticism came from several directions at once, and the buildings supplied
plenty of ammunition. White render on masonry streaked and cracked in northern
weather; flat roofs leaked; the Villa Savoye itself was barely habitable within
a few years of completion. Glass towers cooked in summer and hemorrhaged heat
in winter, a problem nobody had to take seriously while fuel was cheap.

The deeper attack was urban. Jane Jacobs published The Death and Life of Great
American Cities in 1961, arguing from direct observation that the towers-in-a-
park model destroyed the dense, mixed, small-grained street life that made
neighbourhoods safe and workable, and that planners' confidence in their own
diagrams was the main obstacle to noticing. Five years later Robert Venturi
made the aesthetic case in Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture, a
defence of ambiguity, inconsistency, and historical reference that answered
Mies's austerity with the retort that less is a bore.

Then came the image. Pruitt-Igoe, a large public housing scheme in St. Louis
built in the mid-1950s to a conventional modernist plan, was poorly maintained,
racially segregated by the policy of the city that ran it, and starved of the
rent revenue its budget assumed. In 1972 the authorities began dynamiting it.
Charles Jencks later named a date and a time from that demolition as the moment
modern architecture died — a piece of showmanship that has done more than any
argument to fix the movement's reputation, and that quietly blames a building
type for a housing policy.

What actually happened was less like a death than a dispersal. Postmodernism
ran for about fifteen years and then largely exhausted itself. The frame, the
open plan, the curtain wall, and the habit of designing from the section rather
than the facade all stayed, because they were never stylistic preferences in
the first place. The white villa in the field is now a heritage object, restored
and photographed and visited. The office tower that descends from it is still
being built this year, on every continent, with better glass.
