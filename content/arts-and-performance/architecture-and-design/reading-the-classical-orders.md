# Reading the Classical Orders

Walk up to almost any courthouse, bank, or museum built between 1780 and 1930
and you will find a row of columns across the front, and on top of each one a
carved block whose job is to spread the load of the beam above across the
narrow top of the shaft below. That block is the capital, and for about
twenty-five centuries its profile has been the quickest way to tell what a
building thinks it is. A plain cushion under a square slab: Doric, and the
building wants to be taken seriously. A pair of spirals curling down from the
corners: Ionic, and it would like to be thought civilized. A basket of carved
leaves: Corinthian, and no expense has been spared.

Calling these things styles undersells them. An order is a complete system — a
set of proportional relationships governing the column, everything it carries,
and the gaps between one column and the next — so that a builder who knew the
system could produce a coherent building at any size, from a garden pavilion to
a temple platform sixty metres long, without inventing anything. Change one
dimension and every other dimension follows. That is the real content of the
word, and it explains why the orders outlasted the religion, the language, and
the construction technology that produced them.

## The Anatomy

An order has two halves, and the vocabulary is worth knowing because it is
almost entirely descriptive of function. Below is the column: in most cases a
base, then the shaft, then the capital. Above is the entablature, the
horizontal mass the columns carry, which divides into three bands. The
architrave is the beam itself, bearing directly on the capitals. Above it sits
the frieze, the decorated strip. Above that the cornice, which projects forward
to throw rainwater clear of the wall below and casts the deep shadow line that
gives a classical facade its top edge.

Every element in that stack began as a piece of carpentry. Greek temples were
built in timber before they were built in stone, and the stone versions
preserve the joinery in fossil form. The triglyphs of the Doric frieze — the
blocks with two vertical grooves — sit where the ends of the ceiling beams once
projected. The little peg-shaped guttae hanging below them are the wooden pins
that fixed those beams. The mutules, the sloping plates under the cornice, are
rafter ends. None of these serve any structural purpose in a marble building.
They were kept because the shape had become the meaning.

## The Greek Three

Doric is the oldest and the tersest. The shaft rises straight out of the
platform with no base at all, fluted with twenty shallow channels that meet in
sharp edges, tapering as it goes and swelling very slightly in the middle — the
entasis, a convex curve of a few centimetres that keeps the column from looking
pinched. The capital is a flared cushion, the echinus, under a square slab, the
abacus. Greek Doric columns are stocky: in the archaic period four or five
lower diameters tall, growing more slender over the following centuries without
ever reaching Ionic proportions.

Doric also carries a famous unsolvable problem. The rule is that a triglyph
must sit over the centre of each column, with another between each pair. But
the frieze also has to end with a triglyph at the corner, flush with the edge,
and those two requirements cannot both be satisfied by a regular grid. Greek
builders fudged it by narrowing the last bay slightly, pulling the corner
columns closer to their neighbours. Generations of later theorists worried at
this conflict as if it were a moral flaw.

Ionic came from the Greek cities of the Anatolian coast and the islands, and it
is everything Doric is not: taller, thinner, with a moulded base of stacked
convex and concave rings, twenty-four flutes separated by flat bands rather
than sharp edges, and a capital of paired volutes that look like a scroll
pressed flat. The frieze is typically continuous, an uninterrupted band for
sculpture rather than a rhythm of panels. Under the cornice runs a row of small
square blocks, the dentils. The Ionic capital has its own corner difficulty,
since the volutes face front and a corner column needs to face two ways at
once; the standard fix is to splay one volute out at forty-five degrees, which
works well enough that most people never notice.

Corinthian is the latest of the three and began as an interior luxury. Vitruvius
tells a story about the sculptor Callimachus seeing a basket left on a girl's
grave with an acanthus growing up around it — a charming origin myth of the
kind ancient writers supplied for everything. What is certain is that the
earliest Corinthian capitals appear inside temples, one or two of them, as a
special effect, and that Greek builders were slow to put them on exteriors. It
took the Romans to make Corinthian the default.

## Rome, and Why There Are Five

Roman architecture had a problem the Greeks did not. Rome built with arches,
vaults, and concrete, which means its walls carried load and its columns often
did not. So the orders migrated from structure to surface. Half-columns and
flat pilasters were applied to arcaded walls as a framing device, marking the
bays, giving scale, telling the eye how tall the building was. The Colosseum
does this across four levels: a sturdy order at the ground, Ionic above, then
Corinthian, then Corinthian pilasters in the attic storey — heavier below,
lighter above, an entirely visual argument about weight applied to a wall that
would stand up perfectly well without any of it.

Rome also added two orders to the Greek three. Tuscan is Doric stripped down —
unfluted shaft, simple base, no triglyphs — and was understood as the rustic,
Italic root of the family. Composite piles Ionic volutes on top of a Corinthian
basket of acanthus, producing the most ornate order of all, used on triumphal
arches where restraint was not the point. Neither was described as part of a
canonical set in antiquity. Vitruvius, writing under Augustus and leaving us
the only architectural treatise to survive from the ancient world, discusses
four and does not present them as a numbered system.

The set of five is a Renaissance invention. Alberti revived the subject in the
fifteenth century; Sebastiano Serlio, a generation later, was the first to
print the five orders side by side on a single page, and that image — five
columns in a row, ascending from plain to elaborate — did more to fix the idea
than any argument. Vignola then published a rule that reduced the whole
business to arithmetic. Take the radius of the column at its base as the
module, divide it into twelve or eighteen parts, and every dimension in the
order can be specified as a number of those parts. Column heights ran from
roughly seven modules of diameter for Tuscan up to ten for Corinthian and
Composite; the entablature came out at about a quarter of the column height.
Palladio published his own slightly different tables. So did half of Europe.

What the Renaissance treatises were really selling was portability. A provincial
builder in Poland or Peru who owned a copy of Vignola could lay out a
respectable portico without ever having seen a Roman ruin. The orders became
the common grammar of European building for four hundred years, which is why
a customs house in Dublin and a plantation house in Virginia and a church in
Mexico City can all be read by the same eye.

## The Cracks in the System

The rules were always more negotiable than the books implied. Claude Perrault,
translating Vitruvius for Louis XIV's court, eventually argued that the
proportions everyone treated as objectively beautiful were nothing of the kind
— they were customary, learned, agreeable because they were familiar. That was
a genuinely subversive position in the seventeenth century, and the argument it
opened has never really closed.

Nor were the ancient buildings as rule-bound as the tables suggest. The
Parthenon is full of deliberate distortions: the platform curves upward
slightly toward its centre, the columns lean inward, the corner columns are
thicker than the rest and spaced more tightly. These are corrections for the
way the eye misreads long straight lines at a distance, and they mean that
almost no two blocks in the building are identical. The marble was also
painted. The blinding white temples of the neoclassical imagination are
weathered ruins, and the originals carried red, blue, and gilding on the
triglyphs, mouldings, and sculpture — a fact known since the nineteenth century
and resisted for most of it.

By the 1920s the orders had become the enemy. A system that could only add
ornament to a wall had nothing to say about a steel frame, and modernism threw
the whole apparatus out, along with the academies that taught it. What survived
was the habit of looking. Fifty years later postmodernists began putting
capitals back on buildings as quotation marks, oversized and painted and
obviously insincere, which was its own kind of compliment. Meanwhile the
original job remains: a shaft, a thing on top of it to spread the load, and a
long argument about what proportions make that look right.
