# How Recording Rebuilt Music From the Inside

In a recording studio around 1912, a seven-piece band stands jammed together in a
small room, arranged by loudness rather than by section. The cornet player is
nearest the horn. The violinist is on a raised platform, leaning in, playing an
instrument with a metal resonator and a small brass horn bolted to it because an
ordinary fiddle will barely register. There is no drummer, or if there is, he is
playing woodblocks and cymbals only, because a bass drum will knock the cutting
stylus clean out of its groove. Nobody is playing a double bass; a tuba is doing
that job. Everything about the ensemble on that floor has been rearranged by a
machine that can only hear what is loud and close.

That is the first and most underrated fact about recording: before it changed how
music was distributed, it changed how music was played. And it kept doing that,
through every subsequent shift in the technology, usually faster than anyone
noticed.

## Playing Into a Horn

Acoustic recording was purely mechanical. Sound waves struck a diaphragm, the
diaphragm shook a stylus, and the stylus cut a wavering groove into a rotating
cylinder or disc. No electricity touched the signal anywhere along the way, which
meant the only amplifier in the system was the horn itself and the only way to
get louder on the record was to get louder in the room. Brass and banjo thrived.
Strings, guitars and anything played softly barely survived. Singers learned to
step toward the horn on quiet passages and pull back before a high note, a
physical choreography of dynamics that has no equivalent in any other era of
music-making.

The format imposed its own discipline. A ten-inch side spinning at roughly
seventy-eight revolutions per minute held about three minutes of music. That
number did not describe anything musical; it fell out of groove spacing and disc
diameter. But it became one of the most consequential constraints in the history
of popular song. Performances were trimmed, verses dropped, solos shortened, and
within a generation the three-minute song was not a limitation anybody was
working around. It was simply what a song was.

## The Microphone Invents a New Kind of Singer

Electrical recording arrived in the mid-1920s, and the change was abrupt. A
microphone converted sound into an electrical signal that could be amplified
before it drove the cutting head, which meant sensitivity was no longer tied to
volume. Suddenly a room could be recorded rather than a horn's narrow cone.
Strings came back. Bass frequencies appeared. Ensembles could stand where the
music wanted them rather than where the machine demanded.

The deepest consequence was vocal. A singer who had previously needed the
projection to fill a theater could now stand close to a microphone and half
speak, and the intimacy that had been inaudible beyond the third row became the
defining sound of popular music. Crooning was not a stylistic fashion that
happened to coincide with the microphone; it was the microphone. Bing Crosby's
conversational phrasing, and everything downstream of it, is a technique built
for a transducer. Critics at the time found it unmanly and weak, which is what
tends to happen when a technology makes a previously impossible expressive
option suddenly easy.

## Tape Dissolves the Single Performance

Until magnetic tape, a record was a document of an event. Whatever happened in
those three minutes was what you got, mistakes included, and the only remedy was
to do it again from the top. Tape, developed in Germany before and during the
Second World War and brought to American attention afterward by engineers who had
encountered the machines in occupied broadcasting facilities, changed the
ontology of a recording completely.

Tape could be cut with a razor blade and rejoined. That simple physical fact
meant a released performance no longer had to have occurred. The best first half
of one take could be married to the best second half of another, and nobody
listening could tell. Classical producers began assembling movements from dozens
of fragments. Radio, which had been overwhelmingly live, discovered it could be
built in advance; Bing Crosby, again at the hinge, backed the American company
Ampex specifically so he could pre-record his show rather than perform it twice
for different time zones.

Once you can edit, you can also layer. Les Paul spent the late 1940s and 1950s
recording parts on top of each other, building guitar harmonies with himself and
producing records whose sound had never existed in a room, then pushed
manufacturers toward machines that kept several independent tracks side by side
on the same tape. The moment there are multiple tracks, the mixing decision
separates from the performance decision, and a new job appears that is neither
composer nor player.

## The Studio Becomes an Instrument

What followed is the most important stretch in the whole story, because it is
where the recording stopped being a representation of music and became the music
itself. On four-track machines, engineers filled all four tracks, mixed them
down to one track of a second machine, and filled the remaining three again —
bouncing, with a generation of tape hiss and a permanent commitment to the
balance added each time. The Beatles' late-sixties records were made this way,
which is why they sound both lush and slightly compressed, and why decisions on
them are irrevocable in a way modern productions never are.

Producers started using the room and the gear as compositional material. Reverb
chambers, tape delay, deliberately overloaded preamps, close-miked drums, the
same part tracked three times to thicken it — all of this is writing, not
capture. In Jamaica, engineers working on instrumental B-sides went furthest
fastest. Stripping a vocal out, dropping the bass in and out, drenching a snare
hit in spring reverb and letting it decay across four bars, King Tubby and his
contemporaries treated the mixing desk as a live performance instrument and
invented dub, whose logic — the mix as the composition, the absence of elements
as an event — runs directly into remix culture, hip-hop production and most of
electronic dance music.

## When the Record Became Raw Material

By the 1970s there were enough records in the world for the record itself to
become an instrument. Bronx DJs noticed that dancers responded most to the brief
percussion break in a funk record, bought two copies of the same disc, and cut
back and forth between them to extend a four-bar break indefinitely. The
musicians who played those breaks were not consulted and mostly were not paid.
An entire genre grew out of a handful of drum passages, some of them a few
seconds long, played by session players who had no idea what they had made.

Digital sampling industrialized this. Once a sound could be stored as numbers, it
could be pitched, truncated, reversed, looped and layered without generational
loss, and the distinction between playing and quoting collapsed. The legal
system took roughly a decade to catch up, and when it did, the economics of
sample-dense records changed permanently, pushing producers toward replayed
interpolations and original synthesis.

The computer finished the job. Recording to a hard drive rather than tape made
editing granular to the individual note, and then to the individual drum hit.
Performances began to be quantized to a grid, tightening rhythm and, when
overdone, sanding off the microtiming that made a groove feel like a person.
Pitch correction, developed in the late 1990s by an engineer whose previous work
involved interpreting seismic data for oil prospecting, was meant to be
invisible. Set to its most extreme correction speed it produced an obvious
stepped glide instead, and a novelty artifact became first a hit sound and then a
permanent instrument.

Each step has the same shape. A tool appears with a technical purpose, it imposes
constraints, musicians internalize the constraints, and then someone abuses the
tool in a way its designers would have called a malfunction and the abuse becomes
a style. The three-minute song outlived the disc that caused it. Close-miked
intimacy outlived the fragile carbon microphone. Dub techniques outlived the tape
delays that made them. Even the loudness war of the compact disc era — records
mastered ever more aggressively so they would jump out of a radio or a shuffle
playlist, at the cost of dynamic range — left its mark on how people expect a
snare drum to hit, long after streaming services began normalizing playback
volume and removed the incentive.

Which leaves a question worth sitting with while listening to almost anything
made in the last century. The performance you are hearing may never have
happened. The voice may have been sung in fragments over several days, the drums
assembled from the best of forty passes, the bass played on a keyboard, the room
an algorithm. None of that makes it fake. It makes it a different art form from
the one that existed when the only way to hear music was to be standing in front
of someone making it.
