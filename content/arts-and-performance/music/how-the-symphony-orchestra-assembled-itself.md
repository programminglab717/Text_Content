# How the Symphony Orchestra Assembled Itself

The oboe gives an A and ninety people tune to it. There is no acoustic law
requiring the oboe to do this; it happens because the instrument is hard to
adjust quickly, its pitch is stable once warm, and its narrow, reedy tone cuts
through a hall full of scraping strings better than anything else in the room.
A convention of that kind — practical, undocumented in its origins, obeyed
everywhere — is a fair introduction to the orchestra as a whole. Nobody designed
it. It accumulated, one solved problem at a time, and the result is a machine of
roughly a hundred parts that has been more or less stable for a century.

## A Band of Strings, With Things Added

The seventeenth-century ancestor is a string band. Jean-Baptiste Lully's
twenty-four violins at the French court, a group organized in five parts rather
than the four we would expect, became famous across Europe less for its
repertoire than for its discipline: uniform bowing, rehearsed ornaments, players
who started together. That was a novelty. Elsewhere, ensembles were assembled
per occasion from whoever was available, and the notion of a fixed body of
musicians who played together regularly was still forming.

Underneath everything sat the continuo — a harpsichord or organ, usually with a
cello or bass doubling the line, filling in harmony from a shorthand of figures
under the bass notes. Whoever sat at the keyboard was generally in charge.
Winds came and went as the occasion demanded: oboes and bassoons for outdoor and
ceremonial music, trumpets and drums for anything requiring pomp, horns imported
from the hunting field. Their parts were often doublings or reinforcements
rather than independent voices.

## Mannheim, and the Invention of Ensemble

The court orchestra at Mannheim under the Elector Carl Theodor, led from the
1740s by Johann Stamitz, changed what an orchestra could be asked to do.
Visitors wrote about it the way people write about crack military units. The
players could execute a long, sustained crescendo from near silence to full
volume — an effect so unfamiliar that audiences reportedly rose from their seats
— and could turn on a sudden hush, and could play rushing upward arpeggiated
figures in perfect unison. These devices acquired nicknames, the Mannheim rocket
and the Mannheim sigh among them, and they spread across Europe because
composers now had somewhere to write them for.

Out of this came the Classical orchestra: strings in four parts with the basses
doubling the cellos an octave down, pairs of oboes and bassoons, pairs of horns,
flutes and then clarinets added as the century went on, trumpets and timpani for
weight. Forty players, give or take. The continuo faded out, partly because the
winds could now supply the inner harmony the harpsichord used to fill, and
partly because the keyboard's thin plucked tone was simply being drowned.

## Beethoven's Expansion and Berlioz's Catalogue

Beethoven inherited that ensemble and began stretching it almost immediately.
Trombones, which belonged to church and opera, entered the symphony in the
finale of the Fifth, along with piccolo and contrabassoon, and the effect at
that moment is less about new notes than new mass — the sound suddenly has a
floor and a ceiling it did not have four movements earlier. The Ninth adds a
chorus, four vocal soloists, and a percussion section borrowed from Turkish
military bands.

Hector Berlioz took the next step and made the orchestra itself the subject. He
wrote a treatise on instrumentation that is still read, cataloguing what each
instrument could actually do rather than what tradition assumed, and his
*Symphonie fantastique* deploys cor anglais, harps, bells, muted horns and
divided strings as specific colors rather than as reinforcements. Wagner went
further for different reasons: wanting a particular sonority between horn and
trombone for the Ring, he had instruments built to supply it, and he sank the
orchestra at Bayreuth into a covered pit so the singers could be heard over a
band of that size. By the time of Mahler and Richard Strauss the numbers had
reached their practical maximum, with woodwinds in fours, eight horns, offstage
brass and percussion sections requiring several players.

The reaction came quickly. Stravinsky wrote for a seven-piece touring group
during the First World War, Schoenberg reduced his forces to fifteen, and a
generation of composers turned to hard, dry, transparent scoring. But the
institutional orchestra kept the Mahler-sized establishment, because a body that
can play Strauss can also play Haydn, and a body sized for Haydn cannot do the
reverse.

## Valves, Keys, and a Better Bow

None of the expansion would have worked without a run of mechanical inventions
between roughly 1780 and 1850. The modern bow is the first of them. François
Tourte, working in Paris at the end of the eighteenth century, settled the
length, the inward-curving stick, and the ribbon of hair held at higher tension,
producing a bow that sustains evenly at the tip and can be thrown, bounced and
hooked in ways the older outward-curved bows could not manage. Strings moved
from plain gut toward wound and eventually steel, gaining volume and stability.
Louis Spohr is credited with the chin rest, which sounds trivial and is not: it
freed the left hand to shift and vibrate without supporting the instrument.

The brass transformation was more drastic. Before valves, a horn or trumpet
could sound only the notes of one harmonic series, adjusted by swapping lengths
of tubing between movements and, on the horn, by pushing a hand into the bell to
bend pitches at the cost of tone. Valve mechanisms appeared in Germany in the
1810s and made every chromatic note available with the same quality, which is
why brass writing before and after the middle of the nineteenth century looks
like two different crafts. Theobald Boehm redesigned the flute around acoustics
rather than the reach of human fingers, placing tone holes where the physics
wanted them and connecting them with a ring-and-axle mechanism; the system was
adapted to clarinet and saxophone, and Adolphe Sax invented the latter outright
along with a family of valved brass. Pitch itself drifted upward through the
century as orchestras chased brilliance, until governments and conferences began
fixing a standard — and even now Vienna and Berlin tune noticeably sharper than
London.

## Who Is in Charge

For most of the orchestra's history nobody stood in front of it. Direction came
from the keyboard, or from the leader of the first violins, who played and
signaled with the bow and the body. As scores grew longer, more rhythmically
intricate and more dependent on precise balance, that stopped working. Spohr and
Mendelssohn were among the first to beat time with a stick in front of a
symphony orchestra, in the 1820s and 30s, and within a generation the conductor
had become an interpreter rather than a timekeeper — deciding tempo
relationships, shaping phrases, adjusting the balance of a hundred players so
the second bassoon's counter-melody is audible at bar 112.

Wagner wrote a polemic on conducting that reads as a manifesto for flexible
tempo and against mechanical beating, and von Bülow made the job a profession.
Recording turned it into a form of celebrity, since the conductor is the one
member of an orchestra whose contribution appears on every record and whose name
sells it. The relationship with the players has always been strange: a hundred
highly trained specialists, most of whom have opinions, following someone who
makes no sound.

The players got there by a route that has hardened into ritual. A conservatoire
training, then years of auditions in which a candidate plays a concerto movement
and a list of orchestral excerpts — the exposed passages every professional is
expected to have polished, the opening of Strauss's *Don Juan* for violins, the
Mendelssohn scherzo for flute, the *Bolero* solo for trombone. From the later
twentieth century most major orchestras began holding at least the early rounds
behind a screen, with the floor sometimes carpeted so that footsteps give
nothing away, and the composition of orchestras shifted substantially in the
decades that followed. A seat won this way is usually held for a career, which
gives these institutions an unusual continuity: a section may contain players
whose combined tenure spans a century of the same hall.

It also makes them expensive in a way that does not improve with time. A
symphony takes the same number of player-hours to perform now as it did in 1900,
and no technology has ever been invented that lets forty people play a Brahms
symphony with thirty. Costs rise with the general economy; productivity cannot.
The standard response has been the subscription series built around a repertory
that consolidated roughly between the 1870s and the 1920s and has barely shifted
since, supplemented by donors, endowments and, outside the United States, public
money. The consequence is that the modern orchestra spends most of its working
life performing a body of music assembled a century ago for an institution that
was then still changing shape, and the arguments inside the profession about
what to do with that fact are perennial and unresolved.

## The Hall as the Last Instrument

An orchestra is designed around a room it cannot control. Wallace Sabine worked
out the relationship between a hall's volume, its absorbent surfaces and its
reverberation time while consulting on Boston's symphony hall at the end of the
nineteenth century, and that building became the first major concert room
planned with the numbers in hand rather than by imitation. What it imitated
anyway was the shape that already worked: the narrow rectangular box, like
Vienna's Musikverein and Amsterdam's Concertgebouw, whose parallel side walls
throw strong early reflections at the audience from the side and produce the
sense of being surrounded by sound. Later designers tried other geometries —
terraced vineyards of seating wrapped around a central platform, as in Berlin —
and the argument about which approach serves the music better has not ended.

The hall also explains the orchestra's peculiar proportions. Sixty string
players against three trombones looks lopsided until you notice that
uncorrelated sources do not add the way intuition suggests: doubling the number
of violins does not double the loudness, because the players are not perfectly
in phase, whereas a single trombone playing fortissimo can bury a whole section.
The string body has to be large simply to stay in the conversation. Everything
about the layout — first violins nearest the audience on the left, the heavy
brass at the back on risers, percussion behind everything, the conductor placed
where all of it converges — follows from that arithmetic and from centuries of
adjusting by ear in rooms that were not quite right.
