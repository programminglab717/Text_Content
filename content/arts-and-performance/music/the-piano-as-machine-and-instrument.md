# The Piano as Machine and Instrument

Prop open the lid of a grand piano and the first thing that registers is that
this is not really furniture. A gold-painted iron casting fills the case, drilled
and webbed like an engine block, and across it runs a harp of steel wire under
enough strain to tear the instrument apart if the frame ever gave. Underneath
sits a thin sheet of spruce with ribs glued across its back, curved slightly
upward against the load. And between the keys and the strings is a dense little
forest of wooden levers, felt pads, leather-tipped rockers and brass screws —
roughly ninety near-identical mechanisms, one per key, each with about half a
dozen moving parts. It is one of the most complicated machines anyone keeps in a
living room, and its entire purpose is to throw a hammer at a wire and then get
out of the way.

## Cristofori's Problem

The piano exists because of a specific engineering puzzle that the harpsichord
could not solve. A harpsichord plucks: a small plectrum mounted on a jack catches
the string as the key goes down. The pluck is essentially the same every time, so
a harpsichordist cannot play one note louder than the next by pressing harder.
Expression has to come from articulation, ornament and registration. The
clavichord, at the opposite extreme, is genuinely touch-sensitive — a brass
tangent rises and stays pressed against the string, so the player retains contact
and can even wobble the pitch — but it is so quiet it is barely audible in the
next room.

Bartolomeo Cristofori, who looked after the instrument collection of the Medici
in Florence around 1700, built a keyboard that struck the string with a hammer
instead. A hammer's speed can vary with how hard the key is pressed, which gives
you dynamics. But a hammer introduces a problem a plectrum doesn't have: if the
hammer is still connected to the key when it arrives, it will rest against the
string and kill the note instantly. So the hammer has to be thrown. The mechanism
must accelerate it, release it a few millimeters short of the string, let it fly
free, strike, and rebound — and then catch it on the way back so it doesn't
bounce up and hit again.

That release is called escapement, and everything about the feel of a piano
follows from it. The slight notch you can sense near the bottom of a slow, quiet
keypress is the moment the hammer leaves; press any more slowly than that and no
sound comes out at all, because the hammer has been let go without enough speed
to reach the string. In the early nineteenth century the Parisian maker Sébastien
Érard added a second escapement level, letting the hammer be re-thrown from
partway up rather than requiring the key to return all the way. Fast repeated
notes, trills at the bottom of the keybed, the whole athletic Romantic repertoire
becomes mechanically possible at that point. The instrument and the music it
invited grew together.

## Twenty Tons of Wire

The nineteenth century's other contribution was brute force. Wooden-framed
pianos could not hold much tension, and more tension is what you need for volume,
sustain and a bigger hall. Boston makers in the 1820s — Alpheus Babcock among the
first — began casting the frame in a single piece of iron, and once the structure
could take the load, the strings got thicker, tighter and longer. A modern
concert grand carries something on the order of twenty tons of combined string
tension. That enormous number is the reason the case is built like a ship's hull
and the reason a piano goes out of tune when the humidity changes: the wooden
soundboard swells and shrinks under it, and the pitch follows.

The stringing is a set of compromises with physics. Low notes need long, heavy
strings, but a string long enough for the lowest A would make the instrument
absurd, so bass strings are wound with copper wire to add mass without adding
stiffness. Those wound strings are also strung diagonally across the others in
most modern designs, which lets them be longer and places them over a livelier
part of the soundboard. Higher up, one string per note is not loud enough to
balance the bass, so the tenor gets two and the treble gets three, tuned to the
same pitch. The tiny impurities in those unisons are a large part of what makes a
piano sound like a piano rather than a sine tone — and getting them close enough
without making them identical is most of what a tuner is actually doing.

The strings themselves make almost no sound. They are too thin to move much air.
Their job is to transmit vibration through the bridge into the soundboard, which
has the surface area to couple with the room. Spruce is used because it is stiff
along the grain and light, and it is built with a slight crown so the downward
pressure of the strings keeps it under compression. When a soundboard finally
cracks or loses its crown after several decades, the instrument does not go
silent; it goes dull, which is worse.

## What the Player Actually Controls

Here is the uncomfortable part, and pianists argue about it constantly. Once the
hammer has escaped, the player has no contact with it. The hammer is a projectile.
For a single note, everything about the sound — loudness and timbre together — is
determined by one number: how fast that hammer was moving when it hit. There is no
separate control for tone. A finger stroke that is caressing and a finger stroke
that is percussive will produce identical sound if they launch the hammer at the
same speed.

And yet nobody who has heard two pianists play the same instrument believes the
sound is the same, and they are not deluded. What varies is everything around that
single parameter. The relative velocities within a chord, so the melody note
sings a fraction louder than the accompaniment beneath it. The exact moment each
note begins and ends, which is where phrasing lives. The release, since the damper
falling back onto the string is an audible event with its own speed. The
percussive noise of the key bed and the action, which is part of the sound of
close listening. And the pedal, which changes the instrument's acoustics while
the note is sounding.

Piano pedals are badly understood by almost everyone who has taken a few lessons.
The right one does not simply sustain. It lifts every damper in the instrument at
once, so the strings you are not playing are free to vibrate sympathetically with
the ones you are, and the whole soundboard rings with a halo of related
frequencies. That is why a pedaled chord sounds richer, not just longer, and why
good pianists pedal in tiny partial increments, half-lifting the dampers so they
brush rather than clear the strings. The left pedal on a grand slides the entire
action sideways so the hammers strike two of the three strings and land on a
softer, less-pounded part of the felt, which changes color more than volume. The
middle pedal on many grands is the cleverest and least used: it catches only the
dampers that are already raised at the moment you press it, letting a bass note
ring for a page while everything above it stays crisp.

## Why a Piano Is Never Quite in Tune

Real steel wire is stiff, not ideally flexible, and a stiff string's overtones sit
slightly sharp of where simple mathematics says they should. This property,
inharmonicity, is unavoidable and worse on short, thick strings, which is why a
small upright sounds harsher than a nine-foot grand. It has a strange consequence:
if a tuner sets the octaves mathematically exactly, they sound flat to the ear,
because the ear is comparing the upper note against the sharp-leaning overtones of
the lower one. So tuners stretch the octaves, progressively sharpening the top of
the instrument and flattening the bottom, by an amount they judge from the
instrument itself.

On top of that, the piano is tuned in equal temperament, twelve identical
semitones per octave, which makes every key playable and every interval except
the octave slightly wrong. The major thirds are noticeably wide. A string quartet
or a choir does not have to accept this and generally doesn't — they adjust pitch
constantly to make chords lock. A pianist is welded to a compromise, and nothing
about touch or interpretation can move a single pitch by a cent.

There is an odd dignity in that. The instrument is entirely determined before the
player arrives: pitch fixed by the tuner, action regulated by the technician,
voicing set by someone who has poked the hammer felt with needles until it is soft
enough. A violinist carries their sound with them; a pianist walks out on stage and
is handed somebody else's machine, five minutes to feel out how deep the keys are
and how fast the repetition is, and then plays. The expressive range that remains
after all those fixed variables is narrow in theory and, for reasons that remain
slightly mysterious even to acousticians, enormous in practice.
