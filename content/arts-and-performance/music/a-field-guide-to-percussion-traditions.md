# A Field Guide to Percussion Traditions

Stand at the edge of an Ewe drumming ensemble in Ghana and the loudest thing is
not a drum. It is a small iron bell, two cones welded to a handle, struck with a
wooden stick, producing a pattern of seven strokes spread unevenly across a cycle
of twelve pulses. The bell never varies. It repeats for the entire performance,
dry and metallic, cutting through everything, and every other part in the
ensemble is positioned in relation to it. Newcomers instinctively try to hear it
as the beat. It isn't. It is closer to a coordinate system.

That distinction — between a pulse you feel and a fixed pattern everyone
navigates by — is the single most useful thing to carry into any unfamiliar
percussion tradition. What follows is a walk through several of them, organized
not by geography but by the problems each has solved.

## The Bell as Clock

Timeline patterns show up across West and Central Africa and everywhere the
African diaspora reached. The bell figure heard in Ewe music is one of the most
widespread, and versions of it turn up in Yoruba drumming, in Cuban rumba and in
Brazilian samba. Its defining quality is asymmetry: the strokes are unevenly
spaced, so the pattern has a front and a back, and any part played against it
acquires a specific identity depending on where in the cycle it starts.

In Cuba the same principle hardened into clave — five strokes spread over two
bars, played on a pair of hardwood sticks. Musicians describe a piece as being in
3-2 or 2-3 depending on which half of the pattern comes first, and getting this
wrong is not a matter of taste. A band playing cruzado, with parts on opposite
sides of the clave, sounds broken in a way that even listeners who have never
heard the word can detect. The clave itself is often not played at all in a large
ensemble; it is simply assumed, held silently by everyone, the way a Balkan dance
band holds an uneven 9/8 grouping without counting it.

## Parts That Interlock

The second recurring solution is distribution. Rather than one player executing a
complicated rhythm, several players each execute something simple, arranged so
that the composite is something no single person is playing.

Balinese gamelan makes this explicit. In kotekan, two interlocking parts, polos
and sangsih, each play a sparse figure full of holes, and the holes in one are
exactly where the notes of the other fall. Played together at speed the result is
a continuous stream of notes far faster than either musician's hands are moving.
The rest of the ensemble is layered by tempo: small high-pitched metallophones
elaborating, larger ones carrying the core melody, and a hierarchy of gongs
marking the structural points of the cycle, with the deepest gong sounding once
per full rotation like a period at the end of a sentence. A pair of hand drums
steers the whole thing, cueing changes in tempo and dynamics.

A Brazilian samba school's percussion section, the batería, works on a related
logic with a hundred or more players. Surdos — big shoulder-slung bass drums in
three sizes — divide the low end between them, one answering the other in
alternation to produce a swinging two-feel that no single drum states. Caixas
provide a continuous snare wash, tamborins cut small high figures with a flick of
the wrist, agogô bells supply a timeline, and a cuíca, a friction drum played by
rubbing a stick fixed inside the skin, makes a sound somewhere between a laugh
and a moan. The repinique player leads with shouted rhythmic calls that the
entire section answers, which is the only way to conduct a hundred drums outdoors.

West African ensembles are organized by role rather than by size: several support
drums lock into fixed repeating parts, and one lead drummer, playing the largest
and most flexible instrument, improvises against them and signals changes.
Cuban rumba follows the same architecture with congas, where the lowest drums
hold the pattern and the small, high-pitched quinto improvises constantly, often
in direct dialogue with a dancer's body.

## Drums That Speak

Some drums encode language. The hourglass-shaped pressure drums of West Africa
have skins at both ends laced together with tension cords along the body; squeeze
the cords under your arm and the head tightens, raising the pitch. Because Yoruba
and many neighboring languages are tonal, a drummer can reproduce the pitch
contour and rhythm of speech closely enough for listeners who know the
conventions to recover proverbs, praise names and messages from the drumming.
This is not metaphor and not a party trick; it is a parallel channel for the same
language.

The batá drums of the Yoruba, carried to Cuba and absorbed into Lucumí religious
practice, work along similar lines. Three double-headed drums of graded size,
each with a large and a small head, play a repertoire of set patterns, each
associated with a particular deity and often with texts underlying them. The
largest drum leads and converses with the middle drum while the smallest holds a
steady figure. Learning the repertoire means learning which pattern belongs to
whom, in what order, and how to move between them without breaking the ceremony.

## Syllables Before Sticks

Several traditions teach rhythm as spoken language first. North Indian tabla has
an entire vocabulary of bols — na, tin, dha, ge, ke, tirakita — in which each
syllable names a specific stroke: which hand, which part of the head, whether the
drum is allowed to ring or is damped. A student recites compositions aloud long
before playing them cleanly, and a performance can include a section where the
drummer speaks the composition and then plays it.

South Indian musicians take this further with konnakol, in which rhythmic
solkattu syllables are performed as a vocal art in their own right, alongside the
mridangam, a barrel drum played horizontally across the lap with both hands. The
structures involved are arithmetically elaborate: cycles of a fixed number of
beats, phrases that divide those beats into groupings that cut across them, and
cadential figures repeated three times so that the final stroke lands precisely
on the first beat of the next cycle. Everyone in the room knows where that beat
is, and the drummer's job is to disappear from it for a while and then land on it
exactly.

Tabla is also one of the few drum families with real melodic pitch. The bass
drum, played with the heel of the hand, can be bent through an interval by
pressing into the head mid-stroke, while the treble drum is tuned to the tonic of
the raga being performed. Both owe their tone to the syahi, the black disc of
paste applied in layers to the center of the head. It loads the membrane in a way
that pulls its overtones into a nearly harmonic relationship, which is why a
tabla sings a note rather than producing a thud. Other traditions load their
heads with whatever works — rice paste, tuning compound, a smear applied fresh
before a performance and scraped off afterward.

Skins that are not loaded are usually tuned by tension or by heat. Djembe players
tighten rope in a system of vertical pulls and horizontal knots, then tune
further by warming the head at a fire. Frame drums across the Middle East,
Central Asia and Ireland respond quickly to humidity, which is why a bodhrán
player will hold a hand against the inside of the skin, both to damp it and to
push its pitch around while striking the front with a short double-ended beater.

## Everything Else Is an Instrument Too

The most inventive percussion tradition of the twentieth century began with
confiscation. After skin drums were suppressed in colonial Trinidad, Carnival
bands turned to tuned lengths of bamboo stamped on the ground, and when those
were restricted in turn, to metal — biscuit tins, brake drums, and eventually
discarded oil barrels. Steelpan makers sink the head of a barrel into a bowl,
hammer distinct note areas into the surface, and tune each one by working the
metal until its overtones fall into place. A full orchestra of pans, from lead
instruments to bass pans made from several full-length drums, can play
anything, which is the point: an entire chromatic ensemble improvised out of
industrial waste within a generation.

Two other assemblages are worth noting because they are so recent that we can see
the seams. The drum kit was put together in New Orleans and other American cities
when a workable foot pedal let one player operate a bass drum while their hands
handled snare and cymbals — a marching band's percussion section compressed into
a single seat, largely for economic reasons. Korean samul nori was arranged in
the late 1970s, taking the instruments and repertoire of rural outdoor
processional music and seating four players on a stage: two gongs, one large and
one small and piercing, and two drums, one barrel and one hourglass. Japanese
ensemble taiko, with its ranks of drums and choreographed strokes, was assembled
in similar fashion in the mid-twentieth century from older festival and shrine
drumming.

None of these is less authentic for being recently built. The bell pattern in
Ghana was invented by somebody too. Traditions are what assemblages look like
after enough time has passed that nobody remembers the assembling.
