# The Long Argument About Electronic Music

Roland's TB-303 was designed to solve a small and slightly sad problem. A guitar
player practicing alone at home had no bass player, so here was a silver box with
a knob-laden front panel, a sequencer you programmed by ear, and a circuit meant
to approximate a bass guitar. It did not approximate a bass guitar. It sounded
like a rubber band being pulled through a resonant pipe, sales were poor, and
within a few years used units were sitting in pawn shops for very little money.
Teenagers in Chicago bought them. They discovered that if you twisted the
resonance and cutoff controls while a pattern looped, the machine produced a
liquid, squelching line that slid and screamed, and that if you played that loud
in a room full of people something remarkable happened. Acid house came out of a
failed practice aid, used in exactly the way its engineers had not intended.

That story repeats so often in electronic music that it is essentially the
genre's operating principle, and it is worth holding onto while considering the
complaint that has trailed the music for a century: that machines cannot make
real music, and that whatever comes out of them is cold, inhuman, or not played
at all.

## Instruments Before There Was a Genre

Electronic instruments predate electronic music by decades. Around 1900 Thaddeus
Cahill built the Telharmonium, a device that generated tones with spinning
electromagnetic rotors and weighed as much as a small factory, because it
effectively was one. It had no loudspeaker — loudspeakers did not yet exist in
usable form — so its sound was distributed to subscribers over telephone lines.
It failed commercially and was eventually scrapped, but the tonewheel principle
survived and resurfaced in the Hammond organ, which was sold in the 1930s to
churches that could not afford pipes and which ended up defining the sound of
gospel, soul jazz and rock organ instead.

The instrument that captured the public imagination was Leon Theremin's. Two
antennas, two oscillators beating against each other, and a player who never
touches anything: moving a hand toward the vertical antenna raises the pitch,
and the horizontal loop controls volume. It is a purely gestural instrument
with no physical reference points at all, which makes it one of the hardest
things in the world to play accurately and gave it an unearthly reputation it has
never shaken. Around the same time Maurice Martenot built the ondes Martenot,
which was designed from the start to be playable — a ring worn on the finger,
sliding along a wire, with a keyboard beneath — and which composers including
Messiaen wrote for seriously.

## Two Studios That Disagreed

The music itself began in radio stations after the Second World War. In Paris,
Pierre Schaeffer, an engineer and broadcaster, started working with recorded
sound as raw material: train noises, doors, instruments recorded and then cut,
reversed, looped and layered until the source was unrecognizable. He called the
result musique concrète, concrete because it began with actual captured sound
rather than abstract notation, and he argued that a listener could attend to a
sound as an object in itself rather than as a sign pointing back to whatever
produced it.

In Cologne, the composers around the West German radio studio thought this was
undisciplined. Their approach, elektronische Musik, built sounds from the bottom
up out of pure electronic tones, laboriously assembling complex timbres from sine
waves recorded a fraction at a time. The dispute was as much philosophical as
technical — sound as found object versus sound as fully specified construction —
and it dissolved fairly quickly once Stockhausen began combining recorded voice
with synthesized material and discovered that the boundary was not interesting
enough to defend.

Similar rooms opened elsewhere. An American university studio acquired a
room-sized RCA synthesizer programmed by punching holes in paper tape, which
offered composers control over every parameter of every note and, not
incidentally, the ability to work without performers. The BBC built a workshop to
supply sound for radio drama, where Daphne Oram experimented with drawing
waveforms onto film and Delia Derbyshire assembled the Doctor Who theme from test
oscillators and painstakingly measured lengths of spliced tape — a piece of pure
studio construction that reached an audience of millions who had no idea how it
had been made.

## Voltage Control Makes It Playable

What changed everything in the mid-1960s was an engineering idea rather than a
musical one. If you build oscillators, filters and amplifiers whose behavior is
governed by an incoming control voltage, then any of them can control any other,
and a keyboard can simply be a device that emits a voltage proportional to which
key is down. Robert Moog on the American East Coast and Don Buchla on the West
arrived at voltage control at roughly the same moment and drew opposite
conclusions about what to attach to it. Moog fitted a conventional keyboard;
Buchla deliberately refused, building touch plates and sequencers because he did
not want musicians reaching for the habits of the piano.

Moog's choice won commercially, and the instrument reached the public through
Wendy Carlos's album of Bach realized on synthesizer, which was an enormous
seller in the late 1960s. That record is worth remembering for how it was made:
the instrument was monophonic, so every line of every fugue was played
separately, in tune, in time, and multitracked, with tuning drifting under the
studio lights. It was not pushbutton music by any definition. It was months of
work.

Then the tools got easier in rapid steps. The Minimoog folded a small modular
system into a portable instrument with a fixed signal path and no patch cords, so
a touring keyboard player could carry one. Polyphonic instruments arrived at the
end of the 1970s, and crucially so did patch memory, which meant a sound could be
stored and recalled instead of laboriously dialed back. Digital FM synthesis,
developed by John Chowning at Stanford and licensed to Yamaha, produced the
bell-like, glassy timbres that saturate records of the early 1980s. And a
manufacturer-agreed standard for sending note data between machines meant a
keyboard from one company could drive a drum machine from another in sync, which
quietly turned a pile of separate boxes into a single instrument.

## The Machines That Were Used Wrong

Roland's drum machines followed the same path as the 303. The 808 and its
successor were built to give musicians serviceable drum sounds, were judged
unrealistic against sample-based competitors, and sold modestly. Their
descendants are everywhere: the long booming kick that is really just a tuned
sine wave decaying, the thin tick of the hi-hat, the cowbell. Hip-hop producers,
Chicago house DJs and the young Detroit musicians who built techno all reached
for these machines partly because they were cheap and partly because they sounded
like nothing else, and the supposed defect — that they did not convincingly
imitate a drummer — turned out to be the entire point. A sound with no acoustic
original cannot be judged against one.

Kraftwerk had already demonstrated the aesthetic possibilities of embracing
rather than hiding the machinery, and their records fed directly into both
American traditions: into hip-hop, where a New York producer built one of the
genre's foundational tracks over borrowed Kraftwerk material, and into Detroit,
where a handful of suburban teenagers combined that European precision with funk
and made something that sounded like a city that did not exist yet.

## The Accusation Keeps Moving

The charge of inhumanity has been leveled at every one of these developments, and
it never sticks for long, because it gets reassigned to whatever is newest. The
pipe organ was once described as a machine that played itself. The player piano
was received as an affront to musicianship. Electric guitars were denounced as
cheating, turntables were said not to be instruments, and the arrival of the
laptop on stage produced a wave of complaints about performers who might as well
be checking their mail.

There is something real underneath the complaint, and it is worth stating
precisely rather than dismissing. In acoustic performance, the relationship
between physical effort and sound is legible: you can see the bow pressure, hear
the breath run out, watch a drummer's arm and know what is coming. Electronic
instruments sever that link. A barely perceptible gesture can produce an
overwhelming sound, or no gesture at all can, and an audience deprived of that
causal thread loses a genuine channel of information. Performers have spent
decades inventing replacements — controllers that respond to pressure and
position, modular rigs patched in front of an audience, visual systems keyed to
the audio — with mixed success.

What the complaint gets wrong is the assumption that the alternative to effort is
nothing. Removing the constraint of what a body can do in real time moved the
labor somewhere else: into timbre, which becomes composable rather than given;
into rhythm at resolutions no ensemble can hold; into structure assembled over
weeks. A composer working with a modular system is not avoiding the work of
music. They are doing work that has no equivalent in the older practice, and the
skill involved is not transferable from it.

Meanwhile the instruments that were once proof of music's mechanization have all
been quietly promoted. The Hammond is an instrument. The theremin is an
instrument. The 808 is an instrument, reissued and cloned and emulated, its
imperfect circuits studied like a Stradivarius varnish. Give it long enough and
the software everyone currently mistrusts will be somebody's beloved vintage
gear, defended by people insisting that the new thing, whatever it is, isn't
really music.
