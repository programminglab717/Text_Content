# The Blues and What Grew Out of It

Put a finger on the third fret of a guitar's third string and push the string
sideways across the fingerboard. The pitch rises, not in a step but as a smear,
and somewhere in the middle of that smear — short of the note the fret above
would give you — is a place the hand learns to find and hold. That pitch has no
name in standard notation. It is neither the minor third nor the major third,
and the whole expressive vocabulary of the blues lives in the region between
them, along with a similarly unstable seventh and a fifth that can be leaned on
until it sours.

This is the first thing to understand about the music: the notes that define it
are not available on a piano. Blues pianists worked around the problem by
striking the major and minor third together, producing a crushed, ambiguous
sound that approximates a pitch the instrument cannot actually play. Singers
never had the problem at all. The scale was never a scale. It was a set of
places a voice goes.

## Where It Came From

The blues assembled itself in the American South in the decades after
emancipation, out of materials that had been accumulating for a long time:
field hollers sung across distances, work songs organized around the stroke of
an axe or a hammer, the call-and-response structures of church singing, the
narrative ballads circulating among Black and white Southerners alike, and the
banjo and fiddle music of the string bands. It was rural, it was almost entirely
Black, and for its first decades it left no recorded trace. What survives is
secondhand: the bandleader W. C. Handy's account of waiting at a Mississippi
railway station around the turn of the century and hearing a ragged man play
slide guitar with a knife blade while singing about a place where one railroad
crossed another. Handy described the effect as the weirdest music he had ever
heard, and then spent the next decade writing it down and publishing it.

The slide itself may descend from West African one-string instruments; the
homemade version, a wire nailed to a porch wall and played with a bottle, was
widespread enough among Southern children to have its own name. What the
technique gives you is continuous pitch on an instrument that is otherwise
fretted into fixed steps — an approximation, in other words, of the voice.

## Twelve Bars and Three Lines

The form everyone knows is twelve bars long and moves through three chords.
Four bars on the home chord, two on the fourth degree, two back home, then one
on the fifth, one on the fourth, and two to land. Over it, a lyric in three
lines: a statement, the same statement repeated with slight variation, and a
third line that rhymes and resolves.

The repetition looks like an artistic choice and began as a practical one. It
gives a singer who is improvising four bars in which to invent the payoff line,
and it gives the instrument four bars in which to answer the voice, since the
sung phrase occupies only the first half of each four-bar unit and the guitar
fills the rest. Call and response, moved indoors and compressed onto one
performer.

Early blues was not nearly so tidy. Recordings from the 1920s and 30s by Delta
players like Charley Patton and Son House run bars long, drop beats, and
stretch a chord for as long as the lyric needs, following the words rather than
a grid. The regularity came with the sheet music trade, with dance bands that
needed everyone to arrive at the same place, and with the three-minute limit of
a record side. Eight-bar and sixteen-bar variants persisted, and still do.

## A Market Is Discovered

In 1920 a vaudeville singer named Mamie Smith recorded "Crazy Blues" for a
small label, and it sold in quantities nobody in the industry had anticipated.
The lesson drawn was that Black listeners constituted a market, and within a
few years every significant label had a division producing what the trade
catalogues called race records. The first wave of stars were women working the
Black vaudeville circuit — Ma Rainey, Bessie Smith, Alberta Hunter — singing
composed blues with jazz musicians behind them; Louis Armstrong turns up on
several of Bessie Smith's sides, trading phrases with her as an equal.

Only in the mid-1920s did the labels send scouts and portable recording
equipment south to capture solo guitarists. What they found sorted itself into
regions. The Mississippi Delta produced heavy, percussive, slide-driven playing
with a strong relationship to the drone. The Piedmont, up through the Carolinas
and Georgia, produced an intricate fingerpicked style with an alternating thumb
keeping a ragtime bass under a syncopated melody. Texas players favored
single-string melodic lines against a looser accompaniment. Meanwhile, in
Chicago rent parties and Southern barrelhouses, pianists were building
boogie-woogie out of a rolling eight-to-the-bar left hand that would eventually
become the rhythmic foundation of rock and roll.

Robert Johnson, recorded in two brief sessions in Texas hotel rooms in the mid-
1930s, sold modestly in his lifetime and was canonized decades later when his
recordings were reissued to an audience of young guitarists who had never heard
anything like them. The posthumous mythology around him — the crossroads, the
devil, the bargain — has obscured a straightforward fact about the recordings:
they are technically extraordinary, with a bass line, a chordal middle and a
treble commentary going at once under a voice that is doing something different
from all three.

## North, and Plugged In

Between the First World War and the 1960s, several million Black Southerners
left for Chicago, Detroit, St. Louis, Los Angeles and New York. The blues went
with them, and in the cities it changed in ways that followed directly from the
new setting. A solo guitarist who could hold a country juke joint could not be
heard in a crowded urban bar, so the instruments were amplified. Amplification
did not merely make things louder; it made a guitar sustain, which made bent
notes hang in the air, and it distorted when pushed, which added a rasp that
players quickly came to want rather than tolerate.

The Chicago band format settled into electric guitar, harmonica, piano, bass and
drums. Muddy Waters, recorded in Mississippi by a folklorist before he moved
north, rebuilt his Delta repertoire around that lineup. Little Walter cupped a
cheap public-address microphone against his harmonica and drove it through a
small amplifier until the tone became something closer to a saxophone. Howlin'
Wolf brought a voice of frightening physical size. Behind much of it stood
Willie Dixon, a bassist and songwriter at Chess Records whose compositions
supplied a large share of the repertoire that would later be borrowed by
everyone.

## The Descendants

From this point the family tree branches faster than it can be traced. Jump
blues — a small swinging band, a shuffle, a shouting vocal, a honking tenor sax
— fed directly into rhythm and blues. Chuck Berry, recording for the same
Chicago label as Muddy Waters, put country-derived guitar phrasing and
narrative lyrics about cars and school over a boogie bassline and a hard
backbeat, and the result was sold to teenagers as rock and roll. Elvis
Presley's first single paired a blues by Arthur Crudup with a bluegrass
standard, one on each side, which is as concise a statement of the genre's
parentage as anyone could ask for.

Ray Charles took the structure and fervor of gospel singing and attached them to
secular lyrics, which scandalized church audiences and produced soul music. Sam
Cooke came out of a gospel quartet. Aretha Franklin was a preacher's daughter.
The Memphis and Detroit studios of the 1960s built an industry on the
combination, and when James Brown began stripping the harmony back to a single
vamp and pushing everything onto rhythm, funk emerged from it — and, a
generation later, supplied the records that hip-hop producers would sample.

The blues also soaked into musics that are not usually labeled with it. Jimmie
Rodgers, the founding figure of commercial country music, recorded a series of
pieces built on twelve-bar blues with yodels attached. Hank Williams learned
guitar and phrasing from a Black street musician in Alabama known as Tee Tot.
Jazz never left the form: Kansas City bands built entire arrangements from riffs
over the blues changes, Charlie Parker wrote bebop heads on it, and virtually
every significant jazz musician has recorded blues in some form, because it is
the one structure everyone on the bandstand can be assumed to know.

In the early 1960s a scene of young British enthusiasts — record collectors,
initially, obsessed with imported Chess and Sun sides — began playing the music
themselves. Within a few years bands out of London were selling it back to
American audiences at volume, with a fidelity to the source that ranged from
scrupulous to nonexistent. The royalty disputes that followed, some of them
settled only decades afterward, are a reminder that the economics of this music
were never fair and that the disparity between who made it and who was paid for
it runs through the entire history.

What survives across all these mutations is not the twelve-bar form, which most
of the descendants abandoned. It is a handful of gestures: the bent and
worried note, the vocal line that arrives slightly behind the beat and catches
up, the break into falsetto or growl at the emotional peak, the instrument
answering the voice in the gaps. Those things turn up in a country singer's
phrasing, in a saxophone solo, in the way a rapper leans across a bar line. The
form was a container. The gestures were the point, and they were portable.
