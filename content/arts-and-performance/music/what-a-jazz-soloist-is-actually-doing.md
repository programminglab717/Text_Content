# What a Jazz Soloist Is Actually Doing

The bassist counts a tempo with his hand, the drummer catches it on the ride
cymbal, the band plays the melody once through, and then the tenor player steps
up and plays something nobody in the room has ever heard before, including him.
It looks like invention out of nothing. It isn't. Improvisation in jazz is much
closer to fluent speech than to automatic writing. The player has spent years
accumulating vocabulary, internalizing a grammar, and developing a sense of what
a phrase ought to do, and is now composing in real time under the single brutal
constraint that he cannot revise.

Most of what makes that possible is invisible from the audience, because most of
it is agreement. Everyone on the bandstand is holding the same structure in their
heads, counting the same bars, expecting the same harmonic events to arrive at
the same moments. The freedom is real, but it is freedom inside a form that all
four or five people are maintaining simultaneously, without looking at each other
and usually without visible effort.

## The Form Underneath

Nearly all mainstream jazz performance runs on a repeating chorus. A standard
song from the Broadway and Tin Pan Alley repertoire is typically thirty-two bars
long, most often in an AABA shape: an eight-bar phrase stated twice, a contrasting
eight-bar bridge, and a return. The twelve-bar blues is the other backbone, with a
harmonic skeleton so familiar that a band can play one in any key with no
discussion. A third common vehicle is the chord progression of Gershwin's "I Got
Rhythm," reused under so many different melodies that musicians just call the
progression rhythm changes.

The performance format follows from this. The band plays the written melody — the
head — then each soloist takes one or more full choruses over the same harmonic
cycle, then the head returns. A soloist who takes five choruses on a blues is
navigating the same twelve bars five times, and the listener's pleasure comes
partly from hearing the familiar landmarks pass at the expected rate while the
material over them keeps changing. When a soloist gets lost, it is almost always
the form that was lost, not the notes. Musicians talk about "turning the beat
around" the way pilots talk about losing the horizon.

## Harmony as a Set of Expectations

A chord symbol is not an instruction about which notes to play. It is a
description of a region of tension. The most important progression in the idiom
is the ii-V-I: a minor chord, a dominant chord, and a resolution, a three-step
gesture of setup and release that appears constantly in the standard repertoire
and that players can execute in every key without thinking. Learn to hear it and
half the music suddenly has a visible skeleton.

Within a chord, not all notes are equal. The third and the seventh are the notes
that tell you what kind of chord you are hearing, and they are also the notes
that move most efficiently from one chord to the next — the seventh of one chord
often resolves by half step to the third of the next. Improvisers practice these
guide tones as a kind of minimal melody, a thread that makes even a fast
progression singable. Around that thread everything else is coloration: ninths,
elevenths and thirteenths that sound consonant or tense depending on the chord,
and chromatic notes that work if they lead somewhere and sound like mistakes if
they don't.

The bebop players of the 1940s tightened all of this considerably. Charlie
Parker's lines move through chords at a rate that makes eighth notes feel like a
structural unit rather than a subdivision, with chromatic approach notes placed
so that the important chord tones land on strong beats. They also began
substituting chords freely — replacing a dominant chord with the dominant a
tritone away, or inserting extra ii-V pairs into gaps — which increased the
harmonic traffic enormously. John Coltrane pushed that logic to a kind of limit
in the late 1950s with progressions that cycle through keys a major third apart
at high speed, leaving essentially no room for anything but precise navigation.

Modal jazz was the release valve. When Miles Davis's group recorded material
built on one scale held for eight or sixteen bars at a time, the harmonic
pressure dropped to nearly nothing, and the burden shifted. With no chord changes
to articulate, a soloist has to generate motion out of melody, rhythm, register
and sound itself. It turns out to be harder, not easier. Plenty of players who
could run changes cleanly sounded aimless the moment the changes stopped
arriving.

## Vocabulary, and the Question of Licks

Improvisers learn by transcribing. You put on a recording, slow it down, and
write out or memorize somebody else's solo note for note, and then you take it
apart. The phrases you absorb this way enter your playing whether you intend
them to or not. Parker's phrases in particular became common property so
thoroughly that a certain kind of chromatic turnaround is simply part of the
language now, like an idiom whose origin nobody thinks about.

This is the basis of the oldest complaint about jazz soloing: that it is just
licks, prefabricated units shuffled into new orders. The complaint is not
baseless — there are players who sound like a deck of cards being dealt — but it
misunderstands how fluency works. A speaker of a language does not construct
sentences from individual phonemes; whole idiomatic chunks are available and get
deployed, altered, interrupted, and recombined according to the situation. What
separates a strong improviser is not the absence of stored material but the
quality of the judgment applied to it: whether a phrase arrives where it is
needed, whether it answers what came before, whether it is abandoned halfway
through because the drummer did something interesting.

The players who are most admired tend to be the ones who treat a solo as an
argument rather than a sequence. Sonny Rollins is often cited for building long
improvisations out of a single short motif, stretching it, inverting it, and
returning to it so that a chorus has the shape of an essay. Thelonious Monk
improvised in a way that stayed unnervingly close to the tune, poking at the
melody itself rather than abandoning it. Lester Young and Coleman Hawkins, both
working on the same instrument in the same decade, demonstrated two opposite
approaches to phrasing — one light and behind the beat, one vertical and
muscular — and essentially every tenor player since has located themselves
somewhere between them.

## Time Is the Hard Part

Ask working musicians what separates the great from the competent and very few
will say harmony. They will say time. Swing eighth notes are usually described as
a long-short pattern, but the ratio is not fixed: it flattens toward even at fast
tempos and widens at slow ones, and the exact placement is a matter of individual
feel. A soloist can sit slightly ahead of the beat, generating urgency, or lay
back behind it, generating a dragging relaxation, while the bass stays
absolutely steady. The tension between where the pulse is and where the phrase
lands is a large part of what people mean when they say a player swings.

The rhythm section is doing something correspondingly subtle. A walking bass line
supplies both the pulse and the harmony, one note per beat, choosing pitches that
outline the chords and lead smoothly into the next. The drummer keeps the ride
cymbal pattern going and uses the snare and bass drum to comment — accents that
land off the beat, answering the soloist's phrase or provoking the next one. The
pianist comps: chords voiced without their roots, placed in rhythmic gaps, thin
enough to leave room but specific enough to keep the harmony clear. None of this
is accompaniment in the classical sense. It is a continuous stream of proposals.

## The Conversation Is the Point

This is why a great solo cannot really be separated from the band that produced
it. Miles Davis's mid-sixties quintet became famous for a rhythm section that
would deliberately destabilize the form underneath a soloist — shifting the
implied meter, dropping the pulse, changing the harmony out from under a line to
see what happened — and for a group of players who treated that as an invitation
rather than sabotage. Ornette Coleman's bands went further and removed the chord
progression entirely, keeping the melodic logic and the momentum. Even then the
result rarely sounded like everyone doing whatever they wanted; it sounded like
people listening very hard.

There is an older layer under all of it. In early New Orleans ensembles nobody
soloed in the modern sense; cornet, clarinet and trombone improvised
simultaneously, each within a traditional role, weaving three lines at once.
Louis Armstrong is the hinge. When he began taking extended single-voice
choruses in the 1920s, the ensemble reorganized itself around the soloist, and
the whole subsequent shape of the music — the head, the string of solos, the
head — followed from that.

The recording is a strange artifact of the whole business. A studio take fixes
one night's decisions permanently, and generations of students then transcribe
those decisions as if they were a composition, which in a sense they have become.
The player who made them almost certainly never played it that way again.
