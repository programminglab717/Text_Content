# Counterpoint and the Independence of Lines

Anyone who has sung a round in a school hall knows the moment. The first group
is three bars into "Row, Row, Row Your Boat" and the second comes in, and for a
second it feels like a mistake — two things happening at once that ought to be
taking turns. Then it locks. The sound thickens without getting muddy, the
words stay legible, and you can follow your own line while hearing the other one
crossing underneath it. That sensation of two melodies occupying the same
seconds without ruining each other is the whole subject of counterpoint. The
technical apparatus that grew up around it, centuries of rules about what may
move where, exists to protect that one effect.

The word comes from *punctus contra punctum*, note against note. What it
describes is a way of composing in which the horizontal dimension is primary:
you write lines, plural, each of which would be worth singing alone, and you
make them fit. Harmony, in the later sense of chords chosen for their color and
function, is the residue of that fitting. For several hundred years European
musicians thought about music the first way, and it is worth remembering that
the vertical sonorities we now call chords arrived as a byproduct rather than a
starting point.

## From Doubling to Genuine Independence

The earliest documented Western polyphony barely deserves the name. Ninth-
century treatises describe organum, in which a second voice shadows a plainchant
melody a fourth or fifth below, moving in lockstep. Nothing is independent here;
the added voice has no life of its own, and the effect is closer to registration
on an organ than to two people singing. But the shadow voice soon began to
deviate, holding still while the chant moved, or moving opposite to it, and the
moment it did the fundamental problem appeared: what combinations are tolerable
when two lines meet?

By the late twelfth century the composers associated with Notre Dame in Paris
were writing something recognizably contrapuntal. In the works attributed to
Léonin and then Pérotin, the chant is stretched into enormously long held notes
while one, two or three upper voices spin rapid figures above it. The chant
becomes a foundation rather than a tune, and rhythm becomes a compositional
resource, organized into repeating patterns that let several singers coordinate
without a conductor. The fourteenth century pushed structural thinking further:
in the isorhythmic motets of Philippe de Vitry and Guillaume de Machaut, a
rhythmic pattern and a pitch sequence of different lengths cycle against each
other, so that the same rhythm recurs with different notes and the same notes
recur with different rhythms. Nobody could hear this as such. It was an
architectural principle, a way of generating a long piece with internal logic.

What changed in the fifteenth century was the arrival of the third as a stable,
restful sound, which reoriented everything. Dufay, then Ockeghem, then Josquin
built textures in which all voices are equal in dignity and each takes up the
same melodic idea in turn — imitation, the technique that dominates Renaissance
polyphony. A phrase of text gets its own musical motif; the voices enter with it
one by one, overlapping; the overlap thins as the phrase ends and the next point
of imitation begins. Palestrina, working in Rome in the later sixteenth century,
refined this into a style of such evenness and control that it became the model
taught to students for the next three hundred years. The story that his *Missa
Papae Marcelli* single-handedly persuaded the Council of Trent not to ban
polyphony is almost certainly a legend, but it captures something true: his
music answered the complaint that counterpoint buried the words.

## What the Rules Are Actually Protecting

Counterpoint arrives in most people's education as a list of prohibitions. No
parallel fifths. No parallel octaves. Approach a perfect consonance by contrary
motion. Prepare and resolve your dissonances. Do not leap into a dissonance, do
not leap out of one, do not follow a large leap with another leap in the same
direction. Keep the lines within a singable range and give each one a single
high point.

Taken as etiquette, these are arbitrary. Taken as acoustics and perception, they
are close to inevitable. The ear assembles sound into streams, and it uses
whatever cues it can find: pitch proximity, timbre, common onset, common
direction of motion. Two voices singing in parallel octaves supply every cue for
fusion at once. They start together, move together, and stand in the simplest
possible frequency ratio. The ear stops hearing two singers and hears one singer
with a slightly odd tone color. Parallel fifths do nearly the same thing. The
prohibition is not against an ugly sound — parallel fifths are not ugly, and
whole traditions are built on them — but against a sound that destroys the
premise. If you wanted one line, you should have written one.

Everything else follows from the same logic. Contrary motion keeps two voices
perceptually distinct because they are doing opposite things. Rhythmic
differentiation helps more: if one voice moves while the other holds, the ear
has no trouble separating them. Voice crossing is avoided because once two lines
occupy the same pitch region the listener's tracking mechanism hands notes to
the wrong stream. And dissonance is regulated rather than forbidden, because
dissonance is what creates the sense of a line needing somewhere to go. The
suspension — a note held over from the previous chord until it grates, then
falling by step into consonance — is the engine of Renaissance and Baroque
phrase endings, and it works precisely because the friction is prepared,
sustained, and then relieved at a moment the listener can feel coming.

## Species, or How Everyone Learned It

In 1725 Johann Joseph Fux, then at the imperial court in Vienna, published
*Gradus ad Parnassum*, a treatise cast as a dialogue between a student named
Josephus and a master named Aloysius who speaks for Palestrina. Fux's method was
to strip the problem down to a fixed melody, the cantus firmus, and have the
student write a single added line against it under escalating constraints. First
species: one note against each note, consonances only. Second: two notes against
one, which admits passing dissonance on weak beats. Third: four against one.
Fourth: syncopation, which is to say suspensions. Fifth: florid counterpoint,
combining all of the above at the writer's discretion.

The genius of the scheme is that it isolates variables. A student in first
species has nothing to think about but interval choice and melodic shape, and
nowhere to hide. By fifth species the choices have accumulated into something
resembling real music. Haydn worked through the book; Mozart used it to teach;
Beethoven studied it, and then studied more counterpoint with Albrechtsberger
because he suspected, correctly, that he needed it. The exercises describe no
actual historical repertoire — Palestrina did not write first-species
counterpoint, and neither did anyone else — but as a training regime for hearing
two lines at once, nothing better has been invented.

## Fugue, and Afterward

Fugue is what happens when imitation becomes a whole formal principle rather
than a local device. A subject is stated alone; a second voice answers it a fifth
higher, often adjusted slightly so it stays in key, while the first voice
continues with a countersubject; the remaining voices enter in turn until all
have carried the subject. That is the exposition. What follows alternates
episodes, which are usually sequential passages built from fragments of the
material, with further entries of the subject in related keys. A composer with
appetite can invert the subject, stretch it into notes twice as long, compress
it, or overlap its entries so that a new voice begins before the previous one has
finished — stretto, which raises the pressure near the end the way a tightening
rope does.

Bach did not invent any of this and used all of it with a thoroughness nobody
has matched. Offered a theme by Frederick the Great in 1747, he improvised a
three-voice fugue on it at the keyboard and went home to write, among other
things, a six-voice one. *The Art of Fugue*, left unfinished at his death, works
a single subject through canons, inversions, mirror forms and multiple-subject
combinations as though testing how much structure a piece of music can carry.

After Bach, counterpoint stopped being the native idiom of European music and
became a discipline held in reserve — the thing composers reached for when they
wanted density, rigor, or the sense of an argument being driven to its end.
Mozart stacked five themes simultaneously in the finale of the Jupiter symphony.
Beethoven made fugue the vehicle of his late, obsessive manner, in the
*Hammerklavier* and the *Grosse Fuge*, where the rules are audibly straining.
Shostakovich, after judging a Bach anniversary competition in Leipzig, went home
and wrote twenty-four preludes and fugues of his own. And in a different lineage
entirely, the front line of a New Orleans jazz band — cornet stating the tune,
clarinet decorating above it, trombone sliding through the harmony below —
improvises a three-part texture whose logic any Renaissance singer would
recognize: separate registers, separate rhythmic habits, three lines that
stubbornly refuse to become one.

The lasting effect of studying any of this is not the ability to write fugues.
It is that a texture you used to hear as a wash resolves into moving parts, the
way a crowd resolves into individual walkers once you decide to follow one.
