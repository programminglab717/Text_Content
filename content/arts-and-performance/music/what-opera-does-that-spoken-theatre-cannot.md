# What Opera Does That Spoken Theatre Cannot

The standard objection to opera is that nobody sings when their house is on
fire. It is a fair complaint and it misses the point in an interesting way. A
play can show you what a person says. Opera exists because someone wanted a
form that could show you several people's inner weather at the same instant,
and could stop the clock on a single feeling long enough to walk all the way
around it. Both of those are things speech cannot do, and both of them fall out
naturally from the decision to sing.

## A Committee in Florence Tries to Reinvent Greek Tragedy

The form began, unusually for an art, as a scholarly reconstruction that went
wrong in a productive direction. In the last decades of the sixteenth century a
circle of Florentine noblemen, musicians and humanists met at the house of
Giovanni de' Bardi to argue about ancient drama. They had read that Greek
tragedy was sung throughout, and they had persuaded themselves — on the
evidence of the scholar Girolamo Mei's research, which concluded that Greek
music was single-line and text-driven — that the elaborate polyphony of their
own century was a corruption. Vincenzo Galilei, a lutenist and the father of the
astronomer, published a broadside arguing that when four voices sing different
words simultaneously in different rhythms, the listener understands nothing and
feels less.

Their alternative was monody: one voice declaiming over a simple sustained bass,
following the rhythm and contour of impassioned speech. Jacopo Peri built the
first surviving works on this principle around 1600. They are austere, and
mostly of historical interest now, but within a few years Claudio Monteverdi had
seen what the technique could carry. *L'Orfeo*, staged at Mantua in 1607, is
still performed, and it already contains the whole apparatus: declamation that
moves the story, set pieces that stop it, instrumental color used
dramatically, and one central scene — Orpheus singing to persuade a god — in
which the argument of the plot and the act of singing become the same thing.

## Recitative, Aria, and the Two Clocks

Within a generation the form had split itself into two kinds of time, and the
split has never really closed. Recitative runs at roughly the speed of speech.
Its rhythms follow the language, its harmony moves only when the sense moves, and
it is accompanied thinly so the words carry. Information happens here: someone
arrives, a letter is read, a decision is taken.

The aria does the opposite. It suspends the narrative and expands a single state
of mind across a span of time entirely out of proportion to its verbal content.
Eight lines of poetry might occupy six minutes. This is not padding. A soliloquy
in a play has to keep moving or the audience's attention decays; an aria can
circle, repeat, and intensify, because music supplies the forward pull that
language would have to provide on its own. The eighteenth-century da capo aria
made the structure explicit — a first section, a contrasting middle, then the
first section again, returned to with the singer's own ornamentation, so that
the audience hears both the original feeling and the performer's commentary on
it.

Venice industrialized all of this with remarkable speed. The first opera house
open to a paying public opened there in 1637, and within a few decades the city
supported several competing theatres, box-holding families, stage machinery for
descending gods and collapsing palaces, and a repertoire aimed at whoever bought
a ticket. Monteverdi's late Venetian works, including an opera in which the
scheming adulterers win and close the evening with a love duet of extraordinary
tenderness, belong to that commercial world rather than to a court.

## Serious Opera and the Tyranny of the Star

By the early eighteenth century serious opera had ossified into a system.
Libretti by Metastasio, set and reset by dozens of composers, supplied
historical rulers facing conflicts between love and duty. Arias came at the ends
of scenes, and the singer left the stage afterward to collect the applause.
Casts were built around castrati — men castrated as boys to preserve a high
voice, who combined a child's larynx with an adult's lungs and chest, and whose
careers were built on a practice it is hard to write about calmly. Handel in
London worked inside this system and against it, and the surviving accounts of
his companies read like tabloid copy: rival prima donnas, feuds, audiences
taking sides, a business model that collapsed and was rebuilt more than once.

The counterweight came from below. Comic intermezzi, performed between the acts
of serious operas, featured recognizable people rather than emperors, moved
quickly, and used music to characterize rather than to display. When one such
piece was performed in Paris in the early 1750s it set off a full-scale public
argument about the nature of the art — pamphlets, factions, the philosophers
weighing in on the side of the Italians. Gluck's reform operas of the 1760s,
written with the librettist Calzabigi, attacked the same target from the other
direction: no display for its own sake, no da capo repetitions that stalled the
drama, overtures that prepared what followed, music subordinated to the
situation.

## The Ensemble: Several Truths at Once

What Mozart did with the inherited machinery is best seen in the finales he
built with Lorenzo Da Ponte. A play can put six people onstage, but it can only
let them speak one at a time; overlapping dialogue turns to noise within
seconds. Music has no such limit. Different characters can sing different words
simultaneously and stay separately audible, because register, rhythm and melodic
shape keep the lines apart in the listener's ear — the same perceptual trick
that makes counterpoint work.

The second-act finale of *The Marriage of Figaro* exploits this for something
like twenty minutes of continuous music. It starts with two people, adds a
third, then more, each arrival changing the balance of power and the key, none
of the newcomers knowing what the audience knows. By the end there are seven
people onstage holding seven incompatible views of the same situation, and the
audience can hear all of them at once. No spoken scene can produce that
sensation. It is the clearest case of opera doing something that is not merely
a decorated version of theatre but a different capability altogether.

The nineteenth century then found a second thing only opera can do: it gave the
orchestra a mind of its own. Verdi, working across fifty years from patriotic
choruses to the compressed, supple late scores, steadily dissolved the boundary
between recitative and aria until the music ran continuously and the orchestra
commented on the characters rather than accompanying them. Wagner pushed this to
its limit, weaving the whole texture out of recurring motifs attached to people,
objects and ideas, so that the pit can tell you what a character is remembering
while the character says something else entirely. He also built a theatre at
Bayreuth designed around the effect: the orchestra hidden under a hood, the
auditorium dark, no boxes to be seen in, the audience facing forward in rows
with nothing to look at but the stage.

## What the Voice Costs

All of this rests on an unamplified human body filling a large hall over an
orchestra of eighty. The physical solution is a resonance phenomenon: trained
operatic singers cluster several of their vocal resonances into a narrow band a
couple of thousand hertz above the fundamental, producing a peak in the spectrum
where orchestral energy is comparatively low. The voice does not get louder than
the orchestra. It occupies a different part of the sound, and the ear finds it
there. This is why operatic tone sounds the way it does, and why a singer
trained for musical theatre or pop — where a microphone does the work — cannot
simply step into the role.

Building that instrument takes the better part of a decade, and it ages. Voices
are sorted into categories by weight, color and range, and the categories govern
casting with some rigidity, since a light lyric soprano who spends a season
singing dramatic roles may damage herself permanently. Careers are built
cautiously, on repertoire that fits, and the caution is not vanity. There is no
spare instrument.

The twentieth century kept finding new work for all this machinery. Strauss
turned the orchestra loose on biblical and Greek subjects at a level of
harmonic violence that made audiences uncomfortable. Berg set a play about a
poor soldier destroyed by everyone above him, and organized each scene as an
abstract instrumental form nobody in the audience is expected to notice
consciously. Britten wrote about outsiders in provincial communities, Janáček
built melodies out of the inflections of spoken Czech, and Gershwin staged a
street in Charleston. Late in the century composers began setting recent
political events, on the reasonable theory that a form built to show several
people believing incompatible things at once is well suited to history.

The objection about the burning house survives all of this, and the answer is
the same as it was in Florence. Nobody sings while the house burns. But a
person's feelings while the house burns are simultaneous, layered, and mostly
unspoken, and a form that can stack four voices in a single bar can get at that
in a way a line of dialogue never will. The implausibility is on the surface.
Underneath it, opera is the realist form.
