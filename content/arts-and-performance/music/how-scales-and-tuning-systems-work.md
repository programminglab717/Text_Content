# How Scales and Tuning Systems Work

Pluck two guitar strings that are almost but not quite in unison and you hear
something neither string is producing alone: a slow pulsing, a wobble in the
loudness that quickens as you turn the peg the wrong way and slows toward
stillness as the pitches converge. Those beats are the audible difference
between two frequencies. They are also the reason tuning is a solvable problem
at all. The ear is mediocre at judging an interval in the abstract and
astonishingly good at hearing a small frequency difference once it becomes a
rhythm, and every tuning system ever devised amounts to a decision about where
to put the beating and how fast to let it run.

## The Ratios Hiding in a Vibrating String

A stretched string does not vibrate in one way. It swings along its whole
length and, at the same time, in halves, thirds, quarters, and onward until
stiffness and damping snuff out the higher modes. Because those divisions are
whole-number fractions of the length, their frequencies come out as
whole-number multiples of the lowest. This is the harmonic series, and it is
not a cultural convention. It falls out of the physics of nearly any uniform
object vibrating under tension, which is why it turns up in strings, air
columns, and the human vocal folds alike.

Frequency runs inversely to sounding length, so stopping a string at half its
length doubles its frequency. The result resembles the original so closely that
almost every musical culture treats the two pitches as versions of the same
note: the octave, ratio 2:1. Stop at two-thirds and you get 3:2, the perfect
fifth. Three-quarters yields 4:3, the fourth. Four-fifths gives 5:4, the major
third, and five-sixths gives 6:5, the minor third.

Why these particular ratios sound smooth was worked out in the nineteenth
century by Hermann von Helmholtz, and the explanation still holds up. Two notes
whose frequencies form a simple ratio share many upper partials outright. The
third harmonic of the lower note in a 3:2 pair lands exactly on the second
harmonic of the upper one. Nothing is close enough to anything else to beat,
and the pair fuses into a single glassy object. Move one note slightly and those
coincident partials separate by a few hertz and begin to throb. Push further
and the throbbing rises into the range the ear registers as roughness rather
than rhythm. Dissonance, in this account, is not a moral category. It is
interference between overtones.

## The Problem With Stacking Fifths

Give a theorist a pure fifth and a pure octave and the temptation is
irresistible: generate everything from those two. Start on a note, go up a
fifth, up another, keep going, and drop each result down by octaves until it
lands inside the span you started in. After twelve fifths you should arrive
back at your starting note, seven octaves higher. You do not.

Twelve fifths multiply out to roughly 129.75 times the original frequency.
Seven octaves give exactly 128. The gap is about a quarter of a semitone, and
it is not a rounding error you can absorb — it is flatly audible, a sour
wobbling unison. This is the Pythagorean comma, and it exists because powers of
three can never equal powers of two. No amount of cleverness dissolves it. Any
system with a fixed, finite number of notes per octave has to decide what to do
with that leftover quarter-semitone.

There is a second, subtler gap. Stack four fifths and reduce by octaves and you
arrive at a major third of 81:64. The pure major third from the harmonic series
is 5:4, which is 80:64. The discrepancy, 81:80, is the syntonic comma, roughly
a fifth of a semitone. Medieval theory shrugged at this because medieval music
did not treat thirds as consonances — organum was built on octaves, fifths and
fourths, and the tuning that makes those perfect is exactly the one that makes
thirds harsh. But when English and then continental composers of the fifteenth
century began writing full triads as the resting sonority of their music, the
Pythagorean third became intolerable, and the whole edifice needed rebuilding.

## Temperament, or Which Compromise You Prefer

The obvious fix is just intonation: tune everything to pure small-number ratios
and be done. On a single chord it is glorious. Across a piece it collapses. Keep
every interval pure through a simple chord progression that descends by fifths
and rises by fourths, and a choir singing it will land a syntonic comma below
where it started — the so-called comma pump. Repeat the progression and the
music sinks a semitone in four turns. Singers unconsciously correct for this.
Keyboards cannot.

So keyboards get tempered: intervals are deliberately mistuned so the errors
land where they hurt least. Meantone temperament, the standard for most of the
sixteenth through eighteenth centuries, narrows each fifth by about a quarter of
the syntonic comma. Four such fifths then stack up to a perfectly pure major
third. The fifths themselves, five cents flat, beat slowly enough that almost
nobody minds. The cost is that all the accumulated error has to be swept
somewhere, and it piles up in one interval, usually between G-sharp and E-flat,
where the fifth ends up more than a third of a semitone too wide and howls.
Hence its name: the wolf. Organ builders sometimes fought this by splitting the
black keys so a player could choose between G-sharp and A-flat, and a few
instruments went further, with nineteen or thirty-one keys to the octave.

The eighteenth century preferred a different bargain. Well temperaments —
Werckmeister's, Kirnberger's, Vallotti's, and a good many local recipes — spread
the comma unevenly so that no interval is unusable but no two keys sound quite
alike. Keys with few sharps and flats get near-pure thirds and a placid
character; remote keys get wider thirds and a harder edge. The twenty-four
preludes and fugues of Bach's *Well-Tempered Clavier* require a temperament that
circulates through every key, but they do not require an equal one, and the
title's "well" almost certainly meant something more colorful than uniform.

## Twelve Equal Steps and What They Cost

Equal temperament divides the octave into twelve identical ratios, each the
twelfth root of two. The mathematics was understood long before the practice
became universal: the Ming prince and theorist Zhu Zaiyu published the
calculation in the late sixteenth century, and Simon Stevin worked it out
independently in the Netherlands around the same time. Fretted instruments
adopted something close to it early, because a fret is a bar across all the
strings and cannot be in one key and out of another.

The units used to discuss any of this are cents, a logarithmic measure
introduced by the nineteenth-century English scholar Alexander Ellis: twelve
hundred to the octave, a hundred to the equal-tempered semitone. In those terms
the equal-tempered fifth is about two cents narrow, which in the middle of the
keyboard produces a beat roughly once a second — inaudible unless you are
listening for it. The equal-tempered major third is about fourteen cents sharp,
which beats several times a second and shimmers noticeably. Western ears have
been trained since childhood to accept this shimmer as the ordinary sound of a
major chord, to the point that a genuinely pure third can sound flat and
strange on first hearing.

Pianos add a further wrinkle. Real steel strings are stiff, and stiffness pushes
their upper partials slightly sharp of true harmonics. A tuner who sets octaves
to an exact 2:1 will find the instrument sounding dull and out of tune with
itself, so octaves are stretched — widened progressively toward both ends of the
keyboard, until the top notes sit perhaps thirty cents above their nominal
frequencies and the bottom ones below. No two pianos want exactly the same
stretch.

## Scales That Were Never Built From Fifths

All of this describes one tradition's solution to one tradition's problem. A
Javanese or Balinese gamelan is tuned to itself and to nothing else. The
five-note slendro and seven-note pelog systems do not correspond to Western
intervals, and instruments from one gamelan cannot be played with those of
another, because each set is tuned as a single artifact. Paired instruments are
deliberately detuned by a few hertz so that sustained tones beat against each
other, producing the shimmering pulse called ombak — the very phenomenon a piano
tuner works to eliminate, here recruited as an aesthetic.

Indian classical music anchors everything to a drone, which makes pure ratios
easy to hear and easy to sing, and the theoretical division of the octave into
twenty-two shrutis marks out the fine distinctions performers actually use.
Arabic and Turkish maqam traditions include neutral intervals that fall between
the Western major and minor third; the common notation of them as quarter tones
is a convenience rather than an accurate description of what players do. Thai
classical tuning approximates seven roughly equal steps, which produces an
entirely different map of consonance.

Even inside Western practice, fixed tuning is the exception. Singers, string
players and trombonists adjust constantly and by instinct, raising leading tones
for melodic urgency and lowering the thirds of sustained chords toward purity.
A good string quartet holding a final chord is not playing equal temperament.
A barbershop quartet chasing that ringing, over-loud sensation of a locked
seventh chord is chasing exact small-number ratios and can feel the moment the
beats vanish. Put either of them next to a piano and the piano will be the one
that is wrong, and nothing can be done about it, because the compromise was
frozen into the instrument the last time somebody with a tuning lever decided
where to hide the comma.
