# The Regional Logic of Chinese Cooking

Outside Chengdu, in a district called Pixian, broad beans ferment in open clay
jars for years at a time. Salt, chiles, sun, and someone with a wooden paddle
who turns the mash every few days so the top does not crust over. What
eventually comes out is dark red, thick, and smells like soy sauce crossed with
dried fruit. That paste, doubanjiang, is the reason a Sichuanese braise tastes
the way it does, and nothing remotely like it is made in Guangzhou, eight
hundred miles south, where a cook judging a steamed fish would consider its
presence an act of vandalism.

This is the first thing to understand about Chinese cooking: the phrase
describes a landmass roughly the size of Europe, with climates running from
subarctic forest to monsoon coast to high desert, and a written culinary record
going back well over two thousand years. Restaurant menus abroad flatten all of
that into a single category. Inside China, nobody does.

The conventional shorthand is the eight culinary traditions — Shandong,
Sichuan, Cantonese, Jiangsu, Fujian, Zhejiang, Hunan, Anhui. The list as usually
recited is a modern convenience, part taxonomy and part regional advertising,
and it leaves out enormous territories: the wheat-and-lamb northwest, the sour
fermentations of Guizhou, the mountain cooking of Yunnan with its mushrooms and
its Southeast Asian borrowings. Still, the list points at something real. Cooks
in different parts of China work from different pantries, different fuels,
different fish, and different assumptions about what a good dish is supposed to
do to your mouth.

## Grain First, Everything Else Second

A Chinese meal is organized around a distinction that English has no clean word
for. There is fan — the grain, the starch, the substance — and there is cai,
everything served alongside it. The cai exists to make the fan go down. This
sounds like a technicality until you watch it operate. It explains why dishes
are salty and intensely flavored relative to their portion size, why they are
shared from the center of the table rather than plated individually, and why a
banquet of twenty dishes still ends with a bowl of noodles or rice, at which
point a guest who declines is signaling that the host has fed them well.

Which grain depends on rainfall. The rough boundary runs along the Qinling
Mountains and the Huai River, and north of it the staple is wheat. That single
agricultural fact produces an entire cuisine of dough: noodles pulled by hand
from a single rope of gluten, doubled and redoubled until a cook is holding
hundreds of strands; steamed buns; pan-fried breads layered with scallion and
rendered fat; dumplings pleated by the hundred on the eve of the lunar new year.
South of the line, rice. Steamed as long grains, simmered into congee so soft it
barely needs chewing, pounded into cakes, pressed into wide flat noodles,
fermented into wine and vinegar.

## Shandong, and the Weight of the Northern Stock

Shandong cooking, called Lu after the ancient state there, supplied much of the
technical vocabulary of northern restaurant food, including a great deal of what
ended up in imperial kitchens in Beijing. Its signatures are stock and speed.
Serious Lu kitchens keep two stocks: a clear one, clarified by dropping in
minced chicken that gathers the suspended particles and is then lifted out, and
a milky one, made by boiling bones hard enough to emulsify the fat. Both are
seasoning rather than soup. A plate of sea cucumber or a braised cabbage heart
tastes of stock, scallion, and not much else.

The aromatics are also northern: scallion and garlic in quantity, ginger in
support, and vinegar from Shanxi, aged in earthenware until it goes almost
black and picks up a smoky edge. Sichuan pepper appears but rarely dominates.
Technique runs hot. Bao — the character suggests an explosion — means cooking
a small quantity of already-tender ingredient in a screaming wok for under a
minute, so that the exterior seizes before the interior can weep.

Further northwest, the cooking shifts again as Muslim communities and the herding
economy take over. Lamb replaces pork almost entirely. Cumin, dried chile, and
charcoal become the dominant flavors. In Xinjiang a meal might be hand-pulled
noodles under a stew of lamb, tomato, and peppers, eaten with flatbread baked
against the wall of a clay oven — food with obvious relatives in Central Asia
and none at all in Guangzhou.

## Jiangnan Sweetness and the Red Braise

The lower Yangtze — Suzhou, Hangzhou, Yangzhou, Shanghai and the countryside
between them — is water country. Canals, lakes, paddies, and the resulting
obsession with freshwater fish, river shrimp, eel, and the hairy crabs that
arrive for a few weeks in autumn and are steamed whole with nothing but a dip of
vinegar and ginger to cut their richness.

Two habits mark the region. One is knife work of almost absurd refinement: the
Huaiyang tradition prizes a cook who can shave a block of firm tofu into
thousands of threads that bloom apart in a bowl of broth. The other is sugar.
Northerners joke about it, southerners defend it, and both are describing the
red braise — soy sauce, rice wine, rock sugar, sometimes a stick of cassia and
a star anise, simmered around pork belly for hours until the sauce reduces to a
lacquer and the fat gives up entirely. Shaoxing wine, amber and slightly sherried,
does much of the aromatic work in this kitchen, as does the practice of letting
sweetness sit right at the edge of savory rather than sequestering it in dessert.

## Sichuan, Hunan, and the Misunderstanding About Heat

Outsiders reduce Sichuan to chile burn, which is a bit like reducing Burgundy to
alcohol. The chile itself is a recent arrival: capsicums crossed the Pacific
from the Americas by sea and reached Chinese ports late in the Ming dynasty,
then took another century or more to migrate inland and become indispensable in
the humid Sichuan basin. What was already there was the native prickly ash,
huajiao, whose husks contain compounds that do not burn but buzz — a fizzing
numbness on the lips that makes the tongue more receptive to everything else.
The pairing of that numbness with chile heat is mala, and it is one flavor among
many.

Sichuanese cooks recite a principle about a hundred dishes carrying a hundred
flavors, and the repertoire bears it out. Fish-fragrant sauce contains no fish;
it borrows the pickled chiles, ginger, garlic, scallion, sugar and vinegar that
were traditionally used to cook fish and applies them to pork or eggplant.
Strange-flavor sauce stacks sesame paste, soy, sugar, vinegar, chile oil and
huajiao so that no single element wins. Beneath all of it sits a preserved-goods
pantry that the climate makes necessary — the fermented bean paste of Pixian,
salted mustard tubers, sun-dried mustard greens, chile-brined vegetables.

Hunan burns differently. Less numbing, more salt, more sourness from pickled
chiles, and a strong current of smoke: bacon and fish hung in the rafters above
a wood fire through the winter, then steamed with fresh chiles in spring. Guizhou
goes further into sourness, souring soups with fermented tomato and rice water
in a way that owes nothing to vinegar.

## The Cantonese Standard

Cantonese cooking runs on an assumption that would be heresy in Chongqing: that
seasoning should reveal the ingredient rather than reconstruct it. A fish is
steamed whole over ginger and scallion, doused with hot oil and a little soy at
the table, and judged almost entirely on whether it was alive an hour earlier.
Stock is made and kept — the "superior stock" of old kitchens, built from
chicken, pork and cured ham — and used where northerners would use sauce.
Vegetables are blanched and dressed. Sugar appears, but in small corrections.

The region also produced the most physical technique in Chinese cooking: wok
hei, the breath of the wok. A carbon steel pan over a ferocious burner, food kept
in motion and briefly airborne, oil and moisture flaring at the metal surface.
The result is a scorched, almost mineral aroma that cannot be produced at
domestic heat and disappears within minutes of leaving the pan.

Guangdong is not a single kitchen either. Chaozhou cooks use fish sauce, braise
goose in aromatic master stock, and serve rice porridge with a dozen small salty
things. Hakka cooking, shaped by centuries of migration, leans on preserved
mustard greens, salt-baked chicken, and stuffed bean curd — dumpling logic
carried south by people who no longer had the wheat for wrappers. Fujian next
door builds nearly everything on soup and uses the red lees left over from rice
wine as a marinade.

It was Cantonese speakers, and particularly emigrants from a handful of counties
in the Pearl River Delta, who carried Chinese cooking to North America,
Australia and Southeast Asia in the nineteenth century. For a long stretch, what
the rest of the world called Chinese food was their dialect of it, adapted to
foreign produce and foreign palates. The Sichuan and Hunan restaurants that
opened later abroad arrived as a correction, and now compete inside China too:
Chengdu-style hot pot has colonized Guangzhou, and cooks there complain about
it in the way Bolognese cooks complain about pizza.
