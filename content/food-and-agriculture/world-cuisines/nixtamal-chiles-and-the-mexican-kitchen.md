# Nixtamal, Chiles, and the Mexican Kitchen

Put dried field corn in a pot with water and a spoonful of slaked lime, bring it
to a simmer, cut the heat, and leave it overnight. In the morning the water will
be yellow and slippery, the kernels will have swollen, and their skins will slip
off under a thumbnail. The smell coming off the pot is unmistakable and hard to
describe — faintly mineral, faintly sweet, the smell of a tortilleria. What has
happened in that pot is the single most consequential piece of food chemistry
worked out in the Americas, and every serious conversation about Mexican cooking
starts there.

## What the Lime Does

Maize on its own is a mediocre staple. Its niacin is chemically bound in a form
human digestion cannot release, and its protein is short on the amino acids
lysine and tryptophan. Cooking it in an alkaline solution — lime, or wood ash,
which works the same way — changes all of that. The bound niacin comes free. The
amino acid balance improves. Calcium from the lime is absorbed into the kernel,
which is why tortillas are a meaningful source of it. The tough outer skin
loosens and can be rinsed away, and the starches and proteins inside partially
gelatinize so that the ground kernels will bind into a dough that holds together
without any added gluten.

That last point is the practical one. Cornmeal and water make a crumbling paste.
Nixtamalized maize, ground wet, makes masa: cohesive, elastic enough to press
into a disc, strong enough to hold fillings. Everything built on masa — the
tortilla, the tamal, the thick sopes and huaraches, the little boat-shaped
tlacoyos stuffed with beans — depends on that transformation.

The tamal is the older application and the more demanding one. Masa is beaten
with lard or vegetable fat until it lightens in color and a pinch of it floats
when dropped in water, which is the cook's test that enough air has been worked
in for the steamed dough to come out tender rather than dense. It is then
spread on a corn husk or a banana leaf, filled, folded, and steamed in a stacked
pot for an hour or more. Because a batch takes a morning and produces dozens,
tamal-making has stayed communal and tied to occasions — saints' days,
Christmas Eve, the early February feast that follows the finding of a figurine
baked into a ring of bread.

The nutritional half of it had civilizational consequences. Mesoamerican
societies ran on maize for thousands of years without the deficiency disease
pellagra. When maize spread to Europe, Africa and the American South, the grain
traveled and the technique did not, and populations that adopted maize as a
staple without the lime treatment developed pellagra in large numbers. Nobody in
those places asked why the people who had bred the plant always cooked it with
ashes.

Grinding used to mean a metate, a sloping stone slab worked with a stone roller,
and hours of labor daily, almost always by women. Mechanical mills changed that
in the twentieth century, and dehydrated masa flour changed it again, trading a
great deal of flavor for convenience. The difference between a tortilla made
from fresh-ground masa and one made from reconstituted powder is not subtle, and
the current revival of small-batch nixtamal in Mexican and American kitchens is
mostly an argument about that gap.

A tortilla cooked on a hot comal should be turned three times. The first turn is
brief, the second sets the second face, and on the third the disc inflates — a
pocket of steam forcing the two surfaces apart along the seam where the starch
gelatinized unevenly. Cooks read the puff as proof that the masa was hydrated
correctly and the griddle was hot enough.

## Three Plants That Farm Together

Maize did not travel alone. The milpa is a planting system in which maize, beans
and squash share a field, and the three cover each other's weaknesses with a
tidiness that looks almost designed. The maize stalk gives the bean vine
something to climb. The bean, through the bacteria in its root nodules, fixes
nitrogen that the heavy-feeding maize would otherwise strip from the soil. The
squash sprawls across the ground, shading out weeds and slowing evaporation, its
prickly leaves discouraging animals.

The nutritional interlock is just as neat. Maize is low in lysine and beans are
rich in it; beans are low in the sulfur-containing amino acids and maize
supplies them. Eaten together, they approximate a complete protein. Add the
squash and its seeds for fat, the chiles and tomatoes and tomatillos grown at
the edges, the wild greens called quelites that volunteer among the rows, and
the field is not a crop but a diet.

Beans get their own pot and their own liturgy. Dried beans simmered slowly with
onion and a branch of the pungent herb epazote, which is credited locally with
making them easier to digest, produce both the beans and a dark broth that is
served as a soup in its own right. Mashed into hot lard in a wide pan and cooked
down, the same beans become the thick paste that lines a sope or fills a
breakfast plate. Black in the south and southeast, pinto in the north — a
regional split as reliable as any border on a map.

## Chiles Are a Vocabulary, Not a Volume Knob

The most common mistake outsiders make is to treat chiles as a single ingredient
with a dial marked heat. In a Mexican kitchen they function more like the wine,
stock and aromatic vegetables of a French one: they supply body, acidity,
sweetness, color and depth, and the burn is a side effect.

Fresh and dried are effectively different ingredients. Drying is not
preservation alone; it is a transformation that concentrates sugars and creates
whole new aromatic compounds, which is why the dried versions get their own
names. A ripened poblano dried becomes an ancho, broad and soft, tasting of
raisin and mild coffee. A chilaca becomes a pasilla, darker and more herbal. A
mirasol becomes a guajillo, bright and tannic with a green-tea edge. A jalapeño
smoked over wood becomes a chipotle. Working with them follows a sequence: a few
seconds per side on a dry griddle, enough to release the aroma and never enough
to scorch, since a burnt chile turns bitter and cannot be rescued; a soak in hot
water until pliable; then blending, usually with some of the soaking liquid,
garlic, and whatever else the sauce calls for.

Then comes the step that separates a sauce from a purée. The blended mixture is
poured into hot fat — traditionally lard — where it hisses, spits, darkens and
tightens. Cooks call it frying the sauce or seasoning it. Frying drives off the
raw, vegetal taste of the blender, concentrates the liquid, and dissolves the
fat-soluble aromatic compounds into the lard so they distribute through the
whole dish. The sauce is finished when it has thickened and a slick of colored
fat separates at the edge of the pan.

## The Comal and the Molcajete

Dry heat before wet heat is a general habit in this kitchen. Tomatoes,
tomatillos, unpeeled garlic cloves, thick slices of white onion and fresh chiles
go onto the comal and stay there until they blister and blacken in patches,
collapsing and weeping juice. Blended afterward, they make a roasted salsa that
is smoky and sweet and completely unlike the same ingredients used raw, which
give the sharp, grassy salsa that goes on a fish taco. Neither is more correct;
they are two different tools.

Texture is its own decision. A blender cuts and aerates, producing something
smooth and slightly foamy. A molcajete — a basalt mortar, porous and heavy —
crushes and tears, leaving seeds and skins in identifiable fragments and pulling
out a thicker, more clinging consistency. The stone itself matters enough that
new ones are seasoned by grinding rice through them until the grit stops coming
off.

## Moles and the Long Layering

Mole is not a sauce so much as a construction method, and there are many of
them: black, red, yellow, green, almond-thickened, pumpkin-seed-thickened. What
they have in common is a long build. Several chile varieties, each toasted
separately because each burns at a different rate. A thickener drawn from the
pantry rather than from flour: stale bread, a fried tortilla, almonds, peanuts,
sesame, pumpkin seeds. Aromatics — onion, garlic, sometimes fried plantain or
raisin for sweetness. A small quantity of spice, some of it Mesoamerican like
allspice, some arrived from elsewhere like cinnamon and clove. Every component
is fried or toasted on its own, then everything is ground together and simmered
for hours, thinned with stock, and adjusted.

Chocolate appears in a few moles, including the famous Pueblan one, but as a
bitter, rounding element in modest quantity. Describing mole as chocolate sauce
is a category error of the same order as describing a Bolognese as tomato jam.

Underneath the whole national repertoire, regions diverge hard. The Yucatán
Peninsula, isolated by geography and closer to the Caribbean than to central
Mexico, builds on achiote paste ground with sour orange, cooks pork wrapped in
banana leaves in an earth oven, and uses habanero rather than the chiles of the
highlands. Oaxaca is mole country, with its own maize varieties, its
string-pulled cheese and its toasted grasshoppers. The north, where wheat and
cattle arrived with the Spanish and the land suits both, makes flour tortillas
and grills beef over mesquite. The coasts cure raw fish in lime.

That Spanish layer is real and thorough. Pork and lard brought frying into a
cuisine that had mostly steamed, boiled and griddled. Cattle brought beef and
dairy, onions and garlic joined the aromatic base, and the galleons running
between Acapulco and Manila added rice and Asian spices to the exchange. But
nothing displaced the foundation. A Mexican kitchen still organizes itself
around a pot of nixtamal, a comal over the flame, a stack of dried chiles, and
a salsa that gets made fresh because it is not supposed to keep.
