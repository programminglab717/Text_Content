# The Craft of Spice in Indian Cooking

A tablespoon of oil goes into a small steel pan over a high flame. When it
shimmers, a spoonful of black mustard seeds goes in, and for two or three
seconds nothing happens. Then the first seed cracks, and within a moment the
whole spoonful is jumping and rattling against the lid. That sound is a timer.
Pull the pan too early and the seeds taste raw and bitter; leave it fifteen
seconds too long and they taste burnt, which is a different and worse kind of
bitter. The window is narrow, no cook measures it, and every kitchen that does
this does it several times a day.

The operation has many names — tadka in the north, chhonk in Hindi-speaking
kitchens, baghar in Urdu, phoron in Bengali, oggarane in Kannada, thalippu in
Tamil — and it is the hinge on which most Indian cooking turns. Understanding
why it works explains more about the cuisine than any list of ingredients could.

## Why the Fat Comes First

The compounds that make spices smell like themselves are mostly not water
soluble. Boil cumin in water and you extract a fraction of what is there; the
rest stays locked in the seed or evaporates unused. Heat those same seeds in
fat, and the fat pulls the aromatic compounds out, holds them, and then
distributes them through the whole dish, coating every grain of rice and every
lentil. Fat is the solvent and the delivery system at once.

Heat also changes the compounds rather than merely releasing them. Sugars and
amino acids in a spice brown and produce new aromas that did not exist in the
raw seed, which is why dry-roasting coriander before grinding it yields
something nutty and warm rather than citrusy and sharp. The same seed is two
ingredients depending on how it was treated.

Sequence follows from these facts. Whole spices that need to pop go in first,
because they take the most heat. Cumin follows a few seconds later, since it
scorches faster. Dried chiles and fresh curry leaves go in near the end; the
leaves spit violently because of their water content and perfume the oil almost
instantly. Asafoetida, a resin with an aggressive sulfurous smell raw and a
savory allium-like one cooked, goes in last and stays for a heartbeat, because
it burns in seconds. Ground spices, if they are used in the tempering at all,
come in with a splash of water or with the onions, because dry powder in hot oil
has almost no thermal buffer and turns acrid immediately.

The same tempering can be deployed at either end of a dish. Built at the start,
it becomes the flavor base that everything else cooks into. Poured over the top
of a finished pot of dal, the identical spoonful of spices arrives loud and
distinct, hitting the nose before the spoon gets near the mouth. Cooks choose
between these deliberately, and some dishes get both.

## The Long Base and the Late Finish

Between the tempering and the finish sits the least glamorous and most decisive
stage: bhuna, the patient cooking of onion, ginger and garlic in fat until the
onion goes from white to gold to brown and its water is gone. Rushing this is
the commonest reason a home curry tastes thin. The visual cue for doneness is
that the fat separates and pools at the edges of the mass, which tells the cook
that the water has cooked off and the emulsion has broken — the point at which
the ground spices can be added and fried for a minute without stewing.

At the other end sits garam masala, which is almost always a finishing blend
rather than a base one. Its components — green cardamom, cinnamon or cassia,
clove, black pepper, mace, sometimes black cardamom for smoke — carry volatile
top notes that boil away under long cooking. Added in the last minutes, or
sprinkled over the surface off the heat, they stay bright. A cook who dumps
garam masala in at the beginning has essentially paid for perfume and then
opened the windows.

## Blends as Regional Grammar

There is no such thing as curry powder in an Indian kitchen in the sense that a
supermarket jar implies. There are dozens of regional blends, each with a
specific job, and most are ground in small quantities because ground spice goes
flat within weeks.

Bengal uses panch phoron, five seeds left whole and unground: fenugreek, nigella,
cumin, black mustard and fennel, thrown into mustard oil so pungent that it is
often heated to smoking first to tame its bite. Maharashtra has goda masala,
built on toasted coconut and sesame with a dark, almost sweet profile. Tamil
kitchens keep separate powders for sambar and for rasam, both of which include
toasted lentils ground in with the spices, so the blend thickens as well as
flavors. Chettinad cooking leans on stone flower lichen, star anise and a heavy
hand with black pepper. Kashmiri Pandit cooking uses fennel and dried ginger
powder heavily and omits onion and garlic entirely, which gives its dishes a
recognizably clean, high-toned quality. Goa absorbed vinegar from the
Portuguese, and its red masalas are ground wet with it rather than dry.

Wet and dry grinding are different techniques with different results. A wet
masala — coconut, chiles, tamarind, ginger and fresh herbs ground on a stone or
in a blender with water — cooks into a sauce and tastes green and immediate. A
dry masala perfumes. Coastal southern cooking relies heavily on the first; the
northern plains on the second.

## Sour, Salt, and Balance

What outsiders read as heat is usually a much more careful balance in which sour
does most of the structural work. The souring agents are regional and not
interchangeable. Tamarind, fruity and deep, dominates the south and gives rasam
and sambar their backbone. Kokum, a dark dried fruit rind from the western
coast, is more astringent and tinted pink. Dried unripe mango powder, amchur,
sours northern dishes without adding liquid. Pomegranate seeds, lime, yogurt,
and — since its arrival from the Americas — tomato all do related jobs.
Jaggery, unrefined cane sugar with molasses still in it, is used in small
quantities to round the sour rather than to sweeten.

The classical framework behind this is the six-taste scheme of Ayurvedic
thinking: sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent, with the idea that
a complete meal should present all six. Bitter is the one that a Western palate
tends to find strange, and it is deliberate — bitter gourd, fenugreek leaves, a
few fried neem leaves. The same tradition sorts foods into heating and cooling,
which shapes real kitchen habits regardless of what anyone thinks of the
underlying medicine: yogurt and cucumber in high summer, more ghee and dried
ginger in winter.

## What Arrived, and When

A surprising amount of what looks ancient is not. Black pepper and long pepper
are genuinely native to the subcontinent, along with cardamom, turmeric and
ginger, and they were traded west for millennia — the Roman appetite for
Malabar pepper moved real quantities of gold. The chile, however, is a New World
plant. It reached the western coast with Portuguese traders in the sixteenth
century and spread with astonishing speed, displacing long pepper as the default
source of pungency in much of the country and becoming so thoroughly domestic
that whole regional identities now rest on particular varieties: the mild,
intensely red Kashmiri chile used for color, the fierce small ones of Andhra,
the bhut jolokia of the northeast.

The potato, the tomato, the cashew, the peanut and the papaya arrived on the
same ships. Asafoetida comes from Iran and Afghanistan and has never been grown
in India in quantity. Cumin and coriander came from West Asia long ago. Saffron
grows in Kashmir. Cooking fats map their own geography: mustard oil in the east
and north, coconut oil down the southwestern coast, sesame in Tamil country,
ghee everywhere it can be afforded, and increasingly neutral refined oils
everywhere else.

Techniques travel too, and some of the most recognizable came from outside. The
tandoor, a clay pit oven fired to temperatures that cook a flatbread in under a
minute, belongs to a Central Asian and Persian lineage that entered through the
northwest. Dum cooking — sealing a pot with dough and letting the contents
finish in their own trapped steam — arrived with Mughal court kitchens. Both are
now so embedded that arguing about their origins feels pedantic, which is
roughly what happens to every borrowed technique given four or five hundred
years.

None of this is written down in most of the kitchens where it happens. A cook
judges the onion by color, the oil by shimmer, the mustard seeds by sound, the
masala by smell at arm's length, and the finished dish by whether the fat has
come to the surface. The measurements come later, in cookbooks, for people who
learned somewhere else.
