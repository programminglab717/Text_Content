# The Layered Kitchens of Peru

A proper cevichería in Lima opens late in the morning and is shut by mid
afternoon. The hours are not a lifestyle choice. The fish came ashore at dawn,
went to market before seven, and will be at its best for a few hours only; a
cevichería serving dinner is announcing that it is working with yesterday's
catch. So the city eats its most famous dish at eleven or noon, standing or
sitting at plastic tables, with a cold beer, and by four the chairs are stacked.

That schedule is the oldest thing about Peruvian cooking and the newest at once.
The sea has always dictated the coast's timetable. Almost everything else on the
plate arrived later, in waves, from four continents, and was absorbed with a
thoroughness that makes the seams hard to find.

## A Cold Sea and a Vertical Country

Peru sits at the tropics and has a cold ocean, which is the fact that explains
most of the rest. The current running north along the coast carries chilled
water up from the far south, and steady offshore winds drag surface water away
from the shore so that deeper water rises to replace it. That deep water is
loaded with nitrate and phosphate, and when it reaches sunlight it detonates into
plankton. The plankton feeds anchoveta in quantities that have at times made this
one of the largest fisheries on earth by weight, and the anchoveta feed
everything else — bonito, corvina, lenguado, chita, cojinova, along with the
seabirds whose droppings piled up on offshore islands into the guano deposits
that briefly made Peru rich.

The same cold water also chills the air above it, suppresses rainfall, and helps
produce a coastal strip that is effectively desert — a thousand miles of sand
interrupted by green river valleys running down from the mountains. Twenty miles
inland the land begins climbing, and it climbs hard. Within a day's travel you
can pass from sea level through irrigated valleys, temperate slopes, cold high
grassland at four thousand metres, and over the divide into Amazon rainforest.
That vertical compression is why a single country grows tropical fruit, cold-
tolerant tubers and everything in between, and why its cooking never settled into
one idiom.

## What Was Already Growing

The potato was domesticated in the high Andes, and the supermarket notion of a
potato — brown, oval, one of four or five types — is a drastic reduction of what
exists there. Peruvian markets sell dozens of varieties in a single stall: waxy
yellow ones that collapse into a purée, purple ones, spiralled knobby ones with
deep eyes, papa seca that has been cooked and dried. Alongside them sit other
Andean tubers that never travelled — oca, sweet and slightly tart; olluco, small
and crunchy and pink-blotched; mashua, pungent enough that it was traditionally
fed to armies for reasons people still joke about.

Grain is quinoa and kiwicha, both of them seeds rather than true cereals. Maize
grows in shapes the rest of the world does not see, including the giant white
kernels of the Cusco valley eaten as a snack and the deep purple cobs boiled with
cinnamon and clove into chicha morada. And the chilies — ají — are the seasoning
spine. Ají amarillo, which ripens orange despite its name, is fruity and moderate
and turns everything it touches the colour of egg yolk. Ají panca is dried, dark
red, nearly sweet, with almost no heat. Ají limo is sharp and floral and belongs
in ceviche. Rocoto, thick-walled and apple-shaped with black seeds, comes from a
separate species adapted to altitude, and is hot enough that in Arequipa they
stuff and bake it as a whole dish.

The Andes also solved preservation early and ingeniously. Chuño is potato left
out on the altiplano where nights fall below freezing and days are sunny and
thin-aired: the tubers freeze at night, are trodden underfoot to press out
moisture, and dry in the sun, cycle after cycle, until they are light, hard and
storable for years. Meat was dried into ch'arki, a Quechua word that entered
English as jerky. And food was cooked without pots at all in the pachamanca — a
pit of stones heated in a fire, layered with meat, tubers and herbs, buried, and
opened hours later.

## Lime, and the Spanish Century

The Spanish brought onions, garlic, wheat, rice, sugar, dairy, cattle, pigs,
chickens — and citrus. The small, hard, fiercely acidic limes now grown in the
north are the ingredient that made ceviche what it is. Before them, coastal
cooking almost certainly soured raw fish with fermented chicha or with acidic
fruits like tumbo, which produces something related but not the same. Lime is
sharper, faster and cleaner.

What the acid does is denature the proteins in the fish: it unfolds the long
molecules so they tangle and clump, which turns the flesh opaque and firms its
texture, much as heat would. It is not cooking in any other sense — it does not
sterilize, which is why fish quality is not negotiable. For decades the standard
was to leave fish in lime for hours, until it was firm right through. The modern
Lima version is a matter of minutes, sometimes seconds, so the centre stays
translucent and tasting of fish rather than of citrus, and that shortening is the
clearest fingerprint of Japanese influence on the coast.

The liquid left in the bowl — lime, fish juices, ají, onion, sometimes a little
blended fish and garlic — is leche de tigre, and it is not discarded. It is
strained into a glass and drunk, sharp and cold and faintly salty, either as part
of the meal or the morning after a long night, for which it has a reputation.

## Four Diasporas in One Kitchen

Enslaved Africans brought to the coastal haciendas cooked with what the household
did not want, which in a cattle economy meant offal, and that legacy is now
sold on street corners as a delicacy. Anticuchos are beef hearts, marinated in
ají panca, vinegar, cumin and garlic, skewered and grilled over coals until the
edges char. Cau cau is tripe stewed yellow with ají amarillo, potato and mint.
Rachi, sangrecita, chanfainita — a whole register of the national menu descends
from that kitchen.

From the middle of the nineteenth century, after the abolition of slavery, Peru
imported indentured Chinese labourers by the tens of thousands to work coastal
plantations and the guano islands. Those who survived their contracts drifted
into Lima and opened small eating houses, and the food they cooked became chifa
— a word taken from a Cantonese phrase for eating rice. Chifa is not Chinese food
in Peru; it is a Peruvian cuisine with Chinese grammar, and it handed the whole
country the wok, soy sauce, ginger and the habit of high, fast heat. Lomo saltado
is the emblem: strips of beef seared hard in a scorching wok with red onion and
tomato, splashed with soy sauce and vinegar so the pan steams, and served with
both rice and fried potatoes, because nobody was willing to choose.

Japanese immigrants began arriving at the end of the nineteenth century, also for
plantation work, and also moved into the food trade. Their descendants built what
is now called nikkei cooking: Peruvian ingredients handled with Japanese
technique and restraint. The tiradito is the pure case — fish sliced like sashimi
rather than cubed, laid in a fan, dressed at the last moment with a lime-and-ají
sauce, and served without the raw onion that ceviche always carries. Chefs who
later became famous elsewhere passed through this world; the young Nobu Matsuhisa
spent formative time cooking in Lima before he was known anywhere. Italian
immigration left its own mark too, in the tallarines and the minestrón that
Peruvians make with huacatay stirred through it.

## The Sauces That Hold It Together

A pattern repeats across the criollo repertoire: a chili, an emulsion, and a
potato underneath. Papa a la huancaína is boiled yellow potato under a pourable
sauce of ají amarillo blended with fresh cheese and evaporated milk. Ocopa, from
Arequipa, is the same idea rebuilt around huacatay — the pungent black mint that
tastes like nothing else — with peanuts and sometimes a little dried shrimp.
Causa inverts the structure: yellow potato mashed with ají amarillo, lime and
oil until it is dense and bright, then layered like a terrine around chicken,
tuna or crab.

The herbs are as distinctive as the chilies and far less exported. Huacatay,
muña from the high sierra, chincho, paico. They are the reason a competent copy
of a Peruvian dish made abroad with substitutions never quite lands.

## The Boom, and Where It Went Next

For most of the twentieth century this was food Peruvians ate and few others
thought about. That changed quickly, from the 1990s onward, and it changed partly
because a generation of chefs decided to treat the domestic repertoire as
material worth serious work rather than something to escape from. Gastón Acurio
became the public face of it, arguing relentlessly that Peruvian cooking was an
export and a source of national confidence, opening restaurants abroad and
backing cooking schools aimed at students who could never have paid for training.
An enormous annual food fair in Lima ran for about a decade and drew crowds in
the hundreds of thousands, putting village cooks, market vendors and famous
chefs in the same fairground.

The interesting part of what followed was directional. Rather than pushing
outward into international refinement, the most ambitious kitchens pushed inland
and upward — organizing menus by altitude and ecosystem, working with growers in
the sierra, and pulling in Amazonian ingredients that had never reached Lima in
quantity: paiche, the enormous river fish; camu camu, a sour fruit with an
extraordinary vitamin C load; cocona, aguaje, sacha inchi. A country that spent
four centuries absorbing other people's ingredients turned around and started
absorbing its own.
