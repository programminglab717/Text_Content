# The Long Afternoon of the Mezze Table

The arak goes in first, a finger of it in a small glass, and then the water, and
the clear spirit turns instantly milky. What happens is simple chemistry: the
anise oil dissolved in the alcohol has nowhere to go once the alcohol is diluted,
so it drops out of solution as a suspension of droplets too fine to settle. What
it means is less simple. Nobody clouds a glass of arak in order to drink it
quickly. Ice goes in last, never first, and the glass sits beside a plate of
whole radishes and a saucer of salt, and the food begins arriving, and three
hours later it will still be arriving.

A mezze table has no courses and issues no portions. Plates land in waves and
then stay, crowding each other, getting pushed sideways to make room. Nobody is
served. You reach, tear a piece of flatbread, use it as a scoop or a pincer, and
eat from whichever dish you feel like eating from. The word itself travels back
through Turkish to a Persian root meaning something like taste or relish, and it
describes a manner of eating rather than a fixed menu — one that runs from
Beirut and Damascus through Aleppo and Amman and Jerusalem and up into southern
Anatolia, changing its emphasis every hundred kilometres without changing its
shape.

## A Pantry Built on Acid, Answered by Fat

The single most useful thing to understand about Levantine cooking is that it is
sour on purpose, and layered about how it gets there. Lemon is the obvious acid,
used with a liberality that startles people raised on cautious European
seasoning. Sumac supplies a second kind — the dried and ground berries of a
shrub that grows wild across the region, more fruity and less sharp than citrus,
dusted over onions, over fattoush, over grilled fish. Pomegranate molasses is a
third, cooked-down juice with a dark, slightly bitter edge that survives heat in
a way lemon does not. Yogurt brings a lactic sourness, and the vinegar in the
pickle jars brings a blunt one.

Against all of that sits fat, and the fat is almost never butter. Olive oil is
poured, not measured, and finished dishes get another spiral of it on top. Tahini
carries the sesame's oil into places oil alone cannot reach. Walnuts, pine nuts
and almonds thicken and enrich. Yogurt is strained to labneh until it is dense
enough to hold a spoon upright. The tension between the acid and the fat is what
keeps a table of a dozen rich dishes from becoming exhausting — you can eat
hummus for an hour because the lemon keeps resetting your palate.

Garlic belongs to both sides. It is almost always pounded to a paste with coarse
salt rather than chopped, because the salt crystals act as an abrasive and
rupture the cells more completely, and because a paste disperses where minced
garlic sits in lumps. The same mortar turns garlic and salt and oil into toum,
the ferociously pungent white emulsion that arrives with grilled chicken and
that, made correctly, holds together with no egg in it at all.

## What Tahini Does When You Add Water

Anyone making tahini sauce for the first time thinks they have ruined it. You
start with a bowl of loose, pourable sesame paste, add lemon juice and a splash
of water, stir, and it seizes — thickens abruptly into a pale, stiff, unpromising
mass that seems to be curdling. The instinct is to stop. The correct response is
to keep adding water, a little at a time, and somewhere past a certain point the
mass loosens and smooths and becomes a silky cream, paler than it started,
pourable again.

What has happened is a flip. Sesame paste is oil with solids suspended in it;
adding a small amount of water gives the solids something to swell into and
bridges them together. Adding enough water inverts the whole system, so the oil
ends up suspended in water rather than the reverse. That cream is the backbone of
half the table. Thinned further with lemon it becomes tarator, the sauce for
fried cauliflower and for fish baked over onions. Whipped into chickpeas it
becomes hummus. Folded into charred eggplant it becomes mutabbal.

Hummus is the dish people argue about most and the one where technique is most
visible. Dried chickpeas, soaked overnight, cooked until they are genuinely soft
rather than merely done — far softer than any recipe written for a salad would
allow. A pinch of baking soda in the soaking or cooking water makes the
difference; the alkaline environment breaks down the pectin holding the cell
walls together, so the chickpeas collapse quickly and their skins loosen and
float off to be skimmed. The proportion of tahini is generous, the lemon is
assertive, and the result is served warm in a shallow bowl with a well pressed
into the middle and olive oil pooled in it, which is a very different object
from the cold, dense, spoonable paste sold in tubs.

## Smoke, Char, and the Eggplant

Mutabbal begins with an eggplant laid directly on a flame — a gas burner, a
charcoal grill, coals raked out of a bread oven — and left there, turned
occasionally, until the skin is blistered black and the whole vegetable has
slumped and hissed and given up. It looks ruined. Inside, the flesh is pale and
soft and has taken on the smoke through the split skin, and that smoke is the
entire point of the dish. The flesh is scooped out, drained of its bitter liquid,
chopped rather than blended so it keeps some structure, and folded with tahini,
lemon and garlic. Pomegranate seeds on top, oil over that.

Muhammara works the opposite way round, building on roasted red pepper and
walnuts pounded together with pomegranate molasses, Aleppo pepper and enough
breadcrumb to give it body — sweet, tart, faintly hot, nutty, and firmly
associated with Aleppo, a city whose cooking has always been the region's most
ambitious. Beside these sit things that require no cooking at all: labneh under
oil, olives, a bowl of pickled turnips turned shocking pink by a single slice of
beetroot in the brine, whole cucumbers, sprigs of mint, romaine leaves to be
used as scoops.

## Bulgur, and the Salad Everyone Gets Wrong

Tabbouleh is a parsley dish. Flat-leaf parsley, enormous quantities of it,
chopped fine enough to be fluffy but not so fine that it bruises to a paste, with
mint, tomato, a little onion, lemon, oil, and a minority of fine bulgur soaked
only in the tomato's own juice. The version that reached Western delicatessens
inverted those proportions into a bulgur salad flecked with green, which is a
different dish with the same name and about a tenth of the interest.

Bulgur — wheat parboiled, dried and cracked, which is to say a grain
pre-processed centuries ago into something that cooks in minutes — is also the
other half of kibbeh. Fine bulgur and very lean lamb are pounded together in a
heavy stone mortar until they stop being two things and become a smooth, cohesive
paste. From there the paths diverge. Kibbeh nayyeh is served raw, spread on a
plate, scored, oiled, eaten with onion and mint. Kibbeh bil sanieh is layered in
a tray with a filling of spiced minced lamb, onion and pine nuts, scored into
diamonds and baked. Fried kibbeh are hollow torpedoes shaped by hand around a
thumb, and the mark of a good cook is how thin the shell is.

Bread turns up structurally as well as as a utensil. Fattoush is a salad whose
logic is stale flatbread, torn and fried or toasted, added late so it softens
without dissolving, cutting the sourness of sumac and lemon with something to
chew. Fatteh goes further and makes the bread a floor: toasted pieces in the
bottom of a dish, chickpeas over them, warm garlicky yogurt poured on top, pine
nuts browned in butter spooned over that. Both dishes exist because bread was too
valuable to throw away, and both survived long past the point of necessity
because they turned out to be good.

## Putting the Year in Jars

Underneath the restaurant version of mezze sits a village calendar. Late summer
is makdous: small eggplants blanched, salted, drained under a weight for a day
or two, then slit and packed with walnut, red pepper and garlic and submerged
under olive oil in a jar where they will sit for months. Labneh is rolled into
balls and stored the same way. Kishk — bulgur fermented with milk or yogurt,
then dried and crumbled to a pale powder — is a way of keeping both grain and
dairy through a winter, and reconstitutes into a thick, tangy soup. Za'atar, the
wild herb itself, is cut in spring and dried and blended with sumac and toasted
sesame. Pomegranates are pressed and their juice reduced to molasses, which is
how a household keeps acidity on hand when the lemons are gone.

Spring pulls the other direction, toward things that cannot be kept at all: wild
chicory cooked down and topped with onions fried almost black, thistle hearts,
mallow, green almonds eaten whole and sour, the brief weeks of fresh green
chickpeas.

The grilled meat, when it finally comes, arrives at the point where nobody has
room for it. That ordering is not an accident of pacing. A mezze meal is not
built toward a main course the way a European menu builds toward a roast; the
skewers are the last thing to appear precisely because they are not the reason
anyone sat down. The reason is the three hours.
