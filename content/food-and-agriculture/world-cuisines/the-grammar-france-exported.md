# The Grammar France Exported

The chef calls for a brunoise and nobody asks what that means. The kitchen might
be in Seoul or Lagos or Melbourne, the menu might contain not a single French
dish, and the cook dicing the carrot might never have eaten in France — and the
word still lands, because it is understood everywhere that it means an even
cube of about two millimetres and that anything larger will be sent back. The
same is true of half the working language on the whiteboard: mise en place,
sauté, deglaze, reduce, nappe, chinois, bain-marie, sous chef, the pass.

This is the strangest fact about French cooking's influence. The dishes mostly
lost. Outside France, very few restaurants now serve sole meunière or tournedos
Rossini, and a menu built from Escoffier's repertoire would read as a costume
drama. What travelled and stuck was not the food. It was a method for producing
food, a way of arranging the people who produce it, and a vocabulary precise
enough that the whole thing could be written down and taught to someone who had
never seen it done. France exported a grammar, and grammars outlive the
sentences they were invented for.

## From Spice to Stock

The founding decision was made in the seventeenth century and it was a decision
about where flavour should come from. Medieval European cooking of the ambitious
sort was heavily spiced — pepper, ginger, cinnamon, cloves, grains of paradise,
saffron, often in combinations and quantities that read now as aggressive. Spice
was expensive, imported, and a display of wealth, and it sat on top of the
ingredients rather than emerging from them.

La Varenne's cookbook, published in Paris around the middle of that century,
documents a turn away from that. The new cooking leaned on butter, on native
herbs tied into a bouquet, on mushrooms and shallots, and above all on the idea
that the way to make meat taste more like meat is to extract meat flavour into a
liquid and then boil that liquid down. Stock arrives as a concept, and with it
the roux, the reduction, and the notion of a kitchen that manufactures its own
concentrated flavour rather than buying it by the ounce from a merchant.

It is worth pausing on how consequential that is. Once flavour is understood as
something you produce through process, cooking becomes a technical discipline
with steps that can be described, improved, argued about and standardized. A
cuisine of imported spice blends cannot be systematized the same way. A cuisine
of stocks and reductions can.

## Carême, and the Idea That Sauces Have Parents

Antonin Carême, working in the early nineteenth century for aristocratic and
diplomatic households, had a classifier's temperament in an age full of them. He
took the sprawling and unruly collection of French sauces and argued that they
were not a list but a family tree. There were a handful of base sauces —
espagnole, velouté, béchamel, allemande in his scheme — and everything else was a
base plus a defined operation. Add mustard. Add a reduction of white wine and
shallot. Add cream, or tarragon, or tomato. The derivative sauces were not
separate recipes to memorize but transformations to perform.

That single reframing is what makes French technique teachable at scale. A cook
who learns four bases and twenty operations can produce eighty sauces, and can
also generate a reasonable eighty-first that nobody has written down. It is the
difference between memorizing phrases and learning conjugation. Carême also
worked at enormous scale, built architectural centrepieces, and — crucially —
wrote constantly, which meant the system existed on paper and could travel
without him.

## Escoffier, the Brigade, and the Grand Hotel

Auguste Escoffier's Guide Culinaire, published in 1903, did to Carême what
editors do to brilliant sprawling manuscripts. It trimmed, simplified, reorganized
and made usable. The mother sauces became five, with tomato and hollandaise
promoted in; the elaborate garnishes were cut back; recipes were written as
compact reference entries rather than essays.

More consequential than the book was the organization. Escoffier restructured the
professional kitchen into the brigade de cuisine — a chef de cuisine at the top,
a sous chef below, then chefs de partie each owning a station: saucier, poissonnier,
rôtisseur, entremetier, garde manger, pâtissier, with commis beneath them. The
logic is industrial and the debt to military organization is obvious. Each
station holds its own mise en place and its own portion of the menu; an order
arriving at the pass is decomposed into parallel tasks; nobody makes a whole
dish, and everybody makes part of many.

This became necessary partly because of a change in how food was served. Over the
nineteenth century, European dining shifted away from putting every dish on the
table at once and toward serving courses in sequence, plated for each diner. A
kitchen that must deliver forty identical hot plates at a moment's notice, then
forty more twenty minutes later, needs timing, division of labour, and reserves.
Escoffier's answer to reserves was the fonds — stocks and base preparations held
ready, so that a dish is finished rather than started when it is ordered.

The reason this spread worldwide is duller than the technique deserves: hotels.
The late nineteenth and early twentieth century built a circuit of grand hotels
serving an international travelling class, and Escoffier's partnership with César
Ritz at the Savoy and then the Carlton put the system at the centre of it. A
hotel in Cairo, Shanghai, Buenos Aires or Bombay staffed its kitchen on the
French model and printed its menu in French, because that was what the clientele
understood luxury to look like. French cooks emigrated to run those kitchens and
trained local staff in the only method they knew. The cooking schools came later
and formalized what the hotels had already distributed.

## The Operations Worth Stealing

Strip away the repertoire and a set of physical operations remains, and these are
genuinely useful regardless of what you are cooking.

Stock and reduction, first: simmering bones and aromatics extracts gelatin and
savoury compounds, and boiling the result down concentrates them until a liquid
acquires body you can feel on the lips. That body is gelatin, not fat, and
understanding the difference is the difference between a sauce and a puddle.
Emulsions, second: mayonnaise, hollandaise, beurre blanc and a plain vinaigrette
are the same phenomenon at different temperatures, fat broken into droplets and
held in suspension by something that keeps them apart. Beurre blanc is the
elegant case, because butter supplies its own emulsifying milk solids and the
cook's only job is to control heat and add fat slowly enough. Mounting a finished
sauce with cold butter is the same trick in miniature.

Then browning, and what to do about it. Searing meat hard produces the roasted,
complex flavours of the Maillard reaction and leaves much of that flavour stuck
to the pan; deglazing with wine or stock dissolves it and puts it back into the
dish. Then thickening, understood as a family: roux, slurry, liaison with egg
yolk, reduction, emulsion, purée, each with its own texture and stability.
Then temperature discipline — the insistence that a simmer is not a boil, that
stock must be skimmed and never agitated if it is to be clear, that a bain-marie
exists because some things must never exceed a certain temperature.

And mise en place, which may be the most widely adopted idea in the history of
cooking. Everything measured, cut, portioned and arranged before service begins,
so that the act of cooking becomes assembly under pressure rather than
improvisation under pressure. Kitchens that cook nothing remotely French run on
it. So, increasingly, do laboratories, operating theatres and anyone who has read
a productivity book.

## The Bill for All This

The same codification that made French technique portable made it imperial. A
single method installed as the international standard implies a ranking, and for
most of the twentieth century the ranking was explicit: French cuisine was
cuisine, and everything else was regional, ethnic, folk, or simply food. Chefs
elsewhere had their careers graded by proximity to Paris. Cooks with deep command
of their own traditions were treated as untrained because they could not name a
cut in French.

The distortions ran deeper than snobbery. Cuisines organized around simultaneity
— the Thai table where everything arrives at once and the eater does the
balancing, the Levantine mezze that has no climax, the West African plate of
starch and sauce — were reformatted into sequential plated courses in order to be
taken seriously, which meant discarding the structure that made them work. The
tasting menu became the only recognized shape for ambition. A great deal of
excellent cooking spent decades apologizing for not being French.

## The Revolt That Kept the Method

The correction started inside France. Fernand Point, at his restaurant in Vienne,
trained a generation who came out of his kitchen convinced that Escoffier's
system had hardened into ritual — that sauces were being flour-thickened out of
habit, vegetables cooked to surrender, menus frozen regardless of season. Paul
Bocuse, the Troisgros brothers, Michel Guérard and Alain Chapel were the names
that followed, and the critics Henri Gault and Christian Millau gave the movement
its label in the 1970s.

Nouvelle cuisine shortened cooking times, cut flour out of sauces in favour of
reduction and butter, built menus around what the market had that morning, moved
plating from the dining room into the kitchen, and looked openly to Japan for
ideas about restraint and presentation. It was a revolt against the system, and
it was conducted entirely in the system's own technique. The stocks stayed. The
brigade stayed. What went was the fixed repertoire.

Which is roughly where things have ended up. The brigade has softened almost
everywhere, and the shouting that was defended for a century as rigour is finally
being recognized as just shouting. But a kitchen in Lima working with Amazonian
fish, or in Bangkok, or in Copenhagen, still runs its prep list, still holds
stock, still mounts a sauce with butter at the last second, still calls for a
brunoise. The method came unstuck from the menu, which is what happens to any
grammar good enough to say things its inventors never thought of.
