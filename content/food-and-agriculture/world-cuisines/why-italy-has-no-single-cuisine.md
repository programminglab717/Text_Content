# Why Italy Has No Single Cuisine

A cook in Bologna making tagliatelle uses soft wheat flour, egg yolks, and a
rolling pin longer than her forearm, working the sheet until it is thin enough
to read through and slightly rough to the touch so that sauce will cling. Four
hundred miles southeast, in a lane in Bari, a woman making orecchiette uses
durum semolina, water, and the dull blade of a table knife, dragging and
flipping each disc of dough against her thumb at a rate of several per second.
Both products are called pasta. They share a shape category, a name, and
essentially nothing else — not the wheat, not the enrichment, not the tools, not
the sauces they were developed to carry, not even the century in which their
regions could reliably afford them.

Italy has one of the most recognizable food identities on earth and almost no
national cuisine underneath it. The gap is historical, and it is not very old.

## A Country Assembled Late

Until 1861 the peninsula was a collection of separate states with separate
rulers, separate currencies, separate weights and measures, and — decisively for
the kitchen — separate trade networks. The Kingdom of the Two Sicilies looked
south and east across the Mediterranean. Venice had spent centuries as a spice
entrepôt. Lombardy and the Veneto had been under Austrian administration.
Piedmont faced France closely enough that its aristocratic cooking still shows
it. At unification only a small minority of the population actually spoke the
Tuscan-derived language we now call Italian; most spoke regional languages that
were not mutually intelligible.

Sicily shows what separate trade networks do to a larder. Greek colonists,
Arab rulers, Norman kings and Spanish viceroys all left deposits there, and the
Arab centuries were the richest: citrus, sugar cane, almonds, rice, saffron,
eggplant and the practice of setting sweet against sour. You can still eat the
result. Caponata balances vinegar and sugar over fried eggplant. Trapani makes
couscous and serves it with fish. Sicilian pastry work with almond paste and
candied fruit has more in common with North Africa than with anything cooked in
Turin, and the island's sardine pasta, with wild fennel, pine nuts and raisins,
would be unintelligible as Italian food to someone who had only eaten in
Lombardy.

Local loyalty in Italy has its own word, campanilismo, from campanile: the
attachment a person feels to everything within sight of their own bell tower. It
governs food with unusual force. Neighboring towns in the same province will
argue about the correct thickness of a sheet of pasta or whether a soup takes
beans or chickpeas, and they are not performing the argument for tourists.

Something like a shared national cookbook did eventually appear. Late in the
nineteenth century a retired silk merchant named Pellegrino Artusi compiled
recipes gathered from correspondents all over the new country, wrote them in
accessible Italian rather than dialect or French, and published the collection at
his own expense after publishers turned it down. It sold steadily for decades
and put Romagnol, Tuscan and Neapolitan dishes into the same volume, which
almost nothing had done before. Italians learned a common culinary language from
a book, at roughly the same time they were learning a common spoken one from
schools and conscription.

## Fat Draws the Map

If you want a single line that organizes Italian cooking, draw it where the
olive stops growing. The tree needs mild winters; the Po valley does not reliably
provide them. So the historical cooking fat of the north is butter, rendered
pork fat, or cured back fat pounded with herbs, while the center and south cook
in olive oil. The consequences run deeper than flavor. Butter suits gentle
simmering, flour-thickened sauces, and the richness of a filled pasta; olive oil
suits high heat, raw dressings, and vegetables treated as the main event.

The gradient is visible in almost any dish that crosses it. Risotto belongs to
the rice country of Piedmont, Lombardy and the eastern Veneto, where paddies
have been worked since the Renaissance and where the short, high-starch grains
release the creaminess that the technique depends on. Polenta belongs to the
same north, though maize only arrived after the Columbian exchange; it took over
so completely as peasant food in parts of the Veneto that a diet of little else
produced widespread pellagra in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, before
anyone understood that the niacin in untreated maize is largely unavailable to
the body.

Cheese follows the same geography. The Po valley had the flat land, the water
and the winter fodder to keep large herds of cows, so it produced enormous
wheels of hard, slow-aged cow's milk cheese that could be stored, taxed and
traded — a technology as much as a food, developed in monastic estates in the
Middle Ages. The dry hills further south supported sheep instead, which is why
the grating cheese in a Roman or Sardinian kitchen is salty aged pecorino rather
than Parmigiano, and why the two taste nothing alike. The marshy lowlands of
Campania kept water buffalo, whose milk makes a fresh cheese that is best within
a day or two of being made and therefore barely traveled at all until
refrigeration.

Meanwhile the south developed the parallel art of dried pasta. Durum wheat
thrives in hot dry summers, its high protein content makes a dough that holds
its shape without eggs, and the sea breezes of Gragnano, near Naples, and of
parts of the Ligurian coast were good enough at drying extruded pasta that a
real industry grew up around them. Dried pasta keeps for a year. That is not a
culinary preference; it is a solution to a storage problem in a region that
could not count on the next harvest.

## Poverty as a Technique

Much of what the world admires about Italian cooking was invented by people with
very little to cook. Cucina povera is not a style so much as a set of pressures:
use the stale bread, use the parts of the animal nobody wants, use the wild
greens from the verge, stretch beans a long way.

Tuscany bakes its bread without salt, a habit usually explained by old salt
taxes, and the result is a loaf that goes hard fast and is therefore designed
from the start to be finished as something else — soaked with tomato and basil
into a summer salad, cooked down with black kale and beans into a soup, then
reheated the following day into a thicker version with its own name. Roman
cooking absorbed the offal from the city's slaughterhouses and built a whole
repertoire on the fifth quarter: oxtail braised with celery, tripe with mint and
pecorino, sweetbreads. Ligurian pesto exists because basil grows well on that
coast and because a sauce that requires no cooking saves fuel. Mountain villages
ground chestnuts into flour when wheat was out of reach.

The tomato, the plant now most associated with Italian food, was a latecomer and
a suspicious one. Brought back from the Americas in the sixteenth century, it was
grown ornamentally for generations before anyone seriously ate it; a Neapolitan
court steward published a tomato sauce in the Spanish style at the end of the
seventeenth century, which is among the earliest Italian records of the idea.
Only in the nineteenth century did tomato, dried pasta and Neapolitan poverty
converge into the combination that would eventually stand in for the entire
country. The volcanic soils around Vesuvius happened to grow a superb
processing tomato, and the canning industry did the rest.

## The Cuisine That Left

Between the last decades of the nineteenth century and the First World War,
millions of Italians emigrated, overwhelmingly from the south and the poorest
inland provinces. They brought their regional habits to New York, Buenos Aires,
São Paulo and Melbourne — and then, for the first time in the lives of most of
them, they could afford meat.

Italian-American cooking is often described as a corruption of the real thing.
It is better understood as a dialect that evolved in isolation under different
conditions: abundant beef and pork, cheap canned tomatoes, garlic used at
volumes that would have been extravagant at home, dried oregano standing in for
herbs that did not grow well in the new climate, and hard aged cheese available
to anyone. Dishes like meatballs served separately from pasta, or veal
parmigiana, are recognizably descended from southern Italian technique applied
to a level of plenty that southern Italy did not have. The relationship runs both
ways, too: money sent home, and returning emigrants, changed what people cooked
in the villages they came from.

## Invented Tradition, Legally Protected

Italy now defends its regional foods with an apparatus of protected
designations. Parmigiano-Reggiano can only be made within a defined zone from
the milk of cows fed on specified forage, aged for a minimum period, and tapped
with a hammer by an inspector who listens for flaws before the rind is branded.
Prosciutto di Parma, Aceto Balsamico Tradizionale, and hundreds of other names
carry similar rules. The system preserves genuine craft, and it also freezes a
particular moment in the history of each product and declares it authentic.

Which is worth remembering, because a surprising number of canonical Italian
dishes are younger than the grandmothers credited with them. Carbonara appears
in the middle of the twentieth century, around the end of the Second World War,
with a plausible connection to American bacon and powdered eggs in Rome.
Tiramisù is no older than the second half of the twentieth century and comes out
of the northeast. The pizza margherita story, with its patriotic colors and its
visiting queen, is at minimum heavily polished. None of this makes the food less
good. It makes the food what it actually is — a set of local solutions, still
moving, that got frozen into monuments about a century and a half after the
country acquired a single flag.
