# How a Thai Cook Balances a Dish

The som tam vendor asks how many chilies before she asks anything else. The
answer determines everything that follows, and she builds the salad in front of
you in a tall clay mortar, dropping in garlic and chilies and bruising them with
a wooden pestle, then long beans snapped in half, then palm sugar and lime and
fish sauce and dried shrimp, then handfuls of shredded green papaya that she
turns over with a spoon between strokes rather than crushing. Partway through she
stops, dips the spoon, tastes, and adds something. Usually lime. Sometimes sugar.
Then she tastes again.

That pause is the whole method. Thai cooking is not a system of fixed recipes
executed accurately; it is a system of moving targets corrected by tasting at the
end. A written recipe gives ratios to start from, but the tamarind is more or
less sour this month, the limes are thin-skinned or thick, the palm sugar is a
different batch, the fish sauce is a different brand. The cook's job is to take
the dish to a particular place, and the route changes.

## Four Directions, Not Four Ingredients

The place being aimed at is usually described with four words — sour, salty,
sweet, hot — sometimes with bitter added, and the proportions differ by dish and
by region. What matters more than the list is that each of those directions has
several possible agents, and they are not interchangeable.

Salt, first, is almost never delivered as salt. It comes as nam pla, fish sauce,
which is anchovies packed with salt in barrels and left for a year or more until
the flesh breaks down and a clear amber liquid can be drawn off. That liquid is
enormously rich in free glutamate, so every time a Thai cook salts something they
are also adding savoury depth, and a dish seasoned with plain salt instead tastes
thin in a way that is hard to diagnose. The heavier versions of the same idea are
kapi, fermented shrimp paste pressed into blocks, and pla ra, the funkier
fermented fish of the northeast.

The sour side has a range. Lime is bright, volatile and mostly added off the
heat, because boiling it makes it flat and slightly bitter. Tamarind is rounder
and darker and survives cooking, which is why it does the sour work in pad thai
and in sour curries. Green mango, young tamarind leaves, sour bamboo and various
regional fruits fill in. Vinegar appears where Chinese technique does. There is
also a category worth separating out: lemongrass, makrut lime leaf and zest, and
galangal read as bright and citric to the nose without contributing acidity at
all. They smell sour. They are aromatics, and using them where lime is needed
produces a dish that seems fresh and yet somehow limp.

Sweetness comes from palm sugar, boiled down from the sap of sugar palms and sold
in soft discs or pots, and it is not a dessert note. It is a balancing tool, used
in savoury dishes to round off the aggression of fish sauce and chili and to give
the flavour length. Palm sugar carries caramel and a faint minerality that white
sugar does not, and a curry sweetened with refined sugar tastes simply sugary
where the same amount of palm sugar tastes fuller.

The heat has a history. Chilies are American plants that reached Southeast Asia
with Portuguese traders in the sixteenth century, and they took over so
completely that they now define the cuisine in the popular imagination. Before
them, pungency came from black pepper, long pepper, ginger and galangal — and the
language still records the handover. Chili is phrik. Black pepper is phrik thai,
which reads as "Thai chili": the older, native pungent thing renamed in terms of
the newcomer that displaced it.

## The Mortar, and the Order of Operations

Two mortars do two jobs. The tall clay one with a wooden pestle is for salads,
where the aim is to bruise ingredients enough that they release juice and take up
dressing without turning to mush. The heavy granite one with a stone pestle is
for pastes, and it is a genuinely different tool from a blender.

Pounding a curry paste has an order, and the order is not arbitrary. Dried
chilies, soaked and drained, go in first with a pinch of salt — the salt crystals
act as an abrasive and speed everything up. Then the hard fibrous aromatics:
lemongrass, sliced very thin across the grain first, because the fibres run the
length of the stalk and will not break down otherwise; galangal, also sliced;
makrut lime zest; coriander root; sometimes toasted and ground coriander seed and
cumin. Then the soft, wet things — garlic and shallot — which would turn the
whole mass to slurry if added early. Shrimp paste goes in last and is simply
worked through.

A blender chops. A pestle crushes, and crushing ruptures the oil-bearing cells
progressively while the friction warms the paste slightly, which drives aroma out
of the ingredients and into the mix. It also produces a different physical
result: a paste with structure, that holds together in the pan and fries rather
than splattering. Anyone who has made the same recipe both ways can tell them
apart blind.

## Cracking the Coconut Cream

The next step is the one most Western versions of Thai curry skip, and it is why
those versions taste raw. A tin of coconut milk that has not been shaken has
thick cream at the top. That cream goes into a hot pan by itself and is cooked
until it splits — the emulsion breaks, the water drives off, and clear oil rises
out of the white solids. Thais call it breaking the oil. The curry paste then
goes into that separated oil and is fried in it, stirred constantly, for several
minutes, until the kitchen smells of nothing else and the paste has darkened and
tightened.

Only after that does the thinner coconut milk go in, along with stock if it is
being used. Then the protein. Then the seasoning, in sequence: palm sugar first,
because it needs a moment to dissolve and to bloom; then fish sauce; then
whatever sour element the dish calls for. Vegetables go in according to how long
they need. Torn makrut lime leaves and Thai basil go in with the heat off, so
their aromatics do not cook away. And at the end the cook tastes, and adjusts,
and tastes again.

## Balance Across the Table, Not Only in the Bowl

A Thai meal does not arrive in courses. Everything is set down together, and at
the centre is rice — so centrally that the ordinary verb for eating a meal is kin
khao, to eat rice, and every other dish on the table is technically an
accompaniment to it. This changes what balance means.

Because the rice is bland and constant, individual dishes are permitted to be
extreme in a way that a single-plate cuisine cannot allow. A southern curry can
be almost punishing on its own. A nam prik — a pounded chili relish, served with
a platter of raw and steamed vegetables and a piece of fried fish — is far too
salty and hot to eat by the spoonful, and is not meant to be; you take a
fingertip of it with a cucumber spear and a mouthful of rice. A proper array
covers the spread: something soupy, something curried, something fried or
stir-fried, a relish with vegetables, a sharp herb salad. The balancing happens
across the table, and the eater performs it with every mouthful by choosing what
to combine.

Noodle shops make the logic explicit. At almost any of them there is a caddy on
the table with four pots: fish sauce with sliced fresh chili floating in it,
dried chili flakes, white sugar, and vinegar with chili. The bowl arrives
deliberately underseasoned, and finishing it is your job, not the cook's. Nobody
considers this a failure of the kitchen. It is the same tasting-and-correcting
loop as the som tam vendor's, handed over at the last moment to the only person
who knows exactly what they want.

## One Country, Four Kitchens

The central plains produce the version best known abroad: coconut milk, palm
sugar, a rounded and relatively sweet balance, and a repertoire refined in and
around Bangkok, including dishes that came down from court kitchens. Pad thai
belongs to this region and to the mid-twentieth century, when a government
campaign promoted noodle eating as a matter of national policy — a reminder that
even iconic dishes can have dates.

The north is cooler and drier, cooks with much less coconut and much less sugar,
eats sticky rice rather than steamed, and uses fermented soybean discs where
other regions use shrimp paste. Its curries are thin and herbal. Khao soi, the
coconut curry noodle soup with crisp noodles piled on top, came in along trading
routes with Yunnanese Muslim caravaneers and is not really a Thai dish at all in
origin.

The northeast is culturally Lao. Sticky rice rolled into balls by hand, fermented
fish, grilled meat, larb and som tam, and the toasted rice powder called khao
khua that gives northeastern salads their nutty grit and smell. The south is the
hottest and most assertive: turmeric everywhere, heavy shrimp paste, sour curries
built without coconut, bitter sator beans, and — from centuries of Indian Ocean
trade and the region's Muslim communities — massaman, which is the one Thai curry
that smells of cardamom, cinnamon and clove.

Ask a cook from any of them to make a dish from another region and they will make
it, then taste it, and then correct it toward home.
