# The Architecture of a West African Meal

Two women stand over a wooden mortar the height of a kitchen bin, each holding
a pestle about as long as a broom handle. They pound in alternation — one down,
one up — and in the gap between strokes a third hand darts in to fold the mass
of boiled yam back on itself and flick water over it. The rhythm is fast and
unbroken, and missing a beat would cost somebody a finger. After twenty minutes
the yam has stopped being yam. It has become a smooth, elastic, faintly glossy
dough that tears rather than crumbles, and that will hold a thumbprint. This is
pounded yam, and calling it a side dish misses what it is doing. It is half the
meal's structure, and the half that does the hardest work.

Across most of West Africa a meal is built from two parts that are designed
against each other. One part is a starch, worked to a particular consistency
and deliberately underseasoned. The other is a sauce or soup carrying nearly
everything else: salt, fat, chili heat, protein, aroma, acid. The starch is
called a swallow in Nigerian English, which is an accurate description of how
it is eaten. You pinch a piece off with the right hand, roll it into a ball
against the palm, press a dent into it with the thumb, load the dent with sauce,
and swallow without much chewing. Chewing is not really the point. Texture is —
whether the ball is smooth or gritty, whether it stretches or breaks, whether it
is slack or firm enough to hold its load.

The starches vary by region, grain and processing. Pounded yam from true African
yam, a tuber that has nothing to do with the orange sweet potato sold under the
same name in American supermarkets. Amala, darker and softer, from yam that has
been dried and milled to flour. Eba from toasted cassava granules swollen in hot
water. Banku and kenkey from fermented maize dough, sour enough to register as a
flavour in their own right. Tuo zaafi from millet or sorghum across the Sahel.
Attiéké, the Ivorian cassava couscous, steamed into loose granules that behave
more like a grain than a dough. What they share is the job: a bland, absorbent,
textured base that gives the sauce somewhere to go.

## Cassava, and the Labour of Making a Plant Edible

Cassava is not indigenous to Africa. It arrived from South America through
Portuguese trade routes and spread because it does things other staples cannot:
it grows in exhausted soil, shrugs off drought, and can be left in the ground
for months as a living store, harvested when needed. What it demands in return
is processing, because the high-yielding bitter varieties contain cyanogenic
compounds that make raw cassava genuinely dangerous.

The processing is not preservation bolted on afterwards. It is the reason the
plant is food. To make gari, the roots are peeled and grated to a wet mash, then
packed into sacks and left to ferment for a few days while weights press out the
liquid. The fermentation and the pressing between them remove the toxic
compounds; the mash is then sieved and toasted on a wide shallow pan, stirred
constantly, until it dries into pale granules that keep for months without
refrigeration. Attiéké follows a related path — fermented with a portion of
previously fermented mash as a starter, pressed, granulated, steamed. Other
cassava fufu is made by soaking whole roots in water until they soften and sour,
then pounding them.

Every one of those routes leaves a flavour behind. Gari is faintly sour. Attiéké
is distinctly so. Fermented maize doughs are sharper still. A cuisine that is
often described as heavily spiced turns out to be built on a foundation of mild
acidity, which is exactly what a rich, oily, chili-laden sauce wants to sit on.

## The Base That Starts Almost Everything

Watch enough cooks and a single opening move appears again and again. Onions,
tomatoes and scotch bonnet or habanero peppers are blended to a coarse purée,
sometimes with red bell pepper for bulk and colour without more heat. That purée
goes into hot oil and is fried, and fried, and fried — long past the point where
an impatient cook would call it done. The water boils off, the volume drops by
half, the colour deepens from bright red to brick, and eventually the oil breaks
back out and pools at the edges. That separation is the signal. Everything
before it tastes raw and sharp; everything after it tastes like a sauce.

The fat matters as much as the technique. Red palm oil, pressed from the flesh
of the oil palm fruit and left unrefined, is orange-red from carotenoids and has
a savoury, slightly waxy flavour of its own. It is a seasoning as much as a
cooking medium, and dishes built on it — banga soup, many egusi preparations,
Liberian and Sierra Leonean palm butter stews — are unmistakable by colour
alone. Where that flavour is not wanted, cooks use groundnut oil or refined palm
oil, which is bleached and neutral and behaves like any other frying fat.

Jollof rice is that base extended. Stock goes in, then parboiled rice, then the
lid, then low heat and patience while the grains drink the sauce and stain
through. The layer at the bottom catches slightly and is fought over. Nigerians
and Ghanaians argue about whose version is correct with a seriousness that
outsiders mistake for a joke, and both of them are downstream of Senegalese
ceebu jën, the Wolof dish of fish and vegetables and broken rice cooked in
sequence in a single pot, the rice going in last to absorb what everything else
left behind. The broken rice itself is a colonial artifact — cheap fragments
imported through French trade networks that a coastal rice culture absorbed and
then preferred, because broken grains take up sauce better than whole ones.

## Depth Without a Stockpot

European kitchens reach savoury depth by simmering bones for hours. West African
kitchens generally do not, and get there anyway, by fermenting and drying
instead.

The central seasoning across the Sahel and the forest belt is fermented locust
bean — the seeds of the néré tree, boiled, hulled and left to ferment until they
turn dark, sticky and powerfully aromatic. It is iru in Yoruba, dawadawa in
Hausa and much of Ghana, soumbala in Burkina Faso and Mali. The smell is
confrontational, somewhere between aged cheese and ammonia, and a spoonful
disappearing into a pot of soup leaves behind a savoury weight that nothing else
supplies. Fermented melon seed and oil bean do similar work in other dishes.

Alongside the ferments sit the dried proteins. Smoked catfish and mackerel,
stockfish soaked back to life over a day or two, ground crayfish used almost as
a powder, dried shrimp. These are not garnishes but structural seasonings,
carrying salt, smoke and glutamate into the sauce at the same time. A pot of
egusi soup might contain ground melon seeds, palm oil, the pepper base, fresh
meat, smoked fish, crayfish powder, iru and bitterleaf, and the fresh meat is
arguably the least important thing in it.

Ground seeds and nuts also do the thickening. Egusi seeds, milled and stirred
into a hot base, set into soft curds suspended through the soup. Groundnut
paste, in maafe and domoda and the Ghanaian nkatenkwan, builds a thick russet
sauce with a sweetness that gets pulled back into line by chili and sour tomato.
Peanuts, like cassava and maize and chili and tomato, crossed the Atlantic
eastward and were absorbed so completely that they now read as native.

## Texture as a Goal, Not an Accident

Some West African soups are deliberately, unapologetically slippery. Okra
chopped fine and cooked briefly; ogbono, the ground kernel of wild mango; ewedu,
the jute leaf that turns any liquid it enters into something that pours in
ropes. English lacks a good word for the quality, so Nigerian English supplies
one: draw. A soup that draws is doing what it is supposed to do.

Cooks control it precisely. Okra cut coarse and cooked long loses most of its
viscosity; cut fine and barely heated, it delivers maximum draw. The purpose is
functional as well as sensory — a viscous soup clings to a ball of swallow
instead of running off it, and slides rather than sticks. Palates trained
elsewhere read slipperiness as a defect and spend years unlearning that. The
same applies to bitterness: bitterleaf is washed and squeezed repeatedly to a
chosen level of bitterness, not to none at all, because a flat soup is a failed
soup.

## Fire, Skewer, Street

Away from the pot, the other great mode is charcoal. Suya is thin sheets of beef
pressed with yaji — a rub of roasted groundnut flour, chili, ginger, and often
cloves and the fragrant seeds sold as grains of paradise or African nutmeg —
threaded on sticks, grilled hard over coals, dusted with more of the rub, and
served on newspaper with raw onion and sliced tomato. The groundnut flour in the
rub scorches and grips the meat in a way plain spice does not. Whole fish get
slashed and packed with rof, a green paste of parsley, garlic and chili, before
going over the fire. Plantain is used across its entire ripeness range as though
it were several different ingredients: green and starchy, it is boiled or pounded
into fufu; half-ripe, it is cut into chunks and fried; fully ripe and nearly
black, it becomes kelewele, cubed and tossed with ginger and chili and fried
until the edges caramelize.

What travelled with the Atlantic slave trade was not a list of recipes but this
same architecture. Rice-growing knowledge reached the Carolinas. Okra, black-eyed
peas and fierce pepper sauces reached the whole American South. Gumbo carries a
West African word for okra in its name and a West African instinct for
thickening a pot with ground vegetable matter. And the opening move — aromatics
and peppers cooked down in fat until the oil breaks — turns up in Bahia, in
Havana, in New Orleans, wearing different names and doing the same job.
