# Washoku and the Shape of a Japanese Meal

A strip of dried kelp sits in a pot of cold water for half an hour. The pot goes
on a low flame and comes up slowly, and just before it reaches a boil the kelp
comes out. A handful of shavings goes in — pale pink, almost translucent, cut
from a block of bonito that has been simmered, smoked over weeks, inoculated
with mold, dried in the sun and dried again until it is hard enough to knock
against a table like a piece of hardwood. The shavings steep for less than a
minute, then the liquid is strained. Total working time, perhaps two minutes.
What comes out is pale gold, smells faintly of the sea and of smoke, and tastes
of almost nothing you can name, yet makes everything it touches taste more like
itself.

Dashi is the load-bearing wall of Japanese cooking, and the reason so much else
about the cuisine can afford to be restrained.

## The Chemistry of Holding Back

The kelp contributes glutamate. The dried bonito contributes inosinate. Neither
is remarkable alone, but together they interact: the perceived savory intensity
of the combination is several times what either delivers by itself. Japanese
cooks worked this out empirically centuries before anyone could explain it. The
explanation arrived in 1908, when the chemist Ikeda Kikunae isolated glutamate
from kelp broth, argued that its taste was distinct from sweet, sour, salty and
bitter, and gave it the name umami.

The synergy is not limited to fish. Dried shiitake mushrooms carry guanylate,
which pairs with kelp glutamate the same way, and a kelp-and-shiitake dashi is
the backbone of the vegetarian temple cooking called shojin ryori. Small dried
sardines make a rougher, more assertive stock for everyday miso soup in ordinary
households.

Regional taste splits along the same broth. Kansai kitchens, around Osaka and
Kyoto, season dashi with a pale soy sauce that is actually saltier than the dark
kind but leaves the liquid light enough to see the noodles through it. Tokyo and
the east use the darker, sweeter-smelling soy and a stock often built on dried
sardines, producing a broth that arrives nearly brown. Identical bowls of udon,
ordered five hundred kilometers apart, look like different dishes.

Everything downstream follows from having a broth that is already deeply savory
before any seasoning is added. You do not need much fat; traditional Japanese
cooking uses strikingly little, and what there is tends to come from the fish
itself or from a deliberate frying technique. You do not need heavy salting
beyond what soy sauce and miso already bring. You do not need reductions,
roux, or long braises to build depth, because the depth was there in the first
two minutes. A simmered dish of daikon and a piece of fish can consist of dashi,
soy sauce, sake, a little sweetness, and nothing else, and still taste finished.

## One Soup, Three Sides

The everyday structure has a name: ichiju-sansai, one soup and three dishes.
Rice occupies the center, not as a side but as the thing the meal exists to
accompany. A soup, usually miso, sits beside it. Then a principal dish, often
grilled fish, and two smaller ones — something simmered, something dressed — and
pickles, which are not counted among the three because they are assumed.

The centrality of the rice is embedded in the language. Gohan means cooked rice
and also means a meal; the dishes served alongside are okazu, a word that amounts
to accompaniment. Japanese rice is a short-grain japonica high in the starch
amylopectin, which is what makes the cooked grains cohere in a clump that can be
lifted with chopsticks without being sticky in the mouth. It is washed until the
water runs nearly clear, left to soak so the grains hydrate evenly, brought up
hard and then steamed through at low heat, and — the step most often skipped
outside Japan — left covered off the heat for ten minutes so the moisture
redistributes. Rice was also the currency of the feudal state: domains were
measured, and samurai stipends paid, in koku, a volume of rice reckoned as
roughly a year's eating for one person. A food that functioned simultaneously as
staple, tax and salary does not easily become a side dish.

Behind that arrangement sits an older pedagogy from Buddhist monastery kitchens.
A thirteenth-century Zen abbot wrote detailed instructions to the monk assigned
to cook, treating the work as practice rather than chore, and the temple
tradition codified a set of fives: five methods of cooking (raw, simmered,
grilled, steamed, fried), five colors (white, black, red, yellow, green), five
tastes. A cook planning a meal by that scheme will not serve two simmered dishes
in a row, and will notice if a tray is all beige. The five-color rule in
particular is why a Japanese meal so often looks composed even when it is
modest: it was planned as a set of contrasts rather than a sequence of courses.

That temple logic eventually fed into kaiseki, the multi-course meal that grew
out of the food served at tea gatherings and became the high formal cuisine.
Kaiseki inherited the restraint, the seasonal obligation and the attention to
vessels, and added the tea ceremony's insistence that the arrangement of a meal
is a form of hospitality carrying meaning, not just a delivery of calories.

## Shun, and the Tyranny of the Calendar

Seasonality in Japan is not a marketing slogan appended to a menu. It is a
scheduling system with vocabulary. Hashiri is the first arrival of something,
prized for novelty and priced accordingly. Shun is the peak, the few weeks when
an ingredient is at its best and is supposed to be eaten more or less
constantly. Nagori is the end of the run, carrying a distinct flavor of regret
that is considered part of the pleasure.

The calendar is specific. Bamboo shoots and young greens in spring, dug and
cooked the same day because their bitterness rises by the hour. Sweetfish from
clear rivers in summer, salted and grilled whole over charcoal. Matsutake
mushrooms and the oily saury called sanma in autumn, when the fish has fattened
for its migration. Yellowtail and crab in winter. New rice, shinmai, arriving
after the harvest with enough moisture that it needs less water in the pot.

Seasonal signaling extends past the food. A single young sansho leaf is slapped
between the palms and set on a simmered dish to release its scent and to say
spring. Carrot is cut into a maple leaf in November. Glass vessels appear in
July because they look cool; heavy rough-glazed ceramics come back in December.
Arrangements favor odd numbers and asymmetry, food is placed off-center with
deliberate empty space around it, and the vessel is chosen for the dish rather
than matched into a set. Tableware in a traditional Japanese kitchen is
accumulated, varied, and picked out plate by plate.

## Koji, and the Salt That Is Not Just Salt

Nearly every seasoning in the Japanese pantry is the product of the same mold.
Aspergillus oryzae, called koji, is grown on steamed rice or barley until the
grains are furred with white. Its enzymes cleave starch into sugars and protein
into amino acids — which means koji is, functionally, a machine for producing
glutamate. Miso is koji, soybeans and salt, left to ferment for months or years.
Soy sauce is koji, soybeans, wheat and brine, fermented and pressed. Sake is
koji converting rice starch to sugar while yeast converts that sugar to alcohol,
in the same tank at the same time. Mirin, rice vinegar and the sweet drink
amazake all come from the same organism.

Miso is regional in a way that surprises people who only know one kind. Kyoto
favors a pale, sweet, short-fermented white miso with a high proportion of koji.
The northeast makes a darker, saltier red one aged much longer. In the area
around Nagoya, a nearly black soybean miso is aged for years under stacked
stones and tastes almost like chocolate and leather.

Pickles run on similar principles. Vegetables are buried in a bed of fermenting
rice bran that a household feeds and turns by hand, sometimes for generations;
others are salted under a weighted lid overnight for something crisp and fresh.
Plums are salted, sun-dried, and stained red with shiso leaf into umeboshi so
sour they make the jaw ache.

The most consequential of these preservations was fish packed in salt and rice
and left to ferment for months, the rice discarded and the preserved fish eaten.
That was the original sushi, and it existed for its sourness and its keeping
qualities. When cooks learned to shortcut the fermentation with vinegar, the
rice became edible and joined the dish. In the vast city of Edo, vendors
compressed vinegared rice by hand, laid a slice of fish on top and sold it from
stalls as fast food. The most refined-seeming thing in the cuisine began as
street snacking, three hundred years after the technique it replaced.

## The Cut as Seasoning

A Japanese kitchen knife is usually ground on one side only, which lets it be
sharpened to an extremely acute edge and makes it steer slightly in the hand —
a trade of forgiveness for precision. That geometry supports the cuisine's
central manual skill: cutting as a form of seasoning.

A block of tuna is sliced in a single pull, never sawed, because a drawn cut
leaves a smooth face that reflects light and does not weep. Squid is scored in a
fine grid so it curls when it meets heat and so the texture yields. Daikon is
peeled in one continuous sheet, thin enough to see through, then stacked and
shredded into threads that are floated in ice water until they stiffen. Nothing
about this makes the vegetable more nutritious. It changes how it feels on the
tongue and how it looks in the bowl, and in a cuisine that seasons as lightly as
this one, those are not decorative concerns.

Something worth noticing about the whole apparatus: almost none of it involves
adding. Kelp and fish give up their savor and are removed. Salt goes into a vat
and waits. A knife takes material away. The cook's job, in this tradition, is
mostly to find out what an ingredient is at its best moment and then get out of
the way in a sufficiently skilled manner.
