# What a Two-Hectare Farm Actually Does

Most of the farms in the world are small. Not small in the way a hundred-acre
operation in Iowa is small compared with its neighbours, but small in absolute
terms — a hectare, two hectares, sometimes a few scattered parcels that add up
to less than that. The great majority of the planet's roughly six hundred
million farms fall into this category, and they are concentrated in South and
East Asia and in sub-Saharan Africa. They occupy a much smaller share of the
world's farmland than their numbers suggest, because a single ranch in
Australia or a soybean estate in Mato Grosso can cover more ground than ten
thousand of them. And yet in much of the world they grow most of what people
eat.

It is worth being precise about what such a farm looks like, because the word
"smallholder" flattens enormous variety. A two-hectare holding in the Kenyan
highlands might carry maize intercropped with beans, a stand of napier grass
cut daily for one or two dairy cows, a few hundred coffee trees on the steeper
ground, bananas along the drainage line, and a kitchen garden of kale and
onions by the house. A comparable plot in Bangladesh might be three rice crops
where irrigation allows, a pond with fish in it, and vegetables on the bunds.
Neither resembles the mental image of a farm as a single crop covering a field.
They are layered systems, worked mostly by hand and mostly by the household,
and their design logic has very little to do with maximising output per hour of
labour.

## The Inverse Relationship

Agricultural economists have observed the same odd pattern in survey after
survey across more than half a century and many countries: within a given
region, smaller farms tend to produce more per hectare than larger ones. The
finding is robust enough to have its own name — the inverse productivity
relationship — and its persistence has shaped a lot of thinking about land
reform.

The explanation is not that small farmers are better agronomists. It is mostly
about labour. A household farming two hectares has family members whose time
has little alternative use, especially in places where off-farm jobs are scarce
and seasonal. That labour is not costed at a market wage; it is simply
available. So the household does things that would never pay if you had to hire
someone: weeding three times instead of once, transplanting rice seedlings one
at a time at optimal spacing, hand-picking pests, applying manure exactly where
it is needed, harvesting the same plot repeatedly as individual fruits ripen. A
large farm using hired labour or machinery cannot economically do any of that,
so it accepts lower yields in exchange for far higher output per worker.

This distinction — land productivity against labour productivity — is the
central fact about small farms, and it explains the whole shape of their
predicament. A system that gets a great deal out of each hectare while
generating a very low return per day of work is not a system anyone can get
rich in. It is a system that feeds people. The moment a wage job appears within
commuting distance, the calculus changes, the extra weeding stops, yields drift
down, and the household is better off.

## Farming for the Bad Year

The other thing that distinguishes these farms is how they treat risk, and this
is where outside advice most often goes wrong. Development projects arrive with
a package — improved seed, fertiliser, a planting date — that raises average
yields substantially. Farmers adopt it partially, or for one season, or not at
all, and the project writes up something about tradition and resistance to
change.

What is usually happening is that the household is not optimising the average.
It is optimising the bad year. A family with no insurance, no savings buffer
beyond a few goats, and no access to credit that is not ruinous cannot afford
an outcome where the crop fails and there is nothing to eat in March. So it
plants varieties that yield less but survive drought. It spreads planting
across several dates so that a single dry spell at flowering cannot wipe out
everything. It scatters plots across different soils and slopes, which looks
grossly inefficient on a map and is in fact a hedge. It intercrops maize with
beans partly because the legume fixes nitrogen and partly because the two crops
fail under different conditions.

Fertiliser is the sharpest example. Applying it is a bet: you spend scarce cash
up front, often borrowed, and you get a return only if the rains come. In a
region where one season in four is poor, a farmer who fertilises every year may
do very well on average and still go hungry twice a decade. Many respond by
applying small doses, or applying only to the plot nearest the house where they
can watch it. From a distance this looks like underinvestment. From inside the
household it is a considered position on the difference between an expected
value and a survival constraint.

## The Missing Middle

Small farms are also, almost everywhere, at a structural disadvantage in the
market, and the disadvantage compounds.

Consider what a household with eight bags of surplus maize faces. It has no
storage that keeps weevils out for six months, so it must sell soon after
harvest, which is precisely when everyone else is selling and the price is at
its floor. It has no truck, so a trader comes to the farm gate and offers a
price that reflects the trader's cost of collecting from twenty scattered
households. It has no scale it trusts and no price information beyond rumour.
Six months later the same household will buy maize back at a much higher price,
because the hungry season arrives before the next harvest does. Farmers are
frequently net buyers of the staple they grow, and the seasonal price swing
takes a bite out of them in both directions.

Much of what actually helps is unglamorous and organisational rather than
agronomic. Aggregation through cooperatives or farmer groups gives a village
enough volume to attract a buyer willing to pay a better price. Warehouse
receipt systems let a farmer deposit grain, receive a certificate, borrow
against it, and sell later in the season. Mobile money — which spread through
East Africa faster than almost anyone predicted — collapsed the cost of paying
a farmer without either party travelling, and made it possible to sell inputs
and insurance in amounts small enough to matter. Reliable weights and honest
grading do more for farm incomes in some markets than a new seed variety would.

Land tenure sits underneath all of it. A household farming ground it does not
securely hold, under customary arrangements that may not survive a dispute or a
death, has a poor reason to plant a tree that fruits in seven years, dig a
terrace, or build soil organic matter over a decade. Insecure tenure biases
everything toward the annual crop and the immediate return, and that bias shows
up in the soil.

## What the Green Revolution Reached

The mid-twentieth-century transformation of Asian agriculture is the strongest
evidence that small farms can change fast. Dwarf wheat varieties bred in Mexico
and short-statured rice developed in the Philippines responded to fertiliser by
putting energy into grain rather than into stem, and they did not lodge and
collapse when fed. Combined with irrigation and subsidised inputs, they roughly
doubled cereal yields across the Indian Punjab, the Indonesian rice bowl, and
much of East Asia within a generation, on farms that were mostly small. The
gains were real, they were fast, and they were adopted by exactly the
smallholders who are often described as change-averse.

The revolution also showed its own boundaries. It worked where water could be
controlled. Rainfed farming systems — most of sub-Saharan Africa, the dry
plateaus of central India, much of the Sahel — got far less, because the
varieties were bred to exploit reliable water and fertiliser, and those inputs
were the constraint. Africa's staple crops, sorghum and millet and cassava and
yams, received a fraction of the breeding attention that rice and wheat did.
Where irrigation did arrive, the households with the capital to sink a tube
well pulled away from those without, and the groundwater in several of those
regions has been falling ever since.

The lesson most often drawn is that there is no single template. A package that
transformed the Punjab does relatively little on a rainfed slope in Malawi,
where the binding constraints may be soil acidity, a road that is impassable in
the wet season, and the fact that the household head is away working for wages
during the weeks when weeding matters most. Some of the most effective recent
work has been narrow and specific: acid-tolerant varieties, microdosing
fertiliser into the planting hole rather than broadcasting it, drought-tolerant
maize bred for a particular photoperiod, better storage bags that suffocate
weevils without chemicals.

None of this settles the larger question, which is whether the two-hectare farm
has a future as an institution or is simply the form agriculture takes before
people find something better to do. History suggests the second: every country
that has grown rich has seen its farm population shrink, its holdings
consolidate, and its rural labour move to cities. But that transition took
generations in Europe and is running far faster where it is happening now, and
the intervening decades are lived by actual households. For them the question
is not what the sector will look like in forty years. It is whether the maize
lasts until March.
