# How a Bushel of Wheat Gets Priced

A semi pulls onto the scale at a country elevator outside Hutchinson, Kansas,
in the last week of June. The driver is the farmer, or his son, or a neighbour
hired for harvest, and the load behind him is hard red winter wheat cut that
morning. Before anything dumps, someone climbs up with a probe, drives it down
through the grain in three or four places, and pulls a sample into a bucket.
Almost everything that happens to that wheat over the next year — what it is
worth, where it goes, who carries the risk of it losing value — is decided by
what is in the bucket and by a set of numbers on a screen two states away.

The sample goes through a bench of tests that have not changed much in
principle since the nineteenth century. Moisture comes first, because wheat
above about thirteen and a half percent will not keep in a bin without heating
and spoiling; wetter grain gets docked, or gets dried at the farmer's expense.
Test weight measures how many pounds of grain fit in a measured volume, which
is a rough proxy for how plump the kernels filled during grain fill and how
much flour a miller can extract. A dockage machine shakes out weed seed, chaff,
and dirt. An inspector or a machine counts damaged kernels and foreign
material. And for wheat specifically, a near-infrared analyser reads protein
content in a few seconds, because a baker buying flour for pan bread cares
about gluten strength and will pay a premium for it, or discount sharply
without it.

Those numbers resolve into a grade — No. 1 or No. 2 Hard Red Winter, say, with
a protein figure attached — and that grade is the entire point. Before
standardised grading, grain was sold in sacks, identified with the farm that
grew it, and every transaction required inspecting the actual sack. Chicago's
elevator operators in the 1850s changed this by pouring everyone's grain of a
given quality into the same bin and issuing a receipt for a quantity and a
grade rather than for a specific pile. Grain became fungible. That receipt is
the ancestor of every grain contract traded since, and it is why a buyer in
Cairo can purchase wheat from a screen without ever seeing it.

## What Futures Are Actually For

The Chicago Board of Trade opened in 1848, and within a couple of decades it
had turned the informal practice of agreeing a price now for delivery later
into a standardised instrument. The modern descendants are simple objects: a
corn, wheat, or soybean contract covers five thousand bushels, quoted in cents
per bushel, with delivery months keyed to the crop calendar. Corn trades
December after the fall harvest. Winter wheat trades July, just after cutting.
Soybeans trade November. The odd-looking gaps in the month sequence are not
arbitrary — they mark the rhythm of a growing season.

The popular impression is that futures markets exist for speculation. Their
working function is the transfer of price risk, and the clearest way to see it
is from inside the elevator. Suppose the elevator buys fifty thousand bushels
of that Kansas wheat for cash on the day it arrives. It now owns a physical
asset that could lose a dollar a bushel before it finds a buyer. So within
minutes it sells ten wheat futures contracts. It is long the physical grain and
short the paper. If wheat collapses, the grain in the bin loses value and the
short futures position gains almost exactly as much. The elevator is not
betting on the direction of wheat at all; it earns its living on handling and
storage fees, and the hedge is what protects that narrow margin from the market
moving underneath it.

The farmer does the mirror image. He can sell a forward contract to the
elevator in March, before the crop is even headed out, locking a price for
grain he does not yet have — which means he is now short physical wheat he owes
and needs the crop to come in. Or he can buy a put option, paying a premium for
a floor under the price while keeping the upside if a drought in the Black Sea
sends the market higher. Someone has to take the other side of all this
hedging, and that is what the speculative money in the market is doing. It is
paid, on average and over time, for absorbing risk that farmers and millers
would rather not hold.

## Basis, the Number That Actually Matters

Here is the thing that surprises people who follow grain prices on the news:
the farmer near Hutchinson is not paid the Chicago price. He is paid the
Chicago price plus or minus a local adjustment called basis, and in many years
the basis moves more than the futures do.

Basis is the difference between the local cash bid and the nearby futures
contract, and it is where all the physical reality lives. It contains the
freight cost from this particular elevator to the nearest real demand — a
flour mill, a rail terminal, a Gulf export house. It contains how full the
local bins are, because storage that does not exist cannot be rented at any
price. It contains whether railcars are available this week and what the
shuttle train auction cleared at. A wet harvest with full elevators and no
trucks drives the basis sharply negative: the grain is worth less here because
here is full. A feedlot region running short of corn in August will bid the
basis to a premium over futures, because the cattle eat whether or not there is
a railcar.

This is why a hedged farmer still has a decision left. He can lock the futures
in June and leave the basis open until December, hoping the local market
tightens as the harvest glut clears. He can sell the cash grain and roll his
hedge forward. He can do half of each. Commercial grain merchandisers spend
their careers trading basis rather than flat price, because flat price is a
global number set by Argentine rainfall and Chinese buying, while basis is a
local number set by the height of the pile behind the office.

River freight makes this vivid. A long stretch of low water on the Mississippi
means barges load lighter and tow smaller, freight rates spike, and interior
basis from Illinois to Nebraska collapses — sometimes dramatically — while the
Chicago futures board barely twitches. Nothing has changed about the world's
appetite for corn. Only the cost of getting it to a ship has changed, and the
farmer absorbs it.

## The Shape of the Curve

Look at the whole strip of contract months rather than just the front one and
another kind of information appears. If December corn trades meaningfully above
September corn, the market is offering to pay you for holding grain over those
months. Add up commercial storage, interest on the money tied up, insurance and
shrink, and you get what the trade calls full carry. When the spread between
months covers most of that cost, the market is saying: there is plenty, put it
away, we do not need it yet. Elevators fill, storage gets built, and the grain
sits.

When the near month trades above the deferred — an inverse — the message
reverses. The market is paying a premium for grain right now and refusing to
compensate anyone for storing it. That happens when stocks are genuinely tight,
and it is an instruction to empty the bins. Merchandisers read these spreads
the way a sailor reads a barometer. The flat price tells you what people think;
the spread tells you where the physical grain actually is.

## Reports, Rumours, and Chokepoints

The American grain calendar is punctuated by government statistics that move
hundreds of millions of dollars in a few seconds. The monthly world supply and
demand estimates revise production and consumption for every major exporter.
The spring planting intentions survey, the summer acreage report, and the
quarterly stocks numbers are each capable of limit moves. The figure traders
care about most is the ratio of ending stocks to annual use — the cushion
between the world and a shortage. When that cushion is comfortable, enormous
weather events barely register. When it is thin, a dry forecast for a single
week of corn pollination in Iowa can add a weather premium worth more than the
entire crop of a small country.

And then there are the chokepoints, which is where the abstraction of a global
price runs into geography. Most of the world's exportable grain squeezes
through a short list of places: the lower Mississippi and the Gulf, the Pacific
Northwest, the Paraná river down to the Argentine ports, the Brazilian
terminals at Santos and Paranaguá, and the Black Sea. A war, a drought that
lowers the Panama Canal's locks, a strike, or an export ban at any of these can
detach local prices from world prices for months. During the price spike of
2007 and 2008, several major rice exporters restricted shipments to protect
their own consumers, and the world rice price roughly tripled — not because the
crop had failed, but because the grain that existed stopped being allowed to
move.

The farmer on the scale in June knows none of this in detail and all of it by
instinct. He knows that his protein reading is a quarter point above the
discount threshold, that the basis is fifty under and should improve by
October, and that he has already priced a third of the crop. The ticket he
takes back to the truck is the local end of a very long chain, and the chain is
mostly made of promises about the future.
