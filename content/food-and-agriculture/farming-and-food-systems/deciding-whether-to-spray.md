# Deciding Whether to Spray

The sweep net is a canvas bag on a wire hoop, and the technique is unglamorous:
walk into the soybeans away from the field edge, swing the net through the top of
the canopy in a steady arc, twenty-five sweeps, then pinch the bag shut and look
at what is inside. There will be a mess of leaf fragments, a few spiders, some
beneficial wasps, and — the point of the exercise — a countable number of stink
bugs or bean leaf beetles. The scout writes the number down, walks a few hundred
meters, and does it again in a different part of the field. Four or five sets of
counts later there is an estimate, and the estimate is the basis for a decision
that costs real money either way.

That decision is the whole of pest management compressed into one moment. Most
of the discipline exists to make it a better decision: to establish what number
matters, to keep the number from rising in the first place, and to make sure that
whatever gets applied still works next year.

## The Threshold

The organizing idea arrived in the late 1950s from entomologists in California
who were watching insecticide programs fall apart and asked an obvious question
that nobody had formalized. Insects eat crops. How many insects does it take
before the damage is worth more than the cost of killing them?

That gives two numbers. The economic injury level is the pest density at which
the value of the damage equals the cost of control. The economic threshold — the
action threshold — sits below it, at the density where you should act, because
the population is still growing and by the time it reaches the injury level you
will be too late. Both numbers move. They go down when the crop is worth more
and up when the spray is expensive. They depend on growth stage: a soybean plant
can lose a startling amount of leaf area early and compensate for it, while the
same defoliation during pod fill comes straight off the yield. A plant with
plenty of water compensates better than a plant under drought stress.

The important consequence is that the presence of a pest is not a reason to
treat. A field with a countable population of aphids and a healthy population of
lady beetles and parasitic wasps may be heading toward a collapse of the aphids
without any help. Spraying it converts a self-correcting situation into a problem
that needs another spray in three weeks.

## How Spraying Makes Pests

Two failure modes recur often enough to have names. Resurgence is when the target
pest comes back worse than before, because the insecticide killed its predators
and parasitoids along with it and the pest, with a shorter generation time,
rebounds into an empty field first. Secondary pest outbreak is when a species
that was never a problem becomes one, for the same reason: it had been held in
check by natural enemies that are now gone.

The rice fields of Southeast Asia produced the textbook case. Heavy prophylactic
spraying against leaf-feeding caterpillars removed the spiders and mirid bugs
that had been quietly controlling brown planthopper, and the planthopper — a
sap-feeder that transmits viruses and can kill a field outright in patches
farmers call hopperburn — exploded. The eventual response in Indonesia was to
ban most broad-spectrum insecticides on rice outright and train farmers to scout
instead. Planthopper pressure fell.

Cotton in Peru's coastal valleys had already run the same experiment a
generation earlier, with new synthetic insecticides applied on a calendar,
resistance in the target, outbreaks of pests that had not existed before, spray
intervals shrinking, and yields collapsing anyway. The recovery came from backing
off: fewer applications, timed by counts, plus cultural measures and the
deliberate protection of natural enemies. This pattern — escalating inputs
producing worse results — got the name pesticide treadmill, and it is the thing
integrated pest management was invented to avoid.

## Resistance Is Just Selection

Spraying a field is a selection experiment with a very high selection pressure
and an enormous population. If one insect in a million carries a mutation in the
target site of the insecticide, or an overproduced detoxifying enzyme, that
insect's descendants inherit the field. Do it a few more times and the survivors
are the population.

The defenses are all about reducing that pressure. Rotate among chemistries with
genuinely different modes of action — not different brand names, which frequently
share an active site. Industry groups maintain numbered classification schemes
precisely so that a grower can check whether two products that look unrelated are
in fact the same group. Do not use the same group twice in a generation of the
pest. Treat only when the threshold says so, which means fewer selection events.
And leave survivors on purpose.

That last one is counterintuitive and is the logic behind refuges for
insect-resistant transgenic crops. A field of maize expressing a bacterial
insecticidal protein kills essentially every susceptible larva that feeds on it.
If a rare resistant individual emerges, it will mate with another resistant
individual and resistance spreads fast. Plant a block of non-transgenic maize
nearby and it produces a large population of susceptible moths, so the rare
resistant survivor almost certainly mates with a susceptible one, and the
offspring — carrying one copy of the resistance allele — still die on the
transgenic plants. The strategy works when the toxin dose is high enough to kill
the heterozygotes and when refuges are actually planted. Where refuge compliance
lapsed, resistance arrived on schedule.

## The Non-Chemical Half

Classical biological control has the most spectacular wins in the field's
history, usually against an invasive pest that arrived somewhere without its
natural enemies. Cottony cushion scale nearly ended California's citrus industry
in the 1880s until a ladybird beetle collected in Australia was released and
brought the scale under control within about two years — a result so decisive
that it is still cited as the beginning of the practice. A century later, cassava
mealybug spread across the African cassava belt and threatened the staple food of
a very large number of people; a small parasitic wasp found in the crop's South
American homeland was released across dozens of countries and suppressed the
mealybug more or less permanently. Both programs cost a fraction of what a
chemical campaign would have, and once established, they keep working without
anyone paying for them.

Sterile insect technique flips the arithmetic in a different way. Rear enormous
numbers of the pest, sterilize the males with radiation, and release them so that
wild females mate unproductively. It requires that the species mate once, that
sterile males compete well, and that you can outnumber the wild population —
conditions that hold for the New World screwworm, a flesh-eating fly whose
eradication from North and Central America is the technique's great success, and
which is held back at a narrow point in the isthmus by an ongoing release
program.

Pheromones do something subtler. Fill an orchard with synthetic female sex
pheromone from hundreds of dispensers per hectare and the males cannot locate
actual females; mating drops, and so does the next generation of fruit-boring
larvae. Mating disruption is now standard in apple and pear blocks against codling
moth. It is species-specific to the point of harmlessness, which also means it
does nothing about any other pest in the orchard.

Then there is everything a grower does before a pest arrives: resistant varieties,
planting dates chosen to miss a flight, destroying crop residue that harbors
overwintering larvae, and rotation. Rotation is powerful against specialists with
poor dispersal — and it is also a selection pressure like any other. Western corn
rootworm in parts of the American Midwest evolved a variant whose females will
lay eggs in soybean fields, so the larvae hatch into maize the following year and
the rotation no longer breaks the cycle.

## Weeds Are a Different Animal

Weed management runs on the same principles with different constants. There is no
sweep net; the relevant biology is the seed bank in the soil, which can hold
years of future infestations and drains slowly. The critical period of weed
control is a window early in the crop's life — before that, the crop is not yet
being suppressed; after it, the canopy closes and later emerging weeds cost
little yield. Miss the window and the loss is already locked in.

Herbicide resistance has followed exactly the trajectory the insect people warned
about. A decade and a half of near-exclusive reliance on a single systemic
herbicide across enormous acreages selected for resistant pigweeds that now
require a return to tillage, to residual herbicides applied before the weed
emerges, to diversified rotations, and in some places to hand crews walking the
rows. The countermeasure that is easiest to explain and hardest to sell is
diversity: multiple effective sites of action in a season, cover crops that
smother, narrow rows that close the canopy sooner, and zero tolerance for letting
survivors set seed.

## Over a Season

What this adds up to in practice is not a doctrine but a habit. Know which pests
your crop actually gets and when they show up. Get into the field on a schedule
and count. Keep records, because the thing you most want to know in July is what
happened in that field last July. Choose varieties that carry resistance where it
exists. Preserve the predators you already have by not spraying things that do
not need spraying. And when you do apply something, apply it at the right growth
stage, at the labeled rate, in a group you have not used recently.

The scout with the sweep net is expensive — someone has to walk the fields — and
the whole apparatus exists because that expense is smaller than the alternative.
Calendar spraying is easier. It also stops working.
