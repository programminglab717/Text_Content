# What It Takes to Farm a Fish

A salmon pen in a Norwegian fjord looks like almost nothing from the surface: a
ring of black plastic pipe, a narrow walkway around it, a feed barge moored off
to one side. Everything that matters is underneath — a net cone hanging twenty
or thirty meters down into cold water, and inside it a hundred thousand fish or
more moving in a slow torus, all swimming the same direction because that is
what salmon do when you give them a circle. Several thousand kilometers away, a
farmer in the Pearl River Delta walks the dike between two ponds and throws in
an armload of cut grass. Both of them are doing aquaculture. Almost nothing else
about the two operations is alike, and the distance between them explains most
of the arguments about whether farming fish is a good idea.

Aquaculture now supplies something close to half the aquatic animals people eat,
having grown from a rounding error in the 1970s to a rival of capture fisheries
within a single working lifetime. That growth happened because wild catch
plateaued and demand did not. But the industry did not invent itself from
scratch. It inherited a set of very old practices and grafted onto them a
twentieth-century feed industry, and the friction between those two inheritances
is where most of the interesting engineering lives.

## The Older Half of the Story

Chinese pond culture is at least two thousand years old and was built on an idea
that modern aquaculture keeps rediscovering: put species together that eat
different things. A traditional carp polyculture pond might hold grass carp,
which eat vegetation thrown in from the bank; silver carp, which filter
phytoplankton out of the water column; bighead carp, which take the slightly
larger zooplankton; and common or mud carp working the bottom for detritus and
benthic invertebrates. The grass carp's waste fertilizes the algae the silver
carp eats. Nothing is bought in except the grass, and in the classic
mulberry-dike system of the delta even that was integrated — mulberry leaves fed
silkworms, silkworm waste fed the fish, and pond mud was dug out and spread back
on the dikes to feed the mulberries.

Europe's version was the monastic carp pond, drained on a rotation and harvested
before feast days. Romans kept oysters in the shallow coastal lagoons near
Naples, transplanting spat onto bundles of brushwood, a technique recognizable
in shellfish farms today. None of this was a marginal curiosity; it fed people.
What it did not do was produce much protein per hectare, and it did not produce
salmon, shrimp, or any of the other high-value carnivores that drive the modern
trade.

## The Feed Problem

Here is the core tension. A grass carp eats plants. A salmon eats other fish. If
you farm animals at the top of a food chain, you have to supply the food chain,
and for most of the modern industry's history that meant fishmeal and fish oil
rendered from small, oily, schooling species — anchoveta off Peru, menhaden in
the Gulf of Mexico, sand eel and capelin in the North Atlantic. Feeding wild fish
to farmed fish to feed people is a hard thing to defend on arithmetic alone, and
critics were right to press on it.

The industry's answer has been partly efficiency and partly substitution.
Efficiency first: a salmon is a cold-blooded animal supported by water, so it
spends nothing on keeping warm and very little on holding itself up. It converts
feed to flesh at a ratio close to one to one on a dry-weight basis, which no
land animal approaches. A large fraction of its body is edible, too, where a
steer's is not. Substitution second: the composition of salmon feed has been
rebuilt over three decades, with soy protein concentrate, wheat gluten, corn
gluten, rapeseed oil, and increasingly insect meal or fermented single-cell
protein displacing most of the marine ingredients. Fishmeal has gone from the
bulk of a salmon diet to a minority component, often reserved for early life
stages when the fish are fussiest.

The hitch is the oil. The reason people are told to eat oily fish is the long-chain
omega-3 fatty acids EPA and DHA, and salmon do not make much of these themselves
— they accumulate them from their diet, which in the wild traces back to marine
microalgae. Swap fish oil for rapeseed oil and you get a fish that grows perfectly
well and has less of the thing that made it valuable. The current fixes run in
two directions: culturing the microalgae directly in fermenters to produce
omega-3 oil, and breeding oilseed crops engineered to make EPA and DHA in their
seeds. Both work. Both are more expensive than pumping oil out of an anchoveta.

## What Crowding Brings

Put a great many animals of one species in one place and their parasites find
them. For salmon the defining pest is the sea louse, a copepod that grazes on
skin and mucus and which, at high densities, both damages farmed fish and spills
infective stages onto wild smolts migrating past the farms. The response has
cycled through chemical bath and in-feed treatments, resistance to most of those
chemicals, and then a generation of non-chemical methods: freshwater baths,
warm-water delousing, mechanical brushing, and stocking pens with cleaner fish —
wrasse and lumpfish that pick lice off salmon as a living. Lice management is
now one of the largest single costs in the business, and in several jurisdictions
the louse count per fish is the regulated variable that determines how much a
farm is allowed to grow.

Disease has a parallel history with a happier turn. Norwegian salmon farming in
the 1980s used a great deal of antibiotics. Effective vaccines against the main
bacterial diseases, delivered by injection into each smolt before it goes to sea,
cut that use to a small fraction of its peak even as production multiplied. Viral
diseases have no such easy answer, and an outbreak of infectious salmon anemia
gutted Chilean production in the late 2000s, a reminder that a dense monoculture
with shared water is a good substrate for an epidemic.

The other costs are local and physical. Uneaten feed and feces fall out of a pen
and enrich the seabed beneath it, changing the sediment chemistry and the
community of worms and bacteria living in it. The standard mitigation is
fallowing: harvest the site, move the pens, and leave the bottom to recover for a
season before restocking. Escapes are a separate problem — farmed salmon that get
out can breed with wild populations and dilute locally adapted genetics, which is
why containment standards and net inspection regimes have tightened considerably.

## Shrimp, and the Shape of a Boom

Warmwater shrimp farming shows the same forces at a faster tempo. Ponds
proliferated along tropical coasts through the 1980s, often carved directly out
of mangrove, which is a poor trade: mangroves are nursery habitat for wild
fisheries and a considerable store of carbon, and the ponds that replaced them
frequently went sour within a decade as sediment and pathogens accumulated.
Taiwan's giant tiger prawn industry, briefly the world's largest, collapsed in
the late 1980s under a combination of disease and degraded pond conditions.
White spot syndrome virus swept through Asia and then the Americas in the 1990s
and is still the industry's most feared pathogen.

The recovery came from domestication. Most farmed shrimp today are Pacific white
shrimp descended from captive broodstock lines maintained free of specific
pathogens and bred for growth and disease tolerance, rather than from wild-caught
postlarvae of unknown health. Combine that with lined ponds, aeration, and
biofloc or partial-recirculation water management, and yields per hectare rose
enough that new mangrove clearing became a worse business decision than
intensifying existing ground.

## The Half That Takes Nutrients Out

Not all aquaculture adds nutrients to the water. Mussels, oysters, clams, and
scallops are filter feeders; they need no feed at all, only water with plankton
in it, and they remove nitrogen from coastal systems when harvested. Seaweed does
the same with dissolved nutrients and sunlight. These are the fastest-growing
categories by tonnage in several regions, and kelp culture in East Asia alone
represents a very large crop grown with no inputs beyond rope, boats, and labor.

Integrated multi-trophic aquaculture tries to put the two halves together —
finfish pens with mussel lines and seaweed downstream, so that waste from one
becomes feed for the next, which is the carp pond logic rebuilt at sea. It works
biologically. Commercially it has been slower, because the currents that carry
nutrients to the seaweed also disperse them below useful concentrations, and
because a single farm licensed for three products faces three sets of regulations
and three markets.

## Moving the Whole Thing Ashore

The most capital-intensive answer is to stop using the sea as both feed delivery
and waste disposal and build the fish a closed system. A recirculating
aquaculture system runs water through mechanical filtration to remove solids,
then through a biofilter where nitrifying bacteria convert excreted ammonia to
nitrite and then to far less toxic nitrate, then through degassing to strip
carbon dioxide, then back to the tanks with pure oxygen injected. Less than
five percent of the volume may be exchanged daily. Escapes are impossible,
parasites cannot get in, and the farm can sit next to the market rather than next
to a fjord.

The costs are electricity, oxygen, and risk concentration — a pump failure kills
the stock in hours, so everything runs with redundancy and alarms. There is also
a flavor problem: geosmin and related compounds produced by bacteria in the
system accumulate in fish flesh and taste like pond, so fish are purged in clean
water for days before harvest. Land-based systems have become the standard way to
raise salmon smolts and to grow species like barramundi and sturgeon. Whether
they can grow a full-sized salmon at a price people will pay remains, after
several expensive attempts, genuinely unsettled.
