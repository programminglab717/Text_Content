# How Livestock Systems Fit the Land

Something over a quarter of the planet's ice-free land is grazed by domestic
animals, and most of it will never grow a crop. It is too steep, too dry, too
cold, too rocky, too saline, or too short of a growing season. What it does grow
is grass and shrubs, which humans cannot digest. The entire edifice of pastoral
agriculture rests on a biochemical fact: no vertebrate makes an enzyme capable
of breaking the bond that holds cellulose together, and a few of them have
solved the problem by renting the capability from microbes.

A cow is a fermentation vessel with legs. Understanding how livestock systems
are organised, from a Mongolian herding family to a Kansas feedlot, means
starting with what is happening inside that vessel and then asking where the
feed comes from.

## The Fermentation Vat

A ruminant's stomach has four compartments, and the first and largest — the
rumen — is essentially a warm, anaerobic, continuously stirred tank holding
tens of gallons of chopped plant material and a dense population of bacteria,
protozoa, fungi, and archaea. The animal chews, swallows, later regurgitates
the coarse fraction and chews it again to reduce particle size, and buffers the
whole system with enormous volumes of bicarbonate-rich saliva. The microbes
ferment cellulose and hemicellulose into short-chain volatile fatty acids —
acetate, propionate, butyrate — which pass through the rumen wall and serve as
the animal's main energy supply. Glucose, for a ruminant, is something it
manufactures internally rather than absorbs.

Two consequences follow that matter enormously for how systems are built. First,
the microbes are themselves protein, and they wash steadily out of the rumen
into the true stomach and small intestine, where they are digested. A ruminant
therefore eats microbes for a living, which means the quality of protein in its
diet matters far less than it does for a pig, and it can convert non-protein
nitrogen — urea, for instance — into usable protein by way of the microbial
population. Second, fermentation generates hydrogen, and methanogenic archaea
dispose of it by combining it with carbon dioxide to make methane, which the
animal belches. That methane is both a real greenhouse gas and a loss of
several per cent of the feed's energy, which is why so much research effort goes
into suppressing it.

Pigs and poultry have no such apparatus. They are monogastric, they digest
starch, sugar, oil, and protein efficiently and fibre barely at all, and their
feed requirements overlap almost exactly with human diets. That single
distinction — fibre digesters versus starch digesters — sorts the world's
livestock systems more cleanly than any other.

## Rangelands and Mobility

Extensive pastoral systems occupy the land nobody else wants. They share a
common problem: forage that is wildly variable in space and time. In the Sahel,
the rains sweep north in one season and retreat in the next, and the green
follows them. In central Asian steppe, winter forage is buried under snow in
some years and accessible in others. In the Australian inland, a good season
might come twice a decade.

The classic response is movement. Transhumance — moving stock between seasonal
pastures on a regular annual cycle — appears independently wherever there is a
predictable seasonal contrast: alpine summer pastures in Switzerland, Norway,
and the Pyrenees, with herders and cheesemakers moving up in June and down in
September; the north-south migrations of Fulani and Tuareg herds; the vertical
movements of Andean camelid herders. Fully nomadic systems go further, keeping
no fixed base and reading conditions opportunistically.

Ecologists spent a long time misdiagnosing these systems. The standard range
management model assumed a stable carrying capacity and read heavy, mobile
stocking as overgrazing driven by the absence of private property. Closer study
of drylands, where rainfall variability rather than grazing pressure drives most
of the vegetation dynamics, produced a different picture: mobility is not
disorder, it is the strategy, and grazing rights on such land were usually
governed by detailed customary institutions rather than by open access.
Sedentarisation schemes that replaced movement with boreholes and fixed
paddocks frequently produced the degradation they were meant to prevent, by
concentrating animals permanently around water.

## The Mixed Farm, Which Is Still Most of Them

The largest share of the world's livestock is neither on open range nor in
confinement buildings. It is on mixed crop-livestock smallholdings, where a few
animals are one component of a farm that also grows food.

In such a system the animals eat what the crops cannot sell: rice straw, maize
stover, groundnut haulms, weeds from the field margins, kitchen scraps,
roadside grazing, stubble after harvest. Their outputs are equally plural.
Draught power for ploughing and carting was for most of agricultural history the
single most valuable thing a bullock or a buffalo produced. Manure returns
nutrients to the cropped land and, dried, serves as fuel where wood is short.
Milk, eggs, and the occasional sale provide a stream of cash and protein that
does not depend on the harvest. And the animals themselves are a savings
account: a household with three goats has stored value it can liquidate when
school fees fall due or the rains fail.

Mixed systems are also where nutrient cycling actually works. Crops export
nutrients from the field; animals grazing residues and fed crop byproducts
return a large share of them as manure, concentrated where the farmer puts it.
The efficiency is never complete — nitrogen escapes as ammonia, phosphorus
follows the manure and accumulates unevenly — but the loop exists. Its severing
is what makes specialised systems so nutritionally lopsided.

## Specialisation and the Feedlot

The industrial version disaggregates these functions and optimises each one
separately.

North American beef is the clearest example, because it is still visibly a
three-stage pipeline. Cow-calf operations run breeding herds on grass, often on
rangeland that supports nothing else, and calve in spring so that the cow's peak
milk demand lines up with peak forage. Weaned calves in autumn move to
stockers or backgrounders, which put frame and weight on them cheaply on
pasture, winter wheat, or crop residues. Only at the last stage do they enter a
feedlot for a few months of a high-concentrate ration — largely maize, distillers
grains, and a little roughage — where they gain rapidly and deposit the
intramuscular fat that the market pays for. The grass phase is much longer than
the grain phase; the grain phase produces most of the carcass weight gain.

Pigs and poultry went further and largely left the land altogether. Because
their feed is grain and oilseed meal, the animals can be sited wherever the
feed, the labour, the processing plant, and the regulatory climate are most
convenient. Genetics, controlled environment, precisely formulated diets and
tight disease management have driven feed conversion to figures that would have
been implausible a century ago: a modern broiler converts well under two
kilograms of feed into a kilogram of live weight, a pig around three, whereas a
grain-finished steer needs several times that.

What specialisation gains in conversion efficiency it loses in nutrient
geography. Feed is grown in one region, eaten in another, and the manure is left
where the animals are, in quantities far exceeding what the nearby land can
absorb. Surplus phosphorus and nitrogen leach and volatilise; the cropping
region buys synthetic replacements. The loop is not closed, it is cut and
extended into two open ends.

## Grass, Grain, and the Argument About Both

Grazing management is its own discipline and hinges on a simple plant-physiology
point: what harms a pasture is not the number of animals but the interval
between bites on the same plant. A grass defoliated once and then left alone
rebuilds leaf area from its remaining tissue and its root reserves and recovers
fully. The same grass bitten again three days later, before it has restored any
photosynthetic capacity, draws down reserves further, and repeated often enough
it thins out and is replaced by something less palatable. Continuous stocking at
low density allows exactly that, because animals return to the regrowth they
prefer. Rotational systems concentrate the animals into a small paddock for a
short period, force them to graze everything including the species they would
otherwise avoid, and then move them on and rest the paddock for weeks. Intensive
versions move stock daily behind a single strand of electrified wire.

Some of the larger claims made for intensively managed grazing — that it can
reliably reverse desertification or offset agriculture's carbon emissions
wholesale — remain genuinely contested, and the results in controlled
comparisons are far more site-dependent than the advocacy suggests. The narrower
claims are much better supported: rest periods matched to growth rate increase
forage production per hectare, improve species composition, and give a grazier
control over feed supply that a continuously stocked pasture does not.

That distinction matters for the broader argument about whether livestock belong
in the food system at all. The strong version of the case against is that
animals convert grain inefficiently and should be replaced by crops grown
directly for people. Where animals are eating maize and soy that humans could
eat, this is straightforwardly correct on calories and protein per hectare. But
a great deal of what livestock consume is not human-edible at all — rangeland
forage, straw, brewers' grains, oilseed cake left after the oil is pressed,
byproducts from milling and sugar processing — and on land that grows nothing
else, a ruminant is the only technology available for converting solar energy
into human food. The interesting question is therefore not whether livestock are
efficient in the abstract. It is which animals, eating what, in which
landscape.
