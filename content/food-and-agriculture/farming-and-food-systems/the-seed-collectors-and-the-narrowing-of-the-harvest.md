# The Seed Collectors and the Narrowing of the Harvest

Nikolai Vavilov spent much of the 1920s and 1930s on trains, mules, and small
boats, in Ethiopia and Afghanistan and Iran, in the Andes and across Central
Asia, filling cloth bags with seed. He was a botanist with an unusual idea
about where crops come from. Domesticated plants, he argued, are not evenly
spread across the world. Their wild relatives and their strangest, most varied
cultivated forms cluster in a small number of regions — the Ethiopian
highlands, the hills of the Fertile Crescent, the mountains of southern Mexico,
the high Andes, parts of China and India. Those, he proposed, were the places
where the crops had been domesticated, and they remained where the genetic
variation still lived.

The idea was mostly right, and it turned seed collecting from a curiosity into
a strategic activity. If the diversity of wheat is concentrated in a few valleys
in Anatolia and the Caucasus, then a breeder anywhere in the world who needs a
gene for rust resistance or cold tolerance knows where to look. Vavilov's
institute in Leningrad assembled what was for a long time the largest plant
collection anywhere — hundreds of thousands of samples, each one a population
of seeds from a particular place, carefully labelled.

He did not survive the decade. Trofim Lysenko, who rejected Mendelian genetics
as bourgeois and promised faster results than any breeder could honestly
deliver, gained Stalin's backing, and the scientists who contradicted him
became enemies of the state. Vavilov was arrested in 1940 and died of
starvation in a prison in Saratov in early 1943.

The collection nearly died with him. When the German army closed the ring
around Leningrad in September 1941 and the city began to starve, the institute
staff kept working in an unheated building full of edible seed — rice,
groundnuts, potatoes, wheat. Several of them died of starvation at their posts
without touching it. Whatever one makes of that decision, it was not
sentimental. They understood that the seeds represented varieties collected
from places they might never be collected from again, and that a bag of rice
eaten in 1942 was a bag of rice, while a bag of rice preserved was an option
for every plant breeder who came afterwards.

## What Uniformity Costs

The reason anyone bothers is that agriculture keeps demonstrating what happens
without diversity, and the demonstrations are expensive.

The potato reached Europe through a narrow bottleneck — a handful of
introductions from South America, propagated clonally — and by the 1840s much
of Ireland's crop was a single variety planted at enormous density on
subdivided smallholdings. When the water mould *Phytophthora infestans* arrived
from the Americas, there was no genetic variation in the field for it to run
into. The blight moved through the country in two seasons. The famine that
followed had political and economic causes that had nothing to do with plant
genetics, but the agronomic precondition was a monoculture of a crop with
almost no diversity in it.

A cleaner example, because it happened in a country with modern agriculture and
good records, is the American corn blight of 1970. By then, nearly all hybrid
seed corn in the United States was produced using a single cytoplasmic
male-sterility trait, which saved seed companies the enormous labour of
detasselling by hand. That trait came with an unnoticed vulnerability: a
particular race of the fungus that causes southern corn leaf blight could
attack plants carrying it. The blight swept north from the Gulf states and took
a substantial share of the national crop in a single season, with far heavier
losses in the south. The fix was straightforward once understood — go back to
detasselling and diversify the cytoplasm — but the episode made a point that
had been theoretical until then. Uniformity is efficient right up until it is
catastrophic, and the switch flips without warning.

Bananas make the same point in slow motion. The variety that dominated the
export trade for the first half of the twentieth century was largely destroyed
by a soil-borne fungal wilt, and the industry replaced it with a different
clone that happened to resist that strain. Every Cavendish banana in every
supermarket is genetically the same plant. A newer strain of the same disease
is now spreading through banana-growing regions, and there is no obvious
replacement waiting.

Set against this, the successes are quieter. In the early 1970s a virus was
devastating rice across South and Southeast Asia, and breeders screening
thousands of accessions found resistance in a wild rice collected years earlier
from a site in India — a site that had since been flooded by a dam. A single
gene from a population of scruffy wild grass, held in a collection because
somebody had bothered, underpinned the varieties that stopped the epidemic.
That is the argument for seed banks in one sentence, and it is why the 1970s
saw a wave of institutional collecting: the international centres for rice in
the Philippines, maize and wheat in Mexico, potatoes in Peru, sorghum and
millets in India, dryland crops in Syria.

## Cold Rooms, Field Plots, and the Seeds That Refuse to Freeze

A seed bank is less romantic than it sounds. Most of the work is drying,
freezing, counting, and growing things out again.

Seeds divide into two awkward categories. Orthodox seeds — the cereals, most
legumes, brassicas, many vegetables — tolerate being dried to very low moisture
and held near minus twenty degrees, where they remain viable for decades. These
are the easy ones, and they are what fills the shelves of a conventional
collection. Recalcitrant seeds cannot be dried without dying: cocoa, coffee,
avocado, mango, rubber, many tropical trees, and most oaks. They cannot be
banked in a freezer at all. Neither can the crops propagated from tubers or
cuttings — potato, cassava, sweet potato, banana, sugarcane — whose varieties
must be kept as living plants in field collections, or in test tubes as tissue
cultures, or cryopreserved as meristems in liquid nitrogen. Living collections
are vulnerable to everything that can happen to a field: hurricanes, disease,
funding cuts, a change of director.

Even the frozen collections are not static. Viability declines, and every
accession must eventually be planted out, grown, and re-harvested to renew the
seed. Regeneration is where collections quietly degrade. If you sow a hundred
seeds from a population of a thousand, you lose alleles. If two accessions
cross-pollinate in adjacent plots, you have contaminated both. If a variety
needs a specific day length to flower and you regenerate it at the wrong
latitude, you select for whatever plants happen to cope. Doing it properly is
slow, skilled, and permanently underfunded, which is why the condition of the
world's gene banks varies so widely.

The vault in the mountainside on Spitsbergen exists because of exactly these
failure modes. It is not a collection in its own right. It is a backup: sealed
boxes of duplicate seed, deposited by gene banks around the world, which remain
the property of the depositor and can only be withdrawn by them. The permafrost
gives it thermal inertia if the power fails. It went from a hypothetical
insurance policy to a working one when the international centre that had been
based in Aleppo, driven out by Syria's war, asked for its deposits back in
order to rebuild its collections elsewhere.

## Keeping Seeds in Circulation

The other half of conservation happens on farms, and it is not a museum
activity. A landrace kept in a freezer is frozen in the state it was collected
in. A landrace still grown in a valley continues to evolve — meeting new
pathogens, new weather, new tastes — which is the only thing that keeps its
resistance genes current.

This is what small networks of growers are doing when they maintain varieties
nobody sells. The exchange founded in Iowa in the 1970s began with two
plants — a tomato and a morning glory brought from Bavaria by a grandfather —
and grew into a membership that circulates thousands of vegetable varieties by
post. In the Peruvian Andes, a group of Quechua communities above the Urubamba
valley manages a landscape planted with hundreds of potato landraces, including
varieties repatriated from the international collection after being lost
locally. Neither effort is going to displace commercial seed, and neither is
trying to.

Saving seed also runs into the structure of the modern seed trade, and here the
technical and the legal overlap. A hybrid variety is deliberately not stable:
the uniformity and vigour of the first generation come from crossing two inbred
parents, and seed saved from that crop segregates into a variable and generally
disappointing second generation. This is not a trick, it is how heterosis
works, but it does mean a farmer growing hybrid maize buys seed every year as a
matter of biology rather than contract. Open-pollinated varieties can be saved
and resown indefinitely, which is why they persist in exactly the places where
buying seed annually is not realistic.

Layered on top are plant variety rights, patents on specific traits, and an
international treaty that tries to keep the major food crops inside a shared
pool where breeders can draw on material from anywhere in exchange for sharing
benefits. It is an uneasy settlement. The countries holding the most diversity
are generally not the countries with the largest seed industries, and the
question of what a valley in the Caucasus is owed when a gene from its wheat
ends up in a variety grown across three continents has never been answered to
anyone's satisfaction.

What has been settled, more or less, is the underlying point that Vavilov was
making from the back of a mule: the raw material of future crops already exists,
scattered and unglamorous, in places that are not under anyone's protection.
Collecting it is cheap. Replacing it is impossible.
