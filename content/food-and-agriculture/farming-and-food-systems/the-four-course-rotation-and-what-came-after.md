# The Four-Course Rotation and What Came After

For most of the medieval European farming year, a large share of the best
arable land grew nothing at all. Under the three-field system that spread
across northern Europe from roughly the ninth century, a village's plowland was
divided into three great open fields. One was sown in autumn to wheat or rye,
one in spring to barley, oats, or a pulse like peas or beans, and one was left
fallow. The fallow field was not neglected. It was plowed two or three times
over the summer to kill weeds, and the village's sheep and cattle were turned
onto it to pick over the stubble and drop manure. But it grew no grain, and in
an economy where grain was the difference between a lean winter and a fatal
one, surrendering a third of the cropland every year was a staggering
concession.

Farmers made it because they had learned, with no theory to explain the
learning, that land cropped continuously stopped paying. Yields on a field
sown to wheat year after year would hold up for a while and then sag, and no
amount of extra labour brought them back. Something was being used up. The
rest year restored it. What exactly was being used up would not be understood
for another thousand years, but the response was correct long before the
explanation arrived.

## What the Fallow Was Doing

Three things, mostly, and they did not have much to do with each other.

The first was nitrogen. Soil holds a large reserve of nitrogen locked into
organic matter — dead roots, old manure, the bodies of microbes — which plants
cannot use directly. Bacteria and fungi break that material down over time and
release ammonium and nitrate, which plants can. This mineralization runs
fastest in warm, moist, well-aerated soil, which is exactly what repeated
summer plowing produces. A fallow year was, in effect, a year spent letting the
soil digest itself and accumulate a flush of available nitrogen for the crop
that followed.

The second was weeds. Any cropping system selects for weeds that mimic the
crop's life cycle. Autumn-sown cereals grown in sequence build up autumn-
germinating grasses that emerge with the wheat and cannot be pulled out of it.
Bare fallowing, with the plow passing repeatedly through the season, cuts that
cycle apart by killing each successive germination before it sets seed.

The third was disease. Most crop pathogens are narrow specialists, able to
infect one host or a handful of close relatives. Their spores and resting
structures persist in soil and residue for a limited time. A year without the
host is a year of starvation for the pathogen population, and its numbers fall.

The fallow, then, was not one tool but three bundled into a single expensive
package. The whole history of rotation since has been a matter of unbundling
them.

## Turnips, Clover, and Norfolk

The unbundling began in the Low Countries. Flanders and Brabant by the later
Middle Ages were densely populated, short of land, and close to cities that
would pay for butter, cheese, and meat. Flemish farmers had begun growing
turnips and clover on ground that elsewhere would have lain fallow, and the
practice worked well enough that it travelled — into the Rhineland, into
England through the seventeenth century, carried by agricultural writers and
by Dutch drainage engineers working the fens.

Its most famous English expression is the Norfolk four-course: wheat, then
turnips, then barley, then clover, with a quarter of the farm in each phase
every year. The elegance is in how completely it replaces the fallow's three
functions. The turnips are a row crop, hoed between the rows through the
summer, so they deliver the weed control that repeated plowing used to provide
while also producing a bulky crop. Clover, as nobody yet knew, manufactures
nitrogen. And the alternation of broadleaf and cereal, autumn sowing and
spring sowing, root crop and grain, gives no soil pathogen two consecutive
years with its host.

The consequence that mattered most in the short run was the winter feed. Before
turnips, a northern European farm could carry through the cold months only the
animals it could feed on hay and straw, which meant slaughtering most of the
herd in autumn. Turnips and clover hay let far more stock overwinter. More
stock meant more manure, and manure returned to the arable land closed a loop
that had been leaking for centuries. Rotation and livestock were not two
separate improvements. They were one improvement with two visible ends.

English textbooks hand the credit for all this to Charles Townshend, the
eighteenth-century viscount and retired politician nicknamed Turnip Townshend,
and to Thomas Coke of Holkham after him. Both were genuine improvers and
tireless promoters. Neither invented much. They publicised, on large and
visible Norfolk estates, a set of practices that smaller farmers on the light
sandy soils of East Anglia had already been assembling for decades.

## Why It Worked

The nitrogen question was not settled until the 1880s, when German researchers
established that legumes grown in sterilised sand behaved like any other
nitrogen-starved plant, while the same legumes given a little unsterilised soil
grew normally and finished richer in nitrogen than they had started. The agent
was a bacterium living in the nodules on legume roots, trading fixed nitrogen
for sugars from the plant. Clover in a rotation is a fertiliser factory that
runs on sunlight.

But nitrogen is only the most quantifiable piece. Rotations also exploit
differences in rooting depth, so that a deep taproot crop pulls up nutrients
and water from below the reach of a shallow-rooted cereal and deposits some of
them near the surface when it dies. They vary the timing of soil disturbance,
which matters enormously to earthworms and to soil structure. They spread a
farm's labour and weather risk across the calendar rather than concentrating it
in one sowing and one harvest. And they change the chemistry and the phenology
of weed control every single year, which is the single most effective brake
anyone has ever found on the evolution of resistant weeds.

## Cheap Nitrogen and the Collapse of the Rotation

Then ammonia became cheap. The Haber-Bosch process, industrialised in the
first decades of the twentieth century and scaled enormously after the Second
World War on the back of surplus explosives capacity and cheap natural gas,
made nitrogen something a farmer could buy by the tonne. The constraint that
rotation had spent a millennium working around dissolved.

What followed was a rational, and in its own terms successful, simplification.
If nitrogen comes out of a tank, clover has to justify itself on other grounds.
If a farm's machinery, grain storage, drying capacity, marketing contracts, and
accumulated skill are all configured for maize, growing anything else carries a
cost. Livestock, freed from the need to be near the crops that fed them and
near the fields that needed their manure, concentrated in specialised regions
and specialised buildings. The manure loop broke: nutrients piled up where the
animals were and had to be bought in where the crops were. Across large parts
of the American Midwest the rotation shrank to two crops, maize and soybeans,
and in places to one.

## What the Pests Remembered

The pathogens and insects had not forgotten, and they adapted to the new
simplicity with unsettling speed.

The western corn rootworm is the clearest case. Its larvae eat maize roots and
die without them, so alternating maize with soybeans had long been a complete
and free control measure. Two separate evolutionary answers appeared. In parts
of the northern Corn Belt, populations emerged whose eggs remain dormant
through two winters instead of one, hatching into a maize crop that a
one-year rotation had made safe. In Illinois and Indiana, a different variant
appeared whose females will lay eggs in soybean fields, so that the larvae
emerge the following spring into exactly the maize they need. Rotation, in
those regions, stopped working.

Soybean cyst nematode tells a similar story: cysts persist in soil for years,
resistance in soybean varieties has leaned heavily on a narrow genetic base,
and nematode populations have shifted to overcome it. Herbicide-resistant
waterhemp and Palmer amaranth in North America, and blackgrass in northwestern
Europe, all built their numbers inside cropping systems narrow enough that the
same few chemistries were applied at the same season year after year.

Meanwhile some of what rotation did turned out to be hard to replace with a
purchase. Continuous maize yields less than maize following a different crop
even when it is fed all the nitrogen it can use — a gap agronomists call the
rotation effect, and one that stubbornly persists after the accountable inputs
are equalised. It seems to come from soil biology: the microbial community, the
mycorrhizal networks, the balance of root pathogens against the organisms that
antagonise them.

Long-term trials in the Midwest and in northern Europe comparing two-year
rotations against three- and four-year sequences that include a small grain and
a forage legume have found the longer rotations matching or beating the short
ones on the main cash crop's yield while using a fraction of the purchased
nitrogen and far less herbicide. The obstacle is not agronomic. It is that a
farm built for two crops has no market, no machinery, and no livestock for the
third and fourth.

Which returns the argument to Flanders. The reason turnips and clover took hold
there was never that they were agronomically clever, though they were. It was
that a dairy cow and a fat lamb could be sold in Ghent. Rotation has always
needed somewhere for the rotation crop to go.
