# The Working Year of a Dairy Herd

At half past four in the morning the vacuum pump starts, and the sound carries
across the yard before any light does. Cows that were lying down in the barn
get up without being asked and begin drifting toward the holding pen. They have
done this twice a day for years and they have opinions about the order in which
they enter. In the pit, someone is running warm water, pulling on gloves, and
checking that the first units are dropping vacuum properly.

What happens next depends on a reflex that most people who drink milk have never
heard of. A cow's udder does not hold milk the way a jug holds it, ready to be
poured. Most of it sits inside microscopic alveoli, sealed behind a ring of
muscle, and no amount of suction will extract it. Washing and stripping the
teats sends a signal to the brain; the posterior pituitary releases oxytocin
into the bloodstream; a minute later the smooth-muscle cells wrapped around
each alveolus contract and force the milk down into the teat cistern, where a
machine can reach it. That window lasts a matter of minutes and then closes.
Adrenaline blocks it outright, which is why a cow frightened in the parlour
milks out badly and why good dairy operations are conspicuously calm places.
Every routine in a modern parlour — the preparation, the timing between
stimulation and attachment, the quiet — exists to catch that window.

## The Lactation Curve

Everything about a dairy is scheduled by the fact that a cow gives milk because
she has had a calf.

She calves, and within a few days milk output climbs steeply, peaking somewhere
around six to eight weeks afterward. From that peak it declines slowly and
steadily for the rest of the lactation. How slowly is called persistency, and
it is worth more to a farm's total output than the height of the peak, because
the area under the curve is what gets sold. A standard lactation is reckoned at
305 days, after which the cow is dried off — milking simply stops, the udder
involutes and regenerates its secretory tissue, and she gets roughly two months
off before calving again.

Gestation in cattle runs about nine months and a week. To calve once a year, a
cow must be bred back around three months after her last calving, which is
exactly when she is producing most heavily and is at her most metabolically
stretched. There is a deliberate voluntary waiting period of a couple of months
after calving before anyone attempts to breed her, both to let the uterus
recover and because conception rates in early lactation are poor. That leaves a
narrow window. Herds that miss it end up with calving intervals stretching
toward fourteen and fifteen months, which means more of the herd sitting on the
flat tail of the curve at any given time. Reproduction, not milk, is what most
dairy managers actually spend their attention on.

## Three Weeks Either Side of Calving

The transition period — roughly the three weeks before calving and the three
after — is where a lactation is won or lost.

The problem is arithmetic. A fresh cow's energy demand for milk rises faster
than her appetite recovers from calving, so for several weeks she is in negative
energy balance and mobilising her own body fat to make up the difference. Do
that gently and it is normal. Do it too fast — typically because she was too fat
at calving, or is not eating — and the liver is overwhelmed by circulating fatty
acids and starts producing ketone bodies. Clinical ketosis follows: the cow goes
off feed, which deepens the deficit, which produces more ketones. A rumen that
empties as feed intake falls leaves room for the abomasum to migrate and trap
gas, and a displaced abomasum is a surgical problem.

Calcium is the other trap. Producing colostrum abruptly drains calcium from the
blood at a rate the cow's regulatory system has not been primed for, because
during the dry period she barely needed any. Acute hypocalcaemia is milk fever:
the cow goes down, unable to stand, because muscle cannot contract without
calcium. The standard prevention is counterintuitive and effective — feed
anionic salts in the last weeks of pregnancy to slightly acidify the blood,
which keeps the calcium-mobilising machinery switched on and ready. Subclinical
cases, which never put a cow down but weaken uterine and gut muscle tone, are
far more common and quietly cause much of the retained placenta, displaced
abomasum, and poor fertility that follows.

A cow that transitions badly will underperform for the whole lactation and
often gets culled. A cow that transitions well may not need to be looked at
again for months.

## In the Parlour

Parlour design is a study in moving cows past a fixed number of milking units
as fast as possible. In a herringbone, cows stand at an angle along both sides
of an operator pit and are milked from the side. In a parallel or side-by-side,
they stand square to the pit and are milked between the hind legs, which puts
them closer together and shortens the operator's walk. A rotary is a carousel:
cows step on at one point, are attached, ride around, and are detached and
released as they come back, which converts milking into a continuous process
rather than a batch one and suits very large herds.

Whatever the layout, the unit itself works the same way. Four liners — rubber or
silicone sleeves inside rigid shells — enclose the teats, with constant vacuum
inside the liner drawing milk out and a pulsating vacuum in the space between
liner and shell. That pulsation collapses the liner around the teat roughly once
a second, closing it and relieving the pressure. Without it, continuous vacuum
would congest the teat with blood and lymph within a minute and injure it. The
rest phase is not optional; it is most of what keeps teat ends healthy.

Udder infection is the dominant health cost of dairying, and most of it is
transmitted either from the environment where the cow lies or from cow to cow
through the milking machine. The countermeasures are unglamorous and effective:
clean, dry bedding; pre-dipping teats in disinfectant and drying them with
individual towels; post-dipping to seal the teat canal, which stays open for
some time after milking; and rigorous machine maintenance. Infection is
monitored through somatic cell count — the concentration of white cells in the
milk — which rises as the immune system responds. Bulk tank counts are tested at
every collection and both regulators and processors set ceilings, with the
European limit the tighter of the two major regimes, so a herd with a chronic
mastitis problem loses money on price before it loses it on discarded milk.

Robotic milking changes the social arrangement more than the mechanics. Cows
enter a box voluntarily, drawn by a small feed reward, are identified by a
collar transponder, and a laser-guided arm finds and attaches each cup
individually. Cows choose their own intervals, which for high producers means
three visits a day instead of two, and the system weighs, tests conductivity,
and logs each quarter separately. It substitutes capital and data for labour,
which is why it has spread fastest in northwest Europe, where herds are modest
and hired labour is scarce and expensive.

## From Udder to Tank

Milk leaves the cow at body temperature, which is an excellent incubator, and
almost everything that happens in the next two hours is about getting it cold.
It is pulled through the pipeline into a receiving jar, pumped through a plate
cooler — where it runs counter-current against cold water, often well water that
is then reused for stock drinking — and into an insulated bulk tank with
refrigeration and a slow agitator. Target temperature is a few degrees above
freezing, reached fast. A tanker collects every day or two, and the driver
samples and often smells each tank before pumping, because one farm's spoiled
milk can condemn an entire load.

What the milk is worth depends on what is in it. Milk is mostly water, with
lactose, fat, and protein making up the solids that processors actually want.
Most modern payment systems price on components rather than volume, which is why
Jerseys, producing less milk but richer in fat and protein, remain competitive
with Holsteins in cheese-oriented markets, and why nutritionists spend so much
effort on the rumen conditions that govern butterfat.

## Two Ways to Build a Dairy

The industry has converged on two very different models, and the difference
comes down to whether the cow walks to the feed or the feed is brought to the
cow.

The pasture model, developed most thoroughly in New Zealand and Ireland,
compresses calving into a few weeks in early spring so that the herd's peak
demand lines up with the peak of the grass growth curve. Cows harvest their own
feed, walking to a fresh break of pasture after each milking. The entire herd
dries off together in winter, and the farm shuts down for a couple of months.
Costs per litre are low, capital requirements are modest, and the whole system
is hostage to the weather: a dry summer or a late spring hits every cow at once.

The confinement model, dominant in North America and much of continental Europe,
houses cows year-round and delivers a total mixed ration — forage, grain,
protein meal, minerals, and byproducts blended so thoroughly that a cow cannot
sort out the parts she likes. Intakes are higher, yields per cow are much
higher, calving is spread across the year so the milk supply is flat, and the
ration can be adjusted weekly. It also requires far more capital, far more feed
purchased or grown, and constant vigilance against rumen acidosis, which is what
happens when a high-starch ration outruns the buffering capacity of a cow's
saliva.

Both models face the same replacement arithmetic. A meaningful fraction of the
herd leaves every year through infertility, mastitis, lameness, or age, and
those cows must be replaced by heifers that have been fed for two years before
producing anything. Sexed semen made this manageable: breed the best cows to
sexed dairy semen to produce the heifers you need, and breed the rest to beef
bulls so the surplus calves are worth something as beef. The change happened
quickly and quietly, and it altered the economics of dairy calves across the
entire industry within about a decade.
