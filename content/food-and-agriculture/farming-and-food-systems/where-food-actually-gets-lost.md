# Where Food Actually Gets Lost

In a good year for green beans, some fields never get picked. The crop is
there, the plants are healthy, and a crew could be in the rows by morning. But
the price has fallen below what the picking and packing and hauling would cost,
so the grower disks the field under and plants something else. Nothing has
spoiled. Nobody has been careless. A perfectly edible crop has simply been
removed from the food system by arithmetic.

That field is a useful corrective to the way food waste is usually discussed,
because it fits none of the standard images. It is not a half-eaten restaurant
plate, not a forgotten bag of spinach liquefying in a drawer, not a supermarket
skip behind the loading bay. It is a decision, and a rational one at the level
of the individual making it. Most of what gets called food waste looks like
that when you get close to it.

The headline number — that something close to a third of food produced for
human consumption is lost or wasted — has been repeated so often that it has
acquired a false solidity. It came out of a global estimate assembled more than
a decade ago from patchy national data, and the people who produced it were
candid about its limits. Measuring waste is genuinely hard: it happens in
millions of private places, nobody has an incentive to report it accurately,
and the definitions shift depending on whether you count inedible parts, animal
feed diversion, or crops left in fields. The rough magnitude is probably right.
The precision is not. What matters more than the headline figure is that the
losses fall in completely different places depending on where you are, and that
the fixes for one place do almost nothing for the other.

## Two Problems Sharing a Name

The useful split is between loss and waste. Loss happens upstream — in the
field, in transit, in storage, at the mill or the packhouse — before the food
reaches a retailer or a household. Waste happens downstream, in shops,
restaurants, and kitchens, after food has been bought by somebody who intended
to eat it.

Low- and middle-income countries lose most of their food upstream. High-income
countries waste most of theirs downstream. The total percentages come out
oddly similar, which is what makes the global average so misleading. A tomato
that rots in an unrefrigerated truck in a hot country and a tomato thrown out
of a London fridge are counted the same way and have essentially nothing in
common as problems.

Upstream loss is an infrastructure story, and infrastructure stories are
tractable. Grain stored in a sack on a mud floor gets eaten by weevils and
rodents and goes mouldy in humid weather; grain in a sealed hermetic bag or a
metal silo does not, and the cost difference is small. Mangoes picked in the
heat of the day and stacked in a pickup lose days of shelf life before they
reach a market; the same fruit cooled within a few hours lasts a week longer.
Milk that cannot be chilled within two hours of milking has to be sold within
walking distance or turned into something else. There are no clever behavioural
insights required here. What is missing is cold rooms, roads that survive the
rainy season, reliable electricity, packhouses, crates instead of sacks, and
enough market information that a farmer is not harvesting blind.

These fixes also pay for themselves in a way that downstream interventions
rarely do, because every unit of loss avoided upstream is income the farmer did
not receive. Reducing post-harvest loss is not primarily a sustainability
programme. It is a rural income programme that happens to reduce waste.

## The Wealthy Kitchen

Downstream waste is harder, because it is not a failure of equipment. It is the
predictable output of an economy where food is cheap relative to time.

Consider the calculation a household in a rich country makes without ever
articulating it. Food is a small and shrinking share of income. An hour of
labour buys a great deal of it. Meanwhile the cost of running out — an extra
trip to the shop, an improvised dinner, a disappointed child — is paid in time
and hassle, which are the scarce goods. So people buy the larger pack, keep a
buffer, cook generously, and accept that some of it will be thrown away. At the
level of the individual shopper this is not wastefulness. It is a sensible
allocation of an abundant resource to protect a scarce one. Which is exactly
why moralising about it works so poorly.

The same logic scales up. A supermarket that runs out of bread at four in the
afternoon loses the sale and, worse, trains the customer to shop elsewhere; a
supermarket that throws away a tray of unsold loaves loses only the cost of the
loaves. Full shelves are a marketing expense with a waste stream attached. A
caterer sizing a buffet for two hundred people plans for the possibility that
two hundred and thirty show up hungry. A restaurant portion is sized to look
like value. Every actor is responding sensibly to the asymmetry between the
cost of too little and the cost of too much, and the aggregate result is a
system engineered to overshoot.

Household waste is the largest single category in most rich countries, larger
than retail and food service combined, and it is dominated by a short list of
items: bread, salad and fresh vegetables, milk, and prepared leftovers. These
are the things bought optimistically and perishing quickly. It is not, mostly,
the expensive food. People rarely throw out steak.

## What the Date on the Package Means

A genuinely fixable slice of downstream waste comes from date labels, which in
most countries carry two quite different meanings under confusingly similar
wording.

A "use by" date is a safety instruction. It appears on foods where pathogens
can grow to dangerous levels without any obvious sign — fresh meat, fish,
ready-to-eat products, some dairy. That date should be respected, because your
senses will not warn you.

A "best before" date is a quality estimate, set by the manufacturer, generally
conservatively, and it says nothing about safety. Dried pasta, tinned goods,
flour, rice, biscuits, and most hard cheeses remain entirely edible well past
it, sometimes by years. Surveys consistently find that a large fraction of
shoppers treat the two labels identically, and a great deal of perfectly good
food is binned by people trying to be responsible.

Several retailers have responded by removing date labels altogether from items
where they serve no purpose — whole fruit and vegetables, most obviously, where
a person's eyes are a better instrument than a printed date. Some have shifted
milk from "use by" to "best before" on the grounds that sour milk announces
itself. These changes are cheap, quick, and unusually well-evidenced, which
makes them rare in this field.

## What Actually Moves the Number

Three things have measurably reduced waste at scale, and none of them is a
public awareness campaign in isolation.

The first is making the waste visible and expensive to the entity producing it.
South Korea built a national system in which household food waste is separated
and charged for by volume or weight, and the quantity generated fell sharply
once people were paying per bag. When businesses are required to separate
organics rather than dumping them into general refuse, the disposal cost
suddenly appears on a line item, and managers start paying attention to their
ordering.

The second is redistribution infrastructure: the mundane logistics of moving
surplus from where it exists to where it can be eaten. France attracted a lot
of attention for requiring larger supermarkets to offer unsold edible food to
charities rather than destroying it, but the binding constraint is usually not
willingness. It is refrigerated vans, volunteers, insurance, and the fact that
surplus appears at unpredictable times in unpredictable quantities. The apps
that sell end-of-day bakery boxes at a discount work because they solved a
matching problem, not a moral one.

The third is feeding it to animals, which was how every society handled food
scraps until recently. Much of Europe banned swill feeding after disease
outbreaks traced to contaminated feed, and the ban is defensible on those
grounds. But Japan and South Korea both built regulated systems that heat-treat
food waste into licensed animal feed, which suggests the choice is not
straightforwardly between safety and recovery.

One argument should be made carefully, because it is made carelessly all the
time. Reducing waste does not directly feed hungry people. The food thrown out
in a wealthy suburb cannot be transported to a famine, and hunger in most of
the world is a problem of income and access rather than of aggregate calories,
which exist in sufficient quantity. What waste reduction genuinely saves is
everything that went into producing the food and never got used: the irrigation
water, the nitrogen fertiliser and its considerable carbon cost, the diesel,
the land that was cleared, the labour. Rotting food in a landfill also
generates methane, a potent greenhouse gas, which is why diverting organics
from landfill has climate benefits that have nothing to do with whether anyone
eats the food.

That is a serious case, and it is stronger than the one usually made. It also
points somewhere specific. If the aim is to stop wasting the resources embedded
in food, the highest-leverage interventions are upstream, in places where a
concrete floor and a cold room would keep a harvest from rotting, and in the
parts of the rich-world chain where waste is a business decision rather than a
personal failing. The half-eaten sandwich is the most visible part of the
problem and very nearly the least important.
