# The Long Chain Behind a Cup and a Bar

A heap of wet cocoa beans piled on banana leaves at the edge of a farm in the
Ivorian interior is warm to the touch. Not warm from the sun — warm from the
inside, because the pulp clinging to the beans is fermenting, and the heat of
that fermentation is climbing toward fifty degrees in the middle of the pile.
The farmer will turn the heap after a couple of days to get the outside in, and
after five or six days he will spread the beans on raised mats to dry in the
sun. Nothing he does in the following year will matter as much as what he does
in this one week.

Coffee works on a similar clock. A ripe cherry picked in the Colombian
highlands has a window of hours, not days. If it is going through the washed
process, it needs to reach a depulper the same afternoon, get its sticky
mucilage removed by a controlled overnight ferment, be washed clean, and then
dry slowly and evenly to around eleven percent moisture. Cherry left in a sack
overnight in the heat begins to ferment on its own terms, unevenly, and the
result is a cup with a sour, fermenty taint that no roaster can remove.

Both crops share an awkward structure. The flavour that the end consumer pays
for is created almost entirely at origin, in the first week after harvest, by
someone earning very little. The value that the end consumer pays is captured
almost entirely at destination, months later, by processors and brands in
countries that cannot grow either plant. Understanding why that gap is so wide
and so stubborn means following the beans.

## What Fermentation Is Doing

Raw cocoa beans taste appalling — bitter, astringent, vegetal. Fermentation is
not a preservation step but a chemical one. As yeasts consume the sugars in the
pulp they produce alcohol; lactic and then acetic acid bacteria take over as
the pile heats and the pulp drains away; the alcohol and acid penetrate the
bean and kill the embryo. That death matters. Once the seed's cell walls break
down, its enzymes reach proteins and polyphenols they were previously separated
from, and they generate the amino acids and sugars that will become chocolate
flavour when a roaster applies heat months later. Under-fermented beans make
flat, bitter chocolate. Over-fermented beans make hammy, ashy chocolate.
Neither can be corrected downstream.

Coffee's processing choices are more visible in the cup. Washed coffee, with
the fruit stripped off before drying, tastes cleaner and more acidic and lets
varietal and terroir character show. Natural process, where the whole cherry is
dried intact with the seed inside it, pushes fruit and body forward and
tolerates far less error — a natural drying badly is a natural going rotten.
The intermediate styles that leave part of the mucilage on land somewhere
between. Brazil and Ethiopia have long dried coffee whole for climatic and
historical reasons; the recent fashion for naturals in specialty markets is
partly a rediscovery of that and partly a set of new, tightly managed
techniques.

Drying is where most quality is lost on both crops. Cocoa dried too fast on hot
tarmac seals its surface and traps acidity inside. Cocoa dried too slowly in
humid weather grows mould, and mouldy beans are a defect graders count
precisely. Coffee parchment dried on bare earth picks up earthy flavours and
uneven moisture that will show up as stale cups six months later. Raised beds,
shade cloth, and a moisture meter are cheap interventions with large effects,
which is why so much origin-level development work consists of exactly that
rather than anything to do with agronomy.

## Where the Price Comes From

Once dried, both commodities enter pricing systems that the farmer has no
influence over, but the two systems are built completely differently, and the
contrast is instructive.

Coffee is priced off futures. Arabica trades on an exchange in New York under a
contract the trade simply calls the C, and robusta trades on a London contract.
Physical coffee is then bought and sold at a differential to that number: so
many cents over the C for a washed Central American of a given altitude and
screen size, so many under for a lower grade. The differential carries origin,
quality, certification, and the relative desperation of buyer and seller. For
the bottom half of the market, the C is the price, and it is set by Brazilian
weather, currency moves, and the positioning of investment funds who have never
seen a coffee tree.

That exposure has produced genuine disasters. When the international agreement
that had regulated export quotas broke down at the end of the 1980s, and
Vietnam then expanded robusta production with extraordinary speed through the
1990s, the world price fell to levels that did not cover the cost of picking.
The crisis of the early 2000s hollowed out coffee communities in Central
America and East Africa, and a great deal of the specialty movement's structure
— direct relationships, quality premiums, prices negotiated on cup score rather
than on the C — was built in reaction to it.

Cocoa in West Africa works the other way round. Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire, which
between them grow around sixty percent of the world's cocoa, both run state
marketing institutions that sell much of the crop forward on international
markets before the season starts and then announce a fixed farmgate price that
licensed buyers must pay. A Ghanaian farmer knows in advance what he will
receive per bag, which is a real form of insurance and one that coffee growers
do not have.

The limitation showed itself starkly during the price surge of the past few
years, when a combination of disease, erratic weather, ageing trees, and years
of under-investment cut West African output sharply and world cocoa prices rose
to levels without precedent. Because the crop had been sold forward at old
prices and the farmgate rate was fixed, the windfall largely did not reach the
farms in the first season. Beans crossed borders informally toward whichever
country had raised its price, which is what happens when an administered price
and a market price diverge by enough. The two governments had earlier
introduced a joint premium on every tonne exported, explicitly intended to lift
farmer incomes toward a living level; buyers responded in part by squeezing the
quality differentials they paid on top, which is a reminder that a chain with
few buyers and millions of sellers tends to find the give somewhere.

## The Middle, Which Is Where the Beans Go Quiet

Between the drying mat and the port sits a layer that almost nobody sees. In
cocoa it is the itinerant buyer with a scale and a motorbike, then the licensed
buying company's shed, then the exporter's warehouse where beans from thousands
of farms are bulked, graded by cut test, fumigated, and stuffed into
containers. In coffee it is the wet mill, the dry mill that hulls the parchment
off and sorts green beans by size, density, and colour, the exporter's cupping
lab, and the same container.

Bulking is the step that destroys information. Once beans from four hundred
smallholdings are in one pile, nobody can say which farm any of it came from,
which is why traceability schemes are expensive rather than merely
bureaucratic. It also means the farmer's payment reflects an average rather
than his own effort. A grower who ferments carefully for seven days and one who
sells wet beans to a trader on day two can receive the same price per kilogram
at the same buying station. Fixing that requires paying differentially at the
point of collection, which requires assessing quality there, which requires
equipment and trained staff at thousands of villages. The specialty coffee
sector solved a version of this problem by shortening the chain dramatically
and paying enormous premiums for small volumes. It has not been solved at
commodity scale in either crop.

Europe's newer rules requiring importers to demonstrate that cocoa and coffee
did not come from recently deforested land are forcing the issue from the other
end, because demonstrating it means mapping individual plots. Whether the cost
of that mapping is borne by buyers or pushed back onto cooperatives and farmers
will determine a good deal about who stays in these chains.

## Where the Value Lands

Cocoa beans arriving at a grinder are roasted, cracked, and winnowed to nibs,
then ground into a thick liquor and pressed. The press splits the liquor into
cocoa butter and a dry cake that is milled into powder. Butter is the expensive
half, because it gives chocolate its melt; powder goes into biscuits, drinks,
and ice cream. Processors live on the relationship between what beans cost and
what butter and powder fetch together, and that relationship, not the bean
price, drives their decisions about how much to grind.

Then the chocolate is made, and the proportions become revealing. A mass-market
milk bar is mostly sugar and milk solids with a minority of cocoa ingredients
in it. Add packaging, advertising, refrigerated distribution, retail margin,
and tax, and the share of the final shelf price that returns to the person who
turned that heap under the banana leaves is small — a single-digit percentage
in most estimates. Coffee is starker still, because a café is selling labour,
rent, and atmosphere with a few grams of an agricultural product dissolved in
it.

None of this is a conspiracy; it is what happens when a chain adds most of its
measurable value in high-cost economies and buys its raw material in a
competitive market from millions of atomised producers. But it does explain why
the farm end of both crops is so fragile. Cocoa trees in West Africa are old,
many planted decades ago, and replanting means several years without income
from that land — a proposition no household with no savings will accept without
help. Coffee rust swept Central America a little over a decade ago and
destroyed the harvests of growers who could not afford fungicide, at a moment
when prices were too low to justify buying any. Arabica needs cool nights, and
the altitude band where it grows well keeps creeping upslope.

The heap cools. In two weeks it will be a sack of dry beans on a scale, and the
farmer will find out what the season decided for him.
