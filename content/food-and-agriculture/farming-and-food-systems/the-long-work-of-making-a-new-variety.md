# The Long Work of Making a New Variety

In late spring a wheat breeder walks a nursery that looks, to anyone else, like a
badly kept field. The rows are short — a meter or two each — and no two are the
same. One is a hand taller than its neighbors. One has gone pale at the tips.
One is already flowering while the row beside it is a week behind. The breeder
carries a notebook and a bundle of glassine bags, and the work of the morning is
to look at perhaps two thousand of these rows and decide which fifty are worth
another year. Nearly everything gets discarded. That is not failure; discarding
is the method.

A crop variety is a peculiar kind of artifact. It is designed, but not
manufactured. It has to perform in weather nobody can specify in advance, on
soils that differ across a single field, against pests that are themselves
evolving. And the design cycle is punishingly slow, because the fastest way to
find out whether a plant is any good is to grow it, which takes a season, and
one season tells you almost nothing because one season is one weather.

## What Domestication Had Already Done

Breeders inherited crops that had been reshaped so thoroughly that several of
them can no longer survive without people. The wild grasses ancestral to wheat
and barley shatter — the seed head breaks apart at maturity and scatters, which
is how a grass makes a living. Somewhere in the early Neolithic, plants carrying
a mutation that kept the head intact were disproportionately harvested by
sickle, carried home, and sown, and within a relatively short span of
generations the non-shattering type dominated the fields. The same unconscious
selection removed most seed dormancy, enlarged seeds, reduced branching, and
synchronized ripening.

Maize is the most extreme case. Its wild relative, teosinte, is a many-branched
plant bearing small spikes of hard-cased kernels that a person can barely chew.
Changes at a handful of key genes — one controlling branching architecture,
another the hard glume that encased each kernel — converted that plant into a
single thick stalk bearing a cob whose seeds are naked, large, and glued firmly
in place. A maize cob dropped in a field does not produce a stand of maize. It
produces a rotting clump. The plant and the farmer are locked together.

## Selection Before Anyone Knew Why It Worked

For most of agricultural history, improvement meant saving seed from the plants
you liked. That is mass selection, and it works slowly but genuinely, which is
why landraces adapted to particular valleys and particular pests exist at all.
The nineteenth century added a crucial refinement: the progeny test. Rather than
judge a plant by its own appearance, sow its offspring in a row of their own and
judge the row. The Vilmorin family in France used this approach on sugar beet,
selecting individual roots, testing their descendants for sugar content, and
raising the crop's sugar concentration over decades from something marginal to
something an industry could be built on. It was, in effect, quantitative genetics
practiced before genetics had a name.

When Mendel's work was rediscovered after 1900, the practice acquired a theory,
and plant breeding became a profession with experiment stations attached to it.
Breeders learned to make deliberate crosses: emasculate a flower, dust it with
pollen chosen for a reason, bag it, and label it. The wheat nursery described
above is the direct descendant of that practice, and the glassine bags have not
changed much.

## The Hybrid Corn Bargain

Maize breeding took a strange turn that turned out to be enormously productive.
Researchers working in the first decade of the twentieth century found that
inbreeding maize — self-pollinating it generation after generation — produced
progressively weaker, smaller, more uniform lines. Then they crossed two of those
feeble inbreds together and got a plant vastly more vigorous than either parent,
and more vigorous than the open-pollinated population they had started from. The
phenomenon is heterosis. Its genetic basis is still argued over, but its practical
consequences were immediate.

The problem was that the inbred seed parents yielded so little that hybrid seed
was too costly to produce. Donald Jones solved it with the double cross: make two
single-cross hybrids first, then cross those vigorous hybrids with each other,
so that the seed-bearing parent is itself a healthy plant. That made hybrid seed
affordable, and by the 1930s companies — Henry Wallace's among the first — were
selling it to American farmers. The catch is built in: the hybrid's offspring
segregate and lose vigor, so a farmer who saves seed gets a worse crop next year.
Buying new seed every season became normal, and the revenue that produced funded
a century of further breeding.

United States maize yields had been roughly flat for the first fifty years of
record-keeping. From the late 1930s onward they climb in a nearly straight line,
and they have not stopped. Some of that is fertilizer, some is planting density,
some is herbicides — but the varieties that tolerate being planted shoulder to
shoulder and standing up under heavy grain loads came out of the hybrid program.

## Shorter Straw

The mid-century yield problem was not photosynthesis. It was that if you fed a
tall wheat or rice plant enough nitrogen to fill a heavy head of grain, the
plant grew tall, the stem could not carry the weight, and the crop lodged — fell
over in a storm, rotted on the ground, and could not be harvested. Fertilizer was
available and could not be used.

The fix came from dwarfing genes. A Japanese wheat, Norin 10, carried alleles
that shortened the stem by reducing the plant's response to its own growth
hormones. Those genes reached American breeding programs after the Second World
War and, through Norman Borlaug's program in Mexico, reached varieties adapted to
low latitudes. A parallel story ran in rice, where a semi-dwarf gene from a
Chinese variety went into a cross at the international rice institute in the
Philippines and produced, in the mid-1960s, a short, stiff, fertilizer-responsive
plant that spread across Asia within a decade.

What changed was not how much the plant produced in total but how it divided the
result. The fraction of above-ground biomass that ends up as grain — the harvest
index — rose sharply. A short plant puts into seed what a tall one puts into
stem. Combine that with nitrogen the plant could now use without falling over,
and yields in irrigated systems roughly doubled or better.

## What a Breeding Cycle Actually Looks Like

Start with a crossing block: two parents chosen because one has the disease
resistance you want and the other the yield, or the grain quality, or the
maturity. The first generation is uniform and uninteresting. The second is where
the genetics comes apart into every combination, and where selection begins.
From there the breeder's choices are procedural. The pedigree method selects
individual plants each generation and tracks their ancestry. Bulk methods let
natural selection work on a mixed population for several generations before
pulling individuals out. Single seed descent takes one seed per plant and races
through generations in a greenhouse to reach genetic uniformity quickly, then
selects. Doubled haploid technology short-circuits the whole thing: induce a
plant to produce cells with a single set of chromosomes, double them chemically,
and you have a perfectly inbred line in one step instead of six.

Then comes testing, which is most of the calendar. A promising line goes into
small unreplicated plots, then replicated plots, then multi-location trials
across the target region, then several years of the same — because the whole
point is to find lines that perform well across weather the breeder cannot
control. Genotype-by-environment interaction is the thing being measured: a line
that wins in a wet year and collapses in a dry one is not a variety, it is a
gamble. Somewhere around year eight to twelve, a few candidates survive, one gets
a name, and seed multiplication begins — which itself takes several more seasons,
because a handful of grams has to become tonnes.

## Markers, Genomes, and Where New Variation Comes From

Molecular markers changed the tempo more than the logic. If a resistance gene
sits next to a detectable stretch of DNA, a breeder can test a seedling in a lab
rather than waiting to expose an adult plant to the disease, and can carry that
gene through a backcrossing program with confidence that it is still there. Rice
lines carrying a submergence-tolerance gene, which lets a plant survive two weeks
underwater, were moved into popular local varieties this way and handed to
farmers in flood-prone deltas within a few years.

Genomic selection goes further. Instead of tracking a few known genes, genotype a
large training population, measure its performance, and build a statistical model
that predicts performance from genome-wide marker data. Then genotype thousands
of untested seedlings and predict which are worth planting. It is imprecise for
any individual, but it lets a program cull early and cheaply, and the cycle
shortens.

None of that generates new variation. It only sorts what already exists, and a
program eventually exhausts the useful combinations inside its own elite pool.
New variation has to come from outside it. Some of it was manufactured:
mid-century programs exposed seed to X-rays, gamma sources, and mutagenic
chemicals in the hope that something useful would turn up. Most of what emerged
was junk, but a handful of survivors are still commercially important, including
a red grapefruit line and a barley malting variety that brewers and distillers
went on using for decades. Some comes from other species, moved across in wide
crosses that need the immature embryo rescued onto a nutrient medium to produce
a viable seedling at all, or carried in on chromosome segments — a piece of rye
chromosome brought rust and mildew resistance into bread wheat and travelled
into an enormous number of varieties worldwide. Some is transgenic. Some now
comes from edits made at a chosen base pair.

A great deal of it is borrowed back from farmers, out of landrace material that
was never improved and behaves accordingly. This is the slow part, because an
unimproved parent brings a dozen bad traits along with the one good one, and
getting the useful allele out of a useless plant and into something a breeder can
work with — pre-breeding, in the trade — can take as long as making the finished
variety afterward.

The reason anyone bothers is that the target keeps moving. Rust fungi evolve
around a resistance gene on a timescale of a few years, so a wheat variety that
was untouchable at release is frequently defeated before it is old, and the
person who developed it has been working on its replacement since before it had a
name. There is no finished version of a crop. There is only the next one, already
in a nursery somewhere, being walked past by someone with a notebook and a
bundle of bags.
