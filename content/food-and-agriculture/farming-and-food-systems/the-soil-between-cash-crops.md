# The Soil Between Cash Crops

There is a demonstration that agronomists like to do at field days because it
never fails. Two wire baskets hang in two jars of water. In each basket sits a
dry clod of soil the size of a walnut, taken from two fields a short distance
apart. Within a minute one clod has come apart into a plume of silt that clouds
the jar and settles as mud. The other sits there, darkening, still recognisably
a clod, an hour later. The two soils are the same soil series. They have the
same clay content, the same parent material, the same rainfall. What differs is
how they have been managed, and what has been growing on them between harvests.

The clod that collapsed was never really a clod. It was a loose aggregation of
particles held together by nothing much, and water got between them. The one
that held was glued. The glues are biological — polysaccharides secreted by
bacteria, fungal hyphae wrapping particles like packing string, sticky
glycoproteins shed by mycorrhizal fungi, the mucus that passes through an
earthworm's gut. None of them lasts long. They are produced continuously by
organisms that are themselves eating a continuous supply of carbon, and when the
supply stops the glue decays and the structure goes with it.

That is the whole argument for cover cropping in one sentence. Soil structure is
not a stock you can build once. It is a flow, and it is fed by living roots.

## What Structure Buys

A soil that holds together is a soil full of pores, and pores are where the
useful behaviour lives. Large pores — old root channels, earthworm burrows,
cracks between aggregates — conduct water downward fast. Small pores inside
aggregates hold water against gravity where roots can reach it. Between them is
the air-filled space that keeps roots and aerobic microbes breathing.

A collapsed soil has lost the large pores first. Rain then arrives faster than
it can enter. It ponds, it runs off, and it carries the fine particles with it.
On a bare surface, the mechanism that does most of the damage is not the sheet
flow but the raindrop itself: a drop hitting bare soil at terminal velocity has
enough energy to blast aggregates apart, and the freed silt washes into the
remaining pores and seals the surface into a crust. Two centimetres of rain on
a crusted field can produce more runoff than five on a field with residue or a
living canopy over it. The single largest thing a cover crop does is intercept
raindrops.

Organic matter itself is often quoted as the headline number — a soil test
reporting two per cent against four — and it matters, but the figure moves
glacially. Organic matter is a bit over half carbon by weight, and to raise a
field's measured percentage by a single point through the plough layer means
adding a great deal of carbon and holding onto it for years while microbes work
steadily at eating it. Expect a decade, not a season. The structural and
biological changes come much faster than the number does, which is why farmers
who go looking for the payoff in the soil test report are often disappointed
while their infiltration rate has already doubled.

## The Living Fraction

A spadeful of decent topsoil contains billions of bacteria, kilometres of
fungal hyphae, and a food web of protozoa, nematodes, mites, springtails, and
earthworms grazing on them. Almost all of it runs on carbon that came out of a
plant root. Plants push a substantial share of their photosynthate downward and
out through the root surface as sugars, organic acids and amino acids,
deliberately feeding the zone around the root, and in return they get nutrients
mineralised by the organisms that respond.

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi make that trade explicit. They colonise the roots
of most crop plants, extend hyphae far beyond the root's own reach, and deliver
phosphorus — which barely moves in soil and is therefore quickly stripped from
the millimetres nearest a root — in exchange for sugars. They also die and
regrow constantly, leaving behind a residue of tough glycoproteins that is
strongly associated with stable aggregates. Fungi cannot live on dead residue
alone; they need a host. A field left bare for six months between a September
harvest and an April planting has starved its mycorrhizal population for half
the year.

Earthworms make the same point mechanically. The deep-burrowing species that
build permanent vertical channels pull residue down from the surface into their
burrows and feed on it. Remove the surface residue and you remove them, along
with the channels that carry water past a compacted layer.

## Choosing Something to Grow

The practical question is what to plant, and the answer depends almost entirely
on the window available and the job you want done.

Cereal rye is the workhorse of the North American Midwest because it germinates
in cold soil and will establish after a late maize harvest when nothing else
will. It produces enormous fibrous root mass, scavenges leftover nitrate before
winter drainage can carry it into a tile line, and suppresses weeds partly by
shading and partly by releasing compounds that inhibit the germination of small
seeds. Oats do a similar job with less growth and the convenience of dying in a
hard winter, so no termination is needed.

Legumes reverse the nitrogen direction. Hairy vetch is the most productive
overwintering legume in cold climates and can fix a serious quantity of
nitrogen by late spring; crimson clover is quicker and gentler and fits shorter
windows. Both need to be sown early enough to establish before cold weather,
which in a maize-soybean rotation is the difficulty — the window opens late.

Brassicas do something different again. Forage radish sends down a thick taproot
that can penetrate a plough pan, then winterkills and leaves an open channel
behind it along with a slug of nitrogen released early in the spring. Mustards
release glucosinolate breakdown products when their tissue is macerated, which
has genuine suppressive effects on some soil pathogens and nematodes. The
trade-off is that brassicas are not mycorrhizal hosts, so a pure brassica cover
does nothing for the fungal side of the ledger.

For summer windows, buckwheat is unmatched for speed — it will cover the ground
in a month, flower, and feed pollinators — while sorghum-sudangrass produces a
mass of biomass and root growth if there is heat and water to support it.

Mixtures are fashionable, and the logic is real: a grass for structure and
carbon, a legume for nitrogen, a brassica for compaction. Whether a
twelve-species mix outperforms a well-chosen three is much less clear than the
seed catalogues imply, and the complicated mixes are harder to terminate and
harder to plant into.

## Termination, and the Ways It Goes Wrong

The cover crop has to stop before the cash crop starts, and this is where most
of the trouble lives.

The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of the residue governs what happens next. Young,
green, leafy material sits well below the roughly twenty-four-to-one break-even
where soil microbes have more nitrogen than they need to decompose it, and the
surplus mineralises into the soil for the following crop. Mature cereal rye
headed out and going to stem can be far above that ratio, and the microbes
decomposing it will pull mineral nitrogen out of the soil to do the job —
immobilising it for weeks, exactly when young maize needs it. A rye cover
terminated at boot stage feeds the next crop. The same rye terminated three
weeks later starves it. Farmers who report that cover crops cost them yield
have usually met this.

Termination methods are a spectrum. Winterkill is free and reliable for the
species that do it. A roller-crimper flattens a tall stand and crimps the stems
at intervals, killing grasses reliably once they are flowering and leaving a
thick weed-suppressing mat. Herbicide is the default on most acres and
straightforward except when the species is hard to kill, which hairy vetch
frequently is. Tillage works and undoes a great deal of what the cover was
grown to achieve.

Three other risks are worth naming plainly. In dry regions, a cover crop
transpires water that would otherwise have been stored in the profile for the
cash crop, and in a dry spring that debt is not repaid. A dense cover is a fine
habitat for slugs, cutworms, and armyworms, which will move onto the seedlings
when the cover dies — the "green bridge" problem, which can also carry disease
from a cereal cover into a cereal crop. And a cover crop costs seed, an
operation, and management attention in a season already full of both.

## What It Is Reasonable to Expect

Erosion control is immediate and unambiguous. So is nitrate capture: a
vigorous autumn grass cover reliably cuts the nitrate leaving a field in winter
drainage, which matters to everyone downstream whether or not it pays the
farmer. Weed suppression from a heavy mulch is real and visible. Infiltration
improves within a few years, and with it the field's behaviour in both a wet
spring and a dry August, which is the change farmers who have done this for a
decade tend to mention first.

Yield gains in the cash crop are the least certain part. They show up in some
systems and some years, particularly in droughty seasons on sandy soil, and are
absent or negative in others, particularly in cold wet springs on heavy ground.
The honest version is that a cover crop is an investment in the field's physical
and biological condition, paid in cash now and returned in resilience and in
avoided losses that are difficult to see because they did not happen. The clod
in the jar is the clearest evidence anyone has found of what is being bought.
