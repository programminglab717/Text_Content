# Furrows, Pivots, and Drip Lines

From a window seat over western Kansas or the Saudi interior, the ground reads
as a page of circles. Each one is a quarter of a square mile with a green disc
stamped into it, and the corners left dry and pale. The circle is the track of a
single machine: a pipe on wheels, anchored at the centre, walking slowly around
its pivot point and raining as it goes. It is one answer to the only question
irrigation ever asks, which is how to get a given depth of water into the top
metre of soil across a large area, at the right moment, without wasting more of
it than you can afford.

There are broadly three families of answers, and they are not a progression from
worse to better. They are adapted to different water sources, different soils,
different crops, different labour costs, and different energy prices, and all
three are still expanding somewhere in the world.

## Letting Gravity Do It

Surface irrigation is the oldest method and still, by area, the most widely
used. Water is released at the high end of a field and allowed to flow across
the surface, infiltrating as it goes. No pumps are strictly necessary, no
pressure, no nozzles. It is the only method that can run entirely on the
elevation difference between a canal and a field, which is why every ancient
irrigation civilisation used it and why much of the irrigated area of South
Asia, Egypt, and the Central Valley still does.

The three main forms differ in how the water is confined. Basin irrigation
floods a level, diked enclosure and holds the water until it soaks in; flooded
rice is the obvious case, but it suits orchards and cereals on heavy soils too.
Border irrigation runs a sheet of water down a long strip between low ridges,
good for close-sown crops like alfalfa and small grains. Furrow irrigation
sends water down the small channels between planted rows, wetting the ridge
from the side, which keeps the crown of the plant dry and suits row crops:
maize, cotton, tomatoes, sugar beet.

The difficulty with all of them is uniformity. Water applied at the head of a
field has more time to infiltrate than water that arrives at the tail twenty or
a hundred minutes later, so the top of the run gets over-watered while the
bottom gets shorted. The traditional fixes are shorter runs, larger streams
that advance quickly, and fields graded to a precise slope. Laser-guided land
levelling, which lets a scraper hold a grade within centimetres over hundreds
of metres, transformed the economics of surface irrigation wherever it arrived,
and level basins with a laser-flat floor can be as uniform as anything a
sprinkler produces. Surge irrigation — pulsing the water down the furrow in
bursts rather than one steady stream, using an automated valve to alternate
between two sets — exploits the fact that a furrow bottom which has been wetted
and allowed to drain briefly seals slightly and carries the next pulse to the
tail much faster.

Done casually, surface irrigation may put little more than half the diverted
water into the root zone. Done carefully, with levelling and cutback flows and
tailwater recovery ditches that catch the runoff and pump it back to the head,
it can be respectable. The variance between a good surface system and a poor one
is larger than the variance between methods.

## Pressure, Nozzles, and the Walking Pipe

Sprinkler irrigation trades energy for uniformity. Water is pressurised and
thrown through nozzles so that the application rate is set by the hardware
rather than by the soil's infiltration rate and the field's slope. That
decouples irrigation from topography: sprinklers work on rolling ground, on
sandy soils that swallow a furrow stream before it reaches the tail, on shallow
soils that cannot store a large single application.

The older configurations were labour-hungry. Hand-move aluminium lines had to be
uncoupled, carried, and reset every set. Wheel lines, with the pipe itself
serving as the axle of a row of large wheels, could be rolled to the next
position by a small motor at the centre. Solid-set systems bury permanent
mains and leave the sprinklers in place all season, which is expensive in pipe
but cheap in labour and useful for frost protection in orchards, where turning
on the water during a spring freeze coats the buds in ice and holds them near
zero while the ice keeps forming.

The centre pivot changed the arithmetic. A lateral pipe several hundred metres
long, supported on drive towers, rotates around a fixed riser. Each tower has
its own motor and a control system that keeps the line straight by starting and
stopping the towers in sequence; the outer towers run almost continuously and
the inner ones creep. Because the outer end of the pipe sweeps a far longer arc
than the inner end, the nozzles must apply progressively more water per metre
of pipe as you move outward, so a pivot's nozzle package is engineered
position by position. A full revolution takes anywhere from most of a day to
several days depending on the depth being applied.

Early pivots sprayed from high-pressure impact sprinklers on top of the pipe,
which lost a great deal to wind drift and evaporation. Modern pivots hang drop
tubes down to within a metre or less of the canopy and use low-pressure spray
heads or, at the extreme, bubblers that dribble water directly into the furrow
just above the ground. The energy saving is substantial and the losses to the
air nearly disappear. Linear-move machines use the same towers but travel in a
straight line along a ditch or a drag hose, which fits rectangular fields and
avoids the wasted corners — at the cost of a machine that must be walked back to
the start.

## Emitters

Micro-irrigation abandons the idea of wetting the whole field. A drip system
delivers water at a few litres per hour from emitters spaced along a thin
polyethylene line, wetting a bulb of soil around each plant and leaving the
ground between rows dry. The dry interrow suppresses weed germination, keeps
tractors out of mud, and eliminates evaporation from most of the surface.

The principle is old — buried unglazed pots that seep have been used for
millennia — but the modern form waited for cheap extruded plastic. The story
usually told begins with an Israeli water engineer who noticed a tree growing
conspicuously better than its neighbours next to a leaking pipe, and worked out
that a small, steady, local supply beat a large periodic flood. The engineering
problem was making an emitter that discharges a low, consistent flow without
clogging, and the answer was a long, narrow, turbulent labyrinth moulded into
the dripline wall, which dissipates pressure over a path long enough that the
opening itself need not be tiny. Pressure-compensating emitters add a flexible
diaphragm that throttles the passage as pressure rises, so that a line running
uphill or a lateral three hundred metres long still discharges evenly end to
end.

Drip's weakness is that it is a filtration problem wearing a plumbing costume.
Sand, algae, bacterial slime, and above all precipitated calcium carbonate and
iron will close emitters permanently. Every serious system carries screen,
disc, or media filtration, a flush schedule, and periodic acid or chlorine
injection. Subsurface drip, with the lines buried below tillage depth, adds
root intrusion and rodent damage to the list but removes the labour of laying
and lifting tape each season and works well under perennial crops.

Because a drip system is already a pressurised network reaching every plant, it
is also the natural route for fertiliser. Fertigation — metering nutrients into
the irrigation stream — lets a grower deliver nitrogen in small doses matched
to the crop's weekly demand rather than in two or three large applications that
sit in the soil waiting to be leached.

## Deciding When to Turn It On

Method determines how water arrives. Scheduling determines how much and when,
and it is where most of the water is actually won or lost.

The demand side is evapotranspiration: the water a crop transpires plus what
evaporates from the soil. Weather station networks publish a reference
evapotranspiration for a standard grass surface, computed from radiation,
temperature, humidity, and wind. Multiplying that by a crop coefficient —
which changes through the season as canopy cover develops — gives a daily
estimate of what the field has used, and irrigation replaces the running
total. The supply side is the soil, which holds a finite amount of water
between field capacity and the point where roots can no longer extract it.
Tensiometers, gypsum blocks, capacitance probes, and neutron probes all measure
where in that range the profile currently sits.

Some crops are deliberately kept short. Regulated deficit irrigation withholds
water during growth stages when mild stress does little harm or actual good —
in wine grapes, controlled stress after fruit set restrains shoot growth and
concentrates the berries; in some stone fruit and nut orchards, a dry window
in mid-season limits vegetative growth without costing yield.

## Salt and the Efficiency Trap

All irrigation water carries dissolved salts. Crops take up water and leave most
of the salt behind, so a field irrigated for years without drainage
concentrates salt in the root zone until yields fall and then until nothing
grows. The remedy is a leaching fraction: applying somewhat more water than the
crop needs, deliberately, so that a share drains past the roots and carries salt
away. That requires somewhere for the drainage to go, which means tile drains
and outfalls, and it means the salt becomes someone else's problem downstream.
The ruins of irrigated agriculture in southern Mesopotamia, and the white crusts
on abandoned ground in the San Joaquin and the Indus basin, are all the same
failure.

This is why the obvious conclusion — that everyone should convert to drip and
the water problem is solved — is wrong in an instructive way. Efficiency at the
field scale measures the fraction of applied water that the crop transpires. But
in a closed basin, the water a furrow system "wastes" to deep percolation
largely returns to the aquifer or the river and is diverted again downstream.
Replacing it with drip raises field efficiency and very often raises total
consumptive use, because the farmer expands the irrigated area with the water
saved, or plants a thirstier crop, or irrigates through a longer season.
Basin-scale accounting repeatedly finds that efficiency programmes reduce
return flows rather than reduce depletion. Over the High Plains aquifer, the
circles are visible from the air precisely because the machinery got better at
using a fossil water supply that is not being replaced.
