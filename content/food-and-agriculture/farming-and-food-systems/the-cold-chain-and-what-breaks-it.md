# The Cold Chain and What Breaks It

In the first decades of the nineteenth century a Boston merchant named Frederic
Tudor began cutting blocks out of frozen New England ponds, packing them in
sawdust, and sailing them to Havana, Charleston, and eventually Calcutta. Most
people thought he was mad. Enough ice survived the voyage to make him rich, and
the trade he created — horse-drawn cutters scoring ponds into grids, insulated
ice houses at the receiving end — was the first industrial attempt to move cold
around the world rather than move food to where cold already was. Mechanical
refrigeration arrived later in the century, and by the 1880s ships were carrying
frozen mutton from New Zealand and chilled beef from the River Plate to London,
which reorganized global agriculture in a couple of decades.

What Tudor's customers wanted was ice in a drink. What the system became is
something less visible and more consequential: an unbroken sequence of
temperature-controlled steps from a field to a kitchen, in which the weakest link
sets the outcome. A pallet of broccoli that spends four hours on a loading dock
in July cannot be repaired by the best refrigerated truck in the fleet. The chain
metaphor is exact.

## The Crop Is Still Breathing

The first thing to understand about fresh produce is that harvesting it does not
stop it. A head of lettuce cut from the ground is a living tissue, respiring —
taking in oxygen, burning its own stored sugars and acids, and giving off carbon
dioxide, water vapor, and heat. It no longer has roots supplying water or leaves
supplying photosynthate, so it is running down a balance it cannot replenish.
Everything that constitutes deterioration — softening, yellowing, wilting, loss
of sweetness, susceptibility to rot — follows from that metabolism continuing.

Respiration rate is strongly temperature dependent, roughly doubling to tripling
for every ten degrees Celsius. Cooling a crop from thirty degrees to near zero
therefore does not slow its metabolism by a little; it slows it by close to an
order of magnitude, which is why refrigeration buys weeks rather than hours. The
rate also varies enormously between crops. Asparagus, sweet corn, mushrooms,
peas, and broccoli respire furiously and have shelf lives measured in days. Sweet
corn is the cruelest example: it converts its sugars to starch quickly at warm
temperatures, so the difference between corn cooled within an hour and corn cooled
the next morning is something you can taste directly. Apples, citrus, potatoes,
and onions respire slowly and can be held for months.

Respiration also generates heat, which matters in a stack. A full pallet of warm
broccoli in a sealed room will hold its own temperature up through sheer
metabolic output, and the refrigeration has to remove not only the heat the field
put in but the heat the crop keeps making.

## Getting the Field Heat Out

A refrigerated room is good at holding temperature and bad at removing it. Cold
air moving around the outside of a pallet takes a very long time to reach the
boxes in the middle, and during that time the center of the load is spoiling.
Precooling is therefore a distinct operation with its own equipment, and the
industry measures it by how long it takes to remove seven-eighths of the
difference between the crop's starting temperature and the target.

Forced-air cooling pulls cold air through the vent holes in the cartons by
setting up a pressure difference across the stack, cutting cooling time from a
day to an hour or two. Hydrocooling drenches or immerses the product in chilled
water, which is fast, works well for stone fruit, carrots, and sweet corn, and is
useless for anything that must stay dry. Vacuum cooling exploits a different
principle: lower the pressure around a leafy crop until its own moisture boils,
and the evaporation draws heat straight out of the tissue. It works beautifully
for lettuce and leafy greens, whose enormous surface area makes them evaporate
fast, and badly for a dense tomato. Some commodities are simply packed with ice
in the box.

The hard constraint is time. Precooling has to happen close to the field, within
hours, which means capital equipment near production — and that is precisely what
is missing in most of the world's horticulture.

## Ethylene, and the Wrong Kind of Cold

Ethylene is a simple gas that plants use as a hormone, and in storage it is both
tool and hazard. Climacteric fruits — bananas, avocados, tomatoes, pears, melons,
apples — respond to it with a burst of respiration that triggers ripening:
starch to sugar, firm to soft, green to color, the whole coordinated program. This
is why bananas are shipped hard and green, held at a temperature that suppresses
ripening, and then put into sealed rooms and deliberately gassed with ethylene a
few days before they are needed, so an entire container arrives at the same stage
on the same day. Non-climacteric fruits — citrus, grapes, strawberries,
pineapples — do not do this. Picked unripe, they stay unripe, and no amount of
gas will help.

The hazard is the same mechanism running where it is not wanted. Ripening apples
emit ethylene copiously, and a case of apples in a room with lettuce will produce
russet spotting on the lettuce; ethylene near carrots turns them bitter; near
broccoli it yellows the florets. Mixed-commodity storage requires knowing which
products emit and which are sensitive, and the incompatibility lists are long.
Where separation is impossible, scrubbers using potassium permanganate absorb
ethylene from the air, and apples are widely treated with a compound that binds
the fruit's own ethylene receptors and simply blocks the signal from being heard.

Cold has its own failure mode, which surprises people. Crops of tropical and
subtropical origin are injured by temperatures well above freezing. Bananas held
below about thirteen degrees develop dull, grayish skin and never ripen properly.
Cucumbers pit. Mangoes and papayas fail to color. Tomatoes kept in a domestic
refrigerator lose the volatile compounds that make them smell like tomatoes, and
the loss is not reversible on the counter; the texture goes mealy as well. The
damage often does not appear until the product is warmed again, which is an
awkward property, because the symptom shows up at the retailer and the cause was
three steps back.

## Holding an Apple for a Year

The most refined form of long-term storage takes the logic of slowing metabolism
past temperature alone and changes the atmosphere. Seal a room of apples, let the
fruit's own respiration draw down the oxygen, and then hold the atmosphere at a
few percent oxygen with a small percentage of carbon dioxide, at a temperature
just above the fruit's freezing point. Respiration nearly stops. Ethylene
production nearly stops. Softening nearly stops. Apples picked in autumn come out
the following summer firm enough to sell.

Running such a room requires equipment to maintain the balance — nitrogen
generators to displace oxygen, scrubbers to strip carbon dioxide that would
otherwise accumulate to injurious levels, and gas analyzers monitoring
continuously. The limits are biological: below a certain oxygen concentration the
fruit switches to fermentation and produces alcohol and off-flavors, and the
threshold differs by variety, orchard, and season. Newer systems find that
boundary dynamically, watching the fruit's own stress signals and easing the
oxygen down to just above the point where the crop objects. The room is opened
once, months later, and is otherwise a sealed black box that nobody enters
without breathing apparatus.

## The Dry Side: Grain and Tubers

Most of the world's calories are not kept cold at all. Grain is kept dry, which
achieves the same end by a different route: below roughly thirteen or fourteen
percent moisture, the water available to fungi and insects is insufficient for
them to multiply, and the seed sits dormant. Above that, storage molds move in,
some of which produce aflatoxins that are potent carcinogens and that no
subsequent processing removes. Aeration fans pull ambient air through a bin on
cool nights, not to dry it further but to cool the mass and prevent moisture
migration, because a warm pocket in a cold bin sets up convection that carries
moisture to the top where it condenses and cakes.

Small-holder storage has its own elegant technology: hermetic bags, several layers
of plastic sealed tight, in which the insects already present in the grain
consume the oxygen and then suffocate. No chemicals, no power, and a legume crop
that would have been riddled with weevils comes out clean months later.

Potatoes occupy an interesting middle. Straight out of the ground they are wounded
and need curing — two weeks at moderately warm temperature and very high humidity
so the tuber forms a corky layer over its scrapes — before being cooled down for
storage. How cold depends on the buyer. Potatoes for the fresh market can go
quite cold. Potatoes destined for frying cannot, because low temperatures trigger
the breakdown of starch into reducing sugars, and those sugars brown
catastrophically in hot oil, producing dark, bitter fries and elevated acrylamide.
Processing stock is held warmer, which means sprouting must be suppressed some
other way, and if a load does get too cold it is reconditioned by warming it for
a few weeks to let the tuber metabolize its sugars back.

## The Box That Does the Moving

Most of the distance in a modern cold chain is covered inside a refrigerated
container: a standard shipping box with a compressor, condenser, and evaporator
built into one end wall and a plug for the ship's or the terminal's power. Cold
air is blown out under a grooved T-bar floor, runs the length of the container
beneath the cargo, rises up through the vent holes in the pallets, and returns
along the ceiling to the unit. That geometry is why stowage rules are strict. A
load stacked above the marked line blocks the return path. A load with a channel
down the middle lets the air short-circuit back without passing through anything.
Either mistake produces a container that reads correctly at its sensor and is
warm in the center of the cargo.

The set point is not the whole story either. Chilled cargo is generally
controlled on the supply air, because air leaving the evaporator is the coldest
thing the load will encounter and you do not want it freezing the cartons nearest
the front. Frozen cargo is controlled on the return air, since nothing in the box
is at risk of chilling injury and the number that matters is the warmest air in
it. Some containers manage composition as well, scrubbing carbon dioxide and
injecting nitrogen so that avocados or bananas cross an ocean in roughly the
conditions an apple store maintains, which is what makes multi-week sea freight of
ripening fruit possible at all.

Nearly every load now carries a recorder, and the download at the far end is
where arguments get settled. A trace with a six-hour spike in the middle of the
crossing points at the machine. A trace that starts high and never comes down
points at the shipper, who loaded warm fruit into a box that was never going to
cool it — because a refrigerated container, like a refrigerated room, is built to
hold a temperature and not to pull one down. That work has to happen in the first
hours after the crop leaves the ground, within sight of the field, or it does not
happen at all.
