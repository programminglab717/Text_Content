# The Two Ways to Make a Pickle

Set two jars of cucumbers side by side. One was made an hour ago by pouring hot
vinegar over the spears with dill and garlic; it is bright green, sharp, and
will taste essentially the same next week as it does tonight. The other has
been sitting on a counter for five days in nothing but salt water. Its brine
has gone cloudy, it fizzes faintly when you open it, the cucumbers inside have
turned from bright green to olive drab, and it smells sour in a way the vinegar
jar does not — rounder, yeastier, more alive. Both are pickles. Only one of
them is a ferment, and almost everything interesting about pickling follows
from the difference.

The vinegar jar is a chemical intervention. Acetic acid at sufficient strength
makes the cucumber inhospitable to nearly everything that would otherwise eat
it, and the pickle is finished the moment the brine cools. Nothing in it is
producing anything. The salt jar is a biological one. Nobody added acid; the
acid is being manufactured inside the jar by bacteria that arrived on the
vegetables themselves, growing on the vegetables' own sugars. The cook's only
real contribution was to arrange conditions so that the useful organisms won.

## What the Salt Is Doing

Salt is not the preservative. At the concentrations used for vegetables it is
far too weak to sterilize anything. What it does is skew a competition.

Its first job is physical. Salt outside a plant cell draws water out through
the cell wall by osmosis, and with the water come dissolved sugars and minerals
— the food supply for the bacteria. This is why shredded cabbage rubbed with
salt will, within an hour, be swimming in enough of its own liquid to cover
itself. No water needs to be added to make sauerkraut, only salt and
patience and something heavy on top. For firmer vegetables that cannot give up
enough water, a brine is mixed separately and poured over.

The second job is selective. Lactic acid bacteria tolerate salt better than
most of the organisms that would otherwise rot a vegetable, and they tolerate
acid far better still. A salt concentration in the low single digits handicaps
the competition without meaningfully slowing the bacteria you want. Get it
wrong in either direction and the ferment tells you: too little salt and things
go soft, slimy, and off-smelling within a couple of days; too much and
fermentation stalls, leaving you with salty raw vegetables sitting in brine.

Working ranges are narrow and worth knowing by weight rather than by volume,
since a tablespoon of flaky salt and a tablespoon of fine salt are not the same
thing at all:

- Cabbage for sauerkraut or kimchi: roughly 2 percent of the weight of the
  vegetables, salted dry.
- Cucumbers, green beans, and other whole vegetables in brine: roughly 3.5 to 5
  percent of the weight of brine plus vegetables, the higher end in warm
  weather.
- Olives, which are cured rather than merely soured, run far higher and for
  months, because the bitterness has to leach out before anything else matters.

Plain salt is best simply because anti-caking agents cloud the brine and iodine
is unhelpful to the bacteria; beyond that the type hardly matters.

## The Succession in the Jar

A vegetable ferment is not one population but a relay. The vegetables arrive
carrying a mixed flora — mostly organisms that would spoil them, with lactic
acid bacteria present as a small minority.

In the first day or two, as oxygen is consumed and the salt suppresses the
weaker competitors, a heterofermentative species takes the lead, typically
*Leuconostoc mesenteroides*. It is comparatively salt-tolerant, works well at
cool temperatures, and produces a mixture of lactic acid, acetic acid, ethanol,
and carbon dioxide. The gas is the visible sign that the ferment has caught:
bubbles rising when the jar is tapped, brine creeping up, the lid needing to be
burped. That carbon dioxide is also functional, since it displaces oxygen and
blankets the surface.

As the acid accumulates, *Leuconostoc* is inhibited by its own product and
hands off to more acid-tolerant species, chiefly *Lactiplantibacillus
plantarum* and its relatives, which are homofermentative — they make lactic
acid and little else. These drive the pH down to somewhere in the mid-threes
and hold it there, at which point almost nothing else on earth can grow in the
jar and the ferment is effectively self-preserving.

The character of the finished pickle depends largely on how long that first
phase lasts. A cool, slow ferment gives the heterofermentative stage more time
and yields a more complex, faintly effervescent, aromatic result. This is not
incidental in Korean kimchi, which is traditionally fermented cold in buried
earthenware and which depends on that particular group of bacteria for its
fizz and its distinctive sourness. A ferment run warm on a summer counter
rushes past that phase into flat lactic sourness, and runs a much higher risk
of going soft.

## Under the Brine, or Else

The single most common failure has nothing to do with bacteria. It is a piece
of cabbage floating above the liquid.

Lactic acid bacteria do not strictly require the absence of oxygen, but molds
and film yeasts do require its presence, and they cannot get a foothold below
the surface. Anything above the brine is fair game for them. The fix is
mechanical: a weight, a glass disk, a small water-filled bag, a plate, a whole
outer cabbage leaf folded over the shreds and tucked down. Traditional crocks
solve it with a water-filled gutter around the rim that lets fermentation gas
escape while sealing air out.

Two kinds of growth appear on neglected surfaces and they are not equivalent. A
flat, wrinkled, white film that spreads across the brine is kahm yeast. It is
harmless, smells slightly of nail polish or old beer, and can be skimmed off,
though a heavy bloom will have coarsened the flavor of everything beneath it.
Fuzzy growth in any color — blue, black, green, pink — is mold, and mold
raises the surface pH as it grows, opening the door for organisms that could
not otherwise live there. A moldy ferment is not worth salvaging.

## Why Some Pickles Go Limp

Crunch is pectin, the cement between plant cell walls, and pectin can be
destroyed in several ways.

The most avoidable culprit sits on the cucumber itself. Enzymes concentrated at
the blossom end of a cucumber, along with those carried by surface molds, break
down pectin readily in a warm brine. Trimming a slice off the blossom end is
not folklore; it removes most of the problem. Washing the vegetables well
removes more.

Heat speeds the same enzymes, which is why warm ferments soften and cool ones
stay firm. Tannins slow them, which is why the old recipes call for a grape
leaf, an oak leaf, a horseradish leaf, or a few black tea leaves in the jar.
Calcium strengthens pectin directly by cross-linking it, which is the reason
behind the old practice of soaking cucumbers in slaked lime, and behind the
modern shortcut of a pinch of calcium chloride. Very hard water with a high
mineral content does some of this on its own.

The other enemy of crunch is simply time. Fermented pickles keep well in a
refrigerator for months, but they continue to slowly soften, and a two-year-old
jar of sauerkraut, while perfectly safe, will have the texture of wet paper.

## Knowing When It Is Safe

The number that matters is 4.6. Below that pH, *Clostridium botulinum* cannot
grow or produce toxin, and a properly managed vegetable ferment goes well
below it — usually under 4.0 within a week, and often into the low threes. This
is why fermented vegetables have such a long history as winter food in places
with no refrigeration, and why sailors carried barrels of sauerkraut on long
voyages when almost nothing else green survived the trip.

The same threshold governs the vinegar jar. A quick pickle made with vinegar of
standard strength, diluted with no more than an equal volume of water, lands
comfortably under 4.6, and if it is going into a water-bath canner rather than
a refrigerator, that margin is the whole reason the canner is sufficient.
Low-acid vegetables preserved without either acid or fermentation need a
pressure canner, at temperatures above boiling, because nothing else will
reliably kill botulinum spores.

Beyond that, the sensory checks are reliable and the jar is not shy about
communicating. A good ferment smells clean and sour, with a vegetal sweetness
underneath. The brine may be cloudy, and usually is. The vegetables will have
faded — bright green chlorophyll shifts to olive as acid strips the magnesium
out of it, which is the visual signature of a real ferment and the reason
supermarket pickles that are still emerald green were almost certainly made
with vinegar and a short soak.

What smells wrong is wrong: putrid, cheesy, sulfurous, or of anything other
than sour vegetable. That judgment is not delicate. People have been making
this call for thousands of years without instruments, and the failures are
loud.
