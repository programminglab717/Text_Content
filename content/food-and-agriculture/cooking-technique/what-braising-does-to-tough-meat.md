# What Braising Does to Tough Meat

A beef shank is an unpromising object. It is the animal's lower leg, sawn
across, a disk of dark meat strung through with silvery sheets of connective
tissue and wrapped in more of the same, with a bone in the middle. Raw, it is
nearly inedible; grilled, it is worse — a piece of it cooked fast over high
heat will fight back like a rubber band and taste of very little. Four hours
later, in a covered pot in a low oven, the same disk of meat will be falling
into wet, glossy shreds under a spoon, sitting in a liquid that has become
thick enough to coat the back of it and that will set to a firm jelly in the
refrigerator overnight.

Nothing was added to produce that transformation except heat, time, and a small
amount of liquid. What changed is that the material which made the shank tough
was converted into the material that makes the sauce rich. Braising is the
technique built around that conversion, and understanding the conversion
explains nearly every rule of thumb the technique carries.

## Why Hard-Working Muscles Are Tough

Muscle tissue is bundles of fibers wrapped in sheaths of connective tissue,
and the sheaths are made largely of collagen. How much collagen a cut contains
depends on what the muscle did for a living. A tenderloin sits along the spine
and does almost nothing; it is lean, fine-grained, and tender enough to eat
barely cooked. A shank, a shoulder, a neck, a cheek, or a tail bears weight,
absorbs shock, and chews all day, and its fibers are bound in thick,
well-organized collagen.

Age matters as much as anatomy. Collagen molecules are laid down as triple
helices, and over an animal's life they become linked to one another by
chemical bridges. Young collagen has relatively few of these and they are
comparatively fragile; in a mature animal they are numerous and heat-stable.
This is why veal breast surrenders in two hours and an old dairy cow's shoulder
needs five, and why the traditional braises of European and Asian peasant
cooking are what they are — these were the recipes for animals that had been
worked until they could not work any more, and for the cuts that nobody with
money wanted.

## Two Clocks Running in Opposite Directions

Put that shank in a pot and two processes start, and they do not run at the
same speed.

The first is the destruction of the muscle fibers themselves, or more precisely
of their ability to hold water. Myosin begins to denature around 50°C, actin
higher still, and as the proteins unfold and coagulate the fibers contract and
squeeze out moisture. A steak cooked to well done has lost a substantial
fraction of its water and eats dry. By the standards of a steak, a braise is
catastrophically overcooked: braised meat is held far above the temperature at
which those proteins wring themselves out, for hours. On a moisture basis, it
is drier than a rare steak, not wetter.

The second process is the undoing of the collagen. It also begins around 60 to
65°C, where the triple helix unwinds and the sheaths shrink — which is why meat
seizes and toughens in the early middle of cooking, the stage at which an
impatient cook decides the recipe has failed. But if the temperature is held
above roughly 70°C and the tissue stays wet, those unwound strands begin to
hydrolyze: the long molecules are cut into shorter, water-soluble fragments,
and what leaves the meat is gelatin. The process is slow and steeply
temperature-dependent. A modest increase in temperature buys a large decrease
in time, which is the tradeoff behind the pressure cooker and behind the
difference between a three-hour braise and an eight-hour one.

The eating quality of a finished braise is the outcome of the race between
those two clocks. Gelatin is what rescues it. Dissolved in the meat's remaining
moisture and in the surrounding liquid, gelatin coats the mouth and reads
unmistakably as succulence, even though the fibers themselves have given up
most of their water. It also does the shredding: as the collagen holding the
bundles together dissolves, the bundles separate under almost no force at all.

It follows that a braise can be overcooked, and past a certain point a longer
braise is not a better one. Once the connective tissue is gone, nothing is left
to convert, the fibers keep drying, and the meat crosses over from tender into
stringy and mealy — shreds with no cohesion, floating in sauce. There is a
window, and it is wider than the window for a steak but it is not infinite.

## Why the Pot Is Shaped the Way It Is

Braising is not boiling and is not roasting. The liquid comes partway up the
meat, typically a third to a half, and the pot is covered. That arrangement
does several things at once.

Below the waterline, the meat is in direct contact with a liquid that sits
somewhere around 85 to 95°C — hot enough for collagen to hydrolyze steadily,
but held below a rolling boil, since violent agitation shreds the exterior and
drives the pot hotter than it needs to be. Above the waterline, the meat is in
saturated steam, which transfers heat almost as efficiently, and the exposed
top surface continues to darken and concentrate rather than washing out. Turning
the meat once or twice during cooking swaps those two environments and evens the
result.

The lid returns the evaporating liquid. A Moroccan tagine makes this explicit —
its tall cone is a condenser, collecting vapor and running it back down onto the
food, which is a sensible design for cooking with little water over a small
fire. A tight European lid does the same job less dramatically. A round of
parchment pressed onto the surface under a loose lid allows a slow reduction
while still keeping the top from drying, which is the compromise for braises
where the sauce is meant to concentrate.

The oven is preferred to the stovetop for the same reason a kiln is preferred
to a campfire: heat arrives from all sides at a temperature you have set,
rather than from a single hot disk underneath that has to be nursed. An oven
around 150°C will hold the liquid comfortably below boiling for as long as you
like without any attention.

## The Sequence, and What Each Step Is For

Salt the meat well ahead if you can — an hour at minimum, overnight is better
— so that it has time to penetrate rather than sitting on the surface. Dry the
exterior and brown it hard in fat before any liquid goes in. Browning is not
sealing in juices; that idea was proposed in the nineteenth century, repeated
for a century after, and is simply wrong, since a seared surface loses moisture
freely. What browning provides is flavor that cannot be generated any other way
once the pot is wet, because the interior of a braise never exceeds the boiling
point of water and nothing in it will brown again.

Brown the aromatics in the same fat. Then deglaze — wine, stock, beer, tomato,
vinegar, whatever the tradition calls for — and scrape the pan hard, because
the dark film welded to the bottom is the concentrated product of the searing
and it dissolves instantly. Acid in the liquid brightens the finished dish and
helps the connective tissue along; it is not incidental that so many classic
braises are built on wine, beer, tomatoes, vinegar, or sour fruit.

Then leave it alone. Check for doneness with a fork or a skewer rather than a
thermometer: the probe should enter and withdraw with no resistance at all, and
the meat should not cling to it. Temperature tells you almost nothing here,
because everything in the pot reached its final temperature hours before it was
ready.

Cool the meat in its own liquid rather than lifting it out hot. It will reabsorb
some of what it lost and will not dry at the edges. A braise made a day ahead is
genuinely better, not merely more convenient: the flavors distribute, the meat
firms enough to slice cleanly instead of collapsing, and the fat rises and sets
into a cap you can lift off in one piece.

## Everyone Invented This

The technique appears wherever there is a pot, a fire that can be banked, and
an animal too old to sell. Provençal daube simmers beef in red wine with orange
peel, traditionally in a bellied earthenware pot with a concave lid that held
embers so that heat came from above as well as below. Flemish carbonnade uses
dark beer and a slick of mustard. Chinese red-cooking builds its liquid from
soy sauce, rock sugar, wine, and spice, and the master sauce is kept and reused
for years. Filipino adobo leans on vinegar and soy. Korean galbi-jjim, Mexican
birria, Hungarian goulash, Italian osso buco — all of them are the same physics
with different aromatics.

And it is not only meat. Fennel, endive, cabbage, leeks, and artichokes braise
beautifully for a related but distinct reason: sustained wet heat above about
80°C degrades pectin and dissolves the cell walls' cement, turning a fibrous
vegetable silky. Dried beans cooked slowly in a covered pot are a braise in
everything but name. The common thread is a structural material being
deliberately taken apart at a temperature low enough that nothing else in the
pot is ruined while you wait.
