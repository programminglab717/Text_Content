# How Milk Is Turned Into Cheese

Warm a pot of milk to blood heat, stir in a few drops of rennet, and then stop
touching it. For six or eight minutes nothing appears to happen. The milk
looks exactly as it did. Then, if you tilt the pot very slightly, the whole
surface moves as one piece, like a pane of glass sliding, and a clear
yellow-green liquid weeps up along the edge. A liter of liquid has become a
solid with almost none of its water removed. Everything that follows in
cheesemaking — the cutting, the stirring, the pressing, the months in a cellar
— is a matter of deciding how much of that water to take back out and what to
let grow in what remains.

The oldest evidence for the practice is unglamorous: potsherds from Neolithic
settlements in central Europe, perforated all over like a colander, carrying
the chemical residue of milk fat in their clay. They are on the order of seven
thousand years old. Somebody was separating curd from whey before the
invention of writing, in a period when most adults could not have digested
fresh milk at all. That last point is probably the whole reason. Lactose
tolerance in adulthood was rare then and is still a minority trait worldwide.
Turning milk into cheese moves most of the lactose out with the whey and
leaves the bacteria to consume much of the rest, which converts a food that
made people ill into one that did not — and, incidentally, into one that keeps
through a winter.

## Why Milk Sets

Milk is mostly water holding three things in suspension: fat globules, whey
proteins, and casein. The casein is the interesting part. It does not float
around as individual molecules but is bundled into micelles — aggregates of
several kinds of casein protein glued together by tiny clusters of calcium
phosphate, which is a neat solution to the problem of carrying large amounts of
calcium in a liquid without it precipitating out.

Those bundles would happily clump together if nothing prevented it. What
prevents it is kappa-casein, a protein that sits on the outside of each micelle
with a charged, water-loving tail projecting into the surrounding liquid. The
tails form a bristly, negatively charged layer that keeps micelles at arm's
length, both by electrostatic repulsion and by simply getting in the way.

There are two ways to defeat that layer. The first is enzymatic. Chymosin, the
active enzyme in rennet, is remarkably specific: it cleaves kappa-casein at a
single bond, snipping the tail off and letting it drift away into the whey.
Shorn micelles have nothing left to keep them apart, and in the presence of
calcium they begin to bridge to one another, forming a continuous three-
dimensional gel that traps fat and water inside it. The enzyme works fast and
the aggregation lags behind it, which is why the milk sits unchanged for
several minutes and then sets all at once.

The second way is acid. As lactic bacteria ferment lactose into lactic acid,
the pH falls; the charge on the micelles is neutralized as it approaches
casein's isoelectric point, and the calcium phosphate holding the bundles
together dissolves into the liquid. The micelles fall apart and re-associate
into a weaker, more fragile curd. Acid-set cheeses — fresh chèvre, cream
cheese, paneer, the curds in a bowl of yogurt — have a different texture from
rennet-set ones for exactly this reason. They have lost their calcium
scaffolding, which is also why they crumble rather than stretch, and why they
do not melt the way a young rennet cheese does.

## Rennet, Thistles, and Microbes

Nearly every traditional cheese uses both mechanisms, in some ratio. Rennet
came from the fourth stomach of an unweaned calf, kid, or lamb, and for
centuries was made by drying and salting a piece of stomach lining and
suspending a scrap of it in the milk. Vegetable coagulants have their own long
history around the Mediterranean, where cardoon and thistle flowers are used to
set the milk for certain Spanish and Portuguese sheep's cheeses, giving them a
faintly bitter, herbal edge. Most rennet in industrial use today is chymosin
produced by microbes carrying the gene for it — a fermentation product rather
than a slaughter byproduct, and one of the earliest genetically engineered
enzymes to reach the food supply.

## The Decisions in the Vat

Once the gel has set, the cheesemaker's job is expelling whey, and every tool
for doing so is a tradeoff against something else.

Cutting comes first. A wire harp drawn through the curd divides it into cubes,
and the size of those cubes governs almost everything downstream, because whey
leaves a curd particle through its surface. Cut to the size of a walnut and
you get a wet, soft curd destined for a young, spoonable cheese. Cut to the
size of a grain of rice and you get a dry, dense curd that can be pressed into
something that will survive two years in a cellar and be grated over pasta. A
maker of Parmigiano works with curd in fragments smaller than wheat grains.

Heat is the second lever. Raising the temperature of the curd while stirring
makes the protein network contract and squeeze out more whey. Alpine cheeses —
gruyère, emmental, comté — are cooked hot, which was a practical necessity in
mountain dairies where the year's milk had to be converted into wheels heavy
enough to be worth carrying down and durable enough to last until spring. The
copper vats, the high cooking temperature, and the enormous format all belong
to the same logic.

After that the traditions diverge sharply. Cheddaring is a process, not just a
place: the drained curd is allowed to knit into a slab, then cut into blocks
that are stacked and restacked under their own weight while acidity rises,
which squeezes out whey and rearranges the protein into long fibers. The slabs
are then milled into pieces, salted directly, and pressed. Gouda takes the
opposite approach at one crucial step — some whey is drawn off and replaced
with warm water, which washes out lactose before the bacteria can turn it into
acid, giving a sweeter, higher-pH cheese with a supple texture. Pasta filata
cheeses like mozzarella and provolone are held until the curd reaches a narrow
acidity window in which the protein, immersed in hot water, becomes plastic and
can be stretched into ropes, aligning the fibers into the sheets that peel off
a good mozzarella.

Salt goes on last, rubbed into the surface, mixed into milled curd, or applied
by floating the finished wheel in brine for hours or days. It does four jobs at
once: it draws out more moisture, slows the starter bacteria before they
acidify the cheese to death, seasons it, and sets up the conditions for
whatever is meant to grow on the outside.

## What Happens in the Cave

A young wheel is bland, rubbery, and essentially a block of protein, fat, and
water. Aging is the slow disassembly of that block by enzymes.

Proteolysis does most of the work. Residual chymosin left in the curd, the
milk's own proteases, and the peptidases of the bacterial population cut the
casein network into progressively smaller pieces — first large peptides, which
softens the texture from rubbery to yielding, then smaller peptides, then free
amino acids, which are where a great deal of the savory flavor lives. Some
peptides are bitter, which is why badly managed aging can produce a cheese that
tastes harsh, and why choosing a bacterial culture is partly choosing which
peptides will get broken down further.

Fat is attacked in parallel, though more selectively. Free fatty acids released
by lipases give the peppery, faintly goaty bite of a good pecorino or an aged
provolone; some Italian and Greek cheeses get that character deliberately from
rennet pastes that carry the animal's own lipase along with the coagulant. In
blue cheeses, the mold *Penicillium roqueforti* takes this several steps
further, converting fatty acids into methyl ketones, which are the entire
unmistakable smell of a blue.

Different organisms produce different cheeses out of similar curd. A bloomy
rind is *Penicillium camemberti* growing on the surface and consuming lactic
acid, which raises the pH from the outside inward and lets the paste beneath
soften and eventually run. A washed rind is a deliberate cultivation of
*Brevibacterium* and its relatives, encouraged by regular wiping with brine —
orange, sticky, and pungent in a way that is not coincidentally reminiscent of
human skin, since related bacteria live there. The eyes in emmental are carbon
dioxide from propionic acid bacteria, which eat the lactate left behind by the
starter culture and release gas that collects in pockets, along with the
compounds that give alpine cheese its sweet, nutty note.

Then there are the crystals. The white specks in a two-year gouda or an old
parmesan are commonly mistaken for salt; they are mostly tyrosine, an amino
acid liberated in such quantity by proteolysis that it exceeds its solubility
and precipitates. The flatter, shinier crystals found under the rind of aged
cheddar are calcium lactate. Both are evidence that the cheese has been taken
apart to the level of its constituent molecules and has begun to put some of
them back together as minerals — a wheel of cheese, left alone in a cool
cellar, slowly crystallizing.
