# Reading a Pot of Boiling Sugar

Sugar and water go into a heavy pan, and for the first minute or two the mixture
is a cloudy slurry with grit at the bottom. Then it clears. From that moment
until it comes off the heat, the pot looks essentially the same — a clear liquid
boiling hard — and yet it passes through half a dozen entirely different
materials on the way. The difference between a soft caramel and a lollipop is
about thirty degrees and four minutes. Nothing visible marks the boundaries,
which is why sugar work is one of the few kitchen crafts where a thermometer is
not optional.

What the thermometer is really telling you is not temperature. It is
concentration.

## The Thermometer Is a Proxy

Dissolved sugar raises the boiling point of water. A weak syrup boils at close
to a hundred degrees Celsius; as water evaporates, the remaining solution gets
denser, and its boiling point climbs. The relationship is reliable enough that
the reading on the probe corresponds to a specific sugar-to-water ratio,
regardless of how much syrup is in the pot or how fast you are boiling it. At
one hundred and fifteen degrees the syrup is somewhere in the mid-eighties
percent sugar. By the time it reaches a hundred and fifty, almost all the water
is gone and you are looking at molten sucrose with a trace of moisture in it.
Everything the finished candy will do — flow, chew, snap — follows from that
number.

This also means the thermometer must be honest. Water's boiling point falls with
elevation, and a candy target set at sea level will overshoot badly in a
mountain kitchen. The standard correction is to boil plain water first, note
what your thermometer reads, and shift every target by the same difference. It
takes two minutes and it is the difference between fudge and glass.

## The Ladder of Stages

Before thermometers were common, candy-makers judged concentration by dropping a
little syrup into a bowl of cold water and feeling what it did with their
fingers. The names from that practice survive and they are still the most useful
vocabulary in the craft, because each one corresponds to a real change in
behaviour.

At the low end, around a hundred and ten degrees, syrup pulls into a thread
between finger and thumb. This is the range for soaking syrups, candied peel,
and preserving fruit. A few degrees higher, the cooled drop can be pressed into
a soft ball that flattens as soon as you release it — the range for fondant,
fudge, and pralines. Firmer ball, somewhere around a hundred and twenty, holds
its shape but yields to pressure; this is the territory of chewy caramels and of
the hot syrup poured into whipping egg whites to make Italian meringue and the
buttercream built on it. Hard ball, higher still, gives you nougat and
marshmallow.

Then the balls stop being balls. At soft crack, past a hundred and thirty, the
cooled syrup pulls into threads that bend a little before breaking — butterscotch
and taffy live here. At hard crack, around a hundred and fifty, the threads snap
cleanly and the cooled sugar shatters like glass. Lollipops, brittles, hard
candies, and the sheets used for decorative work all come from this stage. Above
that the sugar stops being colourless and starts to cook in a different sense
entirely.

## Keeping Crystals Out — and Putting Them Back In

Sucrose would much rather be a crystal than a syrup. A concentrated solution is
metastable: it holds together until something gives it a template, and then it
seizes up in seconds, turning a smooth pot of syrup into wet sand. One stray
crystal on the side of the pan, one grain flicked off a spoon, and a batch is
finished.

The defences are all about denying it a starting point. Use a scrupulously clean
pan. Dissolve the sugar completely before the mixture reaches a boil, and then
stop stirring altogether — the spoon itself is a crystallisation site. Wash down
the walls of the pan with a wet pastry brush, or cover the pot for the first
couple of minutes so that condensing steam runs down and dissolves whatever has
splashed above the liquid line.

The chemical defence is more interesting. Adding glucose syrup, or a spoonful of
honey, or a pinch of cream of tartar, introduces molecules that are not sucrose.
Acid goes further: at boiling temperatures it splits some of the sucrose into its
two component sugars, glucose and fructose, which are the wrong shape to fit the
sucrose lattice. Scattered through the solution, they physically obstruct the
crystals from growing. A syrup with enough interfering sugar in it can be cooled
to room temperature and stay a clear, glassy solid indefinitely. Too much, and
the candy never sets firm and stays tacky.

Two classic confections invert the whole problem. Fondant and fudge are supposed
to crystallise; they are smooth precisely because they are full of crystals too
small for the tongue to detect. The technique is to cook the syrup, then leave it
strictly undisturbed while it cools to around forty degrees, and only then beat
it hard. Agitating a cool, heavily supersaturated syrup starts an enormous number
of crystals simultaneously, and because they all compete for the same sugar, none
of them gets large. Beat the same syrup while it is still hot and you get a few
big crystals and a gritty texture. Same ingredients, same temperature ladder,
opposite result, decided entirely by when you picked up the spatula.

## From Syrup to Caramel

Past the hard crack stage the water is essentially gone and sucrose begins
breaking down under heat on its own. Caramelisation is not a single reaction but
a cascade: molecules fragment, lose water, recombine, and polymerise, generating
hundreds of compounds along the way — buttery diacetyl, the toasty furans, the
sweet-malty note of maltol — plus a family of brown polymers responsible for the
colour. It requires no protein and no amino acids, which is what distinguishes
it from the Maillard browning of a crust or a seared steak. Make a caramel sauce
by pouring cream into hot caramel and you get both reactions at once.

Caramel is a moving target and it does not wait. The colour deepens from straw
to amber to copper to mahogany over perhaps ninety seconds, and the flavour
tracks it, gaining complexity and then bitterness. Past a certain darkness the
bitterness is all that is left. Worse, the pan keeps cooking after it leaves the
flame — a heavy copper pot carries enough stored heat to push caramel two or
three shades darker while you stand there watching. The professional habit is to
have a basin of water ready and dip the base of the pan the instant the colour is
right.

There are two routes in. A wet caramel starts with water, follows the whole
temperature ladder, and gives you time and control at the cost of the
crystallisation risk. A dry caramel is sugar alone in a pan, melting directly;
it is faster and cannot crystallise, but it browns unevenly and scorches if you
look away. Most people who do this often use the wet method for anything they
care about.

## Pulled, Blown, Cast

Decorative sugar work takes a hard-crack syrup — with glucose and usually a
little acid to keep it docile — and treats it as a thermoplastic. Poured onto a
silicone mat it begins to skin over at the edges; those edges get folded back
into the middle repeatedly until the mass cools into a workable lump that can be
handled with gloved hands.

Pulling it is what produces the satin finish on a competition showpiece. Stretch
the mass, fold it, stretch it again, thirty or forty times, and you fold air into
it in extremely fine layers. Light entering the surface reflects off layer after
layer, and the sugar turns from transparent to a deep pearlescent sheen. From
there it can be shaped into ribbons and petals, or a ball of it can be fixed to a
pump and inflated like glass into a fruit or a bird, thinning as it expands. Cast
sugar, poured into moulds or onto a flat surface, stays transparent and is used
for panels and bases. Spun sugar, flicked from a cut whisk across a rod, sets
into threads finer than hair.

All of this happens in a narrow thermal window — hot enough to be plastic, cool
enough not to run — so the work is done under a heat lamp, dipping back for
warmth and pulling again. Molten sugar at these temperatures adheres to skin and
keeps burning, which is why the bowl of ice water at the bench is not a
suggestion.

Many pastry kitchens have quietly switched decorative work to isomalt, a sugar
alcohol made from sucrose that behaves almost identically under heat but barely
browns, resists crystallising, and can be melted and remelted. The main reason is
water. Sucrose is hygroscopic; it pulls moisture out of the air continuously, and
a pulled sugar rose left out in a humid kitchen goes from crisp and glossy to
weeping and dull in a few hours. Finished pieces live in sealed boxes with
desiccant, and serious sugar work is often scheduled around the weather. Isomalt
is far less thirsty, which buys the person who made the piece an extra day before
it starts to slump.
