# Salt First, Then Smoke

On the drying flakes of Newfoundland and the Gaspé, split cod lay open to the
wind like pages, white with salt, turned by hand twice a day and stacked under
tarps whenever weather threatened. The fish had been caught in cold water,
gutted within hours, and buried in salt in the hold. By the time it reached a
warehouse in Lisbon or Bilbao it was stiff as board, weighed a third of what it
had at the boat rail, and would keep for a year in a dry room without any cold
at all. That trade fed the fast days of Catholic Europe for centuries and
underwrote a great deal of North Atlantic shipping. It ran on one idea: take the
water out, and nothing can live in what is left.

Every preservation technique that involves salt and smoke is a variation on that
sentence, and most of the sophistication lies in how slowly and how evenly you
do it.

## What Salt Actually Does

Microorganisms need liquid water — not water bound up in something else, but
free water they can move solutes across. Food scientists measure the free
fraction as water activity, a number between zero and one where pure water is
one. Salt dissolves into whatever moisture is present and ties it up. As the
concentration rises, the activity falls, and the organisms drop out one by one.
Most of the bacteria that spoil meat and most of the ones that make people
seriously ill stop growing somewhere around the low nineties in hundredths;
a few tougher species, including the staphylococci, hang on considerably
further down. Molds and certain yeasts are hardier still, which is why the
surface of an aged ham grows a white bloom that the interior never sees.

Salt does more than dehydrate. It penetrates muscle by diffusion, which is slow
and depends on thickness, temperature, and fat content — fat is nearly a barrier,
which is why a fatty belly cures differently from a lean loin. Once inside, salt
alters the muscle proteins themselves. At low concentrations it actually makes
meat hold water better by loosening the protein filaments, which is the whole
principle behind brining a turkey. At the much higher concentrations of a real
cure, it goes the other way, drawing moisture out and leaving the protein
network denser and tackier. That tackiness is why a properly cured ham slices
into sheets instead of crumbling, and why cured sausage binds without any
starch.

The oldest method is the dry cure: pack the meat in salt, leave it in the cold,
pour off what drains, repack. It is effective and nearly foolproof and it tends
to over-salt, because you never know exactly how much the meat took up. The
modern refinement is the equilibrium cure — weigh the meat, calculate the exact
percentage of salt you want in the finished product, apply precisely that much,
and seal it so nothing can leave. Given enough time the salt distributes itself
evenly and the result cannot be saltier than the number you chose. It takes
longer and it is far more predictable.

## The Nitrate Accident

For most of the history of curing, the salt people used was not pure. It came
from evaporated seawater or from mined rock deposits, and it carried mineral
impurities, among them nitrates. Cooks noticed that certain salts — from certain
places — produced meat that came out rosy rather than a dull grey-brown, kept
better, and tasted distinctly different, with the particular tang people still
describe simply as "cured." Eventually saltpeter was added deliberately.

The chemistry was worked out much later, and it turns out nitrate is not the
active agent. Bacteria present in the meat reduce nitrate to nitrite, and nitrite
is what matters. It breaks down to nitric oxide, which binds to the myoglobin in
muscle and forms a pigment that survives cooking as a stable pink. That is the
colour of ham, of bacon, of corned beef, of a hot dog. Nitrite also suppresses
the growth of *Clostridium botulinum*, the anaerobe whose spores survive ordinary
salting and whose toxin is the single worst hazard in sealed, low-oxygen cured
products. And it works as an antioxidant, slowing the fat rancidity that
otherwise gives reheated cured meat its stale cardboard note.

Modern curing salts package this as a known quantity rather than a hope. The mix
sold for short cures is a small, fixed percentage of sodium nitrite blended into
ordinary salt and dyed pink so nobody mistakes it for table salt. A second blend
adds nitrate as well, because a ham that hangs for eighteen months will exhaust
its nitrite long before it is finished, and the nitrate acts as a slow reservoir
that bacteria draw down over the length of the cure.

Nitrite has had a complicated public life. At high cooking temperatures it can
contribute to the formation of nitrosamines, which is a genuine concern and the
reason permitted levels were reduced decades ago and ascorbate or erythorbate is
routinely added to interfere with the reaction. The "uncured" bacon sold with a
disclaimer on the label is generally cured with celery juice powder, which is a
concentrated natural nitrate source; the bacteria in the meat do the same
conversion they have always done. The chemistry is identical. Only the
paperwork differs.

## What Is Actually in Smoke

Smoke is not a flavour. It is a suspension of several hundred compounds produced
when wood decomposes under heat, and its usefulness comes from three of its
fractions doing three different jobs.

Wood is mostly cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. Heat the lignin and it
breaks into phenolic compounds — guaiacol, syringol, and their relatives — which
carry nearly all of what people recognise as smokiness, and which also happen to
be antimicrobial and antioxidant. The cellulose and hemicellulose fractions break
down at somewhat lower temperatures into carbonyls and organic acids. The
carbonyls react with surface proteins and give smoked food its mahogany colour,
essentially a Maillard reaction run without an oven. The acids, along with traces
of formaldehyde, lower the surface pH and firm the outer layer into a skin that
is genuinely hostile to bacteria.

All of this depends on how the wood is burning. There is a working range —
roughly the temperature of a healthy, well-drafted fire that has burned past its
initial flare — where pyrolysis yields plenty of phenols and relatively little
soot. Starve the fire of air and you get smouldering, which produces a heavy,
greasy, acrid smoke loaded with creosote that coats food in something closer to
tar than seasoning. Push it too hot and you get more polycyclic aromatic
hydrocarbons, the compounds that have made heavily smoked foods a subject of
health scrutiny. The thin, faintly blue stream that experienced smokers look for
is the visual signature of wood burning in the useful range. Thick white or
yellow smoke means something is wrong with the fire.

Smoke sticks to a surface that is tacky and slightly dry, not wet. After the
cure, meat and fish are rinsed and then air-dried until a pellicle forms: a thin,
burnished protein layer you can feel with a fingertip. Put food into a smoker
while it is still beaded with moisture and the smoke compounds dissolve into the
surface water and drip off with it, leaving a sour taste and a pale colour.

Temperature splits the practice into two crafts. Cold smoking keeps the chamber
below about thirty degrees, so the food never cooks — this is how salmon,
traditional bacon, and many hard sausages are done, and it is only safe because
the salt has already done the preserving. Hot smoking runs the chamber hot
enough to cook the food through, which is a different product entirely: smoked
trout, smoked chicken, American-style barbecue.

## The Long Slow Middle

The part nobody photographs is the hanging. A dry-cured ham spends a few weeks
in salt, then months in a cellar at cool temperature and high humidity, losing
weight the whole time. A third of its original mass or more leaves as water
vapour. Enzymes native to the muscle keep working the entire time, breaking
proteins into free amino acids and fats into aromatic fragments, which is where
the deep savoury character of a long-aged ham comes from. That character is not
put in. It develops.

The humidity is the thing that has to be watched. Too damp and mould takes the
surface faster than you can brush it back. Too dry and you get case hardening:
the outside dries and contracts into a shell before moisture from the centre can
migrate out through it, sealing wet meat inside a dry crust. Case-hardened
sausage looks finished and spoils from the middle. The fix is not heroic — a
cool, humid room and the discipline to let things take the time they take.

Fermented sausages add one more barrier. Lactic acid bacteria, either
back-slopped from a previous batch in the old way or added as a measured starter
culture in the modern one, consume the sugar in the mix and drop the pH. An acid
environment stops a range of organisms that salt alone would not, and the drop
also helps the meat firm up and release water.

This stacking is the actual principle, and it has a name in the food safety
literature: hurdle technology. No single barrier here is lethal on its own. Salt
does not sterilise. Nitrite does not sterilise. Smoke reaches only the outer few
millimetres. Acidity alone is beatable and drying alone is slow. But an organism
that must simultaneously tolerate low water activity, a nitrite environment,
surface phenols, a low pH, and a cold room finds no way through — and a ham that
would rot in three days on a kitchen counter hangs in a cellar for two years and
gets better.
