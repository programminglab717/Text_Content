# What Happens Inside a Rising Dough

A jar of sourdough starter kept on a kitchen counter is a small, tended
ecosystem with a metabolism you can hear if you put your ear to it. Feed it
flour and water, wait a few hours, and it doubles, domes, and gives off a smell
somewhere between yogurt and beer. Leave it alone for a week and it collapses,
separates, and turns the color of weak tea, with a sharp layer of alcohol on
top. It is not spoiling, exactly. It is running out of food and living on the
consequences of its own waste, which is a fair description of what fermentation
is: a population of microbes rearranging a substrate until the substrate, or
the environment, will no longer support them.

What makes bread possible is that this process is both slower and more useful
than it sounds. The gas is only the most visible part of what the microbes do,
and arguably not the most important.

## What the Flour Brings

Wheat flour is roughly three-quarters starch, a tenth protein, and a scattering
of sugars, lipids, minerals, and enzymes. Yeast cannot eat starch. It needs
simple sugars, and flour contains only a little free sugar — enough to get
things started and no more. The bridge is built by the flour's own amylase
enzymes, which are activated as soon as the flour is hydrated and which begin
clipping the ends off damaged starch molecules, releasing maltose. Millers
adjust this deliberately, blending in a small amount of malted barley flour if
a batch is short on enzyme activity, because a dough that runs out of sugar
halfway through fermentation goes flat and bakes pale.

The protein matters for a different reason. Two families of wheat proteins,
glutenin and gliadin, are insoluble in water and inert in dry flour, but once
wetted and worked they begin to associate into a continuous network. Glutenin
comes in enormous linked polymers and contributes elasticity — the springy pull
back when you stretch a dough. Gliadin is smaller and monomeric and
contributes flow, the willingness of the dough to be extended without tearing.
Together they make gluten, a material that is neither a solid nor a liquid in
any ordinary sense but a viscoelastic one, capable of holding a shape and of
slowly deforming under load.

That network does not require kneading to form. It assembles on its own, more
slowly, given time and water; the practice of mixing flour and water and
letting it sit for half an hour before adding salt and leaven — an autolyse —
simply hands that job over to chemistry rather than muscle. What kneading does
is speed alignment and incorporate air, which turns out to matter enormously
for reasons that have nothing to do with strength.

## Two Populations, One Jar

Commercial baker's yeast is a single organism, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*,
selected and propagated in enormous quantity. A sourdough culture is a
community. Wild yeasts are present, often species other than the commercial
one, but they are outnumbered by lactic acid bacteria by something on the order
of a hundred to one. The bacteria are the reason sourdough tastes the way it
does; the yeasts do most of the lifting.

The two groups get along because they eat differently. Some of the
best-adapted sourdough bacteria prefer maltose and, in the course of consuming
it, release glucose they cannot immediately use back into the dough — glucose
that the yeasts, which would rather have it than maltose, eagerly take up. The
bacteria produce lactic acid, and often acetic acid alongside it, plus carbon
dioxide and ethanol depending on species. The acid drops the dough's pH, which
suppresses competitors that might otherwise move in, and which is why a mature
starter is remarkably stable against contamination. A culture maintained on a
kitchen counter for years is not being protected by anything except its own
acidity.

The balance between the two acids is adjustable by the baker, though it is
rarely described in those terms. Warmer, wetter, faster fermentations favor
lactic acid, which tastes mild and creamy. Cooler, stiffer, slower ones, with
more oxygen available, tilt toward acetic acid, which is vinegar and reads as
sharp. A baker who complains that their bread is too sour and is told to keep
the starter stiffer and warmer is being given a piece of applied microbiology.

## The Gas and the Gluten

Here is the detail that surprises people: fermentation does not create bubbles.
It cannot. Carbon dioxide produced by yeast dissolves first into the water
phase of the dough, and it will keep dissolving until that phase is saturated.
Only then does it begin to come out of solution, and it can only do so into gas
pockets that already exist. Those pockets were put there during mixing, as air
folded into the dough. Every hole in a finished loaf traces back to a bubble of
air trapped before the yeast did anything at all. This is why aggressive mixing
gives a fine, even crumb and gentle mixing gives the irregular open holes that
people covet in country bread — the difference is how many nucleation sites
were created at the start, not how much gas was produced later.

As the bubbles inflate, the gluten films between them are stretched thinner.
Folding the dough during its first rise — turning it over on itself every
forty minutes or so — redistributes those bubbles, equalizes temperature, and
stretches the network into a stronger alignment without deflating it the way
punching down does. The dough gets stiffer and more elastic with each fold, up
to a point.

Beyond that point, fermentation starts working against structure. Flour
contains proteases, and the bacteria contribute more; acidity activates them.
Over hours they nibble at the gluten, cutting long chains into short ones. A
dough taken too far goes slack, sticky, and finally soupy, unable to hold gas
at all. Every long fermentation is a race between the network getting stronger
from folding and weaker from enzymatic attack, and knowing where the crossover
lies for a particular flour is most of what bakers mean by feel.

## Time as an Ingredient

The reason to ferment for twelve hours rather than two has little to do with
leavening. Two hours with enough commercial yeast will inflate a dough just
fine. What the long haul produces is everything else.

Proteolysis, the same process that eventually ruins structure, liberates free
amino acids. Amylase keeps liberating sugars. The bacteria produce organic
acids, and the yeasts produce ethanol and a spectrum of esters and alcohols
that account for the fruity, winey notes in a well-fermented dough. Almost
none of these survive the oven unchanged, but they are the raw material for
what happens in the crust, where the accumulated sugars and amino acids brown
into the aromas that make a loaf smell like a loaf. Bread fermented quickly
bakes to a paler, blanker crust simply because there is less there to react.

Long fermentation does other things too. The acidity activates phytase, an
enzyme that breaks down phytic acid, the compound in bran that binds zinc,
iron, and magnesium and carries them through the gut unabsorbed; a long-
fermented whole grain loaf delivers more of its minerals than a fast one. The
fermentable carbohydrates in wheat that trouble some people with sensitive
digestion are substantially consumed by a long fermentation, which is the
likeliest explanation for why some who cannot comfortably eat supermarket bread
have no trouble with slowly made sourdough.

Cold retarding — moving the shaped dough to a refrigerator overnight — works
because the two populations respond differently to cold. Yeast activity falls
off steeply; the bacteria keep producing acid at a reduced but meaningful rate.
The dough therefore acquires flavor and acidity faster than it acquires gas,
and the baker gains a wide window in which the loaf is ready to bake.

## What the Industry Did With the Clock

For most of the history of bread, every loaf was a sourdough, because the only
available leaven was a piece of yesterday's dough or a pot of batter kept
going on a shelf. Brewers' barm — the foam skimmed off fermenting beer — served
bakers in beer-drinking regions as a faster alternative. Isolated, purified
baker's yeast is a nineteenth-century product, arriving alongside the
understanding, worked out by Pasteur and others, that fermentation was the work
of living organisms rather than a spontaneous chemical decay.

The consequence was that leavening could be decoupled from time. If you add
enough of a vigorous single-species yeast, a dough will rise in an hour. What
it will not do in an hour is develop flavor, or break down phytates, or let its
gluten mature — so the industry engineered around those gaps. The best-known
result is the Chorleywood process, developed in Britain in 1961, which
substitutes a few minutes of extremely intense mechanical mixing for hours of
bulk fermentation, aided by oxidizing agents, added fat, extra yeast, and
enzymes. It produces a soft, uniform, long-lasting loaf from lower-protein
wheat at remarkable speed, and it made British sliced bread what it is. It also
explains why so much commercial bread tastes of very little: nearly all of the
chemistry that generates flavor in bread happens on a clock that the process
was designed to eliminate.

## The Last Twenty Minutes

Everything the fermentation built is resolved in the first third of the bake.
Heat expands the trapped gas, dissolved carbon dioxide comes out of solution as
its solubility falls, and water and ethanol flash to vapor — ethanol boils at
78°C and contributes more to oven spring than most people expect. The yeast has
a final, furious burst of activity as the dough warms through its optimum
before dying somewhere around 55 to 60°C. All of this happens while the gluten
is still extensible. The loaf leaps.

Then the structure sets. Starch granules absorb water and gelatinize from
around 60°C upward, swelling and turning the dough's continuous protein network
into a starch-dominated sponge, while the gluten itself denatures and
stiffens. The scored cut opens where the baker put it because that is the one
place the surface has no skin to resist. The exterior dries, climbs past the
boiling point of water, and browns. The interior never exceeds about 98°C, no
matter how long the loaf stays in.

A bread baked without steam sets its crust too early and cannot expand; a bread
given too much steam stays pale and soft. This is the whole reason for the
pan of water, the spray bottle, the lidded pot. It is a way of postponing one
phase of the bake until the other is finished.
