# What Happens Inside a Roast

Open an oven set to two hundred and twenty degrees Celsius and you can hold
your hand in the cavity for several seconds without pain. Put your hand into a
pot of water at a hundred and you will be in the emergency room. The air is more
than twice as hot and does far less damage, and that single comparison explains
most of what roasting is and why it behaves so differently from every other way
of applying heat to food.

## The Oven Is a Poor Conductor on Purpose

Air is a lousy carrier of heat. A given volume of it holds thousands of times
less thermal energy than the same volume of water, and it conducts heat more
than twenty times more slowly. The practical consequence is that an oven
transfers heat into food at a gentle, forgiving rate, which is what makes it
possible to cook a large object all the way through without incinerating the
outside. Boiling water would cook the surface of a rib roast to well-done long
before the middle knew anything was happening.

Three mechanisms are at work in the cavity and they are not equal. Conduction
through still air is nearly negligible. Convection — the slow circulation of
hot air against the surface — does real work, but it is throttled by a layer of
relatively stagnant air that clings to any object in a moving stream. A
convection oven's fan does not make the air hotter; it thins that boundary
layer, and the increase in heat transfer is large enough that most recipes
recommend dropping the set temperature by fifteen or twenty degrees when a fan
is running. Radiation from the oven walls and elements is the third mechanism,
and in a hot oven it is more important than most cooks realize. Radiant heat
travels in straight lines, which is why the side of a chicken facing the back
wall browns faster, why a deep roasting pan with tall sides shields the lower
half of whatever sits in it, and why a rack lifting the meat clear of the pan
changes the result noticeably.

## The Surface Has to Dry Before It Can Brown

Nothing browns at a hundred degrees. The Maillard reaction, which produces the
enormous family of aromatic compounds responsible for the smell of a roast,
becomes fast enough to matter somewhere above about a hundred and forty; sugar
caramelization needs more still. But as long as there is liquid water at the
surface, evaporation pins the temperature there near a hundred, because
vaporizing water absorbs an enormous amount of energy — several times what is
needed to heat the same water from freezing to boiling in the first place.

So the first phase of any roast is a drying phase, and it is unglamorous. Heat
goes into driving off surface moisture and essentially nothing visible happens.
Only once a thin layer of the exterior has actually dried can its temperature
climb past the boiling point and browning start. This is why almost every
technique for better crust is really a technique for starting drier: salting a
chicken a day ahead and leaving it uncovered in the refrigerator, patting a
steak with paper towels, air-drying a duck, or simply not crowding the pan. It
is also why a roast that has been basted every fifteen minutes comes out pale.
Each basting re-wets the surface and resets the clock, and the mechanism is the
same whether the liquid is pan juices or a marinade.

## Meanwhile, Inside

While the exterior is drying and browning, the interior is doing something
completely unrelated: passing through a ladder of protein changes, each at its
own temperature.

Around fifty degrees Celsius the myosin in muscle fibers begins to denature and
the meat firms up and turns from translucent to opaque. Somewhere in the
mid-sixties the collagen sheathing the fiber bundles contracts sharply, and
because that sheath is wrapped around the fibers it squeezes them, expelling
juice. This is the moment a steak goes from dripping to merely moist. Just above
that, near sixty-six degrees, actin denatures, and meat that has passed this
point is unambiguously dry and grainy — the actual physical basis of the
complaint about overcooked pork or chicken breast.

Doneness, then, is a temperature and not a duration. Everything else — the
weight of the roast, the oven setting, whether it came out of the refrigerator
cold — only affects how long it takes to arrive. A thermometer is not a
crutch; it is the only instrument that measures the thing that actually
determines the outcome.

## Tough Cuts Want the Opposite Bargain

If actin denaturation ruins meat above the mid-sixties, why is a brisket held
near ninety-five degrees for ten hours one of the most tender things you can
eat? Because there are two different kinds of toughness and they respond to
heat in opposite directions.

A tenderloin is tender to begin with, has very little connective tissue, and has
nothing to gain from time. Cooking it past the point where its proteins have set
only squeezes out water. A shoulder, a shank, or a brisket is laced with
collagen, and collagen above roughly seventy degrees slowly hydrolyses into
gelatin. The reaction is not fast — it is measured in hours, not minutes — but
it converts the toughest structural element in the cut into a substance that
lubricates rather than resists. The muscle fibers themselves are genuinely dry
by then. The gelatin masks it, coating everything and giving the meat the
succulent quality that no amount of careful low-temperature cooking can supply.
Slow-roasted pork shoulder, pot roast, and confit all exploit this. The rule is
simple enough to state: cook tender cuts to a temperature, cook tough cuts for a
time.

## Managing the Gradient

Because heat has to travel inward by conduction, every roast has a temperature
gradient running from a scorching exterior to a cooler center, and the steepness
of that gradient is set almost entirely by the oven temperature. A hot oven
gives fast browning and a thick band of overcooked gray meat under the crust. A
low oven gives a nearly uniform interior and almost no crust at all.

The reverse sear exists to get both. Roast at a very low temperature — a
hundred and twenty degrees, or lower — until the center is a few degrees below
target, then hit the surface hard with a blast of high heat, a broiler, or a
ripping-hot pan for a couple of minutes. The interior is uniformly pink from
edge to edge and the crust forms too quickly to penetrate. The older order,
searing first and then roasting, still works, but it produces a wider gray band,
and the folklore explanation that searing "seals in the juices" is simply
wrong. A seared roast loses about as much moisture as an unseared one. Searing
is for flavor.

Two more gradient effects are worth knowing. The first is carryover: when the
roast comes out, the hot exterior is still far above the center, and heat keeps
flowing inward for some minutes. A thick roast can climb several degrees after
leaving the oven, and pulling it at the target temperature guarantees
overcooking. The second is resting, which does less than the folklore claims but
more than the debunkers allow. Juices squeezed out of contracted fibers are
thinner when hot and thicker as they cool, and the compressed protein network
relaxes slightly as the temperature drops, so a rested roast loses visibly less
liquid to the board when carved.

Poultry adds a complication, because a bird is two animals thermally. The breast
is lean and wants to stop in the high sixties; the legs are full of connective
tissue and are better at seventy-five or higher. Roasting a whole chicken is a
negotiation between them. Cooks manage it by starting the bird breast down, by
icing the breast before it goes in, by spatchcocking so the whole thing lies
flat and cooks in one plane, or by giving up and cooking the parts separately.
Trussing, incidentally, cuts both ways: it makes a tidier bird and slows the
legs down, which is usually the opposite of what you want.

## Vegetables Play by Different Rules

A roasted vegetable has no collagen and no actin, and its transformation is
about cell walls, sugars, and water. Heat softens pectin in the cell walls, the
cells lose turgor, and the structure collapses from crisp to tender. Meanwhile
the surface dries and browns, and because vegetables carry far more free sugar
than meat does, caramelization contributes alongside Maillard. A roasted carrot
tastes sweeter than a boiled one partly because it has lost water and
concentrated what was already there, and partly because heat has broken some of
its starch and complex carbohydrates down into simpler sugars.

The single most common failure is crowding. Pile vegetables into a pan and the
water they release cannot escape; the tray fills with steam, the surface
temperature stays near a hundred, and an hour later you have something soft,
gray, and disappointing. Spread them in one layer with space between the pieces,
use a hot oven, and use a preheated heavy tray so the undersides get conductive
contact rather than only air. Oil helps for two reasons that have nothing to do
with preventing sticking: it fills the microscopic gaps between the food and the
hot metal, improving heat transfer, and it dissolves and carries aromatic
compounds that are not water-soluble.

Potatoes deserve their own note, because the classic treatment is an engineering
trick. Parboiling until the outsides are just breaking down gelatinizes the
surface starch; shaking the drained pieces roughly in the pan turns that
gelatinized layer into a fuzzy slurry with vastly more surface area; and when
that slurry hits hot fat in the oven it dehydrates into a shell that shatters.
The inside stays fluffy because it has been steamed rather than fried. Nothing
about it happens by accident.
