# Why Cooks Taste Constantly

A pot of lentil soup has been simmering for forty minutes and it is fine. Not
bad. Somebody eating it would say it was nice. A quarter teaspoon of salt goes
in, then a squeeze of lemon, and thirty seconds later the same pot has edges and
depth and a finish, and the person who added them did not measure either one.
They tasted, decided the soup was slightly short in two directions, and moved it.
That small unglamorous loop — taste, judge, adjust, taste again — is most of what
separates cooking that lands from cooking that merely happens, and it is the one
part of the process that recipes are structurally incapable of doing for you.

This is worth saying plainly, because recipe culture has quietly trained a couple
of generations to treat seasoning as a measurement. Recipes list salt in
teaspoons because they must list something. But the quantity that will be right
depends on how much your onion weighed, how salty your stock was, how much liquid
boiled off while you answered the door, how old your lemon is, and what kind of
salt is in your jar. A recipe can give you a starting bid. It cannot close the
deal.

## The Salt in Your Jar Is Not the Salt in Theirs

Start with the most embarrassing source of error. Salt is sold in crystal forms
of wildly different density. A flaky kosher salt is mostly air by volume; a fine
table salt packs tight. A tablespoon of one can weigh close to double a
tablespoon of the other, and in the extremes more than that. Two cooks following
the same recipe to the letter, both of them scrupulous, can end up with dishes
that differ in salinity by a factor a palate notices immediately.

There are two honest ways out. Weigh your salt, which is what professional
kitchens and every serious baker do. Or pick one salt, use it for everything,
and calibrate your hand to it over a few months. The one thing that does not work
is following volume measurements from various sources with whatever is in the
cupboard.

Weighing also lets you use the only genuinely portable rule of thumb in
seasoning, which is a percentage. Soups, braises, and stews land close to right
at somewhere around one percent salt by total weight. Meat dry-brined ahead of
cooking takes roughly the same. The number is not magic and plenty of dishes
want more or less, but it gets you into the neighbourhood on the first attempt
instead of the fourth, and it works regardless of what the recipe said.

## Salt Does More Than Taste Salty

Sodium's obvious effect is that it registers as salty. Its more useful effects
are indirect. Salt suppresses bitterness — which is why a pinch improves black
coffee, grapefruit, and bitter greens, and why under-salted braises of dark meat
can taste harsh. It amplifies perceived sweetness, which is why salted caramel
reads as more caramel rather than less. And it makes savoury depth legible; an
under-salted stock tastes thin and watery even when it is full of dissolved
protein and gelatine.

Most of what people call flavour is not taste at all. The tongue reports five
things: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and savoury. Everything else — the entire
difference between a strawberry and a raspberry, between rosemary and thyme —
arrives as aroma, travelling up the back of the throat into the nasal cavity
while you chew. Pinch your nose shut and eat a piece of fruit and you will get
sweetness, acidity, and texture, and nothing else. Release your nose and the
fruit appears. Salt participates in that system too, by shifting the balance of
what volatiles reach your nose.

A few sensations that everyone calls taste are not taste either. Chilli heat is
a pain signal. The tingle of Sichuan pepper is a touch signal. The drying grip of
strong tea or unripe persimmon is astringency, a tactile effect of tannins
binding the proteins in saliva. Knowing which channel a sensation arrives through
is practical, because they behave differently: you can dilute salt, but chilli
heat is fat-soluble and largely dilution-resistant, and astringency responds to
fat and sugar rather than to water.

## When It Is Flat and Salt Is Not the Answer

The most common diagnostic mistake is adding salt to a dish that does not need
it. A cook tastes, finds the dish dull, reaches for salt because that is the
reflex, and keeps going until the dish is both dull and salty.

Dullness has at least four causes and salt is only one. Acid is the second, and
probably the most underused correction in home kitchens. If a dish tastes heavy,
muddy, or like it stops halfway through, a small amount of vinegar or citrus or
pickle brine will frequently fix it without making the dish taste sour. Fat is
the third — many aroma compounds are fat-soluble, and a dish that has none has
no vehicle for them and no persistence on the palate; a knob of butter stirred in
at the end is doing chemistry, not indulgence. The fourth is savoury depth, which
you add with the ingredients that carry glutamate: anchovy, soy, fish sauce,
parmesan rind, tomato paste, dried mushrooms, kombu.

That last category has a property worth knowing. Glutamate on its own produces a
moderate savoury effect. Combined with certain nucleotides — the inosinate in
dried fish and in meat, the guanylate in dried shiitake — the effect is not added
but multiplied. Japanese dashi is exactly this pairing, kombu supplying one half
and dried bonito the other, which is why a stock made from two ingredients and
ten minutes of steeping tastes like something that simmered all day. The same
trick is why tomatoes and anchovies work, why parmesan on mushroom pasta works,
and why almost every cuisine has independently found a version of it.

## Your Palate Drifts While You Use It

Taste adapts. Sample the same sauce five times in two minutes and the fifth taste
is not comparable to the first — your receptors have partially normalised to it,
and a dish that is genuinely over-salted can start reading as correct. This is a
real and well-documented effect, and it is the mechanism behind the classic
disaster where a cook seasons a sauce progressively over twenty minutes and
serves something nobody else can eat.

The working countermeasures are unglamorous. Step away and come back. Drink
water, eat a piece of plain bread. Taste a neutral reference — unseasoned stock,
plain rice — immediately before the dish, so you have a baseline. Ask someone
else, which is the fastest method and the one most people skip out of pride.

Temperature matters as much as adaptation. Cold suppresses both sweetness and
aroma release; a sorbet base that tastes correctly sweet at room temperature will
be bland frozen, and an ice cream base should taste faintly too sweet when warm.
A cold soup needs noticeably more salt and acid than the same soup served hot. A
sauce judged at scalding temperature, straight off the stove, will read
differently on a plate two minutes later. Taste things at the temperature they
will be eaten, or learn the offset.

And taste from a clean spoon each time. This is a hygiene rule in professional
kitchens for obvious reasons, but it also matters sensorily — a spoon carrying
residue from the last three tastes is not giving you a clean reading.

## Seasoning Is Not a Personal Preference

Here is the part that takes longest to accept. Your own preferred level of
salt is not the target. Salt sensitivity varies between people, declines with
age, and shifts with habit: a cook who eats heavily salted food all day
recalibrates upward without noticing. Professional kitchens handle this by
establishing a house standard and having several people check against it, which
is what a chef is doing when they taste every sauce on the pass. They are not
performing scepticism. They are correcting for the fact that the person who
cooked the sauce has been smelling it for an hour and can no longer judge it.

Cooking at home for other people raises the same problem with none of the
infrastructure. The practical answer is to season to the middle rather than to
your own extreme, get most of the way there in the pot, and put salt, acid, and
chilli on the table so people can finish the job themselves. A dish seasoned to
eighty percent with good condiments beside it will please more people than one
seasoned precisely to the cook's own taste.

None of this can be learned by reading, which is the frustrating part. It is
learned by tasting things constantly, including things that are not finished and
things that went wrong — tasting the stock before and after reduction, tasting
the raw vegetable and then the cooked one, tasting a dish you have over-salted so
you know exactly what that feels like and can recognise the approach next time.
The spoon is the instrument. Everything else is a suggestion.
