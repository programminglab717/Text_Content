# How an Emulsion Holds Together

There is a specific moment in making mayonnaise by hand when the bowl changes
its mind. For the first minute you are whisking a thin yellow slick that slides
around and refuses to thicken, and you are fairly sure you have ruined it. Then,
somewhere around the third or fourth addition of oil, the whisk meets
resistance. The sound changes from a slosh to a dull scraping. The surface
starts holding the marks of the wires. From that point on the sauce will take
oil almost as fast as you can pour it, and it gets thicker the more you add —
which is strange, because oil is thinner than water and you are adding nothing
but oil.

Everything odd about that moment comes from the same source. A mayonnaise is not
a mixture of oil and water in the ordinary sense. It is a suspension of
droplets, and its behavior is governed less by what the ingredients are than by
how small the droplets are and how tightly they are packed.

## Two Liquids That Refuse Each Other

Water molecules are strongly polar and hold onto each other through hydrogen
bonds. Oil molecules are long, mostly nonpolar hydrocarbon chains with nothing
to offer that arrangement. Put the two together and the water is not so much
repelled by the oil as busy with itself; the cheapest configuration is the one
that minimizes the contact area between the two phases. That is why a
vinaigrette shaken hard breaks into two clean layers within minutes. The
droplets find each other, merge into larger droplets, and larger droplets have
less surface per unit volume than small ones. The system slides downhill toward
a single flat interface.

Making an emulsion means forcing the system uphill and then pinning it there.
Whisking, blending, or shaking supplies the mechanical energy that tears the
oil into droplets, creating an enormous new interface — a single tablespoon of
oil broken into two-micron droplets has a surface area measured in square
meters. That interface costs energy to maintain, and the droplets will
immediately start merging again unless something occupies the boundary and
makes merging difficult. That something is an emulsifier.

## What a Yolk Actually Does

An emulsifier is a molecule with a split personality: one end likes water, the
other likes fat. Dropped into a two-phase system it has no comfortable place to
sit except exactly at the boundary, with its water-loving head in the water and
its fat-loving tail in the oil. Enough of these crowded onto a droplet's
surface and two things happen. The interfacial tension falls, so less energy is
required to make droplets in the first place. And the droplets acquire a coat
that physically and electrically resists being pushed into contact with a
neighbor.

Egg yolk is the classic kitchen emulsifier, and its reputation usually gets
assigned entirely to lecithin. Lecithin is genuinely a surfactant, but purified
lecithin on its own is a mediocre one, and it does not explain how well a yolk
works. Most of the power comes from the yolk's lipoprotein particles and its
proteins, which unfold at the interface and spread into a tough, interlocked
film — less like a coating of soap and more like a skin. That film is why a
mayonnaise resists coalescence so stubbornly, and why a single yolk will carry
far more oil than most recipes ever ask of it.

Mustard is the common reinforcement. It contributes some surfactant activity of
its own, but its main gift is mucilage from the seed coat, a soluble
carbohydrate that thickens the water phase. Thicker water slows everything
down: droplets drift into each other less often, and drainage of liquid from
between two touching droplets takes longer. Much of the practical art of
stabilizing emulsions is not about the interface at all. It is about making the
continuous phase syrupy enough that the droplets simply cannot get anywhere.

## Mayonnaise Is Mostly Geometry

Which brings us back to that thickening. Finished mayonnaise is typically
around seventy to eighty percent oil by volume. Equal spheres, packed as
tightly as geometry allows, occupy about seventy-four percent of the space they
sit in. Mayonnaise is at or past that limit, which means its droplets cannot
remain spheres. They are jammed against one another, flattened at the contact
points into something closer to a foam of polyhedra separated by thin films of
water. Nothing can move without deforming its neighbors, and that is the whole
explanation for the texture. Mayonnaise is stiff for the same reason a bag of
gravel is stiff.

It also explains the rule about adding the oil slowly at the start. Early on
there is plenty of water phase and very little oil, so a large glug of oil
arrives as a big blob that the whisk has to subdivide from scratch — and while
it waits, it has every opportunity to reassemble. Once the emulsion is
established and crowded, the situation inverts: the existing droplets act as
grinding media, shearing any newly added oil apart against each other, and you
can pour with something close to abandon. A blender or food processor changes
the arithmetic because it delivers shear far more violently, which is why
immersion-blender mayonnaise can be made by dumping everything in the cup at
once and letting the head work upward from the bottom.

Droplet size is also why the same ingredients can give very different sauces. A
long, hard blend makes smaller droplets, more of them, more interface, a
stiffer and whiter mayonnaise — the whiteness is simply light scattering off a
greater number of small surfaces. Hand-whisked mayonnaise has larger droplets,
looks yellower, and pours a little more loosely. Neither is wrong.

## The Three Ways It Falls Apart

Emulsions do not fail in one generic way; they fail in three distinguishable
ones, and knowing which has happened tells you what to do.

Creaming is the mildest. Oil is less dense than water, so given time the
droplets float upward and concentrate at the top while the water phase clears
below. Nothing has actually merged. A shake or a stir puts it back. This is what
a bottled vinaigrette does overnight.

Coalescence is the real failure. Two droplets touch, the film between them
drains and ruptures, and they become one larger droplet. Repeat a few million
times and you have a slick of free oil and a puddle of weeping yolk. This is
what people mean when they say a sauce has broken, and stirring harder makes it
worse, because you are adding energy to a system that no longer has enough
emulsifier spread across enough interface to use it.

Ostwald ripening is the quiet one. Slightly soluble oil migrates through the
water phase from small droplets to large ones, because pressure inside a small
droplet is higher. The small shrink, the large grow, and the emulsion coarsens
without anything visibly dramatic happening. It is more of a problem for
flavored oils and beverages than for mayonnaise.

The rescue for a broken mayonnaise follows directly from the diagnosis. You
cannot un-merge droplets by whisking. What you can do is start a fresh, small,
stable emulsion — a spoonful of water, or a new yolk, or a spoonful of the
intact mayonnaise from a jar — and then feed the broken sauce into it drop by
drop, exactly as if it were oil. You are not repairing the old emulsion. You are
building a new one out of its wreckage.

## Hot Emulsions Live in a Narrow Window

Hollandaise is the same structure with a complication: heat. Melted butter is
roughly four-fifths fat and the rest water and milk solids, and a hollandaise
suspends that fat in a water phase of yolk, lemon juice, and a little added
water. The yolk proteins are doing the stabilizing, and they are also the thing
most likely to wreck the sauce, because above roughly eighty degrees Celsius
they coagulate with each other instead of spreading at the interface. The sauce
turns granular. Too cold and the butterfat begins crystallizing, the droplets
stiffen, and the emulsion can break from the other direction. The working range
is narrow and the cook's job is to keep the bowl inside it — which is why
double boilers, constant whisking, and the habit of pulling the pan off the
heat every few seconds are all standard.

Beurre blanc gets at the same result by a different route. Butter as sold is
already an emulsion, but an inverted one: water droplets dispersed in fat. Whisk
cold butter into a small amount of reduced wine and shallot, and you flip it,
dispersing the fat in the water instead, stabilized by the butter's own milk
proteins and phospholipids. The reduction matters because a concentrated,
slightly syrupy water phase holds the droplets apart. Let the sauce get too hot
and the fat pools; let it get too cold and it sets.

## The Ones Nobody Calls Emulsions

Once you know the pattern, it shows up everywhere. A cacio e pepe that works is
an emulsion of cheese fat in starchy pasta water, the starch acting as the
thickener that keeps the fat droplets from finding each other; a cacio e pepe
that fails has broken into a greasy pool and a rubbery clump of protein, which
is coalescence plus overheated casein. Milk is an emulsion whose fat globules
are coated in protein, and homogenization is simply the industrial act of
forcing it through a narrow gap at pressure to shrink those globules below the
size at which they can cream. A sausage forcemeat is an emulsion of fat in a
salt-extracted protein solution, and it breaks — grainy texture, fat weeping
out of the casing — if it gets warm enough during grinding for the fat to
smear. Ganache is chocolate's version, and it seizes for recognizably the same
reasons.

The kitchen habit worth taking away is the diagnostic one. Whenever a sauce
turns greasy, split, or grainy, the useful questions are the same three: was
there enough emulsifier for the amount of interface, was the water phase thick
enough to keep droplets apart, and did the temperature leave the window where
the stabilizer still works.
