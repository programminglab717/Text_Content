# How the Maillard Reaction Builds Flavor

Put a dry steak into a pan hot enough to make you slightly nervous, and listen.
The first sound is violent, a hiss that is nothing but water flashing into
steam, and for a minute or so that is all that is happening. The surface is
being dried, not browned. Then the noise drops in pitch, the cloud above the
pan thins, and the smell changes character entirely — from wet meat to
something roasted and nutty with a sweetness underneath it. That change in
smell marks the moment the surface has finally climbed past the boiling point
of water and a different kind of chemistry has taken over.

The chemistry is named for Louis-Camille Maillard, a French physician who in
the years before the First World War was heating amino acids together with
sugars in sealed tubes and watching the mixtures turn yellow, then brown, then
nearly black. He was not thinking about dinner. He was interested in how
living tissue assembles proteins, and in what happens to sugars and proteins
inside the body, and the browning was a curiosity along the way. Food chemists
came to his work decades later, and it took until the middle of the twentieth
century for someone to lay out the reaction's branching pathways in a way
chemists could work from. The name stuck to the whole family of reactions,
which is generous, because what happens in a hot pan is not one reaction at all
but a cascade of hundreds running in parallel and feeding each other.

## What Is Actually Reacting

Two things have to be present. The first is a reducing sugar — glucose,
fructose, lactose, maltose, ribose — meaning a sugar whose carbonyl group is
free to react rather than locked up. Table sugar, sucrose, does not qualify
until heat or acid splits it into its glucose and fructose halves, which under
cooking conditions it readily does. The second is a free amino group: either a
loose amino acid, or the side chain of a lysine residue sticking out of a
protein, or the amine at the end of a peptide chain.

The opening move is a condensation. The amine attacks the sugar's carbonyl
carbon, a molecule of water leaves, and the pair become a glycosylamine, which
promptly rearranges itself into a more stable form — the Amadori rearrangement
for aldose sugars like glucose, the Heyns rearrangement for ketoses like
fructose. Up to this point nothing is brown and nothing smells like much. The
Amadori compound is the loaded spring.

What happens next depends on how hot it is, how acidic, how wet, and which
amino acids are on hand. The Amadori compound can dehydrate and fragment into
a zoo of small, reactive carbonyl compounds: dicarbonyls, furfurals,
hydroxymethylfurfural, reductones. These fragments are the real engine. They
react with each other, with more amino acids, and with whatever else is in the
food, and out of that scramble come the aroma molecules that make browned food
smell like browned food.

## Where the Smells Come From

One pathway matters more than the rest for flavor. A dicarbonyl fragment runs
into an amino acid and strips it of both its carboxyl group and its nitrogen,
leaving an aldehyde with one fewer carbon than the amino acid it came from.
This is Strecker degradation, and it is essentially a translation machine:
feed it a particular amino acid and it hands you a particular smell. Leucine
becomes a malty, chocolate-like aldehyde. Methionine becomes methional, which
smells overwhelmingly of boiled potato and turns up in everything from bread
crust to canned corn. Phenylalanine yields an aldehyde with a honeyed, floral
character that shows up in roasted cocoa.

The nitrogen stripped off in that process does not go to waste. It ends up in
aminoketones, which pair off and cyclize into pyrazines — the compounds
responsible for the smell of roasted nuts, of coffee, of toasted bread, of the
dark crust on a roast. Other rings form too: furans and furanones with caramel
and burnt-sugar notes, pyrroles, and, when sulfur-bearing cysteine is in the
mix, a family of thiols and thiophenes that the nose reads unmistakably as
meat. Ribose, abundant in muscle tissue, reacting with cysteine, produces some
of the most powerfully meaty aroma compounds known, detectable in
vanishingly small amounts. This is why a beef stock and a roast chicken and a
seared pork chop share a family resemblance in the nose despite coming from
different animals: the precursors are similar and the reaction routes are the
same.

The brown color is a separate outcome from the smell. Late in the cascade, the
reactive fragments polymerize into large, ill-defined nitrogen-containing
molecules called melanoidins. Nobody has fully characterized them, because they
are not one compound but a continuum of them. They give bread crust, coffee,
dark beer, and soy sauce their color, contribute bitterness in quantity, and
have some antioxidant activity. Color and aroma tend to track together, which
is why brown is a useful visual proxy for flavor, but they are not the same
process and can be pushed apart — a long, gentle browning can develop enormous
aroma with modest color, and an aggressive one can blacken a surface while
generating mostly bitterness.

## The Four Dials

Temperature is the obvious one. The reaction runs at any temperature, including
body temperature — the slow glycation of proteins in the bloodstream is the
same chemistry, which is one reason Maillard's original interest was medical —
but the rate climbs steeply with heat. Around 140°C it becomes fast enough to
matter on the timescale of cooking. Below that it still works, given time:
black garlic is made by holding whole heads at around 60°C for weeks, and it
comes out mahogany-colored and tasting of molasses and balsamic without anything
being added.

Water is the dial most home cooks underuse. Liquid water caps a surface at
100°C, which is why a wet steak steams instead of browning and why a pan
crowded with mushrooms produces gray mushrooms. But there is a subtler effect:
the early condensation steps release water as a product, so a very wet
environment pushes the equilibrium backward. The reaction actually runs fastest
at intermediate moisture — damp but not wet, which describes a bread crust
partway through baking or the surface of a roast after the first twenty minutes.
Bone-dry is not ideal either; the molecules need enough mobility to find each
other.

Alkalinity accelerates everything, because it is the uncharged amine that
attacks the sugar, and in acid conditions that amine is protonated and inert.
Cooks have exploited this for centuries without the explanation. Pretzels get
their mahogany skin and particular flavor from a dip in lye or a strong
solution of baking soda before baking. Ramen noodles owe their color and aroma
partly to alkaline salts in the dough. A pinch of baking soda thrown in with
onions will carry them to deep brown in a fraction of the usual time, at the
cost of turning them mushy, since alkali also dissolves pectin.

The fourth dial is the raw material. Different sugars react at different
speeds — pentoses like ribose faster than hexoses like glucose, and glucose
faster than disaccharides. Different proteins present different amino acid
profiles, which is why a browned scallop, a browned slice of bread, and a
browned piece of lamb do not smell alike even though the machinery is identical.
Milk, with its lactose and its lysine-rich casein, browns readily and sweetly;
boil a can of condensed milk long enough and it turns into dulce de leche
without any caramelization of sugar in the strict sense.

## Caramelization Is Not the Same Thing

The two are routinely conflated, and they do overlap in the mouth, but
caramelization is sugar alone breaking down under heat with no nitrogen
involved. It needs higher temperatures — sucrose does not begin in earnest
until around 160°C — and it produces its own set of compounds, more purely
sweet and burnt-sugar in character, less savory. Most cooked food is doing both
at once in different proportions. An onion cooked slowly in a pan is
caramelizing its sugars and running Maillard reactions between those sugars and
its own amino acids simultaneously, which is why "caramelized onions" is both
a misnomer and not entirely wrong.

## What It Costs

The same reaction that makes food delicious has two liabilities worth knowing.
Lysine, an essential amino acid, is the residue most likely to be consumed in
the first condensation step, and once it has reacted it is no longer available
to the body. In heavily browned, protein-poor, cereal-based foods this is a
measurable nutritional loss, though in a mixed diet it is negligible. The
second is acrylamide, which forms when the amino acid asparagine reacts with
reducing sugars at temperatures above roughly 120°C. Starchy plant foods —
potatoes, cereals, coffee beans — are high in asparagine, which is why fries,
crisps, toast, and roasted coffee carry more of it than roasted meat does. The
practical advice that follows is modest: cook to golden rather than deep brown,
don't store potatoes in the refrigerator where they convert starch to sugar,
and accept that the darkest corner of the roasting pan is a trade.

Everything a cook does at the stove to encourage browning comes down to
managing those dials. Pat the surface dry. Get the pan hot before the food goes
in and use one heavy enough to hold its heat when the cold food lands. Leave
space, so that steam can escape instead of pooling. Resist turning the food
every thirty seconds, since the surface has to climb back past 100°C each time
it is disturbed. And when the browning is done and there is a brown film welded
to the pan, put liquid in and scrape — that film is melanoidins and everything
that came with them, the most concentrated flavor in the dish, and it dissolves
readily in water or wine.
