# Gelatin, Bones, and the Logic of Stock

Take a container of good chicken stock out of the refrigerator and tip it. If
it sloshes, you made flavored water. If it holds its shape and quivers like a
custard, and you have to spoon it out in wobbling lumps that melt on contact
with a warm pan, you made stock. The distinction is not aesthetic. Almost
everything a stock does in a finished dish — the way a sauce clings to the back
of a spoon, the way a braise coats the lips, the particular richness of a
risotto that no amount of butter quite reproduces — comes from the substance
responsible for that wobble.

## What Is Actually Being Extracted

Connective tissue in an animal is largely collagen, a protein wound into triple
helices and cross-linked into fibers that hold muscle bundles together, wrap
bones, and form tendon and skin. Collagen is tough, insoluble, and useless as a
sauce ingredient in its native state. Heat it in water, though, and the helices
unwind and the chains break into shorter fragments that dissolve. Those
fragments are gelatin. At room temperature they form a loose three-dimensional
mesh that traps water, which is the wobble; at body temperature the mesh melts,
which is why gelatin-rich liquid feels like it is dissolving on the tongue
rather than simply being swallowed.

The conversion is slow and it is driven by both temperature and time. Around
seventy degrees Celsius it proceeds at a crawl; it gets appreciably faster as
you approach a simmer, and faster still under pressure, which is why a pressure
cooker can produce a stock with real body in an hour that would otherwise take
most of a day. It also explains the old division of labor among cuts and bones.
Joints, feet, wings, necks, knuckles, and skin are dense in collagen and
contribute body but relatively little flavor. Muscle meat contributes flavor —
amino acids, nucleotides, the products of long slow cooking — but comparatively
little gelatin. A stock made only from bones is thick and bland. A stock made
only from meat is savory and thin. Nearly every traditional recipe is an attempt
to get both, which is why a French chicken stock wants backs and feet together,
and why a beef stock built on knuckle bones almost always has some shin or
oxtail in it.

## Why It Must Not Boil

The instruction to keep a stockpot below a boil is repeated so often that it
has taken on the tone of superstition, but the mechanism is straightforward.
Rendered fat sits on the surface of a quiet pot in a visible layer that you can
skim. Bring the pot to a rolling boil and the turbulence tears that layer into
droplets and disperses them through the liquid, where proteins and fine
particles coat them and hold them in suspension. You have made an emulsion, and
you cannot unmake it by skimming. The stock goes cloudy, tastes greasy rather
than clean, and stays that way.

Vigorous boiling does the same thing to solids. It batters the softened bits of
meat and vegetable into fine debris that never settles. A pot held at what
French kitchens describe as a smile — the occasional lazy bubble, a barely
perceptible movement — gives convection enough to circulate the liquid without
mechanical damage. The scum that rises in the first half hour is coagulated
protein released by the meat and bones, and skimming it is not fussiness; it is
the difference between a clear liquid and one full of gray flecks.

Salt is the other thing traditionally withheld, and for a practical rather than
chemical reason. Stock exists to be reduced later, sometimes by a factor of ten.
Salt it at the start and by the time it becomes a sauce it is inedible.

## The Brown and the White

Stocks divide into two families that share a method and almost nothing else. A
white stock starts with bones that have been rinsed or blanched, covered in cold
water, and brought up slowly; the resulting liquid is pale, mild, and neutral,
and it is built to disappear into whatever it is used for. A brown stock starts
in the oven. The bones are roasted hard until they are properly dark, the
mirepoix often roasted alongside or caramelized separately, and the roasting pan
deglazed so that nothing is wasted. What roasting buys is Maillard chemistry —
hundreds of new aromatic compounds formed from the reaction of amino acids with
sugars at temperatures water alone can never reach. The stock that follows is
brown, deep, and assertive. It is not simply a stronger version of the white
one; it has a different aroma entirely.

Timing of the aromatics matters more than most recipes admit. Onion, carrot, and
celery release their volatile compounds within an hour or so, and after several
hours of simmering they have given up everything useful and begun contributing a
dull vegetal flatness. Adding them partway through a long bone stock, rather
than at the beginning, keeps their aroma fresh. The same logic applies to herbs
and peppercorns, which is why the bouquet garni goes in near the end.

## The Sauce System That Grew Out of It

The elaborate architecture of French restaurant cooking rests on the fact that a
single pot of stock can be turned into dozens of different sauces, and that the
pot can be running continuously in the background while cooks do other things.
In the early nineteenth century Antonin Carême organized the sauces of the
professional kitchen into a small set of foundational preparations from which
everything else was derived. Escoffier, a couple of generations later, revised
that scheme and built it into the brigade system, where one station's entire job
was maintaining the stocks and the base sauces that every other station drew on.

The economics are worth noticing, because they are the reason the system
survived. Bones, trim, and aromatics are the cheapest things in a kitchen. Time
over low heat is nearly free if someone is already in the building. Reduce a
gallon of brown stock to a pint and you have demi-glace; reduce it further,
until it is a dark syrup that sets firm and can be spooned out like jam, and you
have glace de viande, which will keep for weeks and turn a pan of deglazed
juices into a sauce in thirty seconds. A busy restaurant that has done this work
in advance can produce twenty different finished sauces to order without ever
starting one from scratch. Even the spent bones get a second life: cover them
again with water and make a weaker stock, the remouillage, which then becomes
the liquid for the next day's fresh batch instead of plain water.

Clarification sits at the far end of this tradition. To make consommé, a cold
stock is mixed with lean ground meat, egg white, and chopped aromatics and
brought up very slowly. The proteins coagulate into a floating raft that traps
suspended particles, and the liquid that percolates through it emerges brilliant
and transparent. Chill the result and it sets; that is aspic, which is nothing
more than stock admitting what it always was.

## Other Kitchens, Other Bases

The French model treats stock as a long investment. Japanese cooking arrives at
comparable depth by the opposite route. Ichiban dashi is made in a few minutes:
kombu steeped in water kept below a boil, the kelp removed, then dried bonito
shavings added and strained out almost immediately. The kombu supplies
glutamate, the katsuobushi supplies inosinate, and the two together produce a
savoriness far greater than either would alone — a synergy that is one of the
most reliably demonstrated effects in taste science. Dashi has no body in the
gelatin sense and does not want any. It is meant to be clean and fugitive, made
fresh daily, and it changes the character of everything it touches without
announcing itself.

Chinese kitchens keep a third model. A superior stock is built from old hen,
pork bones, and cured ham simmered for hours, then often clarified by stirring
in minced chicken breast that coagulates and sweeps the liquid clear, sometimes
more than once. It is the base for the most refined banquet cooking, and its
quality is the thing that separates a good soup from a great one in a cuisine
where the soup is frequently the point.

## What It Does on the Plate

The practical payoff is easiest to see by substitution. Cook a risotto with
water and you get rice in starch; cook it with a gelatinous stock and the grains
sit in a liquid with enough viscosity to suspend them, which is what produces
the loose, flowing texture that the dish is judged on. Braise a shoulder in
water and the sauce at the end is thin; braise it in stock and the braising
liquid needs almost no help to become a glaze. Deglaze a roasting pan with
stock, reduce it by half, swirl in a lump of cold butter, and you have a sauce
that clings — the gelatin holding the butterfat in suspension and lending the
whole thing a body that flour cannot imitate.

This is also why the coating quality of a good sauce feels different from a
thickened one. Starch-thickened liquids are viscous but inert; they stay viscous
in the mouth. Gelatin melts at just about body temperature, so a gelatin-rich
sauce goes from clinging to liquid in the act of being eaten, and leaves the
faint tackiness on the lips that cooks take as the signature of a stock made
properly.

There is a reason the stockpot sat at the back of the stove for centuries. It
was never only about flavor. It was a way of converting the parts of an animal
that no one wanted to eat into the one ingredient that made everything else
taste finished.
