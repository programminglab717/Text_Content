# The Fire Sets the Schedule

By the time anything edible goes over it, a good cooking fire is already an hour
old. The logs that were stacked in a pyramid have collapsed into a low bed of
orange and grey, the flames are gone, and the heat coming off it is silent and
steady and strong enough to be uncomfortable at arm's length. That bed is the
cooking instrument. The dramatic part — the crackling, the sparks, the flames
head-high — was only the manufacturing process, and anyone who puts meat over it
at that stage is going to serve something sooty.

Understanding why requires knowing that wood burns in two distinct phases, and
that only one of them is useful for food.

## Flame Is Not the Cooking Part

Put a log in a fire and the first thing that happens is that its water boils off.
Only once it is dry does the wood itself start decomposing under heat, breaking
down into a stream of flammable gases and tars. Those gases rise, mix with air,
and ignite above the wood surface. That is what a flame is: burning vapour,
suspended slightly away from the solid fuel that produced it.

Flames are hot, but they are bad at cooking. They are unsteady, they lick
unpredictably, and they carry unburned particulate that deposits on whatever is
above them as soot, along with the heavy acrid compounds that make food taste of
ashtray rather than woodsmoke. They also cannot be aimed.

When the volatiles are exhausted, what is left is char — close to pure carbon,
with the mineral ash it will eventually become. Char does not flame, because
there is nothing left to vaporise. It burns at its own surface, glowing, and it
throws off an enormous amount of infrared radiation with very little visible
activity. This is clean, directional, controllable heat. Every live-fire cooking
tradition in the world, from a Japanese binchōtan grill to an Argentine parrilla
to a backyard kettle, is fundamentally a method for arranging glowing carbon
underneath food.

Charcoal is simply this process performed in advance. Wood is heated in a
low-oxygen kiln until its volatiles are driven off, and what comes out is char
that lights easily and skips straight to the useful phase. Lump charcoal is
recognisable pieces of carbonised wood: it lights fast, burns hot, throws sparks,
and is gone quickly. Briquettes are ground char pressed with binders and fillers
into uniform pillows; they burn cooler but far more steadily and for much longer,
and leave a great deal more ash. Neither is better. One is for a forty-minute
grill session, the other for a six-hour cook.

## Distance Is the Dial

Because the dominant mode of heat transfer from a coal bed is radiation, the
single most powerful control you have is the gap between the coals and the food.
Radiant intensity falls off with the square of the distance. Double the height
and you get roughly a quarter of the heat. That is a startlingly steep curve,
and it means a grill grate that can be raised and lowered, or a fire that can be
built up and raked down, gives you finer control than any dial on a stove.

The traditional way to read the bed is with your hand. Hold a palm at the height
the food will sit and count until you have to pull it away. A couple of seconds
means a searing fire, good for thin steaks, squid, flatbread. Five or six seconds
is a working medium heat for chicken pieces and vegetables. Eight seconds or more
is the gentle heat you want for anything thick enough that the outside will burn
before the middle warms. It is crude, it is completely reliable, and cooks have
been doing it for a very long time.

Radiant heat has a particular signature. It hits the surface facing the fire and
almost nothing else; the interior of the food has to get warm by conduction from
that surface, which is slow. So live fire is superb at browning and at cooking
thin things fast, and it is unforgiving with anything thick. A two-inch chop over
direct coals will have a black crust and a cold centre. The fix is not a lower
fire — it is a second place to put the food.

## Two Zones, Always

The single most useful habit in fire cooking is refusing to spread the coals
evenly. Bank them to one side, or into the middle with clear ground around them,
and you have created two cooking environments in one vessel: a hot zone directly
over the bed, and an indirect zone beside it where the food is warmed by
convection and reflected heat instead of direct radiation. Almost every
difficult item is cooked by using both — sear hard over the coals to build the
crust, then move to the side to come up to temperature slowly. Put a lid on and
the indirect zone becomes a genuine oven.

The other reason to keep an empty zone is that it is where you put things when
fat starts dripping. Rendered fat falling onto hot coals ignites, and the flames
that result will blacken food in seconds and leave it tasting of scorched grease.
The instinct to spray the flare-up with water is the wrong one: water lifts ash
into the air and onto the food, and it quenches part of the bed unevenly. Move
the food instead, let the fat burn off, move it back.

Fires also do not hold still. A bed peaks and then declines over the following
hour, which means the cook is working against a slowly falling curve. Serious
practitioners solve this by keeping a separate fire burning off to one side and
shovelling fresh coals under the grill as needed — the arrangement built into an
Argentine parrilla, where a firebox at the back produces embers continuously and
a rake and shovel are as important as the tongs.

## What the Fuel Decides

Hardwoods burn longer and hotter per log than softwoods because they are denser,
and they leave a more persistent coal bed. Oak is the general-purpose choice:
steady, moderate, unobtrusive. Hickory is assertive enough to overpower delicate
food. Mesquite burns very hot and very fast and gives a strong, slightly resinous
character that suits beef and ruins fish. Fruitwoods — apple, cherry, pear — are
mild and faintly sweet. Alder is light enough for salmon.

The negative rules matter more than the positive ones. Wood must be seasoned;
green wood can be nearly half water by weight, and a fire fed on it spends its
energy boiling that off while producing thick, sour smoke. Never burn anything
treated, painted, glued, or salvaged from unknown lumber, and never burn
driftwood, whose absorbed salt produces chlorinated compounds when it combusts.
Construction offcuts and old pallets are not fuel.

## Straight Into the Embers

Once you accept that coals are the instrument, an older set of techniques opens
up: putting the food directly into them. Whole onions, beets, sweet potatoes,
leeks, aubergines, and peppers can be buried in a hot bed and left. The outer
layer chars to carbon and is peeled away, and the interior steams inside its own
skin, coming out sweet and smoky in a way no oven produces. Meat can be done this
way too — a thick steak laid directly on clean hardwood coals develops a crust
faster than any grate allows, and the air rising through the bed blows most of
the ash clear. Clean hardwood ash is mineral matter and a little on the surface
is harmless.

Scale it up and you get the great slow fire cookery. A carcass spread on an iron
cross and leaned toward a fire several feet away is cooking at a radiant
intensity low enough to take five or six hours, turned and re-angled the whole
time. American barbecue runs at a smoker temperature not far above the boiling
point of water for eight or ten hours, which is long enough for the collagen in
tough cuts to convert into gelatine — a transformation that needs time far more
than it needs heat. Anyone who has done this knows about the stall, the hours in
the middle when the internal temperature simply stops climbing because
evaporation from the surface is carrying away heat as fast as the fire delivers
it. You wait it out or you wrap the meat to stop the evaporation. There is no
third option.

## Fires With Walls

Enclose a fire and the physics changes again. A masonry oven is fired from
inside until the dome is saturated with heat — the soot deposited on the ceiling
burns clear and the brick turns pale, which is the traditional signal that it is
ready. The coals are then raked aside or out entirely, and the oven cooks on
stored heat radiating down from the dome and conducting up from the floor. Pizza
goes in first, at the top of the range, where a thin round bakes through in about
a minute. As the oven falls it takes bread, then roasts, then pots of beans left
overnight in the declining warmth. One firing, a full day of descending
temperatures, each stage matched to a different job.

Bury the same principle and you get the earth oven, which appears independently
in Polynesia, in Mexico, in the American South, and on New England beaches.
Stones are heated in a pit, food is packed in with wet leaves or seaweed or
sacking, and the whole thing is covered with earth. The stones release their heat
slowly into a sealed, saturated environment, and what comes out hours later is
not roasted but steamed — falling apart, faintly smoky, and impossible to
reproduce with dry heat.

All of it asks the same thing of the person doing it, which is attention on the
fire's terms rather than your own. You cannot pause a coal bed. It was ready
twenty minutes before you were, it will be past its best in another forty, and
the cooking has to fit inside that window.
