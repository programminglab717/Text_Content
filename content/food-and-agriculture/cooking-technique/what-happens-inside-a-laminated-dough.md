# What Happens Inside a Laminated Dough

Cut a good croissant lengthwise and look at the end grain. The interior is not
bread. It is a honeycomb of irregular cells walled off from each other by
membranes thin enough to hold light, and those membranes are arranged in curved,
roughly parallel sheets that spiral inward from the crust. Nothing that went
into the mixing bowl looked remotely like that. A slab of dough and a brick of
butter went in; a structure came out. Lamination is the set of procedures that
gets you from one to the other on purpose rather than by luck.

The ingredients are plain. A lean yeasted dough — flour, water or milk, a little
sugar, salt, yeast — and butter. In a French kitchen they are called the
détrempe and the beurrage, and the entire craft depends on never letting them
become one thing. Every failure mode in laminated pastry is some version of the
butter and the dough merging when they were supposed to stay in ranks.

## Butter That Behaves

Butter is an emulsion, not a fat. Roughly four-fifths of it is milk fat, most of
the remainder is water, and a small fraction is milk solids. The fat itself is a
mixture of many different triglycerides whose individual melting points are
spread across a wide range, which is why butter has no crisp melting temperature
the way a wax does. It has a softening range instead. Below about fifteen
degrees Celsius the crystal network is rigid and the block is brittle: roll it
and it fractures into shards that tear through the dough above them. Above
roughly twenty-two degrees, enough of the crystal network has liquefied that the
butter flows, wets the flour it is pressed against, and disappears into the
dough as shortening rather than lying on top of it as a sheet.

The useful window between those two failures is narrow, and it is the reason
laminating rooms are kept cold and the reason every recipe tells you to rest the
dough in the refrigerator between folds. Inside that window butter is plastic:
it will deform under the rolling pin and stay deformed, spreading into a
continuous film instead of shattering or melting. Bakers who do this for a
living often reach for a higher-fat butter — the European-style bars at
eighty-two to eighty-four percent fat rather than the eighty percent minimum —
partly because the extra fat makes the block firmer and more workable at a given
temperature, and partly because the reduced water content means less liquid
leaking into the dough layers. Some large bakeries buy a dedicated tourage
butter formulated to stay plastic over an unusually wide range.

Getting the butter into a usable sheet is its own small operation. The
traditional method is to take cold butter, place it between sheets of parchment,
and beat it flat with a rolling pin, working from the middle outward. The
beating warms and shears the fat crystals just enough to make the block
malleable without melting it. What you want at the end is a square of even
thickness and roughly the same firmness as the dough you are about to wrap it
in. Matched firmness matters more than any particular temperature reading. If
the butter is harder than the dough, the dough squeezes out around it and the
butter cracks. If the butter is softer, it squirts out the edges.

## The Arithmetic of Folding

With the butter locked inside the dough — enclosed like a letter in an envelope,
edges sealed — you have exactly one layer of butter and two of dough. Everything
after that is multiplication.

A single fold, sometimes called a letter fold, means rolling the packet into a
long rectangle and folding it in thirds. Each fold multiplies the butter layers
by three. One becomes three, three becomes nine, nine becomes twenty-seven. A
croissant dough typically gets three of these, ending with twenty-seven butter
films and twenty-eight dough sheets. A double or book fold — fold both ends to
meet in the middle, then close the whole thing like a book — multiplies by four
instead. Classic puff pastry gets six single turns, and the arithmetic gives the
famous figure of seven hundred and twenty-nine layers.

The multiplication is seductive and it has a ceiling. Roll a croissant dough out
to a final thickness of three or four millimetres with fifty-odd alternating
layers inside it, and the average layer is a few tens of micrometres thick —
thinner than a sheet of paper. Push the count higher and the butter films become
too thin to remain continuous. They break into islands, the dough sheets above
and below touch and weld, and the end result is not a more delicate pastry but a
denser one. Very high layer counts produce something closer to shortcrust: fat
dispersed through the dough instead of separating it.

Between each turn the dough has to rest, cold, usually for half an hour or more.
Two things are happening during that rest. The butter, which warmed and softened
under the pin, firms back into the plastic range. And the gluten network, which
was stretched in one direction by the rolling, relaxes. Gluten has elastic
memory; a dough rolled without rest will shrink back as soon as you stop
pushing, and it will shrink unevenly, dragging the layers out of register. Skip
the rests and you can feel the dough fighting you, springing back a centimetre
every time you lift the pin.

## What the Oven Does

The proof is the awkward step. Yeast wants warmth and butter wants cold, so
shaped croissants are proofed at a compromise temperature — warm enough for the
dough to inflate in a couple of hours, cool enough that the butter stays in its
sheets. Get it wrong in one direction and the butter melts out onto the tray
before baking even starts, leaving pastries that sit in a slick of fat and bake
up greasy and flat. Get it wrong the other way and the layers never separate
properly; the finished croissant has a tight, doughy core.

Then heat. Several things happen almost at once, and the order matters. Water
inside the dough sheets flashes to steam and expands enormously, pushing the
sheets apart. The butter melts and releases its own water, which adds to the
steam. Simultaneously the melted butter coats the dough surfaces on both sides
of every gap, waterproofing them and frying them from within — that fat contact
is why laminated pastry goes crisp rather than merely dry. The yeast gets one
final burst of activity as the dough warms, contributing its own gas, and then
dies somewhere around fifty-five degrees. As the temperature climbs past that,
gluten proteins coagulate and starch granules gelatinise, and the inflated
geometry locks in place. Only after the structure is set does the long tail of
baking do its real job, which is driving off moisture until the walls are dry
enough to shatter when you bite them.

## Where It Goes Wrong

Almost every lamination fault traces back to one of three things: temperature
drift, uneven pressure, or a sealed edge.

Temperature drift is the most common. A warm kitchen, a slow baker, or too many
turns without a rest, and the butter crosses out of its plastic range. You can
usually see it happen — the dough surface turns slightly greasy and the sheet
stops feeling springy. The remedy is a refrigerator and patience.

Uneven pressure produces layers of varying thickness, which bake into pastries
with one beautifully open end and one dense one. Rolling from the centre out,
turning the dough ninety degrees between passes, and using a pin long enough to
span the full width all help. So does not over-flouring: excess flour trapped
between layers absorbs moisture, stiffens into a dry seam, and weakens the wall
it sits in. Brush it off before every fold.

The sealed edge is the one beginners discover last. Layers can only separate if
their cut ends are open. Slicing croissant triangles with a dull blade crimps
the edges shut and glues the sheets together, so the pastry rises only where it
was not cut. Egg wash running down the sides does the same thing, sticking the
laminations into a fused band. Sharp knife, light hand, wash the top only.

## The Wider Family

Puff pastry is the same technique without yeast; all of its lift comes from
steam, which is why it rises so much more violently and why it needs no proof.
Inverse puff pastry turns the geometry inside out, wrapping the dough inside a
butter-and-flour block, which gives a more even rise and a distinctly crisper,
more fragile bite at the cost of being far harder to handle. Rough puff skips
the block entirely, leaving chunks of cold butter in the dough and building
layers through folding alone — irregular, uneven, perfectly good under a pie.
Danish pastry adds egg and sugar to the détrempe, which makes it richer and
softer and more forgiving. Kouign-amann folds sugar into the turns so that it
melts, runs to the bottom of the pan, and caramelises against the tin.

Strudel and phyllo sit outside the family despite the visual resemblance. They
are not folded at all; they are stretched into single translucent membranes,
brushed with fat, and stacked. The separation is achieved by hand, sheet by
sheet, rather than by multiplication — a different solution to the same problem,
and one that arrives at a brittler, drier result.

The evidence that any of it worked is on the plate afterward. A properly
laminated pastry sheds. Break one open and the table around it fills with flakes
that came loose because the walls between the cells were thinner than they had
any right to be.
