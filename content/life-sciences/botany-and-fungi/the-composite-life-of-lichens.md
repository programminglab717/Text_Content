# The Composite Life of Lichens

A gravestone old enough to have lost most of its lettering to weather will often still carry a scattering of pale green or orange-gray patches across its surface, spreading in slow, irregular rings that look almost like a stain. Those patches are lichens, and in a real sense a single one of them may be older than the inscription it has been quietly erasing. Lichens are among the slowest-growing organisms on the planet, expanding outward sometimes at a rate closer to a millimeter a year than an inch, and that very slowness is part of what makes them so useful as a kind of natural instrument, recording the passage of decades or centuries on surfaces that would otherwise carry no obvious record of time at all. What makes a lichen stranger still, though, has nothing to do with speed. A lichen is not a single organism at all, but a working partnership between at least two entirely different kinds of life, bound together so completely that biologists spent a long time debating how to even classify the result.

## Not One Organism But at Least Two

The bulk of a lichen's body, called the thallus, is built by a fungus, almost always a species that has never been found growing independently anywhere in nature outside of its lichen partnership. That fungus, called the mycobiont, supplies structure, anchors the lichen to rock, bark, or soil, and provides some protection from drying out and from ultraviolet light. What the fungus cannot do on its own is photosynthesize, and this is where the second partner comes in: embedded within the fungal tissue, in a thin layer usually just beneath the lichen's outer surface, live populations of either a green alga or a cyanobacterium, collectively called the photobiont, which carry out photosynthesis and share a portion of the sugars they produce with the surrounding fungal tissue. The fungus gets fed; the photosynthetic partner gets housing, protection, and often better access to moisture and minerals than it would find growing exposed on its own. Neither partner, by itself, resembles the lichen they form together in any obvious way, and pulling the two apart in a laboratory typically leaves both looking like unremarkable, ordinary members of their own separate groups rather than anything resembling the composite structure they built jointly.

## Three Shapes, One Partnership

Lichens are conventionally sorted into three broad growth forms, and the differences are visible without any special equipment. Crustose lichens grow as a thin crust fused tightly to whatever surface they are on, often impossible to peel off without damaging it, and they account for the pale, mottled patches so common on stone walls, gravestones, and exposed rock faces. Foliose lichens are leafier and looser, forming flattened, lobed structures that are only loosely attached to their substrate and can often be lifted at the edges with a fingernail, giving them a somewhat plant-like appearance despite having nothing to do with plants. Fruticose lichens go furthest from the crust-like original, growing as small shrubby, branching, or hanging structures, sometimes upright like a miniature bush and sometimes trailing in long strands from tree branches, as with the pale, hair-like lichens that drape from trees in humid, undisturbed forests and are often mistaken by casual observers for a kind of moss, despite being neither.

## Pioneers on Bare Rock

Lichens are frequently among the very first living things to establish themselves on newly exposed rock, whether freshly scraped clean by a retreating glacier, blasted apart by a landslide, or cooled from a volcanic flow. Bare mineral rock offers essentially no organic matter and no soil, conditions hostile to almost any plant seed that might land there, but a lichen needs almost nothing beyond a stable surface, sunlight, and periodic moisture to get started. Once established, lichens begin the slow work of breaking that rock down, partly through the simple mechanical stress of expanding and contracting with moisture and temperature, and partly through mild chemical weathering caused by acids the fungal partner secretes, which can etch and dissolve minerals in the rock surface over time. As generations of lichen grow and die on the same patch of rock, their remains contribute a thin trace of organic material to whatever mineral dust and grit has weathered loose, gradually building the first rudiments of soil in a place that previously had none. Mosses are often the next colonizers to take advantage of this newly created foothold, followed eventually by small vascular plants, in a slow successional sequence that can unfold over a very long timescale and that traces its beginning, in many cases, directly back to a lichen willing to grow somewhere nothing else could.

## Breathing the Air, Recording Its History

That same lack of true roots or a protective outer skin comparable to a plant's, which makes lichens so good at colonizing bare surfaces, also makes them unusually vulnerable to airborne pollution. A lichen absorbs water, minerals, and gases directly across its entire surface from rain and air, with no selective barrier comparable to a root system or a waxy leaf cuticle to filter out what it takes in, which means pollutants dissolved in rainwater or present in the surrounding air are absorbed just as readily as anything beneficial. Sulfur dioxide, a common industrial and vehicle emission pollutant, is particularly damaging to the photosynthetic partner inside many lichen species, and sustained exposure can kill a lichen outright or prevent new colonization entirely. For this reason, ecologists have long used the presence, absence, and species diversity of lichens growing on trees and stone surfaces as a rough, low-cost biological indicator of local air quality, a living gauge that requires no equipment beyond a trained eye and some patience: a city block thick with diverse lichen growth on its trees generally has cleaner air passing through it than one where the same tree species stand conspicuously bare.

## A Partnership With More Than Two Members

For roughly a century and a half, the standard textbook description of a lichen involved exactly two partners, a single fungal species paired with a single photosynthetic species. More recent research using genetic sequencing techniques not available to earlier generations of lichenologists has complicated that tidy picture considerably, revealing that many lichens traditionally described as a simple two-way partnership also host additional yeast species embedded within the same thallus, apparently contributing something to the lichen's overall chemistry or structure that is still only partly understood. Some lichen species also appear capable of associating with more than one photobiont species depending on local conditions, or of sharing a single fungal partner species across what had been described as several distinct lichen species, the visible differences between them apparently driven by which photosynthetic partner happened to be involved rather than by any difference in the fungus itself. None of this overturns the basic picture of lichens as a working symbiosis rather than a single organism, but it does suggest the arrangement is considerably more flexible, and more crowded, than the simple two-partner model that satisfied biologists for so long.

## Food, Dye, and a Reindeer's Winter

Beyond their role as pioneers and pollution monitors, lichens have found their way into a surprising number of practical human uses. Certain lichen species produce distinctive pigments that have been used historically as natural dyes, and others contain unique acids, usnic acid among the better known, found nowhere else in nature and studied for mild antimicrobial properties. In the far north, reindeer lichen, despite the name, is not a moss but a pale, branching, fruticose lichen that carpets huge stretches of tundra and boreal forest floor, and it becomes a critical winter food source for caribou and reindeer once snow has buried most other forage; the animals paw through snow cover to reach it, and entire migratory patterns are shaped around the location of dependable lichen ranges. A patch of lichen quietly spreading across a gravestone, then, sits at one end of a spectrum that runs all the way to entire tundra food webs organized around the same basic organism, doing much the same slow, patient work of surviving somewhere almost nothing else can.
