# Yeast, the Domesticated Fungus

A jar of sourdough starter sitting on a kitchen counter looks, at a glance, like nothing more than flour and water gone slightly bubbly. It smells faintly sour, occasionally alcoholic, and if fed on a regular schedule it will keep behaving the same way indefinitely, sometimes for years, occasionally for generations within the same family or bakery. What is actually happening inside that jar is a population of living organisms, mostly wild yeast, being kept alive and fed on purpose, in a domestic arrangement not so different in principle from keeping a hive of bees or a flock of chickens, except that the livestock in question is a single-celled fungus too small to see and was being cultivated by people for a very long time before anyone understood what it actually was.

## An Invisible Livestock

Yeasts are unicellular fungi, a fact that surprises people used to thinking of fungi primarily as mushrooms or as the mold on old bread. Most yeast species live quite happily on the surfaces of fruit, in flower nectar, in tree sap, or in soil, feeding on the sugars present in these environments without any assistance from anyone. Long before anyone knew what a yeast cell was, people had noticed that if grape juice, grain mash, or a flour-and-water paste was simply left out, it would begin to bubble, change smell, and eventually turn alcoholic or sour on its own, and had learned, through pure trial and repetition passed down over a very long stretch of human history, how to encourage this transformation reliably and use the results to make bread rise and drinks ferment. This qualifies as domestication in a very real sense, even though the earliest brewers and bakers had no concept of yeast as a discrete living organism separate from the dough or liquid it was transforming. They were managing a population of a species they could not see, selecting without knowing it for the specific strains that performed most reliably in their particular kitchen, brewery, or bakery, simply by continuing to reuse whatever starter or batch had worked well the last time.

## What a Yeast Cell Actually Does

A single yeast cell survives by breaking down sugar for energy, and it can do this in two different modes depending on the conditions available to it. When oxygen is present, yeast, like most other organisms, prefers aerobic respiration, a highly efficient process that extracts a large amount of energy from each sugar molecule and produces mostly carbon dioxide and water as byproducts. When oxygen is scarce or absent, yeast switches to fermentation, a far less efficient process that extracts much less energy from the same sugar but allows the cell to keep functioning without any oxygen at all, converting sugar instead into ethanol and carbon dioxide. This second pathway is, from a human perspective, the entire reason yeast has mattered so much to civilization: the carbon dioxide produced during fermentation is what makes bread dough rise, trapped as bubbles within the dough's gluten structure and later fixed in place by baking, while the ethanol produced by the same basic process is the alcohol in beer, wine, and every other fermented drink built around yeast. A single organism, switching between two metabolic modes depending mostly on how much air it can get, turns out to be responsible for two of the most fundamental transformations in the entire history of human food and drink. Yeast reproduces primarily by budding, a small new cell growing outward from the surface of an existing one and eventually pinching off as an independent cell, visible under even a fairly basic microscope as a distinctive chain or cluster of unequal spheres.

## Pasteur and the Argument Over What Fermentation Really Was

For a long stretch of the nineteenth century, chemists were divided on a question that seems almost quaint now: was fermentation a purely chemical process, a kind of spontaneous decomposition requiring no living organism at all, or was it caused by something alive? Prominent chemists of the era, working from a purely chemical framework, argued strongly for the former. Louis Pasteur, investigating problems with fermentation on behalf of the French wine and beer industries, built a careful body of experimental evidence pointing the other way, demonstrating that fermentation reliably occurred wherever living yeast cells were present and multiplying, and that carefully excluding living organisms from a sugar solution prevented fermentation from happening at all, no matter how long the solution sat. His work helped settle the argument decisively in favor of a biological explanation and fed directly into his broader case against the idea of spontaneous generation, the belief that living organisms could arise on their own from non-living matter given the right conditions. Fermentation, it turned out, was not a chemical process happening to sugar; it was something an organism was actively doing to it, and once it became clear that different strains and species of microorganism could ferment the same sugar into quite different results, brewers and vintners had a real scientific reason to care about exactly which organism was doing the fermenting in their particular vats.

## Breeding Yeast Without Knowing It

That realization set off a period of practical innovation in how yeast was handled commercially. A Danish scientist working at the Carlsberg brewing company in the late nineteenth century developed reliable techniques for isolating a single yeast cell and growing it into a pure culture free of the wild, mixed populations of yeast and bacteria that had previously done the fermenting work in most breweries, a mix that produced results that could vary noticeably from one batch to the next depending on exactly which organisms happened to dominate that particular fermentation. Pure, single-strain cultures gave brewers something they had never had before: consistency, the ability to reproduce a specific flavor profile and fermentation behavior batch after batch by using a known, isolated strain rather than whatever combination of wild organisms happened to be present. The technique spread quickly through the brewing industry and, more slowly, into commercial baking, where specific strains of baker's yeast eventually became available as a standardized product rather than something a baker needed to maintain personally as a living starter culture passed down from batch to batch, though traditional sourdough baking, dependent on exactly that kind of maintained wild culture, never disappeared and has seen a considerable revival among people who prefer the flavor and unpredictability of a less standardized, locally adapted population of wild yeast and bacteria working together.

## A Single-Celled Fungus at the Center of Modern Biology

Yeast's importance did not stop at the bakery or the brewery. The most common laboratory species, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, turned out to be an unusually convenient organism for studying the basic mechanics of cell biology: it is a simple single-celled eukaryote, meaning its cells share the same fundamental architecture, a true nucleus and similar internal organelles, as far more complex organisms including humans, but it reproduces quickly, is cheap and easy to grow in enormous quantities, and can be genetically manipulated with comparative ease. Generations of researchers studying how cells manage their division cycle, repair damaged DNA, or regulate gene activity have used yeast as a stand-in for more complex organisms whose cells are far harder to study directly, and a considerable share of what is now understood about the basic machinery running inside every one of your own cells was first worked out, at least in a preliminary form, inside a yeast cell in a laboratory flask.

## A Fungus That Never Stopped Working

It is an unusual kind of legacy for an organism whose original relationship with humanity was strictly culinary. Cultivated first for bread and beer with no notion of what it actually was, yeast ended up becoming one of the most thoroughly studied living things on the planet, valuable in the laboratory for precisely the same qualities, fast growth, simple needs, and reliable behavior in culture, that once made it valuable to a baker who never thought of it as an organism at all. The wild ancestors of the strains living in a laboratory freezer or a commercial yeast packet are still out there doing what they have always done, quietly fermenting the sugars in a piece of overripe fruit or a smear of tree sap, indifferent to whether anyone is collecting them. Every loaf of leavened bread, every glass of beer or wine, and a fair portion of modern molecular biology all trace back to the same basic, ancient trick: a single-celled fungus finding a way to make a living from sugar when the air runs short, long before anyone thought to ask what was making the bubbles.
