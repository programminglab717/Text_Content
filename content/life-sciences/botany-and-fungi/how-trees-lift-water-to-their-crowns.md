# How Trees Lift Water to Their Crowns

A coast redwood can stand over ninety meters tall, and every one of those meters must be supplied with water pulled from soil that is often dry at the surface within hours of a rain. No animal circulatory system offers a good analogy for how this happens. A heart is a muscular pump that generates pressure from below and pushes blood outward through the body. A tree has no equivalent organ anywhere in its trunk. Root pressure, the closest thing a tree has to a pump, can raise water only a few meters under good conditions, nowhere near enough to reach a crown lost in fog two hundred feet above the forest floor. And yet the leaves at the top of that redwood stay hydrated through a summer afternoon, running photosynthesis at full tilt, seemingly unconcerned with the plumbing problem beneath them. The solution botanists eventually settled on is elegant, slightly counterintuitive, and depends on pulling water rather than pushing it.

## The Trouble With Pumping From Below

Early physiologists assumed water rose through a tree because roots forced it up, the way pressure in a home's plumbing forces water out of a faucet. Root pressure is real — it is what causes water droplets to bead at the tips of grass blades on a humid morning, a phenomenon called guttation — but it is a weak and inconsistent force. It depends on the osmotic pull of dissolved minerals inside root cells drawing water in from the soil, and that pull generates only a modest amount of pressure. Measured directly, root pressure in most trees could lift water a story or two at best. It also tends to fade during the day, exactly when a tree needs water most, because the roots are competing with a much stronger process happening far above them.

That stronger process is evaporation. Every leaf is covered in a waxy cuticle that resists water loss, but the leaf still needs to open pores called stomata to take in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis. Water vapor escapes through those same pores whenever they are open, a process called transpiration, and on a warm dry day a single large tree can lose an amount of water that would fill a good-sized barrel before evening. Pumping from below could never keep pace with losses on that scale. The tree needed a different mechanism, one that treats the water loss itself as the engine rather than the problem.

## Pulling From the Top

The explanation that finally fit the evidence is called the cohesion-tension theory, and it inverts the intuitive picture entirely. Instead of being pushed from the roots, water is pulled from the top, and the pulling force is transpiration itself. As water evaporates from the moist cell walls inside a leaf, it creates a slight deficit — a drop in water potential, in the language of plant physiologists — at the evaporating surface. Water is drawn out of the nearest xylem conduit to replace what left, which in turn creates a deficit one step further back in that conduit, which pulls on the water behind it, and so on in an unbroken chain leading all the way down through the trunk and into the finest root hairs touching the soil.

This works because water molecules have an unusual property: they cling to each other tenaciously through hydrogen bonding, a quality called cohesion, and they also stick to the cellulose walls of the conduits they travel through, a related property called adhesion. Together these forces let a column of water behave almost like a solid cable under tension rather than a liquid being pushed through a pipe. Pull on one end and, as long as the column stays intact, the whole thread of water moves in response, from the leaf's evaporating surface down to the root tip. The energy driving the entire system comes from the sun, indirectly, by way of the heat that makes water evaporate in the first place. The tree itself spends almost no metabolic energy moving water at all; it simply provides the plumbing and lets physics do the lifting.

## Why the Water Column Doesn't Snap

The idea that a column of water under tension could stretch continuously from root to crown without breaking troubled plant physiologists for decades, and reasonably so. Pull hard enough on a column of most liquids and it will cavitate, meaning dissolved gases come out of solution and form a bubble that breaks the continuous thread, the same way a stretched rubber band eventually snaps. Water under the tensions found in a tall tree's xylem is, by ordinary standards, in a metastable state, held together mostly by the cohesive strength of the hydrogen bonds between molecules and by the narrowness of the conduits it travels through.

The conduits themselves are part of the solution. Xylem vessels, and the tracheid cells that serve the same function in conifers, are extremely narrow, often less than the width of a human hair, and narrow tubes resist the formation of bubbles far better than wide ones. The vessel walls are also reinforced with lignin, allowing them to withstand the inward-pulling tension without collapsing, rather like a drinking straw stiff enough not to pinch shut when you suck hard on it. Even so, breaks do happen. Botanists can hear them: sensitive acoustic equipment clamped to a stressed tree trunk picks up a faint, continuous clicking, each click the sound of a water column failing somewhere inside the wood.

## When the System Fails

A snapped water column creates an embolism, an air-filled gap in a conduit that can no longer carry water, functionally similar to an air bubble in a hospital IV line. Trees have several ways of coping with this damage. Many species produce far more xylem conduits than they strictly need in an average year, so that losing some to embolism during a drought still leaves enough intact plumbing to survive. Some conifers and hardwoods can refill embolized conduits under the right conditions, using root pressure or osmotic tricks to push water back into a gas-filled vessel and force the bubble back into solution, though this ability is limited and not universal. In prolonged, severe drought, though, the failures cascade. As more conduits embolize, the remaining functional ones must carry a larger share of the flow, which increases the tension on them and makes them more likely to fail too. This runaway process, sometimes called hydraulic failure, is now understood to be one of the main ways trees actually die during extreme drought, quite apart from starvation or heat damage. A tree that looks green in June can already be carrying a large number of air-filled vessels by August, the damage only becoming visible once whole branches fail to leaf out the following spring.

## Wood as Living Plumbing

None of this would work without the specific architecture of wood. In a conifer, water travels through tracheids, elongated dead cells connected to their neighbors by pit membranes that let water pass sideways while filtering out air bubbles that might otherwise spread from one damaged cell to the next. In most flowering trees, water instead moves mainly through vessels, wider tubes formed by stacks of cells that dissolve the walls between them at maturity, creating a continuous pipe that can run for a considerable length and that is generally more efficient than a tracheid, at some cost in structural safety, since a single embolism can compromise a much longer run of plumbing at once. Every one of these conduits, whether tracheid or vessel, is already dead by the time it does its job; the living cells built it, then died, leaving behind a lignified skeleton perfectly shaped for carrying tension. The tree's true achievement is not muscular effort but architecture: a network of microscopic conduits, precisely sized, chemically reinforced, and renewed every growing season, that lets the sun's heat do the work of lifting water higher than any biological pump ever could.

Foresters and physiologists still study the edges of this system, because it is not without weaknesses that matter in a warming climate. Species differ enormously in how much tension their xylem can tolerate before conduits start failing, and that difference turns out to predict, better than almost any other single trait, which trees in a mixed forest survive a severe drought and which ones do not. A tree adapted to a reliably wet climate often has wider, more efficient conduits optimized for fast water transport under ordinary conditions, which leaves it with less of a safety margin when the soil dries out further than it ever has in that tree's lifetime. A tree from a naturally drier habitat tends to run narrower, more conservative plumbing, sacrificing some efficiency in exchange for resistance to exactly the kind of catastrophic, cascading embolism that kills less cautious competitors. Neither strategy is universally better; each is a bet about what kind of water stress the tree is most likely to face over a lifetime that, for some species, can run to centuries.
