# The Pecking Order: Dominance and Social Rank

A Norwegian schoolboy named Thorleif Schjelderup-Ebbe spent his summers watching his family's chickens and noticed something that everybody who has kept hens has half-seen without registering it. The pecks were not random. Hen A pecked hen B and B never pecked back. B pecked C. C pecked nobody except D. Written out as a table, the flock resolved into a near-perfect line, and once he had the line he could predict almost every encounter in the yard. He kept at the problem into adulthood and published it in the 1920s, giving the phenomenon the German name Hackordnung, which came into English as the pecking order and then, unfortunately, into everyday speech as a metaphor for office politics.

The part of his finding that matters most is the part the metaphor loses. A settled pecking order is not a yard full of fighting. It is a yard in which fighting has largely stopped. The subordinate hen steps aside before the dominant one arrives; the dominant one rarely has to do anything. What looks like a structure for distributing aggression is better understood as a structure for avoiding it.

## What Rank Actually Buys

Dominance is not a trait an animal carries around, like body mass. It is a description of a relationship: the statement that in contested encounters between these two individuals, one of them consistently yields. Change the partner and the relationship can invert. Change the context — move from a feeding site to a sleeping site, or from a dry season to a wet one — and it sometimes inverts too.

What rank delivers is priority of access to whatever is scarce and worth competing for. Food at a concentrated source. Shade. Water in a drought. Mates. A safe position in the centre of a herd rather than the exposed edge. The crucial qualifier is scarcity: in a field with more grass than anyone can eat, a hierarchy exists but is functionally invisible, because there is nothing to defer over. Pour that same herd's ration into one trough and the structure appears within minutes, fully formed.

This is why hierarchies are so common in group-living species and so variable in how much they matter. A hierarchy is a bookkeeping device. Each animal maintains, in effect, a ledger of who it has lost to, and consults that ledger instead of re-testing the question. Fights are expensive — energetically, in injury risk, in time not spent feeding — and a population of animals that fought every time a resource came into view would do worse than one that fought a few times and then remembered the answer.

## How the Order Gets Established

The first encounters are the ones that do the work, and their outcomes depend on the things you would expect: size, condition, weaponry, experience, and how much each contestant wants the thing in question. Many species have evolved elaborately ritualized contests that let both parties assess each other without a serious fight — roaring matches in red deer stags, parallel walks, lateral displays that show off depth of body, horn-locking that tests strength without aiming for damage. These are not politeness. They are cheap ways to gather the information that a fight would otherwise have to provide at a much higher price.

Once outcomes start accumulating, they become self-reinforcing. An animal that has just won is more likely to win its next encounter, and one that has just lost more likely to lose again — winner and loser effects that have been demonstrated in animals from crickets to fish to mice, and that operate partly through circulating hormones and partly through something closer to a change in willingness to escalate. A run of losses produces an animal that concedes early, which produces more losses. Rank, in this sense, is partly a prophecy that fulfills itself.

But plenty of hierarchies are not settled by fighting ability at all. In spotted hyenas, which live in large female-dominated clans, a cub acquires a rank immediately below its mother's, not by being formidable but by being backed: the mother and her allies intervene on the youngster's behalf, and other clan members learn to defer to an animal that comes with support attached. The result is a stable matrilineal structure in which a small daughter of a high-ranking female outranks adult females who could easily defeat her alone. Similar inherited ranks run through many macaque and baboon matrilines. In other species, rank is simply a queue: acquired by waiting, with position determined by age or length of residence, so that an individual's standing rises year by year as those above it die or depart.

## The Alpha Problem

The word alpha did enormous damage to public understanding of this subject, and it did so through an accident of study design. Early descriptions of wolf packs were drawn largely from captive groups — unrelated adults confined together, competing over food they had not hunted, in an enclosure nobody could leave. Those animals fought, and formed the tense, contested, constantly challenged hierarchy that became the popular image of wolf society.

Wild packs turned out to look almost nothing like this. A typical pack is a family: a breeding pair and their offspring of the last few years. The animals at the top are not victors of a tournament but parents, and what looks like dominance is mostly the ordinary asymmetry between adults and their own young. L. David Mech, the researcher whose early book did more than anything else to popularize the alpha framing, later argued publicly and repeatedly against his own terminology, pointing out that calling a wolf father an alpha is roughly as informative as calling a human father one.

The broader lesson generalizes past wolves. Hierarchies observed in captivity, in crowded conditions, with resources concentrated and dispersal impossible, are systematically harsher than the same species' structures in the wild. A large part of what makes natural hierarchies tolerable is the option of walking away, and enclosures remove it.

## Steep, Shallow, and Contested

Species differ enormously in how much rank determines, and the difference is measurable. Rhesus macaques run a steep, intolerant hierarchy in which high-ranking individuals monopolize resources and subordinates show constant appeasement. Other macaque species, closely related and similar in ecology, maintain hierarchies so shallow that low-ranking animals feed alongside dominants and reconcile after conflicts with high frequency. Chimpanzee males compete for a top position that confers real reproductive advantage; bonobo groups, sharing a common ancestor with chimpanzees not long ago, are structured around female coalitions that constrain male aggression.

Coalitions are the great complicating factor in any account of rank. Where individuals can form alliances, physical superiority stops being decisive, because two or three cooperating subordinates can defeat a stronger animal. Primatologists have documented male chimpanzees rising to and holding top rank through the support of partners rather than through fighting ability, and losing it when the alliance dissolves rather than when they lose strength. This makes rank in such species partly a social skill: knowing whom to court, whom to groom, when to support a rising individual and when to abandon a falling one.

## The Health Economics of Rank

It is tempting to assume that being dominant is straightforwardly good for you, and often it is. But the physiological accounting is more interesting than that. Robert Sapolsky's long-running work on olive baboons found that in stable troops, subordinate males carried the marks of chronic stress — elevated resting levels of stress hormones, and a sluggish response when a genuine emergency arrived. Harassment from above, with little control and nowhere to go, is a reasonable recipe for that.

Yet when a troop's hierarchy destabilized, the pattern shifted, and it was the high-ranking animals, defending contested positions, whose stress profiles deteriorated. In species where the dominant individual does the expensive work — a meerkat matriarch enforcing reproductive suppression, a wild dog breeder, a stag holding a harem through the rut without eating — the cost of the top position can be severe enough to shorten a life. Whether rank is good for an animal's health depends on what that particular society requires of the animal that holds it, and on whether the hierarchy is currently settled or in play.

## What the Metaphor Gets Wrong

The persistent popular version of all this treats dominance as a kind of personality: some individuals are alphas, others are not, and the trait travels with them. Almost nothing in the animal literature supports that reading. Rank is relational, contextual, frequently inherited from a mother rather than earned, often dependent on allies, and reversible. A hen at the top of one flock introduced into another starts again from a position determined by that flock's existing relationships.

Schjelderup-Ebbe's chickens were never really a model of competition. They were a demonstration that a small group of animals with imperfect memories and no language can converge on a shared representation of who yields to whom, and then spend most of their time not testing it.
