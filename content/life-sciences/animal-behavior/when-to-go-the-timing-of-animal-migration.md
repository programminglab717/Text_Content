# When to Go: The Timing of Animal Migration

An osprey leaves a northern lake in the last days of August while the fishing is still excellent. Nothing has gone wrong. The water is warm, the perch are shallow and abundant, the weather is mild, and the bird could plausibly stay another six weeks without suffering for it. It goes anyway, and it goes at roughly the same point in the season that ospreys from that lake have been going for as long as anyone has kept records.

That is the central oddity of migration timing. Migration is a response to conditions that have not happened yet. By the time a northern lake freezes, an osprey that waited for the ice would be a thousand kilometres short of anywhere it could feed, burning reserves it never built. The same logic runs in reverse in spring: arrive too late at the breeding grounds and the best territories are taken and the food peak has passed; arrive too early and you starve in a snowstorm. The margin for error is narrow in both directions, and the animal has to commit to a schedule long before the evidence for that schedule is available.

## Why Weather Makes a Poor Calendar

The obvious cue — how things feel right now — turns out to be almost useless for anything but the final decision. Temperature, rainfall, and food abundance are exactly the variables an animal cares about, but they are noisy. A warm week in October says nothing reliable about November. A cold snap in April is not evidence that spring has been cancelled. An animal that read current conditions as a forecast would be pulled off schedule several times a year by weather that reverted to normal within days.

Day length has the opposite properties. It carries no information about food or temperature directly, but it is perfectly regular, identical from year to year at a given latitude, and unaffected by whether the season happens to be early or late. It is a clock signal rather than a measurement, and that is precisely what makes it valuable. An animal that can read photoperiod has access to a noiseless statement of where the year is, which it can then use to start a preparation sequence that takes weeks to complete.

## Reading Day Length, and What Reading It Sets Off

Birds do not measure day length primarily with their eyes. Light penetrates skull and brain tissue well enough that photoreceptive cells deep in the hypothalamus respond directly to it, which is why blinded birds can still track the seasons. What matters is not simply how many hours of light accumulate but whether light falls on a particular window of the daily cycle — a photoinducible phase set by the circadian system. Light arriving during that window is read as a long day; the same total light distributed differently is not. The measurement is therefore a coincidence detection between an internal rhythm and the external light cycle, which is an elegant solution to the problem of counting hours without a clock face.

Once the threshold is crossed, the consequences are not subtle. The hypothalamus increases release of the hormone that drives the pituitary, the pituitary drives the gonads, and the animal's reproductive system, which has been regressed to a fraction of its breeding size, begins to rebuild. Alongside that, a migrant starts eating far more than it needs, sometimes doubling its body mass with subcutaneous and visceral fat over a matter of weeks. Flight muscles thicken. In long-distance shorebirds the digestive organs actually shrink before departure, discarded as dead weight for an animal about to fly for days without eating, and then regrown at the far end.

The most telling sign is behavioural. Caged migratory birds develop a night-time restlessness in the weeks when their free-living relatives are on the move, hopping and wing-whirring in the dark in a way they never do outside the migratory window. German ornithologists named it Zugunruhe, migratory restlessness, and its onset, intensity and duration track the real migration of the species closely enough that it can be used as a laboratory proxy for a journey the bird is not allowed to take.

## The Clock That Keeps Running in the Dark

Photoperiod cannot be the whole story, because plenty of migrants spend their non-breeding months near the equator, where the difference between the longest and shortest day is measured in minutes. A bird wintering in central Africa has essentially no photoperiodic signal to tell it that March has arrived in Sweden.

Keep such a bird in constant conditions — unchanging light schedule, constant temperature, food always available — and it does not drift into an undifferentiated state. It continues to moult, fatten, and become restless in a recurring cycle that persists for years. The cycle's period is close to a year but not exactly a year; in captive birds it typically runs somewhat short, so the animal slowly drifts out of phase with the real calendar. This is a genuine endogenous circannual rhythm, and it is the deep timer underneath the seasonal machinery. Photoperiod, where it is available, does not create the schedule so much as correct it, nudging the internal year back into alignment with the actual one the way a radio signal disciplines a wristwatch that keeps decent but imperfect time on its own.

Species differ in how much they lean on each system. Long-distance migrants with tightly constrained schedules tend to be strongly controlled by the internal clock and relatively deaf to conditions. Short-distance migrants, whose journeys are cheaper and more easily aborted, are far more responsive to weather and food, and will delay or advance departure substantially from year to year.

## The Last Decision Before Leaving

None of this determines the actual night of departure, which is settled by something much more immediate. A bird in migratory condition, fat and restless, still waits for weather. The specific trigger is usually wind: a tailwind component can change the energetic cost of a leg of the journey by a large margin, and migrants across many taxa show a marked preference for departing behind a passing front, when the wind has swung into a favourable quarter and the air is clearing. Radar operators in autumn watch the sky fill with echoes on the first night after a cold front passes, then empty out again.

For birds that cross oceans or deserts in single non-stop efforts, the sensitivity to this final cue is extreme, because there is no option to set down partway and reassess. Godwits leaving the coast of Alaska for the southwest Pacific pick departure windows associated with weather systems that will give them assistance for the first and most expensive portion of the crossing. Getting the seasonal schedule right gets the bird to the launch point in condition; getting the departure night right determines whether the crossing is survivable.

## Other Species, Other Clocks

Photoperiodic control of an annual timer is a vertebrate solution, and not the only one. Monarch butterflies solve a harder version of the problem, since the individuals that fly south to Mexico are several generations removed from those that flew north, and no monarch makes the round trip. The late-summer generation develops differently from its parents — reproductively arrested, long-lived, oriented — in response to shortening days and cooling nights. The return leg depends on a second trick: the prolonged cold of the overwintering site appears to reset the animals' response to the sun compass, flipping the direction they fly when they warm up and move north again in spring.

Large mammal migrations often run on cues far closer to the ground. Wildebeest tracking the rains across East African grassland are reacting to where the grass is now, not to an internal calendar, and their routes and timing vary accordingly from year to year. Caribou calving is pinned to a narrow window when new plant growth is at its most nutritious, a target that moves with the season's progress. Amphibians heading for breeding ponds often need nothing more than the first warm, wet night after the soil thaws, and will sit tight through a cold spring for weeks.

## When the Cues Come Apart

The weakness of a system built on a fixed astronomical signal shows up when the biological year stops tracking the astronomical one. Spring in the temperate north has been advancing: buds break earlier, insect larvae peak earlier, but the equinox does not move. A long-distance migrant taking its departure orders from an internal circannual clock calibrated at a wintering site thousands of kilometres away has no way to know that the caterpillar peak it is flying toward has shifted forward by a fortnight.

The consequence, documented most clearly in European insectivorous birds, is a mismatch between the arrival of hungry nestlings and the brief flush of food that the breeding season was tuned to exploit. Populations in the areas where the food peak has advanced the most have declined the most, and the birds able to compensate — by laying earlier, or by being short-distance migrants capable of reading local spring conditions directly — have fared better. The precision that makes migration work is bought by ignoring short-term information, and the cost of that trade only becomes visible when the long-term signal it relies on turns out to be moving.
