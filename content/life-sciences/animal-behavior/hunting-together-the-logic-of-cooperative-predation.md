# Hunting Together: The Logic of Cooperative Predation

A pod of orcas working the edge of an ice floe in Antarctic waters will sometimes swim in tight formation directly at the floe, generating a wave that breaks over the ice and washes a resting seal into the water, where other members of the pod are already waiting. No single orca could produce a wave large enough on its own, and no single orca hunting alone could reliably separate a seal from solid ice in the first place. The behavior only works, and apparently only occurs, as a coordinated group effort, refined and passed down within particular family groups that specialize in it while other orca populations, hunting other prey in other waters, never do anything like it at all.

Cooperative hunting shows up across a strikingly wide range of unrelated animals: wolves, lions, chimpanzees, several dolphin species, some birds of prey, and even a few fish. That breadth is itself informative. When a behavior this specific and this costly to coordinate evolves independently in lineages as different as canids, cetaceans, and primates, it suggests cooperative hunting solves a real and recurring problem well enough, under the right circumstances, to be worth the difficulty of getting multiple individuals to act in concert.

## Why Share the Kill at All

From the point of view of natural selection, cooperative hunting requires an explanation that solitary hunting does not. An animal that hunts alone keeps everything it catches. An animal that hunts cooperatively has to eventually divide the carcass, often unevenly, among however many individuals took part, and unless something about hunting together also increases the total amount of food obtained, or the reliability of getting any food at all, cooperation should lose out to solitary hunting on simple arithmetic grounds.

The clearest cases where cooperation pays involve prey that is either too large, too fast, or too well defended for a lone hunter to tackle safely or successfully. A single wolf attacking an adult moose risks serious injury for very poor odds of success; a coordinated pack, with some members harassing from the front and others attacking the flanks, converts a nearly impossible task into a difficult but frequently successful one. The math only favors cooperation, though, up to a point. Studies of group-hunting carnivores have generally found that success rates per hunt rise with group size only up to some intermediate number of participants, beyond which additional hunters add little further advantage and simply dilute the food available to each one, which likely explains why hunting group sizes in many cooperative predators cluster within a fairly narrow range rather than growing without limit.

## Division of Labor

The more sophisticated cooperative hunters do not simply pile onto prey in a mob; they occupy distinguishable roles. Lionesses hunting in open savanna have been described taking up consistent positions relative to one another, some moving to flank and block potential escape routes while others approach more directly to flush prey toward the waiting ambush, with individual lionesses showing some tendency to specialize in one role or another across repeated hunts rather than choosing randomly each time. Chimpanzees hunting colobus monkeys in some forest populations show an even more explicit division: certain individuals drive the monkeys through the canopy, others move ahead to block likely escape routes along particular branches, and one or more wait in ambush at a position the prey is likely to be forced toward, a level of anticipation of where another animal's actions will push a third party that edges toward the kind of reasoning about others' behavior more commonly associated with primate social cognition generally.

## Communication Without Language

None of this requires anything like human planning or conversation, but it does require some means of staying coordinated in real time, and different species rely on different channels to manage it. Wolves use a mix of visual attention to pack mates, body posture, and vocalization to stay organized during a chase. Dolphins and orcas rely heavily on underwater vocal signals, and different orca populations, described as distinct ecotypes, have developed entirely different hunting cultures passed down within family lines: some specialize in fish, some in marine mammals, some in the seal-washing behavior described earlier, and populations that have specialized differently for generations show little tendency to hunt like their distant, differently specialized relatives even when the same prey is locally available to both, evidence that the specific techniques are learned traditions rather than fixed instinct.

## When Cooperation Breaks Down

Cooperative hunting is not immune to the basic problem that afflicts cooperation generally: the temptation to let others do the work while still claiming a share of the payoff. Some group hunts show evidence of individuals hanging back from the riskiest positions, such as a direct frontal approach to dangerous prey, while still moving in promptly once prey is down to claim access to the carcass. Groups also lose kills outright to kleptoparasites, other predators or scavengers strong or numerous enough to displace the original hunters, a persistent risk for cooperative hunters like African wild dogs and cheetahs, which invest heavily in the chase itself but are comparatively poorly equipped to defend a kill afterward against larger, more aggressive competitors drawn in by the commotion.

## Convergent Solutions

What ties these disparate cases together is not shared ancestry but shared circumstance: prey worth more than one animal can catch or subdue alone, a social structure stable enough that the same individuals hunt together repeatedly and can therefore develop workable coordination, and often, extended time for young or naive individuals to watch and learn technique from more experienced group members before hunting seriously themselves. Where those conditions line up, cooperative hunting appears to be one of evolution's more reliable answers, showing up again and again in lineages that share almost nothing else in common except the problem it solves.

Even outside the more famous mammalian examples, the pattern repeats in unexpected corners of the animal kingdom. Some groupers on coral reefs have been observed hunting alongside moray eels, using head shakes and other signals to indicate a hiding place holding prey that the grouper cannot reach into a crevice to catch but the eel can flush out, a cross-species partnership between two animals with no social bond beyond the immediate benefit of splitting a meal neither could reliably get alone. Harris's hawks, unusual among birds of prey for hunting in coordinated groups, will similarly take turns flushing rabbits from cover toward waiting packmates, rotating roles across a single hunt in a way that looks, for a bird, remarkably like a relay.
