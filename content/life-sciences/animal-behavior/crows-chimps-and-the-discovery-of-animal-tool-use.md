# Crows, Chimps, and the Discovery of Animal Tool Use

In 1960, at a research camp on the shore of Lake Tanganyika, a young researcher named Jane Goodall watched a wild chimpanzee approach a termite mound, select a stem, strip the leaves from it, and insert it carefully into a tunnel in the mound. When the chimpanzee withdrew the stem, termites were clinging to it, and the chimpanzee ate them off the stem before repeating the process. Goodall had been sent to Gombe by the paleoanthropologist Louis Leakey specifically to look for behavioral clues about early human evolution, on the theory that a close living relative of humans might retain hints about the behavior of a shared ancestor, but even Leakey seems not to have anticipated quite how directly the observation would land on one of the traits then used to define humanity itself.

## A Line That Wasn't Supposed to Be Crossed

For much of the twentieth century, tool use was treated by many scientists and by popular culture alike as one of the clearer dividing lines separating humans from every other animal, on the reasoning that toolmaking required a kind of forethought, an understanding of cause and effect, and a capacity to modify the environment deliberately that other species were assumed to lack. Textbook definitions of tool use were sometimes written narrowly enough, whether deliberately or not, to exclude almost anything an animal might plausibly be observed doing, so that any candidate example could be waved away as instinct, accident, or not really a tool in the intended sense.

Goodall's termite-fishing chimpanzees were hard to dismiss so easily. The chimpanzees were not simply picking up a convenient stick; they were selecting particular stems, stripping them to a usable shape, and in some cases carrying a chosen tool some distance to a termite mound before using it, behavior that involved a real element of preparation and anticipated future use rather than a purely reflexive response to an immediate stimulus. News of the observation, along with Leakey's now well-known reaction that scientists would have to redefine tool use, redefine what it meant to be human, or else accept chimpanzees within a category previously reserved for humans alone, forced a genuine reckoning in a field that had not expected to need one.

## Crows Join the List

If Gombe reshaped assumptions about primates, a very different animal eventually forced an even more uncomfortable question: whether sophisticated tool use required a large-brained mammal at all. New Caledonian crows, native to a small Pacific island chain, turned out in field studies to manufacture tools with a level of standardization that had no obvious precedent outside primates. Wild individuals fashion hooked implements from twigs, bending a small hook into one end to help drag insect larvae out of holes in wood, and they cut and trim strips from the barbed edges of pandanus leaves into tapered tools, sometimes following a recognizable, population-specific template, so that tools collected from different areas of the crows' range show subtly different characteristic shapes, consistent with locally learned traditions rather than a single fixed instinct expressed identically everywhere.

Captive studies pushed the question further into the territory of causal reasoning rather than mere manufacture. One widely reported New Caledonian crow, given the name Betty during laboratory testing, was presented with a task requiring a hooked tool to retrieve a small bucket of food from a vertical tube, and when the hooked tool she had been using was accidentally unavailable, she bent a straight piece of wire into a hook shape herself and used it successfully, a spontaneous solution she had not been trained to produce and had never been observed performing before, in a species with no evolutionary history of manipulating wire at all.

## Elephants, Otters, Octopuses

Once researchers began looking with the assumption that tool use might be more widespread than previously believed, plausible examples accumulated steadily across the animal kingdom rather than remaining confined to a small, exceptional handful of species. Sea otters use rocks, sometimes carried for extended periods tucked into a loose fold of skin, as anvils to crack open shellfish while floating on their backs. Elephants have been observed modifying branches, stripping them to a more convenient length for swatting flies or scratching hard-to-reach parts of their own bodies. Some populations of capuchin monkeys in Brazil use stone hammers and anvils to crack open palm nuts, a practice stable and skilled enough within particular social groups that researchers studying the sites afterward have found accumulated layers of cracked nutshells and worn stone tools, evidence of tool traditions persisting at particular locations over long spans of time. Octopuses, despite belonging to a lineage with no bones and an utterly different nervous system from any of the animals mentioned so far, have been observed carrying coconut shell halves to use later as portable shelters, arguably qualifying as tool use under some of the same definitions applied to vertebrates.

## Redefining the Question

The cumulative effect of these findings was not simply to add more species to a checklist, but to shift the scientific question itself. Rather than asking whether a given species could clear a fixed bar for true tool use, researchers increasingly asked what any particular instance of tool use revealed about the cognitive machinery behind it: whether an animal appeared to understand the causal relationship between the tool and the outcome, or was simply repeating an inherited motor pattern; whether tool designs were learned socially and passed between individuals as a genuine tradition, evidenced by regional variation in technique among populations with the same access to raw materials; and whether an animal could improvise a tool for a genuinely novel problem it had never previously encountered, as opposed to only ever deploying tools in the specific, familiar contexts in which it had learned to do so.

That reframing turned tool use from a single yes-or-no boundary marking humans off from the rest of nature into a graded, comparative window onto animal cognition generally, one that has ended up saying at least as much about the flexibility of crow and octopus brains, built along evolutionary paths entirely separate from the primate lineage, as it ever said about chimpanzees.

## What the Long View Suggests

Looking back over the six decades since Gombe, the more striking pattern may not be any single species' cleverness but how many independent times comparable solutions have appeared. Stick tools for extracting hidden food have now turned up in chimpanzees, orangutans, and crows despite tens of millions of years of separate evolutionary history among the three. That kind of convergence suggests tool use is less a rare accident restricted to one favored lineage than a solution that becomes available, again and again, to almost any animal with enough manual or manipulative dexterity, a flexible enough brain, and a food source just out of reach of its own body.
