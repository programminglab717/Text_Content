# DNA Structure and the Genetic Code

Unspool the DNA from a single human cell and lay it end to end and you would
have roughly two meters of thread. It is packed into a nucleus a few millionths
of a meter across, wound around protein spools, coiled and supercoiled and
folded into loops, and yet it can be read, copied, and repaired in place without
turning into an irreversible tangle. That trick of packaging is impressive. What
is more impressive is that the thread carries instructions at all — that a
molecule built from four repeating chemical units can specify an eye, a liver
enzyme, or the timing of a flower's bloom.

The molecule itself is a polymer with a dull backbone and an interesting edge.
The backbone alternates a five-carbon sugar, deoxyribose, with a phosphate
group, linked in a chain that repeats identically from one end to the other. It
carries no information; it is scaffolding. The information hangs off the side of
that scaffolding as a sequence of nitrogen-containing bases: adenine, guanine,
cytosine, and thymine. Adenine and guanine are purines, built on a double ring.
Cytosine and thymine are pyrimidines, built on a single ring. That size
difference turns out to matter enormously.

## A Ladder Under Tension

By the early 1950s the chemistry of DNA was reasonably well characterized and
the architecture was not. Erwin Chargaff had noticed something suggestive: in
DNA from any organism he examined, the amount of adenine matched the amount of
thymine, and guanine matched cytosine, even though the ratio of one pair to the
other varied widely between species. Nobody knew quite what to make of it.
Meanwhile Rosalind Franklin, working with Maurice Wilkins at King's College
London, was producing X-ray diffraction images of DNA fibers of unprecedented
quality. The now-famous photograph of the B form showed a cross pattern that a
crystallographer could read almost immediately as a helix, along with spacings
that constrained its dimensions.

The structure James Watson and Francis Crick assembled from these constraints in
1953 is two chains wound around a common axis, running in opposite directions —
antiparallel, one oriented five-prime to three-prime, the other three-prime to
five-prime. The sugar-phosphate backbones spiral on the outside, where their
charged phosphates face the water. The bases point inward, stacked flat like
coins in a roll, hydrophobic faces shielded from the solvent. The helix is
roughly two nanometers wide, advances a third of a nanometer per base pair, and
completes a turn about every ten and a half pairs. Because the two backbones are
not diametrically opposite each other, the winding leaves grooves of unequal
width — a major groove and a minor one — and proteins that need to recognize a
particular sequence without unzipping the molecule read the edges of the bases
through the major groove.

The pairing rules fall out of geometry and hydrogen bonding together. A purine
paired with a pyrimidine spans the space between the backbones exactly; two
purines would be too fat, two pyrimidines too thin. Within that constraint, only
adenine and thymine present complementary hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors to
each other, and only guanine and cytosine do. A-T pairs share two hydrogen
bonds; G-C pairs share three, which is why sequences rich in G and C melt apart
at higher temperatures and why laboratory protocols care about the composition
of a short DNA primer.

Complementarity is the whole point. Each strand implies the other. When the
double helix opens, either strand can serve as a template for rebuilding its
partner, and the resulting daughter molecules each retain one old strand and one
new — semiconservative replication, confirmed a few years after the structure
was proposed by density labeling experiments that tracked heavy nitrogen through
successive generations of bacteria. Watson and Crick's paper contained a famously
restrained sentence noting that the pairing they had proposed immediately
suggested a copying mechanism. It did, and the mechanism was right.

## From Sequence to Protein

Structure explains heredity. It does not by itself explain function. A gene has
to do something, and what most genes do is specify a protein: a chain of amino
acids that folds into a shape and then catalyzes a reaction, binds a ligand,
carries a signal, or forms part of a structure. Proteins are built from twenty
standard amino acids. DNA has four bases. Translating between the two alphabets
requires a code, and the arithmetic of that code was worked out before anyone
knew a single one of its entries.

One base per amino acid gives four possibilities — far too few. Two bases give
sixteen, still short. Three bases give sixty-four, comfortably more than enough.
The triplet hypothesis was therefore the obvious guess, and experiments with
frameshift mutations in bacteriophage confirmed it in an elegant way: inserting
or deleting one base wrecked a gene, and so did two, but three insertions or
three deletions could restore a nearly functional protein, because the reading
frame was pushed back into register with only a short garbled stretch in
between. The code was read in non-overlapping groups of three from a fixed
starting point.

Getting from DNA to protein takes an intermediary. RNA — chemically almost
identical to DNA, but with ribose in place of deoxyribose and uracil in place of
thymine — is transcribed from one strand of the gene, exported from the nucleus
in eukaryotes, and fed through the ribosome. The ribosome does not read bases
directly. It reads them through transfer RNAs, small folded molecules that
present a three-base anticodon at one end and carry a specific amino acid at the
other. Crick had predicted the existence of such an adaptor on essentially
logical grounds: nothing about the shape of a codon resembles the shape of the
amino acid it specifies, so something had to bridge the gap physically. The
specificity actually lives in the enzymes that charge each tRNA with the correct
amino acid, which makes those enzymes the true dictionary of the code.

## Cracking It

The first entry came in 1961, when Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei fed
a synthetic RNA consisting of nothing but uracil into a cell-free extract and
recovered a polypeptide of nothing but phenylalanine. UUU meant phenylalanine.
Within a few years, using synthetic RNAs of defined repeating sequence made in
Har Gobind Khorana's lab and a filter-binding assay that measured which charged
tRNA stuck to which triplet, the entire table was filled in. Robert Holley
independently sequenced a transfer RNA, the first nucleic acid ever sequenced,
revealing the cloverleaf folding that makes the adaptor work.

The finished table has a shape to it. Of the sixty-four codons, sixty-one
specify amino acids and three — UAA, UAG, and UGA — specify termination. AUG
specifies methionine and also serves as the usual start signal. Because there
are sixty-one sense codons for twenty amino acids, the code is redundant:
leucine, serine, and arginine each have six codons, while methionine and
tryptophan have only one apiece. The redundancy is not scattered at random.
Synonymous codons almost always differ at the third position, and the pattern of
which amino acid sits where is strikingly non-arbitrary. Codons with uracil in
the second position tend to specify hydrophobic amino acids; codons with adenine
there tend toward hydrophilic ones. Mutations at third positions frequently
change nothing at all, and mutations at first positions often substitute an
amino acid with similar chemical properties.

This arrangement buffers the code against error. A copying mistake that lands on
a third position is likely to be silent; one that lands elsewhere is likely to
produce a conservative substitution rather than a catastrophic one. Whether that
robustness arose because selection reshuffled an earlier, cruder assignment, or
because the code grew outward from a small set of chemically related amino acids
in an order that happened to produce the pattern, is still argued. The
degeneracy also has a mechanical explanation at the level of the tRNA: the
pairing between the third base of a codon and the first base of an anticodon is
looser than standard base pairing, so a single tRNA can read several synonymous
codons. Crick called this wobble, and it explains why cells manage with far
fewer than sixty-one distinct transfer RNAs.

## Almost, But Not Quite, Universal

The same table operates in bacteria, in archaea, in oak trees and octopuses and
people. That near-universality is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that
everything alive descends from a common ancestor in which the assignments were
already fixed. It is also why a human gene can be spliced into a bacterium and
produce recognizable human insulin — the machinery is interchangeable because
the dictionary is shared.

The exceptions are informative rather than fatal. Mitochondria, which carry
their own small genomes and their own translation apparatus, use slightly
altered tables; in human mitochondria, UGA specifies tryptophan instead of stop.
Some ciliates reassign one or two stop codons to glutamine. A handful of
organisms insert selenocysteine or pyrrolysine at particular UGA or UAG codons
using dedicated machinery and specific signals in the surrounding RNA. Each of
these variants sits on a short branch of the tree of life, a late local
modification of a system that was otherwise frozen long before there were cells
worth calling modern. Changing a codon assignment means changing the meaning of
every occurrence of that codon in every gene at once, which is why the code has
barely moved in perhaps three and a half billion years, and why the few places
where it has moved involve genomes small enough to absorb the damage.
