# Human Evolution and the Fossil Record

In the summer of 1856, workmen clearing a small cave in a limestone gorge near
Düsseldorf shoveled out a skullcap and some limb bones and threw them on the
spoil heap with the rest of the fill. The gorge was called the Neander valley
and was being quarried away for cement; within a few decades the cave itself no
longer existed. A local schoolteacher with an interest in natural history heard
about the bones, collected what could be recovered, and brought them to an
anatomist who concluded they belonged to a human of a very ancient and very
unusual kind. Germany's most eminent pathologist disagreed, arguing that the
heavy browridges and bowed thighbones were the marks of rickets and a hard life.
The argument ran for decades and was only settled when similar skeletons began
turning up across Europe, too many and too consistent to be a catalogue of
individual diseases.

That episode sets the pattern for nearly everything that followed. A fossil
appears, usually by accident, usually damaged, usually with no context that
survived the finding. Its significance is contested. It is resolved not by
argument but by the eventual arrival of more material, or by a technique that
did not exist when the bone was dug up.

## Going Out to Look

For the first half-century, discovery was passive. Then a Dutch army surgeon
named Eugène Dubois decided that if human ancestors existed they could be found
on purpose, reasoned that the tropics where apes live were the place to look,
and got himself posted to the Dutch East Indies. Along the Solo River in Java
his crews recovered a low, thick skullcap and, nearby, a thighbone that was
unmistakably built for upright walking. He named the creature Pithecanthropus
erectus and spent years being told he had combined an ape's skull with a human's
leg.

The next shift came from South Africa. In 1924 a consignment of blasted limestone
from a quarry at Taung reached the anatomist Raymond Dart, who found in it a
fossilized natural cast of a small brain, and the face and jaw that went with
it. The teeth were a child's. The brain was ape-sized, but the opening for the
spinal cord sat underneath the skull rather than behind it, where it belongs in
an animal that carries its head over an upright spine. Dart argued for an
upright African ape near human ancestry and was dismissed for twenty years,
partly because the expected story ran the other way round — big brain first,
apish body later — an expectation propped up by the Piltdown remains from Sussex,
which combined exactly those features and which turned out in the 1950s to be a
deliberate forgery of a human skull and an orangutan jaw, stained and filed to
match.

By then, patient work at other South African caves had produced adult specimens
that made Dart's case for him, and the Leakeys had begun the long excavation of
Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania, where a robust-jawed cranium found in 1959 was dated
by the newly developed potassium-argon method to around 1.75 million years. The
antiquity of the human line, which had been a matter of speculation, became a
measurement.

## What the Ground Keeps

The record is not a fair sample, and understanding its biases is most of the
skill in reading it. Bone survives where it is buried quickly by sediment that
is chemically kind to it, and where those sediments are later exposed by
erosion. Two settings dominate. The East African rift is an actively splitting
basin that repeatedly floods, silts up, and is blanketed by ash from nearby
volcanoes; the faulting then tilts and exposes the layers. The South African
highveld is riddled with dolomite caverns into which animals fell or were
dragged, their bones cemented into breccia.

That is why the human family tree, as fossils tell it, is an East African and
South African story. It may also be roughly true, but the sampling would produce
that appearance whether or not it was. The forested basins of central and west
Africa, where the rainfall is high and bones dissolve, have yielded almost
nothing. The scale of the gap is easiest to grasp from the other side: the
chimpanzee, an animal with a range spanning much of equatorial Africa, has a
fossil record amounting to a handful of teeth.

## Putting Numbers on the Layers

Dating a hominin fossil almost never means dating the bone. It means dating
something nearby that contains a usable clock. Volcanic ash layers are ideal,
because the argon trapped in their crystals began accumulating the moment the
ash cooled, and the ash from a single eruption can be fingerprinted by its
chemistry and traced across a whole basin — which means a fossil bracketed
between two identified tuffs can be dated even at a site with no eruption of its
own.

When the clocks disagree, the fight can last years. A famous cranium from east
of Lake Turkana was initially placed at more than two and a half million years
by dating of the overlying ash, an age that made its relatively large braincase
hard to accommodate. The date was challenged on the basis of the pigs. Fossil
suids and elephants evolved fast enough that their species succeed one another
in a known order across East Africa, and the pigs in the Turkana sediments
matched assemblages that were demonstrably younger elsewhere. Remeasurement
eventually brought the ash down to around 1.9 million years, in line with the
animals. Biostratigraphy, the oldest dating method there is, corrected the
radiometry.

## Feet First

What the record shows most clearly is that the traits we think of as human did
not arrive as a package. Upright walking came first, by a very wide margin.

Lucy, recovered from eroding sediments at Hadar in Ethiopia in 1974 and
something like two-fifths complete, has a short, broad, basin-shaped pelvis and
a thighbone angled inward toward the knee, so that her feet fell beneath her
center of mass with each stride. Her braincase was in the range of a
chimpanzee's. She lived roughly three million years ago, and at Laetoli in
Tanzania a group of her kind walked across a bed of damp volcanic ash a few
hundred thousand years earlier and left a trail of footprints with a deep heel
strike, an arch, and a big toe in line with the others rather than sticking out
to the side. The ash set, and then buried them.

Brains enlarged later, and slowly, and body proportions changed alongside them.
A nearly complete adolescent skeleton found on the western side of Lake Turkana
in 1984 belongs to a population already tall, long-legged, narrow-hipped and
built for walking or running long distances in open country — a body plan much
closer to ours than to Lucy's, carrying a brain roughly two-thirds of modern
size. Earlier still, material from the Middle Awash pushed the record back
toward four and a half million years, to a creature with a grasping foot that
nonetheless appears to have walked upright on the ground, and fragmentary
candidates from Chad and Kenya reach back to six or seven million years, close
to where genetic comparisons put the split from chimpanzees.

## A Bush, Not a Line

The most durable popular image of human evolution is a row of figures
straightening up as they march to the right, and it is wrong in the way a
single-file line is always wrong about a family. At several points in the past,
multiple hominin species were alive at once, sometimes in the same landscape:
lightly built and heavily built australopiths sharing East African basins,
several kinds of early Homo overlapping, and as recently as the last hundred
thousand years a world containing our own species, Neanderthals, Denisovans, the
small-bodied population of Flores, and probably others.

How many species there actually were is a question the fossils cannot fully
settle, because a fossil species is defined by the shape of bones rather than by
who bred with whom. Palaeoanthropology has long been split between researchers
inclined to name a new species for each distinctive specimen and those inclined
to fold variation into existing ones, and both camps can point to real
difficulties. Living primates vary enormously within a species — male and female
gorillas differ in size more than some named hominin taxa differ from each
other — so a large robust skull and a small gracile one from the same deposit
may be two species or one dimorphic population. Individual age, geography and
sheer breakage add more noise. Genetics has since shown that even groups
separated for hundreds of thousands of years interbred where they met, which
means the boundaries the bones suggest were leakier than the naming implies.

Our own origin looks less like a birthday than like a slow convergence of
features. Material from a Moroccan site, long misidentified and eventually
redated to around three hundred thousand years, has a face that would not draw a
second glance today attached to a braincase that is still long and low rather
than globular. Ethiopian remains a hundred thousand years younger look more
familiar. The modern skull shape assembles piecemeal, across the continent,
among populations that were exchanging genes with one another, and the search
for a single ancestral place and moment may be a category error.

That last group makes the point about surprise. The Flores skeletons, found in a
cave floor in Indonesia, belong to an adult barely a meter tall with a brain
smaller than an australopith's, living far later than anything with those
proportions had any business living. Deep in a South African cave system,
hundreds of bones of another small-brained species were recovered from a chamber
reachable only through a chute a few centimeters wider than a shoulder, and the
dating placed them not millions but a few hundred thousand years ago.

Molecules have now joined the record and in places surpassed it. Sequencing
recovered from a finger bone in a Siberian cave revealed a population no one had
suspected, defined by its genome before anyone could say what it looked like,
and detectable today as a percentage of ancestry in living people across island
Southeast Asia and Oceania — including a high-altitude adaptation carried by
Tibetans that appears to have been inherited from them. Fragmentary jaws from
the Tibetan Plateau have since been assigned to the same group by the proteins
surviving in their enamel, and hominin DNA has been pulled straight out of cave
sediments in layers containing no bones at all. The next chapter of this record
may be written mostly from dirt.
