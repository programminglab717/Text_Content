# Genetic Drift and the Random Walk of Evolution

Flip a coin ten times and you would not be shocked to get seven heads. Flip it ten million times and the result will land almost exactly on five million and five million, because with enough trials the laws of probability smooth out into near-certainty. Something similar happens inside populations of living things, except the "coin flips" are which individuals happen to survive, which happen to mate, and which of their gene copies happen to make it into the next generation's fertilized eggs. In a huge population, these chance effects average out and the more consistent hand of natural selection tends to show through. In a small population, chance can dominate the outcome entirely, pushing a gene variant toward extinction or toward fixation for no reason connected to whether it helps the organism survive. Biologists call this genetic drift, and it is one of the most underappreciated forces in evolution, mostly because it produces no obvious design, no elegant adaptation, nothing that looks like a solution to anything. It is noise, but noise that leaves a permanent mark on the genome.

## Selection's Quiet Counterpart

Natural selection gets most of the attention in popular accounts of evolution because it does something intuitive: it makes organisms better suited to their surroundings, and better-suited organisms make for satisfying stories. Genetic drift does not work that way. It changes which versions of a gene are present in a population purely as a side effect of random sampling — the same way a small sample of colored marbles drawn from a large jar can, purely by chance, contain a higher or lower proportion of red marbles than the jar as a whole, even though nobody was selecting for or against red.

Every generation, only some individuals in a population happen to reproduce, and even among those that do, only some of their gene copies happen to end up in surviving offspring, purely because of who mated with whom, how many eggs got fertilized, which embryos happened to develop normally, and a hundred other events with no systematic bias toward one gene variant over another. Over many generations, these random fluctuations accumulate. A gene variant that was fairly common can, by nothing more than bad luck across several generations, dwindle away entirely, while a rare variant can, with equally undeserved good luck, become the only version left. Once a variant disappears from a population entirely, it is gone for good unless a new mutation happens to recreate it, and once a variant becomes the only version present, biologists say it has become fixed.

## Population Size Is the Whole Story

The single biggest factor determining how much drift matters is population size, and the relationship is not subtle. In a population of many millions, the random noise from generation to generation is a rounding error against the signal produced by selection, and helpful or harmful traits move toward fixation or elimination in something close to the pattern selection alone would predict. In a population of a few dozen individuals, drift can overwhelm selection almost completely. A mildly beneficial gene variant can vanish from a small population by chance before selection ever gets enough generations to boost its frequency, and a mildly harmful variant can become fixed despite working against the organisms carrying it, simply because the small number of individuals available made chance events more influential than any measurable difference in fitness.

This is why island populations, isolated valleys, and species that have gone through a period of scarcity often carry genetic quirks that have nothing to do with local adaptation. The pattern is not always "this trait must be useful here since it is so common." Sometimes the honest explanation is that a small founding group happened to carry a particular set of variants, and there were never enough individuals afterward for chance to average itself back out.

## Founder Effects

A particularly clean version of drift occurs when a small number of individuals break off from a larger population and establish a new one — a handful of birds blown to a remote island, a small human community migrating and settling somewhere isolated, a few insects colonizing a patch of habitat separated from the rest of their species' range. Whatever genetic variants those founders happen to be carrying, in whatever proportions they happen to carry them, become the entire genetic starting point for everyone descended from them. If none of the founders happened to carry a particular gene variant that was common back in the original population, that variant is simply absent from the new population from day one, with no amount of subsequent breeding able to reintroduce it short of a new mutation or contact with outsiders.

Human communities that grew from small founding groups and remained relatively isolated afterward, marrying mostly within the community for generations, sometimes show this pattern clearly. Certain rare recessive conditions turn up at unusually elevated rates in specific communities of this kind, not because the community has unusually bad luck with mutations, but because one or more of the original founders happened to carry a recessive gene variant, and generations of marriage within a relatively small, closed group gave that variant many more opportunities to pair up with a matching copy than it would ever get in a large, freely mixing population. Old Order Amish communities in parts of North America, descended from a comparatively small number of eighteenth-century founders, are a frequently cited example, showing elevated rates of several specific recessive conditions that are rare in the broader population from which the founders came.

## Bottlenecks

A related but distinct pattern, the population bottleneck, occurs when an already-established population is suddenly and drastically reduced in size, whether by disease, climate catastrophe, habitat loss, or hunting, and then recovers from whatever few individuals survive. The genetic diversity lost during the crash does not necessarily come back just because the population's numbers do. The survivors' descendants can rebuild a large population quickly, but that population's total genetic variety is capped by whatever the handful of survivors happened to be carrying.

Cheetahs are the textbook case. Genetic studies of living cheetahs have found remarkably little variation across the species compared to most other large mammals, consistent with the idea that the species passed through a severe bottleneck at some point in the past, long before modern conservation concerns arose, leaving today's cheetahs all closely related in genetic terms even though they are spread across a wide geographic range. Northern elephant seals tell an even more recent version of the same story: hunted to the edge of extinction in the nineteenth century, down to what was apparently a tiny remnant population, they have since rebounded to number in the tens of thousands, yet genetic surveys show they carry far less variation than related seal species that were never reduced so drastically. The population recovered; the genetic diversity mostly did not, because it cannot be rebuilt by growth in numbers alone.

## Neutral Change and the Molecular Clock

Drift matters most obviously for traits with no strong effect on survival or reproduction at all — so-called neutral variants, changes to a gene's sequence that do not alter how well an organism functions. Because selection has essentially nothing to grip onto in these cases, the fate of a neutral variant is governed almost entirely by drift, rising and falling in frequency across generations by chance alone until it either disappears or becomes fixed. This turns out to be enormously useful to biologists in an unexpected way: since neutral mutations accumulate at a comparatively steady average rate over long stretches of time, comparing the number of neutral genetic differences between two living species offers a rough molecular clock for estimating how long ago their lineages diverged from a common ancestor, a technique that complements, and sometimes corrects, estimates drawn from the fossil record alone.

## Drift and Selection Working Together

None of this means selection is unimportant or that drift explains everything. In practice the two forces operate simultaneously, and which one dominates for any given gene variant depends on the strength of the fitness difference involved and the size of the population experiencing it. A gene variant with a large fitness advantage in a large population will tend to spread predictably regardless of drift. A gene variant with only a slight advantage, in a small population, is essentially at the mercy of chance no matter what selection would prefer.

This is part of why evolutionary biologists resist any single, tidy story about why a given trait exists. A trait's presence in a population is not automatically evidence that it was favored by selection; sometimes the honest answer is that a population went through a period of small numbers, isolation, or lucky sampling, and a mediocre or even mildly harmful variant simply came along for the ride.
