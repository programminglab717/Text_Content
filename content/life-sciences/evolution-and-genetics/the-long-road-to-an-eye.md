# The Long Road to an Eye

A living Nautilus has, on either side of its head, an eye about the size of a
pea with a hole in the front of it and no lens at all. Seawater circulates
freely through the opening. The animal is looking at the world through a pinhole
camera, with all the limitations that implies: an image dim enough to be nearly
useless in bright conditions, and fuzzy in proportion to how wide the aperture is
opened to compensate. It is a poor eye by the standards of the squid and octopus
that share its phylum. It is also a working eye, on an animal that has been
making a living with it for a very long time, and it is the plainest available
answer to an objection Darwin took seriously enough to address head-on.

The objection is the one about half an eye. An eye is a system of parts — a
photosensitive surface, an optical element to form an image on it, an aperture
to control light, a nervous apparatus to interpret what arrives — and each part
seems useless without the others. Darwin conceded the force of this in the
Origin, calling the supposition that such an organ could have formed by natural
selection absurd on its face, and then spent several pages explaining why the
concession was rhetorical. His argument was empirical: look across living
animals and you find eyes at every grade of complexity, each one adequate for
the life of its owner. The intermediates are not hypothetical. They are swimming
around.

## The Chemistry Was Already There

The deepest part of the answer is that the hardest component, the one that looks
like it would need inventing, was in place long before eyes existed.

Nearly all animal vision runs on opsins — proteins that thread seven times
through a membrane and cradle a small molecule derived from vitamin A. That
chromophore has a kink in it. When a photon of the right energy strikes, the
kink straightens, the protein deforms around the change, and the deformation is
handed to a G protein waiting on the inner face of the membrane. Everything else
is amplification: one activated opsin switches many G proteins, each of which
drives an enzyme that alters the concentration of a second messenger, which
opens or closes ion channels by the thousand. A single photon can be made to
produce a measurable electrical event.

The opsin family is old. It belongs to the vast class of G-protein-coupled
receptors that animals use to detect odors, hormones and neurotransmitters, and
it arose by co-opting that existing machinery for light. Cnidarians — jellyfish,
corals, anemones, animals without anything most people would call a brain —
carry opsins. Box jellyfish go considerably further, with clusters of sensory
structures around the bell that include genuine lensed eyes. Whatever the last
common ancestor of jellyfish and vertebrates possessed, it already had the
molecular detector. What remained was optics, and optics is geometry.

## Cup, Pinhole, Lens

The sequence that gets from a light-sensitive patch to a focused image is not
mysterious, and every stage in it is occupied by some living animal.

Start with an unshielded patch of photoreceptors on the skin. It reports the
ambient light level, which is enough to tell a larva whether it is near the
surface, or to trigger a withdrawal reflex when a shadow passes. It cannot
report direction, because light from any angle reaches it equally.

Now add pigment beside the patch, or let the patch sit in a slight depression.
Light from one side is blocked; light from the other is not. The animal can now
tell where illumination is coming from and turn toward or away from it. Deepen
the depression into a cup and the directional resolution improves steadily,
because each receptor sees a narrower cone of sky. There is no threshold to
cross here, no point at which the organ suddenly becomes useful. Every increment
of deepening buys a small increment of information, which is exactly the
condition natural selection needs.

Keep going and the cup's mouth constricts until you have Nautilus: a pinhole,
which for the first time forms a real image. Cover the opening with transparent
tissue to keep out debris and parasites, and the eye becomes a closed chamber.
Let that covering thicken unevenly, and it begins to refract. Even a crude,
irregular blob of denser tissue gathers more light onto the retina than an open
pinhole of the same resolving power, which relaxes the trade-off between
brightness and sharpness that cripples the Nautilus design. From there,
refinement is continuous: adjust the curvature, grade the refractive index from
the center outward so that a spherical lens can focus without the spherical
aberration that would otherwise blur its edges, add muscles to change focus, add
an iris.

Someone eventually did the arithmetic on this. A model that stepped through the
sequence in small increments — each generation changing a dimension by a
fraction of a percent, with conservative assumptions about how much selection
would favor each improvement — suggested that a flat photosensitive patch could
become a fish-grade camera eye in a few hundred thousand generations. For a
small animal breeding annually, that is a geological eyeblink. The result is
worth treating as an order-of-magnitude statement rather than a measurement, but
it inverts the intuition the objection rests on. Eyes are not hard to evolve.
Given light, a chromophore and time, they are close to inevitable.

## The Same Problem, Solved Repeatedly

The strongest evidence for that inevitability is how often it happened
independently. Estimates of the number of separate origins of image-forming eyes
in animals run into the dozens, and the engineering solutions differ in ways
that betray their separate histories.

Vertebrates and cephalopods both built camera eyes with a single lens throwing
an image onto a retina, and the resemblance is close enough that it was long
used as the textbook case of convergence. But the retinas are installed
backwards from each other. In an octopus the photoreceptors face the light and
their nerve fibers run away behind them. In a vertebrate the receptors face away
from the light, so that incoming photons must pass through layers of neurons and
blood vessels before reaching them, and the nerve fibers gather on the inner
surface and dive through the retina in a bundle, producing a blind spot the
brain edits out. No engineer would do this. It is a frozen accident of how the
vertebrate eye develops, as an outgrowth of the brain rather than an infolding
of the skin, and the lineage has spent the subsequent hundreds of millions of
years compensating — partly with glial cells that act as fiber-optic guides
funneling light through the obstruction.

Arthropods took a different route entirely, replicating a whole small optical
unit hundreds or thousands of times into a compound eye. Each ommatidium
contributes essentially one pixel, which makes compound eyes poor at resolution
and superb at detecting motion and covering a wide field. Trilobites pushed the
design somewhere no other animal has: their lenses were made of calcite,
oriented along the crystal axis that avoids double refraction, and in some
groups built as a two-element doublet whose curved internal interface corrects
spherical aberration — a solution described by seventeenth-century opticians,
several hundred million years late.

Scallops do without refraction. Behind each of the dozens of small blue eyes
along the edge of the mantle sits a concave mirror built from stacked crystals,
which focuses light back onto a retina in front of it, the same principle as a
reflecting telescope. Some deep-sea fish use mirrors too, as a supplement to a
conventional lens, in water where every photon counts.

What ties these together at the genetic level is a shared developmental
vocabulary. A transcription factor called Pax6 sits near the top of the
regulatory hierarchy for eye formation in animals as different as flies and
mice, and the mouse version, expressed in the wrong place in a fly larva, will
induce the fly to build an ectopic compound eye on its leg. The gene does not
specify an eye's design. It licenses the construction of whatever eye that
lineage knows how to make.

Color vision followed the same opportunistic pattern, built by duplicating
opsin genes and letting the copies drift apart until their peak sensitivities
separated. A visual system can only compare wavelengths if it has at least two
receptor types to compare, so every expansion of color perception traces back to
a duplication. Most mammals see with two, a legacy of ancestors that spent a
long stretch of the Mesozoic being nocturnal and losing what their reptilian
forebears had; catarrhine primates regained a third by duplicating a gene on the
X chromosome, which is also why color blindness is so much commoner in men, and
why some New World monkeys have females that see in three channels and males
that do not. Mantis shrimps abandoned the comparison strategy altogether and
carry a dozen or more receptor classes, apparently trading fine discrimination
for speed of classification.

The fossil record of all this is sparse but not absent, because lenses and
lens-bearing cuticle sometimes mineralize. Cambrian deposits have yielded
compound eyes of startling quality, including stalked eyes belonging to large
swimming predators with tens of thousands of lenses apiece — acuity comparable
to a modern dragonfly, in an animal from the first great radiation of
bilaterians. The appearance of good eyes at roughly the moment that armor,
burrowing and obvious predatory hardware appear in the record has prompted the
suggestion that vision itself was a trigger: once one animal could see another
across a distance, everything about being prey changed. It is a hypothesis
rather than a demonstration, and the correlation may run the other way. But the
timing is suggestive.

## Undoing It

Eyes are also lost, and lost quickly, which says something about their cost.
Cave populations of the Mexican tetra descend from surface-dwelling fish that
swam into limestone systems. The embryos still begin building eyes — the optic
cup forms, a lens begins to differentiate — and then the program aborts, the
lens tissue dies, and the socket fills in with connective tissue and skin.
Different cave populations have lost their eyes independently, by somewhat
different genetic routes, and the loss is entangled with changes that look
beneficial in darkness: expanded jaws, more taste buds, enlarged mechanosensory
lines. A structure that is metabolically expensive and no longer earning
anything is dismantled about as readily as it was assembled.
