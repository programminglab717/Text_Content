# Convergent Evolution and the Shapes That Keep Returning

Put a tuna, an ichthyosaur, and a dolphin in a row and the silhouettes are
nearly interchangeable: a stiff fusiform body, a pointed head with no neck to
speak of, paired forelimbs reduced to control surfaces, a dorsal fin for roll
stability, and a tail that does the propulsion. The tuna is a bony fish. The
ichthyosaur was a reptile that returned to the sea in the Triassic and died out
before the end of the Cretaceous. The dolphin is a mammal whose ancestors were
walking on four legs in the early Eocene. Their common ancestor was a fish-like
creature in the Devonian with none of these features, and the three lineages
arrived at the same body independently, separated by hundreds of millions of
years.

This is convergent evolution: unrelated lineages, exposed to similar problems,
producing similar solutions. It is one of the more instructive patterns in
biology, partly because it shows that the space of workable designs is not
infinite, and partly because it constantly threatens to mislead anyone trying to
reconstruct the history of life.

## Homology Versus Analogy

The distinction that organizes all of this was drawn in the nineteenth century,
before anyone had a mechanism to explain it. Structures are homologous when they
descend from the same structure in a common ancestor, however much their
function has diverged. The bones in a bat's wing, a horse's leg, a whale's
flipper, and a human hand are the same bones in the same arrangement, stretched
and fused and reproportioned. Structures are analogous when they perform the
same function without that shared ancestry. A bat's wing and an insect's wing
are analogous; a bat's wing and a whale's flipper are homologous.

Only homology carries phylogenetic information. Analogy carries information
about the environment instead — about what a fluid medium demands of anything
that moves quickly through it, or what a dry climate demands of a plant trying
to hold water. The trouble is that from the outside the two look alike, and
sorting them requires attention to details that have nothing to do with
function: the sequence of bones, the embryonic tissue a structure grows from,
the nerves that innervate it. A marsupial mole and a golden mole both have
cylindrical bodies, reduced eyes, and spade-like forelimbs, and the similarity
disappears the moment you look at the reproductive tract.

## The Catalogue

Once you start looking, the repetitions are everywhere, and their frequency is
itself the point.

Camera eyes — a lens, an aperture, a chamber, a sheet of photoreceptors at the
back — evolved separately in vertebrates and in cephalopods. The convergence is
close enough to be uncanny and imperfect in a revealing way. The vertebrate
retina is wired backward, with nerve fibers running across the front of the
photoreceptors and bundling into a nerve that punches through the sheet, leaving
a blind spot. The octopus retina faces forward and has no blind spot. Two
lineages solved the same optical problem and happened to fold the tissue in
different directions early on, and neither can now reverse that decision.

Powered flight arose at least four times: in insects, pterosaurs, birds, and
bats. Each built an airfoil from different material — a membranous outgrowth of
the body wall, skin stretched over an elongated fourth finger, skin stretched
over four fingers, feathers on a fused forelimb. Echolocation arose separately
in bats and in toothed whales, and when the genes underlying high-frequency
hearing were sequenced, the same substitutions turned up in both groups in a
protein of the inner ear's outer hair cells, an instance of convergence reaching
all the way down to individual amino acids.

The crab form has evolved at least five separate times within the decapod
crustaceans, from ancestors resembling lobsters or hermit crabs: the abdomen
folds under the body, the carapace broadens, and the animal starts walking
sideways. Carcinization is common enough that it is treated half as a joke and
half as a serious question about what makes that body plan so accessible.

Australia's marsupials, isolated for tens of millions of years, reconstructed a
familiar cast from their own stock — marsupial moles, gliders convergent on
flying squirrels, a marsupial that hunted like a wolf and whose skull was so
dog-like that it takes a specialist to tell the difference at a glance. Saber
teeth appeared in cats, in the unrelated nimravids, and in a South American
marsupial relative. Electric organs built from modified muscle evolved
independently in African and South American freshwater fish.

Plants converge just as readily. The succulent, spiny, water-storing column is
the standard answer to hot deserts, and it was arrived at separately by New
World cacti and by African euphorbs to the point that nurseries mislabel them.
The C4 photosynthetic pathway, an elaborate modification that concentrates
carbon dioxide around the enzyme that fixes it, has evolved dozens of times
across unrelated plant families, apparently whenever atmospheric carbon dioxide
dropped low enough to make the older pathway inefficient. Antifreeze proteins
evolved in Antarctic notothenioid fish and in Arctic cod from entirely different
starting sequences, in oceans on opposite ends of the planet.

## Constraint, Not Coincidence

The reason the same shapes keep returning is that physics and chemistry impose
the same requirements regardless of ancestry. Moving fast through water rewards
a particular distribution of volume along the body. Focusing light requires a
transparent medium of graded refractive index. Flying requires lift exceeding
weight at achievable speeds. These are engineering constraints, and there are
not many ways to satisfy them with organic materials.

But raw physics is only half the explanation. The other half is that lineages
are not free to explore the design space evenly. Development channels what is
possible. Vertebrates have four limbs and consequently keep inventing wings out
of forelimbs rather than growing a fifth pair. Insects with six legs keep
solving problems six-legged. Convergence is partly an indication that a solution
is good and partly an indication that the starting material was already similar
— which is why convergence is more common between closely related lineages than
distant ones, and why the tightest examples, like the marsupial and placental
carnivores, involve animals that share a great deal of underlying architecture
despite the long separation.

Molecular biology added a further complication. Eyes in flies and in mice, long
treated as textbook analogues, turn out to be specified by versions of the same
master regulatory gene, inherited from a common ancestor that presumably had
some light-sensitive patch. The structures are not homologous; the genetic
switch that builds them is. This deep homology means many apparent convergences
are recruitments of a shared and very old toolkit, which makes the outcomes less
improbable than they look. A lineage evolving a limb-like outgrowth is not
starting from scratch; it is redeploying a genetic circuit that has been
available since before the Cambrian.

## The Same Mutation, Twice

The sharpest cases of convergence are the ones where independent lineages have
hit not just the same solution but the same change in the same gene. Insects
that feed on milkweed — monarch butterflies, certain beetles, aphids, bugs —
must tolerate cardiac glycosides that jam the sodium-potassium pump every animal
cell depends on. Across these unrelated groups, the same handful of amino acid
positions in that pump have been substituted, because those are the residues
that line the binding site and the tolerable changes there are few. Snakes that
eat toxic newts and fish that eat pufferfish have similarly converged on
substitutions in the sodium channel that tetrodotoxin blocks.

Repetition also shows up on much shorter timescales, where the history is still
legible. Freshwater stickleback populations that colonized lakes after the last
glaciers retreated have lost their bony pelvic spines again and again in
separate lakes, and in population after population the change traces to
disabling the same regulatory switch upstream of the same developmental gene,
while leaving the gene's other uses intact. The switch is a convenient target
because it can be broken without collateral damage, and selection finds
convenient targets.

Sometimes the phenotype converges while the genetics does not. The ability to
digest milk sugar into adulthood arose independently in European and in East
African pastoralist populations, and the responsible variants are different
mutations in different regulatory regions near the same gene. Human adaptation
to thin air followed still more divergent routes in Tibetan and Andean
highlanders, who differ in whether they respond by raising hemoglobin
concentration at all. The lesson is that the number of available routes depends
on how tightly the problem is specified: block one specific molecule and there
may be only three workable substitutions, but supply oxygen to tissue and there
are many.

## Replaying the Tape

Stephen Jay Gould used convergence's opposite as his central image: rewind the
tape of life to the Cambrian, play it again, and the result would be
unrecognizable, because contingency — mass extinctions, chance survivals,
accidents of geography — dominates the outcome. Simon Conway Morris, working on
the same Cambrian fossils, drew the opposite conclusion. If the same solutions
appear over and over in independent lineages, then the outcome is substantially
constrained, and replaying the tape would produce something broadly familiar,
perhaps including a large-brained social animal.

The evidence does not settle this cleanly, because both readings are right about
different things. What converges is function under constraint: streamlining,
optics, insulation, jaw mechanics, the biochemistry of oxygen transport. What
does not converge is the particular. No second lineage has reinvented the
mammalian middle ear from the same jaw bones, or feathers, or the specific
architecture of a flowering plant. Anole lizards colonizing the Greater
Antilles produced the same set of ecological types — twig specialists, trunk and
ground dwellers, canopy giants — independently on each large island, which is
convergence operating as something close to a law. But they did it as anoles.

For practical work, convergence is mostly a nuisance. A phylogeneticist who
groups organisms by shared traits will place the ichthyosaur with the dolphin,
the swift with the swallow, and any number of cave-dwelling animals together on
the strength of lost eyes and lost pigment. Molecular data helps but is not
immune: the same substitution can arise twice by chance, and lineages evolving
fast can be drawn together in a tree simply because they have both accumulated
many changes. The methods developed to handle this — explicit models of how
sequences change, tests for whether similarity exceeds what independent
evolution would produce — exist precisely because the same answers keep being
found by lineages that never spoke to each other.
