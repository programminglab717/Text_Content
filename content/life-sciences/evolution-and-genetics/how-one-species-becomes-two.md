# How One Species Becomes Two

A ground squirrel living on the north rim of the Grand Canyon looks, to most observers, basically the same as one living on the south rim, some twenty kilometers away as a bird flies. Look more closely and there are real differences — the north rim's Kaibab squirrel has a mostly white tail and dark belly, distinct enough that biologists once classified it separately from its south rim relative, the Abert's squirrel, before settling on treating them as forms of the same species. The two populations have not shared genes in a very long time, because between them lies a mile-deep gorge that squirrels, which do not swim well and do not care for open desert, will not cross. Given enough additional time and no bridge across the canyon, these two squirrel populations are a plausible candidate for eventually becoming two separate species. The question of how that transition actually happens — how a single interbreeding population turns into two populations that can no longer interbreed even if given the chance — is one of the oldest problems in evolutionary biology, and one that turns out to have more than one answer.

## What "Species" Even Means

Before getting to mechanisms, it helps to be honest about how slippery the concept of a species is. The definition most commonly taught, the biological species concept, holds that a species is a group of populations whose members can interbreed and produce fertile offspring, and which are reproductively isolated from other such groups. This works reasonably well for a lot of animals but runs into trouble constantly at the edges: it says nothing useful about organisms that reproduce asexually, it struggles with cases where supposedly separate species occasionally do interbreed and produce viable young, and it cannot really be applied to fossils at all, since nobody can test whether two extinct populations could have interbred. Biologists keep the concept because it captures something real and useful about how genetically isolated populations behave, while acknowledging that nature does not organize itself into the tidy boxes any definition would like.

What matters for understanding speciation is less the definition than the process: two populations that once freely exchanged genes gradually accumulate genetic differences until they can no longer do so, or no longer do so successfully. That gradual accumulation is the engine, and it needs something to get started — usually, though not always, some kind of separation that stops the gene flow in the first place.

## Splitting by Distance: Allopatric Speciation

The most common and best understood route to speciation begins with geography. A river changes course, a mountain range rises, a land bridge floods, a few individuals are blown or carried to an island far from the mainland — by whatever means, a single population ends up divided into two groups that can no longer physically reach each other to mate. This is allopatric speciation, from the Greek roots for "other" and "homeland," and it accounts for the large majority of documented and inferred speciation events.

Once gene flow stops, the two populations are free to drift apart genetically, and several things push them in that direction simultaneously. They face different local conditions — different climate, different predators, different food sources — so natural selection pulls their gene pools in different directions. They also accumulate different mutations independently, and small populations on the isolated end, such as an island population founded by a few stray individuals, experience genetic drift that has nothing to do with adaptation at all. Given enough generations, enough of these independent changes accumulate that even if the geographic barrier later disappeared — the river changes course again, a storm builds a new land bridge — the two populations would no longer function as one. They might no longer recognize each other as potential mates, or their courtship signals might have diverged, or, if they did mate, the resulting hybrids might be weak, sterile, or simply less successful than either parent type.

The squirrels of the Grand Canyon illustrate the early stages of this process nicely: visibly different, clearly on separate evolutionary trajectories, but not yet incompatible in any absolute sense. Whether they will ever become fully separate species depends on how long the canyon keeps them apart and how much further their respective gene pools drift.

## Splitting Without a Wall: Sympatric Speciation

Geographic separation is the easy case to picture, but biologists have also documented speciation happening without any physical barrier at all, among populations that continue to live in the same place and could, in principle, keep mingling. This sympatric speciation is rarer and was long treated with skepticism, since it is harder to explain why gene flow would stop between neighbors with nothing keeping them apart.

One well-studied route involves a shift in habitat use or food source that happens to correlate with when and where individuals mate. The apple maggot fly in North America offers a documented example: the flies originally laid their eggs exclusively in the fruit of a native hawthorn tree, but after apple trees were introduced and became widely available, some flies shifted to laying eggs in apples instead. Because the flies mate on or near the fruit their mothers used, and because apples and hawthorn fruit ripen at slightly different times of year, the apple-feeding flies and the hawthorn-feeding flies increasingly mate within their own groups rather than across them, simply as a side effect of showing up in different places at different times. Over many generations, the two groups have been drifting apart genetically even though a hawthorn tree and an apple tree can grow within sight of each other.

Cichlid fish in some of the African Great Lakes present an even more striking case, with a great many closely related species packed into a single lake, differing mainly in coloration, mating displays, and subtle feeding adaptations. Here, sexual selection seems to be doing much of the separating: females choosing mates based on color patterns can, given enough generations, split a population into groups that no longer interbreed even though nothing physical prevents it, purely because preference itself has diverged.

## The Barriers That Keep Species Apart

Whatever route gets a population started down the road to separation, biologists sort the resulting barriers into two broad categories depending on when they act. Prezygotic barriers prevent mating or fertilization from happening in the first place: individuals might breed in different seasons or times of day, use courtship signals the other group no longer responds to, occupy different micro-habitats, or simply have physically incompatible reproductive structures. Postzygotic barriers act after fertilization has already occurred, reducing the fitness of any hybrid offspring that do result — the hybrid might fail to develop properly, might be weak or infertile, as with a mule, the sterile offspring of a horse and a donkey, or might be healthy and fertile itself but produce feeble offspring in the next generation, a delayed failure called hybrid breakdown.

Postzygotic barriers are, in an evolutionary sense, wasteful: producing an unfit or sterile hybrid costs both parents a wasted reproductive attempt. Where postzygotic incompatibility already exists between two diverging populations that still come into occasional contact, selection tends to strengthen prezygotic barriers instead, favoring individuals who avoid the wasteful cross-mating altogether. This process, called reinforcement, is one reason closely related species that overlap in range sometimes show exaggerated differences in mating calls or coloration exactly where their ranges meet, compared to populations further apart that never have to worry about mistaken identity.

## Ring Species and Blurry Boundaries

A small number of documented cases make the gradual, non-instantaneous nature of speciation almost physically visible. Certain salamanders in California, for instance, form a chain of populations encircling the state's Central Valley, each population able to interbreed successfully with its immediate neighbors along the ring, showing a smooth gradient of physical differences from one end to the other. But where the two ends of the ring finally meet, after looping around the valley in opposite directions, the populations there are different enough that they no longer interbreed successfully, even though an unbroken chain of interbreeding populations connects them all the way around. Neighboring links in the chain are the same species by any reasonable test; the two ends of the same chain are not. There is no single point along the ring where anyone could draw a clean line and say a species boundary sits there — the transition is smeared out across the whole loop, which is exactly what you would expect if speciation is a gradual accumulation of difference rather than a discrete event with a start date.

What ring species make visible in space, most speciation events accomplish in time alone: a slow drift from one interbreeding population into two, with no single generation in which anyone could point to the moment it happened. The label arrives only in hindsight, applied by biologists looking back at a divergence that, to the organisms living through any single stage of it, would not have looked like anything in particular.
