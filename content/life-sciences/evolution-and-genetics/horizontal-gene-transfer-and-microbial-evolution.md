# Horizontal Gene Transfer and the Shape of Microbial Evolution

In Japanese hospitals toward the end of the 1950s, physicians treating
bacillary dysentery began seeing something that ought not to have happened.
Shigella isolates were arriving at the laboratory resistant not to one
antibiotic but to several at once — sulfonamide, streptomycin, chloramphenicol,
tetracycline — drugs with unrelated chemistry and unrelated targets. Resistance
to any single one could be explained by mutation and selection, given a large
enough population and enough divisions. Resistance to four at once, in a patient
who had been given one, could not. Stranger still, when the resistant Shigella
were grown in the same broth as a susceptible laboratory strain of E. coli, the
E. coli came out of the flask resistant too, and resistant to the entire set.

What was moving between the cells was a plasmid: a small, self-replicating loop
of DNA carrying resistance genes along with the apparatus needed to copy itself
into a new host. The recipient's chromosome had barely changed. Its phenotype
had changed completely. That distinction — between evolving by modifying what
you inherited and evolving by acquiring what someone else already worked out —
is the most important single fact about how bacteria and archaea change over
time.

## Three Ways In

Prokaryotes take up foreign DNA by three broad routes, all of them discovered in
the middle of the twentieth century and all of them still being characterized.

Transformation is the simplest: a cell pulls naked DNA in from its surroundings
and, if the sequence is similar enough to something it already has,
recombination splices the fragment into the chromosome. Dozens of species are
naturally competent, and many of them regulate competence tightly, switching it
on under stress, at high cell density, or in particular habitats. Vibrio
cholerae becomes competent when it settles on chitin — the material of crab
shells and copepod carapaces, where it spends much of its life outside a human
gut, and where the DNA of dead relatives is abundant. The bacterium effectively
scavenges the genome of whatever died nearby.

Conjugation is a physical transfer between two living cells. A conjugative
plasmid encodes a pilus that draws a donor and recipient together, then feeds a
single strand of its own DNA through the junction, so that both cells end up
with a copy. The process is indifferent to species boundaries in a way that
still surprises people: a plasmid can move from a gut bacterium to a soil
bacterium to an environmental strain that has never seen a human, and some
broad-host-range plasmids will establish themselves in almost any
Gram-negative recipient. Related elements called integrative and conjugative
elements do the same trick but splice themselves into the chromosome once they
arrive, riding along quietly until conditions favor another round of transfer.

Transduction is mediated by viruses. Bacteriophages package DNA into capsids as
part of their own reproduction, and they package it imperfectly. Sometimes a
capsid gets a chunk of host chromosome instead of phage genome; sometimes a
prophage excising itself from the chromosome takes neighboring host genes with
it. Either way, the next cell the phage infects receives bacterial DNA delivered
by a machine exquisitely optimized for getting DNA across a cell envelope. The
genes for cholera toxin arrived in Vibrio cholerae this way, carried by a
filamentous phage. A great many of the traits that make a bacterium dangerous to
us are, on inspection, phage cargo.

Layered on top of these delivery systems is a set of elements whose entire
business is rearrangement: insertion sequences that hop around the genome,
composite transposons that carry cargo genes between their flanking repeats, and
integrons, which behave like a filing system for gene cassettes, capturing them
from the environment and lining them up behind a single promoter. A
multi-drug-resistant plasmid is usually not a tidy object. It is a sediment of
cassettes, transposons and fragments accumulated over decades, and its structure
records the history of the selection pressures applied to it.

## The Cell Fights Back

Because most incoming DNA is useless or parasitic, cells invest heavily in
refusing it. Restriction-modification systems methylate the host's own
recognition sites and cleave any unmethylated DNA carrying them, which is why
DNA transferred between distantly related strains is so often destroyed on
arrival. CRISPR-Cas systems go further, keeping a chronological archive of short
sequences captured from past invaders and using transcripts of that archive to
guide nucleases onto matching DNA. Bacterial genomes also carry abortive
infection systems, toxin-antitoxin modules, and an expanding catalogue of
defenses that were invisible until people went looking for them in the clusters
of genes that sit next to known ones.

Defense is not free, and neither is acceptance. A newly arrived gene must be
transcribed by the host's polymerase, folded by the host's chaperones, and
supplied with substrates the host actually makes, and a surprising fraction of
transferred DNA fails on one of those counts. Genes encoding subunits of large,
highly connected protein complexes — ribosomes, the replication machinery —
transfer rarely and persist badly, because a foreign subunit has to interface
with a dozen partners that coevolved without it. Genes encoding standalone
enzymes with a defined substrate transfer easily. This is why the accessory
genome is so heavily weighted toward metabolism, transport and defense, and why
the genes used to reconstruct deep relationships are the ones least likely to
have moved.

The result is a genuine conflict, and its outcome varies. Some lineages are
promiscuous; others are nearly sealed. Obligate intracellular symbionts, which
live inside host cells and almost never meet a stranger, tend to have small,
stable, streamlined genomes and acquire almost nothing. Free-living generalists
in soil or the gut, meeting everything, have large genomes full of recent
arrivals.

## Core Genomes and Accessory Genomes

Sequencing many strains of the same species made the consequences unmistakable.
Two isolates labeled E. coli — one a harmless commensal, one a strain that
causes hemorrhagic colitis — may share only about half their genes. The genes
they hold in common form the core genome: replication, transcription,
translation, central metabolism, the machinery a cell cannot do without. The
rest is accessory, distributed unevenly across strains, and it is where most of
the interesting biology lives. Toxins, adhesins, secretion systems, resistance
determinants, unusual sugar-degrading pathways, and the ability to colonize one
tissue rather than another are nearly all accessory.

The union of all genes found in any strain of a species is its pangenome, and
for many bacteria it is several times larger than any individual genome and
shows no sign of closing as more strains are sequenced. This forces an awkward
question about what a bacterial species even is. A single cell carries a sample
drawn from a much larger communal pool, and it can draw a different sample
tomorrow. Recently acquired regions often give themselves away by their
composition — a GC content or codon usage bias that does not match the rest of
the chromosome — which is how pathogenicity islands were first recognized as
foreign blocks inserted into otherwise ordinary genomes.

Much of what a cell picks up is junk, or worse. Plasmids impose a replication
burden; prophages can lyse their host; transposons multiply at the chromosome's
expense. Left unselected, foreign DNA decays into pseudogenes and is deleted,
and bacterial genomes are efficient at deleting. What survives is what paid for
itself, which means the accessory genome of any lineage is a fairly honest
record of the environments that lineage has recently had to cope with. Read that
way, a plasmid is not merely a vector of resistance but a document.

## What This Does to the Tree

Building phylogenies from ribosomal RNA gave microbiology its first credible map
of deep relationships and revealed the archaea as a domain distinct from
bacteria. But as whole genomes accumulated, it became clear that different genes
in the same organism frequently support different trees. If you reconstruct the
history of one gene you get one branching pattern; take the gene next door and
you may get another. Some researchers concluded that for prokaryotes the tree
metaphor is at best a statistical summary of a network, with a conserved core of
information-processing genes tracing something like vertical descent while
metabolic and defensive genes circulate laterally.

The disagreement about how radical this is has not fully settled, and it
matters, because the deepest events in the history of life involved wholesale
genome mergers. Mitochondria and chloroplasts began as free-living bacteria
engulfed by a host cell, and over time most of their genes migrated into the
nuclear genome, leaving the organelles with a token chromosome and a dependence
on proteins imported from outside. That transfer is still happening; plant and
animal nuclear genomes contain recognizable fragments of organellar DNA of
recent origin.

## Beyond the Microbial World

Eukaryotes transfer genes far less often than bacteria, but the exceptions are
instructive. Agrobacterium tumefaciens infects plants by injecting a segment of
its own plasmid into plant cells and integrating it into their chromosomes,
where it directs the host to build a tumor and manufacture compounds only the
bacterium can eat — an entirely natural cross-kingdom transfer that was later
turned into the standard tool of plant genetic engineering. Cultivated sweet
potato carries ancient Agrobacterium sequences acquired long before anyone
cultivated it. Pea aphids make their own carotenoids using genes that appear to
have come from a fungus, an ability otherwise almost unknown in animals. Various
plant-feeding insects and nematodes carry cell-wall-degrading enzymes of clear
bacterial ancestry.

For practical purposes, though, the important consequence remains the one the
Japanese physicians stumbled into. Antibiotic resistance does not have to evolve
in the pathogen that kills you. It can evolve anywhere — in a soil bacterium
defending itself against a fungal competitor, in a gut commensal exposed to a
fraction of a therapeutic dose, in the bacterial community of a river receiving
pharmaceutical runoff — and then be delivered, intact and already optimized,
into a pathogen that has never encountered the drug. The environmental pool of
resistance genes, the resistome, is enormous and considerably older than the
antibiotic era; resistance determinants have been recovered from permafrost
sediments far predating any clinical use.

This is why resistance appears so much faster than mutation rates alone would
predict, and why restricting a drug's use sometimes fails to reverse it. The
gene is not confined to the population you are treating. It sits on a mobile
element in a reservoir you cannot see, waiting for a cell to arrive that needs
it.
