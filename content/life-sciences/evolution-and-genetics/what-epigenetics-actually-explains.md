# What Epigenetics Actually Explains

Nearly every calico cat is female, and the reason is written into her fur. Two
of the genes that control coat color in cats sit on the X chromosome. A female
cat with one orange allele and one black allele has both instructions in every
one of her cells, and yet no individual hair is a blend. Each patch of orange is
a clonal descendant of an early embryonic cell that silenced the X carrying
black; each patch of black descends from a neighbor that silenced the other one.
The choice was made once, early, at random, and then held through every
subsequent division for the rest of the animal's life. The genome did not
change. What changed was which copy the cell was willing to read, and that
decision was inherited by daughter cells as reliably as the DNA sequence itself.

That is epigenetics in its useful, unglamorous sense: heritable information
carried by cells that is not encoded in the order of the bases. It is also,
increasingly, a word that gets stretched to carry claims it cannot support. The
gap between what the mechanisms demonstrably do and what they are popularly said
to do is wide enough to be worth mapping carefully.

## The Marks Themselves

Two molecular systems do most of the work, and they are physically
straightforward.

The first is DNA methylation: the addition of a methyl group to cytosine, almost
always where a cytosine sits immediately before a guanine in the sequence. Such
CpG sites are relatively scarce in vertebrate genomes but cluster in islands
near gene promoters. Heavy methylation of a promoter island typically means that
gene is off, either because methylation blocks the binding of activating factors
or because it recruits proteins that pull in repressive machinery. The elegance
is in the copying. Because the CpG dinucleotide is palindromic, both strands
carry a methylatable cytosine, and after replication the new duplex is
hemimethylated — old strand marked, new strand bare. A maintenance
methyltransferase recognizes exactly that asymmetry and restores the mark on the
new strand. The pattern propagates through cell division by the same logic that
propagates the sequence.

The second system is chemical modification of histones, the proteins around
which DNA is wound. Each nucleosome is a spool of roughly 147 base pairs coiled
around eight histone subunits, and the tails of those subunits protrude where
enzymes can reach them. Acetylation of lysines neutralizes their positive
charge, loosens the grip on DNA, and correlates with active transcription;
removing it tightens things up again. Methylation of histone tails is more
ambiguous and depends on the residue — one position marks actively transcribed
genes, another marks genes held in developmental reserve by the Polycomb system,
a third marks the dense heterochromatin of centromeres and silenced repeats.
These modifications recruit reader proteins, which recruit further writers, and
the whole arrangement is capable of spreading along a chromosome and
reestablishing itself after replication.

Long non-coding RNAs supply targeting. X inactivation in the calico cat is
orchestrated by an RNA transcribed from the chromosome that will be silenced; it
coats that chromosome in cis, recruits repressive complexes, and converts an
entire chromosome into the condensed body visible in the nuclei of female
mammals. One chromosome is shut down while its identical twin, a micron away in
the same nucleus, keeps working.

## What They Are Really For

The core job of this machinery is developmental memory. A liver cell and a
neuron in the same person carry the same genome and behave nothing alike, and
the difference is not merely which transcription factors happen to be present at
this instant — it is that each cell type has locked away the regions it will
never use and kept accessible the ones it needs. Without such locking,
differentiation would be unstable, and cells would drift between identities.

The lock is strong but not absolute, which was proved by taking the nucleus of a
differentiated frog cell, putting it into an enucleated egg, and getting a
tadpole. Dolly the sheep made the same point with a mammal. Later work showed
that a small set of transcription factors, forced into an adult cell, can drive
it backward to a pluripotent state. That result is often described as erasing
epigenetic marks, and in a sense it does, but the mechanism is worth noting: the
factors came first, and the chromatin followed. This ordering recurs throughout
the field and is the main reason for caution about the strong version of
epigenetic claims. Chromatin marks are frequently downstream of the regulatory
proteins that establish them rather than an independent layer issuing orders.

Genomic imprinting is the clearest case where the mark is the message. A small
number of mammalian genes are expressed only from the maternal copy or only from
the paternal copy, tagged by methylation laid down in the germ line according to
which parent produced the gamete. Delete a region of one chromosome and the
consequences depend entirely on whose chromosome it was: the same missing
segment produces two clinically distinct syndromes depending on parental origin.
Sequence identical, outcome different.

A second clean case is the way plants remember winter. Many species will not
flower until they have experienced prolonged cold, which prevents them from
blooming during a mild spell in autumn. The memory is held at a single floral
repressor gene. Sustained low temperature causes Polycomb complexes to
accumulate at that locus and progressively silence it, nucleating at one region
and spreading; the silencing is quantitative, deepening with the length of the
cold, and once established it persists through subsequent cell divisions in the
warmth of spring so that the plant flowers. The state is then reset in the next
generation, and the seedling has to earn its own winter. A cell has recorded the
duration of an external condition in the chromatin of one gene and carried the
record forward for weeks after the condition ended.

## The Environment Question

Because methylation responds to cellular conditions, it is natural to ask
whether the environment writes durable marks — and it does, at least sometimes.
The agouti mouse offers the tidiest demonstration. A particular strain carries a
transposable element upstream of a coat-color gene, and the methylation state of
that element varies between genetically identical individuals, producing a range
from yellow and obese to brown and lean. Supplementing a pregnant female's diet
with methyl donors shifts her offspring's distribution toward the brown end.
Diet, methylation, phenotype, all in one experiment.

Human evidence is thinner and harder to interpret. Individuals conceived during
the Dutch famine of the last winter of the Second World War, whose mothers
endured severe caloric restriction while pregnant, show small but measurable
differences in methylation at certain loci decades afterward, alongside
differences in metabolic health. The correlation is real. Whether the
methylation difference causes the health difference, or merely accompanies it as
a marker of some other developmental change, is not established by the data.

Methylation has also turned out to track chronological age so closely that
composite measures across a few hundred sites predict a person's age from a
blood or tissue sample with considerable accuracy, and deviations from that
prediction correlate modestly with health outcomes. These clocks are useful
instruments. They are not evidence that methylation drives aging; a clock face
is not a mainspring.

Social insects supply a third, more contested example. A honeybee larva fed
royal jelly develops into a queen — long-lived, fertile, physically larger —
while its sisters on a plainer diet become sterile workers, from identical
genomes. Knocking down the enzyme responsible for de novo DNA methylation in
larvae pushes a larger fraction toward the queen phenotype, which implicates
methylation somewhere in the pathway. What it does not establish is that
methylation is the switch rather than a consequence of the nutritional and
hormonal signaling that constitutes the switch. Distinguishing those two
possibilities is the recurring difficulty of the whole subject, and the reason
so many striking correlations remain hard to convert into mechanism.

## The Limits of Inheritance

The claim that generates the most enthusiasm — that your grandmother's
experiences are chemically inscribed in you — is where the evidence gets
thinnest for mammals, and the obstacle is mechanical. Between generations,
mammalian genomes are stripped twice. Shortly after fertilization most
methylation is erased; later, in the primordial germ cells of the developing
embryo, what remains is erased again, more thoroughly. Imprinted loci and some
repeat elements escape. Most other marks do not survive.

Plants have no such barrier. They set aside no early germ line, growing
reproductive tissue from meristems that have been dividing all along, and they
do not conduct a wholesale reprogramming. Stable epialleles are consequently
ordinary in plants. Linnaeus described a toadflax with radially symmetric
flowers instead of the usual bilateral ones and thought he had found a new
genus; the variant turns out to be a methylation change at a single
flower-patterning gene, transmissible for generations and occasionally
reverting. Paramutation in maize, where one allele durably alters the expression
of its partner and the altered state is passed on, has been studied for decades.
In nematodes, RNA-based silencing can persist for a number of generations
through small RNAs carried in the germ line.

None of that establishes the mammalian case. Reports of transgenerational effects
in mice and rats exist, some of them carefully done, but the field has struggled
with replication and with ruling out simpler explanations — effects transmitted
through maternal physiology, through the microbiome, through behavior, or
through direct exposure of the fetal germ cells, which means an effect seen in
grandchildren must be traced to the great-grandchildren before it counts as
truly transgenerational.

Where epigenetic mechanisms have proved indispensable is in medicine's less
romantic corners. Cancer cells display characteristic derangements — broad loss
of methylation across the genome alongside dense hypermethylation that silences
tumor suppressors — and drugs that inhibit methyltransferases or histone
deacetylases have genuine clinical uses in certain leukemias and lymphomas.
Diagnostic tests read methylation patterns to identify the tissue that a tumor
came from. This is the field doing real work, on the timescale it actually
governs: the life of a cell lineage, not the moral history of a family.
