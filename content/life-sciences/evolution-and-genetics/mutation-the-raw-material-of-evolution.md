# Mutation, the Raw Material of Evolution

Every child is born with a few dozen mutations that neither parent carried.
Somewhere in the production of the sperm and the egg, in the copying of three
billion base pairs, the machinery slipped — a base misread, a repeat expanded,
a lesion left unrepaired — and the result was written permanently into a new
genome. Most of these changes do nothing detectable. A handful sit in regions
where they matter. Occasionally one is the reason a person has a disease their
family has never seen before. And in aggregate, across a species, across
millions of years, this dribble of copying errors is the sole original source of
every heritable difference that selection has ever had to work with.

That last point is worth sitting with, because it is easy to talk about
evolution as though natural selection were the creative force. It is not.
Selection is a filter. It can only sort what already exists, and what exists
arrives by mutation, recombination shuffling mutations into new combinations,
and gene flow moving them between populations. Take mutation away and a
population's variation would be ground down within a few thousand generations
into uniformity, after which nothing further could happen no matter how intense
the selective pressure. Adaptation runs on error.

## The Shapes an Error Can Take

The smallest mutations swap one base for another. Biologists distinguish
transitions, which exchange a purine for a purine or a pyrimidine for a
pyrimidine, from transversions, which cross between the two classes.
Transitions happen several times more often, partly because the chemistry of
mispairing favors them and partly because of one specific and very common
accident: cytosine that has been chemically tagged with a methyl group, as much
of it is in vertebrate genomes, deaminates spontaneously into thymine. The repair
enzymes are good but not perfect, and the sites where a cytosine sits next to a
guanine are consequently mutational hotspots, eroding measurably over
evolutionary time.

Within a protein-coding gene, a substitution can be silent, changing a codon
into a synonym and leaving the protein untouched. It can be missense, swapping
one amino acid for another, with consequences ranging from nothing at all to
sickle-cell disease. It can be nonsense, converting a codon into a stop signal
and truncating the protein. Insertions and deletions are cruder. If the number
of bases added or removed is not a multiple of three, everything downstream is
read in the wrong frame and the protein is usually ruined outright.

Then there are the larger rearrangements, which matter more for evolutionary
novelty than the point mutations do. Chunks of chromosome get duplicated,
inverted, or moved. Whole genes are copied, sometimes by unequal crossing over
between misaligned repeats, sometimes by an RNA transcript being reverse
transcribed and inserted back into the genome. Entire genomes double, which in
animals is usually lethal but in plants is so routine that a large fraction of
flowering plant species are polyploid — bread wheat carries three complete
ancestral genomes at once. Transposable elements, the mobile sequences that make
up something close to half of the human genome, copy and paste themselves around
and occasionally land inside a gene or drag a regulatory sequence somewhere new.

Gene duplication deserves particular attention because it solves a problem that
point mutation alone cannot. A gene doing a job is constrained; mutations that
break the job get removed. But if there are two copies, one can keep doing the
old job while the other drifts, accumulating changes that would have been
intolerable before. Most redundant copies simply decay into pseudogenes. A
minority acquire a new function, or split the ancestral function between them.
The globin genes that carry oxygen in vertebrate blood are a family produced
this way, with distinct members expressed in the embryo, the fetus, and the
adult. The opsins that give primates color vision are another, which is why the
red and green pigments sit adjacent on the X chromosome and misalign often
enough to produce common forms of color blindness.

## Random With Respect to Fitness

The word "random" does a lot of work in descriptions of mutation, and it is
regularly misunderstood in both directions. Mutation is not uniform. Some
sequences mutate far more often than others; some kinds of change are far more
likely than others; the rate varies across the genome, between the sexes, with
parental age, and between species. Fathers contribute more new mutations than
mothers, and more of them the older they are, because sperm precursors keep
dividing throughout life while a woman's oocytes do not.

What randomness means here is narrower and more important: mutations do not
arise because they would be useful. The environment does not induce the specific
change that would help. This was not obvious, and it was settled experimentally
in the 1940s. Salvador Luria and Max Delbrück reasoned that if bacteria acquired
resistance to a virus only in response to encountering it, then parallel cultures
plated after exposure should each yield a similar small number of resistant
colonies, as in any process with a constant per-cell probability. If instead
resistance arose from mutations occurring at random before exposure, the counts
would vary wildly between cultures, because a mutation early in a culture's
growth produces a large clone of resistant descendants and a late one produces a
few. The counts varied wildly. A few years later Joshua and Esther Lederberg
made the point more directly with replica plating, showing that resistant
colonies could be located on a plate that had never met the selective agent at
all.

This is also the cleanest answer to a persistent intuition that evolution ought
to be able to anticipate. Bacteria in a patient do not sense an antibiotic and
manufacture a defense. A population of billions simply already contains, by
accident, a few cells with a mutation in the drug's target — a change in the
RNA polymerase subunit that rifampicin binds, say — and the drug does the rest
of the work by killing everything else. The speed of resistance evolution
reflects population size and generation time, not foresight.

## Damage Is Not Yet a Mutation

It helps to separate two things that get conflated. DNA takes chemical abuse
constantly: ultraviolet light fuses adjacent pyrimidines into dimers that kink
the helix, oxygen radicals from ordinary metabolism convert guanine into a form
that pairs wrongly, spontaneous hydrolysis knocks bases off the sugar backbone
thousands of times a day in every cell, and compounds in smoke and charred food
bind covalently to bases and distort them. None of that is a mutation yet. It is
damage, and damage is recognized and excised by a large repair apparatus that
cuts out the offending stretch and rebuilds it against the intact
complementary strand — which is one more thing the double helix's redundancy
buys.

A mutation is what happens when damage is either missed or repaired wrongly, or
when the cell, faced with a lesion it cannot fix before replication must
proceed, calls in a specialized polymerase that can copy past the obstruction at
the cost of guessing. That trade — a sloppy copy instead of a stalled fork — is
where a substantial share of mutations in damaged cells actually comes from.
People who inherit defects in these repair pathways show what the baseline
protects against: those with a broken nucleotide excision repair system develop
skin cancers in childhood from sun exposure that would be unremarkable for
anyone else.

The practical upshot is that a mutagen does not usually act by writing a
specific change into DNA. It raises the amount of damage, saturating repair and
increasing the frequency with which the guessing machinery is invoked. The
spectrum of resulting mutations carries a signature of the agent — ultraviolet
exposure leaves a characteristic pattern of changes at adjacent pyrimidines,
tobacco smoke leaves another — and tumor genomes can be read backward to
identify which processes have been operating in a tissue over a lifetime.

## Why the Rate Is What It Is

Fidelity is expensive. DNA polymerases select the correct base, then proofread
with an exonuclease that excises mismatches, and mismatch repair sweeps through
afterward to catch survivors. Each stage improves accuracy by orders of
magnitude, and together they bring the error rate in a human germline down to
something on the order of one mutation per hundred million bases per generation.
It could be lower. Some organisms manage better. The reason it does not go to
zero is that proofreading costs time and energy, and selection on fidelity
weakens once mutations become rare enough that most individuals carry no new
deleterious one in any particular gene.

There is an additional wrinkle that makes mutation rate itself an evolving
trait. An allele that raises the mutation rate produces extra deleterious
mutations, which drag it down, but it also produces the occasional beneficial
one, and in an asexual population it stays linked to that beneficial mutation
and hitchhikes along with it. Mutator strains with broken mismatch repair turn
up at surprisingly high frequency in natural bacterial populations and in
long-running laboratory evolution experiments, rising when a population is
poorly adapted and often being displaced later. Sexual reproduction breaks that
linkage, which is one reason mutator alleles do not sweep through sexual species
the same way.

Sex matters here in another respect. In an asexual lineage, deleterious
mutations accumulate and cannot be shed: once the least-loaded class of
individuals is lost by chance, no recombination can reconstruct it, and the
lineage ratchets irreversibly downhill. Recombination lets a sexual population
purge damage and combine separately arising beneficial mutations into the same
genome rather than forcing them to compete. Two useful mutations that appear in
different individuals of an asexual population are rivals; in a sexual one they
can meet.

## The Long Odds, Repeated Constantly

The overwhelming majority of mutations are neutral or harmful. Estimates of the
fraction that are beneficial are small enough that any particular new mutation
is a bad bet. But a bacterial culture in a flask contains more individuals than
there are people on Earth, and turns over in hours. A population of a million
insects, each carrying a handful of new mutations, samples a considerable slice
of the possible single-step changes to its genome every generation. Rarity per
event and abundance of events are not in conflict.

What emerges from this is a picture of evolution with two quite different
temperaments in it. Mutation is blind, profligate, and indifferent — it throws
variants outward with no regard for whether anything needs them. Selection is
relentlessly discriminating, and retains almost nothing. The apparent
purposefulness of a wing or an enzyme comes entirely from the asymmetry between
those two processes running side by side for a very long time.
