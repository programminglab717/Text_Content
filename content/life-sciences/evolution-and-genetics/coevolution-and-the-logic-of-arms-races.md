# Coevolution and the Logic of Arms Races

A rough-skinned newt is a slow, soft, conspicuous animal that walks across open
ground in daylight in the wet forests of the Pacific Northwest, and when
threatened it arches its back to display a bright orange belly rather than
running. It can afford the advertisement. Its skin holds tetrodotoxin, the same
molecule that makes pufferfish dangerous, which plugs the voltage-gated sodium
channels that nerves and muscles use to fire. A single newt in some populations
carries enough to kill a great many adult humans, which is a strange amount of
poison for an amphibian that eats worms.

The reason it carries so much is crawling through the same leaf litter. Common
garter snakes in parts of that range eat the newts and survive, because
substitutions in the gene for their own sodium channel have reshaped the pocket
where tetrodotoxin binds, so that the toxin no longer fits properly. Newts with
more poison are eaten less; snakes with more resistance eat better; and in the
places where both have been escalating longest, the newts are extraordinarily
toxic and the snakes can swallow them whole and crawl away.

This is the cleanest illustration biologists have of reciprocal
selection — two lineages each acting as the other's environment, each
responding to changes the other made. It also demonstrates most of the reasons
such races do not simply accelerate forever.

## Why the Race Has Brakes

The first brake is cost. The snake's altered sodium channel works less well as a
sodium channel. Resistant snakes crawl more slowly than their susceptible
relatives, which matters to an animal that escapes predators by moving. A
resistant snake in a population with no toxic newts is simply a slower snake. So
resistance is favored only where the prey is dangerous, and penalized where it
is not, and the trait cannot ratchet upward independently of the local ecology.

The second brake is asymmetry of stakes, sometimes put as the difference between
running for your dinner and running for your life. A fox that fails to catch a
rabbit loses a meal; a rabbit that fails to escape loses everything. Selection
therefore presses harder on the prey, and the two sides are not investing
equally. This keeps many predator-prey relationships lopsided rather than
locked in symmetric escalation.

The third and most general brake is geography. Species are not uniform; they are
patchworks of populations experiencing different conditions. In some valleys the
newts are barely toxic and the snakes barely resistant, because something else
limits both. In others the interaction is intense. Gene flow between such
patches drags each population away from its local optimum, and the result is a
shifting mosaic of hotspots and coldspots rather than a single species-wide
trajectory. Once you start looking for this pattern you find it everywhere. The
crossbills of the Rocky Mountains have bill shapes matched to the cones of local
pines, but only on isolated ranges where red squirrels are absent; where
squirrels are present they, not the birds, are the dominant seed predator, the
cones evolve in response to squirrels instead, and the crossbills are left
maladapted to a target that was never aimed at them.

## Deception, Detection, and the Cuckoo

Not every arms race is about chemistry. Some are about information.

A common cuckoo lays a single egg in the nest of a much smaller songbird, and
the hatchling levers the host's own eggs over the rim before settling in to be
fed by parents a fraction of its eventual size. The cost to the host is total
reproductive failure, so selection on hosts to detect the intrusion is about as
strong as selection gets. Many parasitized species have become skilled at it:
present them with a foreign egg and they will puncture it, eject it, or abandon
the nest.

The cuckoo's counter is mimicry. Female cuckoos belong to lines specializing on
particular host species, and each line lays eggs matching that host's — the
blue of a redstart's clutch, the speckling of a meadow pipit's — a trick made
possible because egg coloration in birds is influenced by genes on the female
sex chromosome, allowing host-specific lines to persist within one
interbreeding species. Hosts respond by making their own eggs harder to copy.
Some African species targeted by parasitic finches have evolved individual egg
signatures, with distinctive colors and scribbled markings that vary sharply
between females of the same species, so that a parasite cannot match the
population average and succeed. A female recognizes her own pattern and rejects
anything that departs from it.

The arms race is legible in where each pair currently sits. Host species with a
long history of parasitism are discriminating and their parasites are excellent
mimics. Species that have only recently come under attack accept almost
anything. And some hosts that escaped parasitism long ago have let their
defenses lapse, which is why an old relationship and a brand-new one can look
deceptively similar.

## Night Combat

The most elaborate sensory arms race runs in the dark above our heads every
summer. Bats hunting by echolocation shout in ultrasound and listen for the
returning echo, and the moths they hunt have responded by growing ears — simple
ones, sometimes a single membrane with one or two sensory cells attached, which
is all that is required to detect a loud pulse in an otherwise quiet band. Such
ears have appeared independently many times in unrelated moth and butterfly
lineages, always tuned to the frequencies bats use, and almost always in groups
that postdate the origin of echolocation. A moth that hears a distant bat turns
away; one that hears a close bat folds its wings and drops.

The counters accumulate on both sides. Some moths jam the incoming sonar with
rapid trains of their own clicks that degrade the bat's ranging. Others click to
advertise that they are chemically defended, and still others mimic that signal
without the defense. Some large silk moths trail long twisted hindwing tails
that throw back a misleading echo, so that the bat aims for a streamer and the
moth escapes with a torn wing. Bats in turn have shifted their calls to
frequencies outside moth hearing, or gone quiet, approaching with echolocation
pulses so faint that the prey does not register them until too late.

Chemical versions of the same escalation are everywhere in plants and insects.
Milkweeds defend themselves with cardenolides, compounds that poison the sodium-
potassium pump every animal cell depends on. Monarch caterpillars eat milkweed
anyway, because a handful of substitutions in their own pump makes it
insensitive — and the same substitutions, at the same positions, have arisen
independently in beetles, bugs and aphids that feed on the same plants. The
number of ways to solve the problem is small, so unrelated insects keep finding
the identical one.

## Cycling Rather Than Climbing

Escalation is the intuitive picture, but much coevolution goes in circles.

When a parasite must match a host's molecular machinery to infect it, the
advantage belongs to whatever host genotype is currently rare, because parasites
are adapted to the common ones. Rare types therefore increase; as they become
common, parasites adapt to them; and they decline again. Nothing improves in any
absolute sense. Both parties run to stay in place, which is the image that gave
the Red Queen hypothesis its name, borrowed from the chess landscape Alice walks
through.

This cycling is one of the better explanations for why sex persists despite its
obvious inefficiency. A freshwater snail in New Zealand comes in both sexual and
asexual forms, infected by a trematode that sterilizes it, and the proportion of
sexual individuals tracks the local intensity of infection: where parasites are
abundant, sex is common. Clonal lineages do well for a while and then get caught
by parasites that have matched them, while sexual reproduction keeps shuffling
genotypes faster than the parasites can track. The same logic operates at a
molecular level in the immune system, where the genes of the major
histocompatibility complex are among the most variable in vertebrates precisely
because rarity is an advantage.

Bacteria and their viruses run the fastest version of this. In a flask, a
bacterial population and a phage population will coevolve through repeated
rounds of resistance and counter-resistance over days, with bacteria altering or
losing the surface receptors the phage docks onto and acquiring CRISPR spacers
matching the phage genome, while phages mutate to evade the spacers.

## The Same Machinery Builds Mutualism

Reciprocal selection is not inherently antagonistic; the same feedback that
sharpens a toxin can deepen a partnership. Darwin examined a Madagascan orchid
whose nectar sits at the bottom of a spur nearly a foot long and concluded there
must be a moth with a tongue to match. The moth was found decades later, after
his death. Hummingbirds and the flowers they service show the same reciprocal
elongation, most extravagantly in a South American hummingbird whose bill is
longer than the rest of its body and which feeds at passionflowers built to the
same specification.

Mutualisms still require enforcement, because a partner that takes without
giving does better than one that reciprocates. Yuccas are pollinated only by
moths that deliberately pack pollen onto a flower's stigma and then lay eggs in
the developing ovary, where the larvae eat some of the seeds. A moth that lays
too many eggs would consume the whole crop, so the plant selectively aborts
flowers carrying excessive egg loads, destroying the offspring of overexploiting
moths. Figs discipline their wasps by similar means. Cooperation between species
is generally held in place not by good will but by the cost of defection.

Over long spans, escalation does leave a physical record. Through the later
Mesozoic, as crabs, lobsters and shell-crushing fish diversified, the shells of
marine snails and bivalves became thicker, more tightly coiled, more heavily
ribbed and spined, with narrowed apertures that are harder to pry open. Drilling
predators appear, and so do repaired drill holes and healed breaks that record
failed attacks. The whole shallow-marine fauna was rebuilt around the fact that
something had learned to open it.
