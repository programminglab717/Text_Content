# Sexual Selection and the Peacock's Tail

Darwin once wrote to a friend that the sight of a feather in a peacock's tail
made him feel sick. He was not being precious. A train of iridescent feathers a
meter and a half long, dragged behind a bird that cannot fly well and lives
where leopards do, is difficult to reconcile with a theory in which traits
persist because they help their bearers survive. Every argument he had built
about the sculpting power of survival pointed the other way. The peacock should
have been quietly edited out of existence long ago.

His answer, developed at length in *The Descent of Man* in 1871, was that
survival is not the currency that matters. Reproduction is. A trait that shortens
its owner's life but more than compensates by increasing the number of offspring
produced will spread anyway, and the two effects can run in opposite directions
for a long time. He called the process sexual selection, and he identified two
routes by which it works: competition among members of one sex for access to the
other, and choice exercised by members of the other sex among available
partners.

## Two Mechanisms, One Controversial

The first route was easy for Victorian naturalists to accept, because it looked
like ordinary competition with ordinary weapons. Red deer stags roar at each
other through the autumn, assess each other's size and stamina, and if neither
backs down, lock antlers and shove. Male elephant seals arrive at a breeding
beach weighing several times what the females weigh and fight until a small
number of them monopolize most of the matings. Stag beetles have mandibles too
large to be much use for eating. Nothing here is puzzling. Bigger, better armed
males leave more offspring, and the traits escalate until the cost of growing
them balances the advantage.

The second route caused a fight that lasted more than a century. Darwin proposed
that female birds have aesthetic preferences — that they look at the males on
offer and choose — and that those preferences, applied generation after
generation, are what built the peacock's train, the bird of paradise's plumes,
and the extravagant songs of so many small brown birds. To many of his
contemporaries this was unacceptable. It attributed a faculty of taste to
animals, it made the female the active agent, and it seemed to leave the outcome
of evolution dependent on caprice. Alfred Russel Wallace, who had arrived at
natural selection independently and agreed with Darwin about nearly everything
else, rejected female choice for the rest of his life, arguing that bright male
coloring was a byproduct of vigor and that the real evolutionary action was in
female drabness as camouflage.

The dispute went cold rather than being resolved. For decades, male combat
remained respectable and female choice was treated as a quaint Victorian
embarrassment, mentioned in textbooks as an idea Darwin had once entertained.

## Why the Sexes Differ in the First Place

The modern rehabilitation of the idea rests on a piece of logic that Darwin did
not have. It starts with gametes. In almost every sexually reproducing lineage,
one sex produces small, cheap, mobile gametes in enormous numbers and the other
produces large, expensive, provisioned ones in small numbers. That asymmetry —
anisogamy — is what defines male and female, and it has consequences that ripple
all the way up to behavior.

Because eggs are costly and sperm are not, a female's reproductive output is
limited by the resources she can convert into offspring, while a male's is
limited mainly by how many females he can inseminate. Add any further asymmetry
in parental investment — gestation, lactation, incubation, feeding of young —
and the divergence widens. The sex that invests more becomes the limiting
resource, and the sex that invests less competes for access to it. Robert
Trivers laid this out explicitly in the early 1970s, building on earlier
experimental work by Angus Bateman with fruit flies suggesting that male
reproductive success varied much more widely than female success did. Bateman's
specific results have been re-examined and criticized since, but the general
framework held: variance in reproductive success is what sexual selection feeds
on, and it is usually higher in males.

This also explains the exceptions, which are the strongest evidence that the
framework is right rather than merely being a rationalization of familiar
patterns. In pipefish and seahorses, the male carries the brood, and the
competition and the ornamentation are reversed: females are larger and more
brightly marked, and they compete for males. In several shorebird species where
the male incubates, females are the bigger and more aggressive sex. The rule is
not about maleness. It is about who is scarce.

## What Choosy Females Get

Granting that choice happens leaves the harder question of why any preference
would be maintained, given that preferring an inconvenient trait imposes costs.
Several answers coexist, and they are not mutually exclusive.

R. A. Fisher proposed the strangest and in some ways the most elegant. Suppose a
preference exists for some reason — any reason — and a trait exists that
satisfies it. Females who choose ornamented males have sons who are ornamented
and therefore attractive, and daughters who carry the preference. Trait and
preference become genetically correlated, and each now selects for the other. The
process feeds on itself, driving the ornament well past any point justified by
the male's underlying quality, until viability costs finally halt it. Runaway
selection of this kind can start from almost nothing and explains why ornaments
are often arbitrary and why closely related species diverge so wildly in
display.

Amotz Zahavi offered a rival account that sounded paradoxical when he proposed
it in the 1970s. The cost of the ornament is the point. A signal is only
believable if it cannot be faked, and an extravagant handicap can only be borne
by a male in genuinely good condition — free of parasites, well fed, developing
without stress. A scrawny bird cannot grow a perfect train. Choosing the most
ornamented male therefore delivers either good genes for the offspring or a
mate likely to survive the season, and the honesty of the signal is enforced by
its expense. Formal models eventually showed that this works under specifiable
conditions, and the handicap principle went from ridiculed to standard.

A third possibility is that preferences are not about males at all. Sensory
systems are tuned by the demands of finding food and avoiding predators, and a
display that happens to stimulate an existing bias will be attractive without
any prior history of selection on the preference. Where a preference for a trait
can be demonstrated in species that lack the trait, sensory bias is the most
economical explanation, and the ornament is the thing that evolved to exploit a
pre-existing taste.

The experimental turning point for the whole field came from work on African
widowbirds, whose males grow tails many times their body length. Malte Andersson
cut tails from some males and glued the cuttings onto others, producing
artificially elongated and artificially shortened birds along with
appropriately handled controls. The lengthened males attracted markedly more
females to nest in their territories. Territory quality and fighting ability had
not changed; only the ornament had. Female choice was doing measurable work in a
wild population, and once that was established the results multiplied — in
guppies, in frogs, in sticklebacks, in fiddler crabs waving oversized claws on a
mudflat.

## Beyond Choosing

Sexual selection does not stop when mating begins. In species where females mate
with more than one male, sperm from different males compete inside her
reproductive tract, and selection acts on ejaculate volume, sperm morphology,
seminal chemistry, and structures for displacing a rival's contribution.
Testis size across primates tracks the mating system closely, with the largest
relative to body size in species where females mate promiscuously.

What follows from this is less comfortable than a story about beauty. The
interests of the sexes do not coincide. A male's optimum number of matings is
rarely a female's, and traits that increase male success at her expense can
still spread. Male water striders have evolved grasping structures and females
counter-structures for dislodging them, an arms race with no obvious endpoint.
Male fruit flies transfer seminal proteins that boost short-term egg laying and
shorten female lifespan. Bed bugs bypass the female genital tract entirely,
puncturing the abdominal wall to inseminate. Sexual conflict of this kind
produces rapid, escalating divergence in reproductive anatomy between
populations, and it may contribute to the formation of new species as
independently escalating lineages become incompatible.

One puzzle has never quite gone away. If females have been choosing the best
males for thousands of generations, the genetic variance in male quality ought
to be exhausted, leaving nothing left to choose between and no reason to keep
paying the cost of choosiness. Yet variation persists and preferences persist.
Mutation constantly restores some variance; parasites evolving on their own
schedule keep shifting which genotypes are healthy; condition depends on
hundreds of loci, so there is always a large target for new mutations. The lek
paradox is less a hole in the theory than a reminder that the populations these
displays evolved in are never at rest.
