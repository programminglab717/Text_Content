# A Field Guide to the Cytoskeleton

Cut open a cell and look for its skeleton, and you will not find anything resembling bone. What you find instead is a dense, restless network of protein fibers running through the cytoplasm, constantly assembling at one end while disassembling at the other, hauling cargo along their length, and yanking whole chromosomes apart when the time comes to divide. Unlike a vertebrate skeleton, which is built once and then mostly maintained, the cytoskeleton is rebuilt continuously, on a timescale of minutes, and a cell that could not do this would be unable to change shape, move, divide, or transport anything internally. This is a guide to its three main fiber types, what distinguishes them, and what each one is actually for.

## Three Kinds of Fiber

Biologists sort cytoskeletal filaments into three broad categories based on their thickness and the protein subunits they are built from. Microfilaments, the thinnest of the three, are built from a globular protein called actin that polymerizes into long, twisted double helices. Microtubules, the thickest, are hollow tubes built from a different protein called tubulin, arranged in rows around a central lumen like staves around a barrel. Intermediate filaments, as their name suggests, fall in between the other two in diameter, but they are built from a much more varied set of proteins depending on the cell type—keratins in skin cells, for instance, are intermediate filaments, and so are the proteins that reinforce the nuclear envelope from inside.

That last point matters: intermediate filaments are not built from a single universal protein the way microfilaments and microtubules are. They are a structural category defined by size and behavior rather than by one shared building block, which reflects a genuinely different evolutionary and functional role. Where microfilaments and microtubules are primarily involved in generating force and movement, intermediate filaments are built mainly for mechanical resilience—for taking a hit without tearing.

## The Actin Filaments: Cables for Shape and Crawling

Actin filaments concentrate most heavily just beneath the cell's outer membrane, in a mesh called the cell cortex, where they give the cell surface its shape and mechanical strength. This is not a static shape, either. Actin filaments grow by adding subunits at one end, called the barbed end, faster than they lose subunits from the opposite, pointed end, and a cell can exploit this asymmetric growth to push its own membrane forward. A crawling white blood cell chasing down a bacterium does exactly this at its leading edge, extending thin, sheet-like protrusions called lamellipodia and finger-like ones called filopodia, both built from rapidly polymerizing actin pushing the membrane outward from underneath.

Actin also partners with a motor protein called myosin, the same family of proteins responsible for muscle contraction, though the partnership shows up far beyond muscle tissue. In an ordinary dividing cell, a ring of actin and myosin assembles around the equator of the cell late in division and contracts like a purse string, physically pinching the cell in two. In muscle cells specifically, the arrangement is far more elaborate and highly ordered, with actin and myosin filaments interleaved in a repeating pattern that allows coordinated, powerful contraction across an entire tissue, but the underlying chemistry—myosin heads walking along actin filaments, powered by the same energy currency that fuels most of the cell's work—is a variation on a very old theme.

## Microtubules: Rails and Rigid Struts

Where actin filaments favor the cell periphery, microtubules typically radiate outward from an organizing center near the nucleus, extending toward the cell's outer edges like the spokes of a wheel, though far less regularly arranged than that image suggests. Their hollow, tube-like structure makes them considerably stiffer than actin filaments of similar length, well suited to resisting compression and maintaining the overall shape and internal organization of a cell, especially in cell types with long, thin extensions that need internal reinforcement, such as neurons.

Microtubules also serve as the tracks along which much of a cell's internal cargo travels. Vesicles, organelles, and even mitochondria get physically dragged from one part of the cell to another along microtubule tracks rather than drifting there by chance diffusion, which would be hopelessly slow and undirected across the scale of a large cell. This directed transport becomes especially critical in neurons, where a microtubule track running down the length of an axon may need to move cargo over distances that, relative to the cell's own diameter, are staggeringly long.

Microtubules take on perhaps their most dramatic role during cell division, when the organizing centers duplicate, move to opposite ends of the cell, and each begins nucleating a radiating array of microtubules that together form the mitotic spindle. Some of these microtubules reach out and physically attach to the chromosomes; others interact with microtubules growing from the opposite pole. When the moment comes, the spindle apparatus captures every chromosome, aligns them, and then reels them apart to opposite ends of the dividing cell—a process that depends entirely on microtubules being able to grow, shrink, and generate pulling force with real precision.

## Intermediate Filaments: Tension Cables

If microtubules resist compression and actin filaments generate force and movement, intermediate filaments do something more modest but equally essential: they resist being pulled apart. Unlike the other two fiber types, intermediate filaments do not typically disassemble and reassemble on short timescales, and they are not directly involved in generating movement. Instead they form durable, rope-like networks that spread mechanical stress across a cell or a tissue, absorbing tension rather than transmitting force.

This is why intermediate filaments show up so heavily in tissues that take a beating. Skin cells are packed with keratin filaments that give the outer layers of skin their toughness and resistance to abrasion. A separate class of intermediate filaments forms a mesh lining the inner face of the nuclear envelope, providing structural support to the nucleus itself and helping organize the chromatin within it. Mutations affecting intermediate filament proteins tend to produce diseases with a distinctly mechanical character—skin that blisters under mild friction, or muscle that tears more easily than it should—consistent with a structural role focused on withstanding physical stress rather than generating it.

## Motors on the Rails

None of these fibers would be nearly as useful without the specialized motor proteins that use them as infrastructure. Along microtubules, two major families of motor proteins do most of the work: kinesins, which generally walk toward the outer edge of the cell, and dyneins, which generally walk in the opposite direction, back toward the organizing center near the nucleus. Each motor protein has a pair of leg-like domains that alternately bind and release the microtubule surface, taking directional steps roughly the length of a single tubulin subunit, powered at each step by breaking down a molecule of ATP.

A vesicle destined for the cell membrane, loaded with kinesin motors, gets walked outward along a microtubule until it reaches its destination. A used-up organelle marked for recycling might instead get grabbed by dynein and hauled inward toward the compartments that handle degradation. Actin filaments have their own dedicated motor, myosin, in forms adapted for cargo transport over shorter distances as well as the contractile forms already mentioned. The overall picture is of a cell that behaves less like a bag of chemicals sloshing around by chance and considerably more like a small, wired city, with dedicated roads, directional traffic, and motors built specifically to move the right cargo to the right place.

## A Structure That Rebuilds Itself Constantly

The single strangest feature of the cytoskeleton, compared to any skeleton built from bone or chitin, is how impermanent it is. Actin filaments and microtubules exist in a state of continuous turnover, subunits added at one end and removed at the other, or entire filaments disassembling and their components recycled into new filaments forming somewhere else in the cell, often within a matter of minutes. A cell changing shape, crawling toward a chemical signal, or preparing to divide does not build a new set of scaffolding tools from scratch. It reorganizes the tools it already has, tearing down filaments in one location while building them in another, guided by regulatory proteins that respond to signals arriving at the cell surface. That capacity for rapid, controlled rebuilding is arguably the entire point. A truly rigid skeleton would make a cell strong but immobile. What the cytoskeleton provides instead is structure with the built-in ability to change its mind.
