# How Cities Became Ecosystems

A peregrine falcon does not naturally care whether the cliff it nests on is limestone or concrete. It wants height, an unobstructed approach for a stoop that can exceed two hundred miles an hour, and a reliable supply of pigeons or other birds to catch on the wing. A skyscraper ledge satisfies all three requirements about as well as any natural cliff face, which is a large part of why peregrine falcons, once seriously endangered across much of their range, now nest on tall buildings and bridges in cities around the world, hunting pigeons that are themselves descended from rock doves that took to ledges and eaves for exactly the same reason peregrines eventually followed them there. Neither species treats the city as an imitation of its real habitat. For both of them, at this point, the city simply is the habitat, structurally similar enough to what they evolved for that the distinction has stopped mattering much biologically.

This is roughly the argument at the center of urban ecology as a field: that a city is not the absence of an ecosystem, a place where ecology used to happen before humans paved it over, but an ecosystem in its own right, operating by recognizable ecological rules even though many of its physical parameters — temperature, hydrology, the availability of food and shelter — have been reshaped dramatically from whatever existed on the same ground before development.

## A Climate of Its Own Making

Cities generate their own local climate, and the effect is large enough to measure without much specialized equipment. Asphalt, concrete, and dark roofing absorb solar radiation during the day and release it slowly after dark, while the same materials in a nearby rural or forested area cool much faster once the sun sets, because vegetation and soil handle heat very differently, releasing much of the day's absorbed energy through evaporation rather than storing and re-radiating it after dark. The result is the urban heat island effect: city centers running measurably warmer than the surrounding countryside, especially at night, with the difference most pronounced on calm, clear evenings when there is little wind to mix urban and rural air together. This has consequences that ripple well beyond human comfort. Plants in urban cores frequently leaf out earlier in spring and hold their leaves later into fall than the same species growing just outside the city, effectively experiencing a longer growing season, and some insects and birds have shifted the timing of key life-cycle events to track this artificially warmed, artificially extended urban calendar.

Water moves differently through a city too, for equally physical reasons. Rain falling on a forest or a field soaks into soil, gets taken up by plant roots, or moves slowly through the ground toward a stream. Rain falling on a parking lot or a roof does none of that; it runs off immediately across an impervious surface, picking up oil, road salt, and whatever else has accumulated on the pavement, and reaches a storm drain and then a stream far faster and in a far more concentrated pulse than natural infiltration ever would. Urban streams downstream of heavily paved watersheds show a recognizable, well-documented set of symptoms as a result — flashier flooding, eroded and unstable channels, degraded water quality, and a shift toward a small number of pollution-tolerant species dominating what aquatic life remains. Ecologists have a specific name for this recurring pattern: urban stream syndrome, distinct enough in its symptoms and causes to be studied as its own phenomenon rather than just a vague catchall for "polluted city water."

## New Combinations With No Precedent

What lives successfully in this reshaped environment is rarely a simple, diminished subset of what lived there before development. It is often a genuinely novel combination: native species that happen to tolerate the new conditions, non-native species introduced deliberately as ornamental plantings or accidentally as stowaways, and species that were rare or absent in the pre-urban landscape but find something in the city's peculiar mix of resources and disturbance that suits them unusually well. Vacant lots and other neglected urban ground, often dismissed as ecological wastelands, frequently support a surprising diversity of insects and wildflowers precisely because they combine disturbed, weedy conditions with an absence of the mowing, spraying, or redevelopment that would otherwise reset them, producing plant communities with no obvious equivalent in the surrounding rural landscape. Ecologists increasingly describe assemblages like this as novel ecosystems, a deliberately neutral term acknowledging that they do not map onto any historical baseline and are not simply degraded versions of something that used to be there, even though they emerged entirely from human disturbance.

Animals adapt behaviorally as well as simply showing up where conditions happen to suit them. Birds in noisy urban environments have been documented singing at a higher pitch and greater volume than members of the same species in quieter rural settings, a shift that helps their songs cut through the low-frequency hum of traffic and machinery that would otherwise mask them, in what amounts to a real-time behavioral adjustment layered on top of, and much faster than, any genetic evolutionary change. Coyotes, raccoons, and foxes have adjusted their activity patterns to be more strictly nocturnal in cities than the same species are in areas with less human presence, avoiding the daytime hours when encountering a person is most likely even in places where they are in no particular danger from doing so. None of these are passive tolerances. They are active behavioral solutions to problems the pre-urban landscape never posed.

## Designing With the Ecosystem Rather Than Against It

Recognizing a city as an ecosystem rather than an ecological void has practical consequences for how cities get built and maintained. Green infrastructure — rain gardens that capture and slowly filter stormwater rather than routing it straight to a drain, green roofs that reduce both runoff and the heat-island effect at the same time, strips of native vegetation along streets and parking lots — attempts to reintroduce some of the water-handling and temperature-moderating functions a paved landscape lost, without pretending the underlying land use is going back to forest or prairie. Urban tree canopy, once treated mainly as a matter of aesthetics, is now understood to measurably reduce local temperatures, capture stormwater, and provide habitat corridors connecting isolated green patches, most useful specifically where placed with the underlying ecological function in mind rather than for looks alone.

None of this reintroduces anything like a pristine ecosystem, and urban ecologists are generally not trying to. The goal running through most of this work is closer to acknowledging what has already been true for as long as cities have existed dense enough to have their own climate and their own wildlife: the ecosystem was never absent. It was just running on a different set of physical rules than the landscape it replaced, and understanding those rules turns out to matter for a lot more than curiosity about the falcons overhead.
