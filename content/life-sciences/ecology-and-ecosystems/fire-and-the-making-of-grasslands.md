# Fire and the Making of Grasslands

For most of the year, a tallgrass prairie looks like the most flammable place imaginable and nothing happens to it. Grass cures to straw-gold by late summer, stands dense and dry through fall, and by the following spring, before new green growth pushes up through it, a single spark can carry flame across miles of it in an afternoon, driven by wind with almost nothing to stop it. This is not a flaw in the ecosystem. It is close to the central organizing fact of it. Grasslands and fire have been so tightly bound together for so long that trying to describe one without the other misses most of what makes either work the way it does.

## A Landscape Built to Burn and Regrow

The key to understanding why grasslands tolerate fire so well, rather than being destroyed by it the way a forest can be, sits underground. A grass plant keeps its growing points — the meristem tissue responsible for new growth — at or below the soil surface, protected by soil and by the base of the plant itself, rather than at the tips of exposed branches the way a tree does. Fire moves fast across a grassland, consuming dry standing biomass in a matter of seconds at any given point and rarely generating enough heat at ground level to damage what is sitting an inch or two underground. Within days of a burn, often before the char has finished cooling, new green shoots push up through blackened ground, drawing on root reserves built up over the previous growing season. Trees and shrubs trying to establish in the same grassland do not have this advantage. Their growing tips are up where the fire can reach them, and repeated burning kills back woody seedlings again and again before they can grow tall enough to survive the next one.

This is the mechanism by which fire maintains a grassland as a grassland rather than letting it succeed, the way abandoned farmland does, into shrubland and eventually forest. Remove fire from a fire-adapted grassland and, in most climates wet enough to support trees at all, woody plants creep in steadily: first scattered shrubs, then thickets, then trees, until what was open prairie becomes a savanna and then something closer to a forest, all without a single acre being formally cleared or planted. Fire is not a disaster interrupting the "natural" grassland. In wetter climates, fire is the reason a grassland exists at all rather than reverting to woodland.

## Fire Set on Purpose, Long Before Anyone Wrote It Down

Lightning ignites some of this fire, especially in regions with a pronounced dry season followed by dry-lightning storms. But across the grasslands and savannas of North America, Africa, and Australia, humans have been setting fire deliberately for a very long time, for reasons that map directly onto the ecological effects fire produces. Indigenous peoples across the North American plains and prairies used fire to green up grazing land earlier in the season, drawing bison toward newly burned patches where nutritious new growth came in ahead of schedule, and to keep encroaching woody vegetation from closing in on travel corridors and hunting ground. Similar practices, developed independently, shaped savanna landscapes across other continents. In all of these places, what later arrivals sometimes mistook for a natural, untouched grassland was in part a landscape actively maintained by regular, intentional burning, and its openness depended on that maintenance continuing.

## The Suppression Era

That understanding was mostly lost, or actively rejected, by the land management institutions that took over large parts of these landscapes from the nineteenth century onward. Fire came to be seen, understandably enough after a run of genuinely destructive wildfires, as an unambiguous threat to be extinguished as quickly and completely as possible. In the United States, this philosophy hardened into policy through much of the twentieth century, popularized to the public through campaigns built around a cartoon bear warning that only they could prevent forest fires, with grassland and rangeland fire suppression following a similar logic even though the underlying ecology was quite different from the forest fires the campaign had originally been designed around.

The consequences took decades to become fully visible, because they operated exactly through the slow successional process fire had been suppressing all along. Woody encroachment crept across grasslands that had burned regularly for centuries, converting open prairie to scrubland in a process that was almost invisible year to year and unmistakable looking back after a few decades. Fuel that would once have burned off in frequent, low-intensity fires accumulated instead, so that when fire did eventually occur, whether from lightning or human accident, it often burned hotter and more destructively than the fires the landscape had evolved with — a bitter irony, since suppression had been intended to prevent exactly that outcome.

## Life Timed to the Burn

Plants are not the only organisms built around a recurring fire schedule. Many grassland animals survive a passing fire by going underground, literally: prairie dogs, ground squirrels, tortoises, and countless invertebrates retreat into burrows deep enough that the heat pulse from a fire moving overhead, intense but brief, never reaches them, emerging afterward into a landscape suddenly free of standing cover but rich in the flush of nutrients a burn releases. Grassland birds that nest on the ground time their breeding around the local fire regime where they can, since a nest caught in a burn is a nest lost, and some species specifically seek out recently burned patches for the sparse, easy-to-forage-in vegetation structure a fresh burn creates, distinct from the dense cover other species prefer in long-unburned patches. A healthy fire-adapted grassland landscape is rarely burning uniformly everywhere at once; it is a patchwork of recently burned, moderately recovered, and long-unburned sections, and much of its animal diversity depends on all three stages being available somewhere within reach at any given time.

Fire also resets the grassland's nutrient economy in ways that go beyond simply clearing dead material. Standing dead grass decomposes slowly, tying up nitrogen and other nutrients in tough, fibrous tissue that soil microbes break down only gradually. A fire converts a large fraction of that standing biomass to ash within minutes, releasing minerals like phosphorus and potassium directly onto the soil surface where growing roots can take them up far faster than they could have recovered them from slowly rotting thatch. Nitrogen is something of an exception, since a meaningful share of it volatilizes and is lost to the atmosphere in the heat of the burn rather than settling into the ash, which is one reason nitrogen-fixing legumes are often disproportionately represented among the plants that recover quickly on recently burned ground.

This entire dynamic is not unique to the tallgrass prairie of North America. The Cerrado savanna of central Brazil, one of the most extensive tropical savanna regions on the planet, depends on a comparable fire regime to hold back forest encroachment and maintain its distinctive mix of fire-tolerant, deep-rooted trees and grasses, and large stretches of African savanna operate under a similar logic, burning on a cycle shaped by rainfall seasonality as much as by anything humans or lightning contribute deliberately. Wherever grass, a dry season, and an ignition source coincide, something close to the same set of rules tends to apply.

## Bringing Fire Back on Purpose

The shift back toward using fire as a management tool rather than treating it purely as an enemy took hold gradually across the second half of the twentieth century, as ecologists accumulated evidence for what land managers who still practiced traditional burning had continued to understand all along. Prescribed burning, conducted under carefully chosen weather and fuel conditions with control lines already in place, is now a standard tool across many grassland and savanna preserves specifically because it does what natural or historically human-set fire used to do: it knocks back woody encroachment, recycles nutrients tied up in standing dead grass, clears thatch that would otherwise smother new growth, and resets the successional clock before enough fuel and enough time have passed for an eventual fire to become unmanageable.

The timing and frequency of these burns is itself a kind of applied ecology, tuned to the specific grassland in question. Burn too rarely and woody encroachment gets ahead of what fire alone can reverse. Burn on exactly the wrong seasonal schedule, year after year, and species that need to set seed before a burn or resprout after one at a particular time can decline even as the vegetation superficially still looks like grassland. Getting the interval and timing right, often by drawing on exactly the kind of accumulated, place-specific knowledge that Indigenous land managers had developed over centuries, has become one of the more quietly successful reconciliations between historical practice and modern land management, even in places where that reconciliation arrived a long time after the original knowledge had first been dismissed.
