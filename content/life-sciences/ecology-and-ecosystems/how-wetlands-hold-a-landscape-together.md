# How Wetlands Hold a Landscape Together

A wetland rarely announces itself as valuable. From a road, it often looks like waste ground: standing water, black mud, cattails and sedges instead of a mowed field or a stand of timber, mosquitoes thick in the summer air. For most of the twentieth century, this is more or less how policy in a lot of places treated it too — as land waiting to be drained and put to better use. Millions of acres were converted to farmland or development on exactly that reasoning before ecologists and engineers built a convincing case for what a wetland is actually doing while it sits there looking useless: filtering water, storing floods, and holding an outsized share of the carbon that ends up locked away in soil rather than in the atmosphere.

## What Makes a Wetland a Wetland

The technical definition rests on three overlapping features rather than any single one. There needs to be water at or near the surface for at least part of the year, long enough to matter biologically rather than just after a heavy rain. There needs to be hydric soil, meaning soil that has developed under saturated, low-oxygen conditions long enough to take on distinctive chemical and physical properties, often a gray or mottled color from iron compounds behaving differently without oxygen around. And there needs to be hydrophytic vegetation, plants specifically adapted to tolerate root systems sitting in oxygen-poor, waterlogged soil, which ordinary land plants cannot do for long.

Marshes, swamps, bogs, and fens are all wetlands by this definition, but they differ enough in water source, vegetation, and chemistry that lumping them together can obscure as much as it explains. A marsh is dominated by grasses, sedges, and reeds in mineral soil. A swamp is a wetland forest, with trees like cypress or red maple standing in water for at least part of the year. A bog accumulates its own soil in the form of peat, fed mainly by rainwater rather than groundwater or streams, acidic and nutrient-poor enough that specialized plants like sphagnum moss and carnivorous pitcher plants dominate where ordinary vegetation cannot compete. A fen is peat-forming too, but fed by groundwater rich enough in minerals to support a much more diverse plant community than a bog ever will.

## The Filter

Water entering a wetland rarely leaves in the same condition. As it slows down, moving through dense vegetation and shallow, convoluted channels instead of a straight ditch, suspended sediment drops out of suspension and settles, the way a river slows and drops its silt at a delta. Nutrients dissolved in the water, especially nitrogen and phosphorus that have washed off fertilized fields or leaked from septic systems, get taken up by the dense growth of wetland plants or transformed by microbial communities living in the saturated soil. Denitrifying bacteria, which need exactly the low-oxygen, waterlogged conditions a wetland provides, convert dissolved nitrate back into nitrogen gas, releasing it harmlessly to the atmosphere instead of letting it travel downstream to feed an algae bloom somewhere else. A wetland positioned between farmland and a river, in effect, does a meaningful fraction of what a water treatment plant does, for free and continuously, as long as it is left in place to do it.

## The Sponge

Wetlands also reshape how water moves through a landscape over time, not just how clean it is. A wetland can absorb a large volume of floodwater during heavy rain or snowmelt, spreading it across a broad, shallow area rather than letting it all rush downstream at once, then releasing that stored water gradually over the following days and weeks. This buffering effect is most obvious in its absence. Watersheds where wetlands have been drained and channelized tend to flood harder and faster after storms, because water that used to spread out and soak in now has nowhere to go but straight into an already swollen river. Coastal wetlands do something similar with storm surge, absorbing wave energy and slowing the inland push of water during hurricanes and other large storms, functioning as a buffer zone between open water and whatever is built behind it.

The same sponge-like quality works during dry periods too, though less dramatically. Wetlands that stay saturated release water slowly into groundwater and connected streams during droughts, helping keep base flow in rivers from dropping as far as it otherwise would. A landscape with intact wetlands tends to have a gentler hydrograph in both directions, less prone to the flood-then-drought whiplash that characterizes a heavily drained one.

## The Nursery

Beyond water quality and flood control, wetlands do something else that is easy to undervalue precisely because it does not show up in any engineering ledger: they provide breeding and nursery habitat unlike almost anything else in the surrounding landscape. Vernal pools, the small, temporary wetlands that fill with snowmelt or spring rain and then dry out completely by summer, are essential breeding habitat for many salamanders and frogs specifically because they dry out. Fish cannot survive in a pool that disappears every summer, which means amphibian eggs and larvae laid there face no fish predation at all, a rare and valuable condition that permanent water bodies cannot offer. Coastal marshes serve a parallel role for young fish and shellfish, offering shallow, structurally complex, food-rich water where juveniles can grow with far less exposure to the larger predators patrolling open water nearby.

Migratory waterfowl depend on wetlands scattered across their flight paths as staging and resting areas, much as shorebirds depend on stopover beaches, needing a reliable chain of marshes and shallow lakes to refuel at as they move between breeding and wintering grounds. The prairie pothole region of the northern Great Plains, a landscape once dotted with an enormous number of small, glacially formed wetlands, produces such a large share of North America's breeding waterfowl that its condition in any given year measurably affects duck populations across the whole continent, a striking illustration of how a landscape-scale mosaic of small wetlands can matter far more than the modest total area any one of them covers. None of these functions depend on a wetland being large. A single isolated marsh or vernal pool can be disproportionately important to local biodiversity even though it would barely register as a rounding error in a survey of a watershed's total water storage capacity, which is part of why wetland protection policy has had to grapple, often contentiously, with how to value countless small, unconnected wetlands that individually look negligible but collectively support species with nowhere else to go.

## The Vault

Peat-forming wetlands do something that has nothing to do with water quality or flood control and everything to do with the planet's carbon budget. In a bog, plant material dies and falls into standing water so oxygen-poor that the fungi and bacteria which normally break dead plant matter down into carbon dioxide can barely function. Instead of decomposing, dead sphagnum and other plant tissue accumulates, layer on layer, compressing slowly into peat that can be many feet deep and thousands of years old at its base. Each layer represents carbon that was pulled out of the atmosphere by a living plant and then never given back, locked away indefinitely as long as the peat stays wet.

This makes peatlands remarkably effective carbon stores relative to the land area they occupy, holding a disproportionate amount of the carbon stored in soils worldwide despite covering only a small share of the land surface. It also makes them unusually dangerous to disturb. Drain a peatland to farm it or harvest the peat, and the same oxygen-starved conditions that had kept centuries of dead plant matter from decomposing disappear. Decomposition resumes all at once, releasing carbon that took millennia to accumulate over a comparatively short span of years, and in dry conditions drained peat becomes a fire risk unlike almost any other landscape, capable of smoldering underground for months.

## Putting a Number on the Useless Land

The shift in how wetlands are treated by policy did not come from sentiment about mud and cattails. It came from adding up what a drained wetland actually costs a landscape once it is gone: the flood damage a community pays for downstream, the water treatment infrastructure a city has to build to do what a marsh used to do upstream, the fish and shellfish nurseries a coastal economy loses when nursery habitat disappears. None of that shows up on the deed to a piece of drained farmland. It shows up later, distributed across insurance premiums, municipal budgets, and fisheries, in a form that took decades of accounting to trace back to the specific acres that used to hold it all in place.
