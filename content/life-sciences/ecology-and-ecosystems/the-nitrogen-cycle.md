# The Nitrogen Cycle

Stand in an open field and nearly four-fifths of the air around you is nitrogen gas. It fills your lungs with every breath and does nothing there, passing back out unchanged, because the two nitrogen atoms in every N2 molecule are bound together by one of the strongest bonds in chemistry. And yet the plants rooted in the soil beneath your feet are, more often than almost anything else, starving for nitrogen. This is the central paradox that the nitrogen cycle exists to solve: the element that life needs most for building proteins and genetic material is also, in its most abundant form, essentially unusable by nearly all living things.

Breaking that bond takes serious energy, and only a small number of organisms, plus a couple of physical processes, can manage it. Everything else in the cycle is really about what happens to nitrogen after it has been pried out of gas form and made available — biologists call this fixation — and how it eventually gets converted back to N2 and released to the atmosphere again, closing the loop.

## Fixation: Getting Nitrogen Into the Game

Lightning can do it. The heat of a bolt is enough to force nitrogen and oxygen in the air to combine into nitrogen oxides, which dissolve in rainwater and reach the soil as a dilute trickle of nitrate. It is a real contribution to the cycle, but a minor one on a global scale.

The far larger natural source is biological: a small set of bacteria and archaea that carry an enzyme called nitrogenase, capable of breaking the nitrogen triple bond at ordinary temperatures, something industrial chemistry can only do by brute force with heat and pressure. Some of these microbes are free-living in soil or water. Others have entered into partnership with plants, and the most consequential of these partnerships is the one between legumes — peas, beans, clover, alfalfa, and their relatives — and bacteria in the genus Rhizobium. The plant grows small nodules on its roots, supplies the bacteria living inside with sugar and a low-oxygen environment, and receives fixed nitrogen in return, delivered directly into its own tissue rather than having to compete for scraps in the soil. Farmers have exploited this relationship for a very long time, rotating legume crops through fields specifically to rebuild nitrogen that other crops had drawn down.

In the twentieth century, humans learned to do industrially what those bacteria had been doing quietly all along. The Haber-Bosch process, developed in Germany in the early 1900s by the chemists Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch, forces nitrogen and hydrogen gas together at high temperature and enormous pressure over a catalyst to produce ammonia. It remains one of the most consequential pieces of applied chemistry ever developed, because the synthetic fertilizer it enabled is a major reason the planet can feed a population far larger than it could on natural soil fertility alone. It also means that humans now fix roughly as much nitrogen artificially each year as all of nature's biological fixers combined, a shift large enough to have measurably altered the planet's nitrogen budget.

## From Ammonia to Something Roots Can Use

However nitrogen gets fixed, it first takes the form of ammonia or its water-soluble cousin ammonium. Plants can absorb some ammonium directly, but much of it is transformed further by soil bacteria in a two-step process called nitrification. Bacteria in the genus Nitrosomonas oxidize ammonium into nitrite, and a second group, including Nitrobacter, oxidizes that nitrite into nitrate. Nitrate is the form most plants prefer, soluble and easily drawn up through roots along with water.

There is also ammonification, the process by which nitrogen already locked up in living tissue gets released back into this cycle. When any organism dies, or when an animal excretes waste, the organic nitrogen in proteins and nucleic acids is broken down by decomposers — fungi and bacteria — back into ammonium, which can then be nitrified again or taken up directly. This is the recycling loop that keeps most ecosystems running without needing constant new fixation: nitrogen already present gets used, released, and used again many times before any of it escapes back to the atmosphere.

## Closing the Loop: Denitrification

Eventually some nitrate finds its way into waterlogged or oxygen-poor soil, wetland sediment, or the bottom layers of a pond, where a different set of bacteria take over. Under these low-oxygen conditions, denitrifying bacteria — several species in the genus Pseudomonas among them — use nitrate instead of oxygen to breathe, in the process converting it stepwise back into nitrogen gas, which bubbles out of the soil and returns to the atmosphere it came from. Denitrification is the cycle's exit door, and it happens almost exclusively in wet, oxygen-starved places, which is one reason wetlands are so disproportionately important to nitrogen cycling at a landscape scale: they are where nitrogen gets returned to the air rather than accumulating indefinitely in soil and water.

## Why So Much of Life Runs Short on It

Despite nitrogen's abundance in the air, it is the nutrient most likely to limit plant growth across a huge range of ecosystems, from temperate forests to the open ocean. Phosphorus, potassium, and other mineral nutrients come from the slow weathering of rock, present in soil in amounts that vary by local geology but do not depend on any biological gatekeeper to become available. Nitrogen has no comparable mineral source at any meaningful scale; essentially all of it has to pass through fixation first, which means the entire nitrogen supply available to a plant community is bottlenecked by how much fixing bacteria and archaea can manage, or how much decomposition can recycle from what is already there. Ecologists sometimes invoke Liebig's law of the minimum to describe this kind of situation: growth is limited not by whatever resource is most abundant but by whichever essential resource is scarcest relative to what an organism needs, and for plant growth across much of the planet, that scarcest resource is usually nitrogen. This is precisely why synthetic nitrogen fertilizer had such an outsized effect on agricultural yields when it became widely available. It was not adding some marginal nutrient to a system that already had enough of everything else. It was removing the single tightest bottleneck constraining growth almost everywhere it was applied, which is also why its effects on the rest of the cycle turned out to be so large.

## When the Cycle Runs Too Fast

For most of the planet's history, the nitrogen cycle was tightly conservative, limited on the input side by how much fixation biology could manage. Fertilizer changed that. When more nitrate and ammonium reach a field than crops can absorb, the surplus does not simply wait around. Rain washes it into streams, streams carry it into rivers, and rivers eventually deliver it to lakes, estuaries, and coastal waters that were never adapted to that kind of nitrogen load.

There, the same nutrient that made the crops grow makes algae grow, explosively, in blooms that eventually die and sink. Bacteria decomposing all that dead algae consume the oxygen dissolved in the water, sometimes fast enough to leave a layer near the bottom with too little oxygen for fish, shellfish, or almost anything else to survive. The Gulf of Mexico develops a seasonal zone like this every year, fed largely by fertilizer runoff carried down the Mississippi River from farmland across the country's agricultural interior, and it is far from the only coastal water dealing with the same problem. This is eutrophication, and it is essentially the nitrogen cycle overshooting the capacity of an ecosystem to keep pace with it — too much fixation, not enough denitrification and uptake to balance it, all compressed into a much shorter stretch of river and coastline than nature ever routed that much nitrogen through before.

Denitrification carries its own complication as well. One of its intermediate products, nitrous oxide, escapes into the atmosphere in meaningful quantities wherever heavily fertilized soil is also imperfectly aerated, and nitrous oxide is both a long-lived greenhouse gas and, with other ozone-depleting emissions now in decline, one of the more significant remaining threats to the stratospheric ozone layer. A cycle already running too fast on the input side turns out to leak trouble in more than one direction at once, and neither leak shows up at the field where the fertilizer was actually spread.

The nitrogen cycle, in other words, is not a fixed piece of planetary machinery running the same way it always has. It is a set of biological and chemical reactions in a rough, self-correcting balance, and for the first time in its history one species has learned to push hard enough on one side of it to change how the whole system behaves.
