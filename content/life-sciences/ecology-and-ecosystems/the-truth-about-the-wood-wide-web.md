# The Truth About the Wood-Wide Web

Kick through leaf litter on a forest floor and you are standing on top of something far busier than the trees above you suggest. Threaded through the top layers of soil, usually invisible unless you know to look for the faint white filaments clinging to a pulled-up root, is a mesh of fungal tissue that may connect a majority of the trees in the stand to one another, indirectly, through fungal partners they each share. This is the raw biological fact behind the phrase "wood-wide web," popularized over the last couple of decades as shorthand for the idea that forests are less a collection of competing individuals than a single cooperating, even communicating, superorganism. The phrase captures something real. It has also, in the retelling, drifted well past what the underlying science actually supports.

## What a Mycorrhiza Actually Is

Start with the relationship itself, which is not controversial at all. A mycorrhiza is a symbiosis between a fungus and a plant root, and it is less an exotic special case than the default condition for most land plants, going back deep enough in evolutionary history that some researchers think the arrangement helped plants colonize land in the first place. The fungus sends out a network of extremely fine filaments, hyphae, extending through soil volumes a root could never physically reach on its own, dramatically increasing the surface area available for absorbing water and scarce nutrients, phosphorus especially. In exchange, the plant supplies the fungus with sugars built from photosynthesis, something the fungus cannot make for itself.

Two broad types cover most of the relationship: arbuscular mycorrhizae, where fungal structures actually penetrate into root cells, found in the majority of plant species including most crops and grasses; and ectomycorrhizae, where the fungus wraps around the outside of root tips in a dense sheath without penetrating the cells, characteristic of many forest trees including pines, oaks, and birches. Both arrangements are ancient, well studied, and essentially universal wherever healthy soil and healthy plants coexist.

## The Plants That Cheat

Not every participant in a mycorrhizal relationship holds up its end of the bargain, and the exceptions are informative precisely because they reveal how much traffic a fungal network can carry when something is willing to exploit it. A small number of plants, lacking chlorophyll entirely and incapable of photosynthesis, have abandoned the trade altogether and simply take carbon from their fungal partners without giving anything back. Ghost pipe, a waxy white plant that pushes up through forest leaf litter in deep shade and looks more like a fungus itself than a flowering plant, is one of the more familiar examples in North American forests. It survives entirely on carbon it draws from a mycorrhizal fungus, which in turn is often getting that same carbon from the roots of a nearby photosynthesizing tree, making the ghost pipe an indirect parasite on the tree, several steps removed, using the fungal network as a conduit rather than a genuine partner. Botanists call this strategy mycoheterotrophy, and while ghost pipe is the most visible example to an ordinary hiker, a number of orchids run a similar scheme, at least during part of their life cycle, drawing down fungal carbon before they are large enough to photosynthesize productively on their own; some, like the aptly named coral-root orchids, never develop the ability at all and remain dependent on their fungal partner for life.

Mycoheterotrophs matter to the wood-wide-web debate because they are hard evidence that a fungal network can and does move carbon between recipients with no close relationship to each other, since a ghost pipe is not remotely related to the tree indirectly subsidizing it. That fact alone does not settle how much material moves between two ordinary, photosynthesizing trees connected by the same kind of network, which remains the more contested question, but it rules out any argument that the physical plumbing for this kind of transfer does not exist. The plumbing clearly exists, and clearly carries real carbon under real forest conditions. What is still being worked out is how often it moves material between two willing, self-sufficient partners rather than being quietly siphoned by whichever organism in the network is best positioned to take without giving back.

## Where the Network Idea Comes From

The leap from "every tree has fungal partners" to "wood-wide web" comes from a further observation: a single fungal individual, especially among ectomycorrhizal species, can connect to the roots of more than one tree at once, sometimes many trees, of the same or different species, creating what researchers call a common mycorrhizal network. Once that physical connection exists, it becomes possible, at least in principle, for something to move through it between trees rather than just between one tree and its fungal partner.

This is where the work of the Canadian forest ecologist Suzanne Simard enters the story. In a series of experiments beginning in the 1990s, Simard used isotopically labeled carbon to trace whether carbon assimilated by one tree could show up later in a neighboring tree of a different species, with a shared fungal network as the suspected pathway. She found evidence that it could, particularly in a shaded seedling receiving a small subsidy from a larger, sunlit neighbor. Simard went on to become one of the most visible public voices in forest ecology, popularizing the idea through a widely watched public talk and later a memoir, using language that leaned heavily on metaphors of communication, cooperation, and even maternal care between what she called "mother trees" and the seedlings around them.

## Where the Science Pushed Back

That popularization ran well ahead of what most of the underlying field considers settled, and in recent years a number of mycorrhizal researchers, including the ecologist Justine Karst and colleagues who reviewed the accumulated evidence, have published pointed critiques of how far the wood-wide-web narrative has traveled from what the data actually show. Their concerns cluster around a few recurring issues. Many of the foundational experiments were conducted in controlled settings — potted seedlings, lab microcosms — that may not translate cleanly to how forests behave; documented cases of shared mycorrhizal networks actually linking specific individual trees in an undisturbed forest are fewer than popular accounts imply; and even where carbon transfer between connected trees has been measured, the amounts involved are often small enough that their ecological significance for the receiving tree's growth and survival remains genuinely unclear, as opposed to being a decisive lifeline. There is a real difference between "fungal networks physically connect many trees, and some material demonstrably moves through them" and "trees use fungal networks to nurture their offspring and warn each other of danger," and a fair amount of the popular coverage has treated those as equivalent when the scientific evidence supports the first far more comfortably than the second.

None of this amounts to the network being disproven, and it would be its own kind of overcorrection to conclude that mycorrhizal connections are ecologically trivial. Shared fungal networks almost certainly do redistribute some resources among connected plants, and there is reasonable evidence that seedlings growing near an established, compatible mycorrhizal partner establish more successfully than seedlings without one nearby, whatever the precise mechanism turns out to be. Signaling compounds released by a stressed or damaged plant have also been shown, in some experiments, to travel to neighboring plants through shared fungal connections and trigger a defensive response there before the threat — an insect, typically — arrives. That is a real and interesting phenomenon, documented under real experimental conditions, even if it is a long way from the deliberate, almost intentional cooperation implied by talk of trees "helping" one another.

## Why the Distinction Is Worth Making

It would be easy to treat this as an argument about nothing more than word choice, scientists being precise about language the public was never going to use as carefully anyway. But the stakes are a little higher than that, because the more anthropomorphized version of the story has started to shape actual forest management decisions, including harvesting practices designed around preserving "mother trees" as hubs of a cooperative network. If the underlying mechanism is weaker or more limited than the popular version suggests, management built on the strong version could be prioritizing the wrong things, protecting individual large trees for their supposed role as network hubs when the evidence for that specific role remains thin.

The more accurate picture is less romantic and, to anyone who actually finds fungal biology interesting, not obviously less remarkable: a genuinely ancient, genuinely widespread symbiosis, capable of linking plants into networks that move real material and real information under specific conditions, operating within limits that are still being mapped rather than already fully understood, and resistant to being flattened into a single tidy metaphor about a forest as one big cooperating family.
