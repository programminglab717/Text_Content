# Why Invasive Species Succeed

In the early 1890s, as the story is usually told, a wealthy drug manufacturer with a fondness for Shakespeare released a small number of European starlings into New York's Central Park, reportedly motivated by a wish to introduce to America every bird species mentioned anywhere in Shakespeare's plays. Whatever the precise motive, the birds took. European starlings now number among the most abundant birds on the continent, spread from coast to coast, competing aggressively with native cavity-nesting birds for nest sites and gathering in flocks large enough to damage crops and foul infrastructure. Thousands of other species have been introduced to new continents by human activity, deliberately or by accident, and the overwhelming majority of them do not do this. Most either fail to establish at all or settle into some modest, unremarkable presence that nobody notices. Explaining the difference between an unremarkable introduction and a starling is most of what invasion ecology is actually about.

## The Numbers Game, and Its Limits

The simplest predictor of whether an introduced species establishes at all is how many individuals, and how many separate introduction events, actually occur. A single pregnant female arriving alone has to survive, find a mate or reproduce asexually, and avoid every source of bad luck that could wipe out a founding population of one. A species introduced repeatedly, in larger numbers, across multiple separate events, gets many more chances, and each additional attempt increases the odds that one of them lands during a stretch of favorable weather, low predation, or whatever else establishment happens to require. This is sometimes called propagule pressure, and it explains a great deal about which introductions succeed purely as a numbers game, independent of anything special about the species itself.

But propagule pressure alone does not explain why some successfully established species stay quiet, occupying a narrow niche without much fanfare, while others explode across a continent and rewrite the ecology of everywhere they reach. That distinction is where the more interesting biological explanations come in.

## Leaving the Enemies Behind

One of the oldest and best-supported ideas is the enemy release hypothesis. In its home range, any species is kept in check by a long co-evolved list of enemies: specialist predators, parasites, pathogens, herbivorous insects fine-tuned over evolutionary time to exploit exactly that species and no other. Transplant the species to a new continent and most of that list does not come along. General predators in the new range may take some individuals, but the specialists that evolved specifically to overcome that species' particular defenses are, in most cases, absent entirely. A plant that spent countless generations investing energy in chemical defenses against a particular set of specialist insect herbivores can suddenly redirect that energy into growth and reproduction instead, because the insects that made those defenses necessary never made the trip. Freed from the predators and pathogens holding its population in check at home, an introduced species can reach population densities its native range never allowed for, not because the new environment is more hospitable in any absolute sense but because the species has been let off a leash it had been straining against for a very long time.

## Bringing Weapons Nobody Has Seen Before

A related but distinct idea, the novel weapons hypothesis, flips the emphasis from what the invader left behind to what it brings with it. Some plants succeed in a new range not primarily by escaping enemies but by deploying biochemical weapons that native species in the new ecosystem have no evolved resistance to, having never been exposed to that particular chemistry before. Spotted knapweed, an invasive plant across parts of North America, releases root compounds into the soil that appear to suppress the growth of many neighboring plants, an effect called allelopathy, and there is reasonable evidence that native North American plants are considerably more sensitive to this particular chemical arsenal than the European plant communities knapweed evolved alongside, which had presumably built up some resistance to it over a much longer shared history. The invader is not just unopposed. It is actively poisoning competitors that never developed the biochemical countermeasures a longer coexistence might have produced.

## An Empty Niche, or a Willing Ecosystem

Neither hypothesis fully explains cases where an invasive species does not so much escape enemies or deploy new weapons as simply exploit an ecological role nothing else in the new ecosystem was filling. The brown tree snake, introduced to the Pacific island of Guam most likely as an accidental stowaway in military cargo around the middle of the twentieth century, encountered an island with no native snake predators and a population of forest birds that had evolved for millions of years with no reason to develop defenses against a nocturnal, tree-climbing snake predator. The birds had no evolved fear response to the right cues, no nesting strategy adapted to defend against this particular threat, because nothing like it had ever been part of their evolutionary environment. The snake did not need novel weapons or enemy release in any elaborate sense. It needed prey that had never learned to be afraid of it, and Guam's forest bird community was reduced drastically within a few decades, several species pushed to extinction in the wild or surviving only in captivity, a collapse that traces back to a predator finding an ecosystem with an empty slot exactly shaped for it.

## Why "Invasive" Resists Easy Definition

All of this complicates what looks, from a distance, like it should be a simple category. Not every introduced species is invasive; ecologists generally reserve that term for introduced species causing demonstrable ecological or economic harm, which leaves plenty of non-native species that have naturalized without causing much trouble at all sitting in a kind of definitional limbo, neither native nor invasive in the strict sense. Context matters too: a species can be a well-behaved, unremarkable part of the landscape in one region and a serious problem a few hundred miles away, depending on local competitors, predators, and climate. And the label carries enough emotional charge, evoking metaphors of invasion and warfare, that ecologists have increasingly pushed for more neutral, functional language, describing what a species actually does to an ecosystem's nutrient cycling, native species abundance, or physical structure rather than reaching first for whether it belongs by some historical accident of geography. The species itself, after all, is only ever doing what species do: reproducing and spreading wherever conditions allow. What makes a particular introduction a crisis is almost always a property of the receiving ecosystem's missing defenses as much as it is a property of the species doing the invading.
