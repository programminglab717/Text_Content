# Forest Succession After Disturbance

When Mount St. Helens erupted in 1980, the blast leveled several hundred square miles of Pacific Northwest forest in a matter of minutes, scouring some slopes down to bare volcanic ash and leaving others buried under mudflows, downed timber, and pumice. Biologists who went in afterward expected almost nothing to be alive. What they found instead, within the very first growing seasons, were pockets of survival: plants that had been under snowbanks during the blast, roots and rhizomes that resprouted from underground, insects and small mammals that had been sheltered in burrows. That mix of near-total destruction and stubborn survival is the starting condition for one of ecology's oldest and most useful ideas: succession, the predictable-in-outline, unpredictable-in-detail process by which a damaged or newly formed piece of land is recolonized and rebuilt over time.

## Primary Succession: Starting From Nothing

Ecologists distinguish two broad flavors of succession by what they start with. Primary succession begins on substrate that has no biological legacy at all — bare rock exposed by a retreating glacier, fresh lava, dredged sand, the ash fields at the heart of the St. Helens blast zone. There is no soil in the meaningful sense, no seed bank waiting underground, nothing left over from whatever community existed before, because in most cases nothing did.

The first colonists in this setting tend to be organisms that ask almost nothing of their environment: lichens, which are themselves a partnership between a fungus and an alga or cyanobacterium, capable of extracting a living from bare rock and slowly breaking its surface down through weak acids they secrete. Mosses often follow, trapping wind-blown dust and organic fragments in their low mats. Both groups are doing something essential beyond simply surviving: they are manufacturing the beginnings of soil, a slow accumulation of mineral grit and organic matter that eventually becomes deep enough to support something with actual roots.

Glacier Bay in Alaska has become something of a living laboratory for this process, because the glacier there has been retreating steadily enough that a single walk inland from the current ice front crosses through decades of succession compressed into a few miles. Bare till near the ice gives way to mats of mountain avens and other early colonizers, then to thickets of alder, and eventually, in the areas the ice retreated from longest ago, to spruce and hemlock forest indistinguishable from old growth elsewhere in the region. Alder's role there is not incidental. Like legumes, it hosts nitrogen-fixing bacteria in its roots, and on nitrogen-starved glacial till, alder thickets function as a kind of fertility bridge, building up soil nitrogen levels fast enough that less specialized trees can eventually take over the ground alder itself prepared.

## Secondary Succession: Starting From Something

Far more common, and far more relevant to most forests people actually encounter, is secondary succession: recovery on ground that already has soil and often a seed bank, even though the vegetation growing on it has been cleared or killed by fire, windthrow, logging, disease, or agriculture that was later abandoned. Because the soil and its biology are already intact, secondary succession moves dramatically faster than primary succession, sometimes visibly within a single growing season.

The general sequence is well worn enough to have become a kind of ecological cliché, though clichés earn their status by being true often enough. Fast-growing annual and perennial herbs and grasses — the classic disturbed-ground pioneers, quick to flower and produce seed — dominate the first year or two. Shrubs and fast-growing, shade-intolerant trees follow: species like aspen, birch, and pin cherry in eastern North American forests, which grow quickly in full sun but cannot reproduce in their own shade. As these pioneers grow tall enough to cast real shade, they inadvertently create the conditions for their own eventual replacement, because slower-growing, shade-tolerant trees — sugar maple, beech, hemlock, depending on the region — can germinate and survive in the dim understory in a way the sun-loving pioneers cannot. Over decades, the shade-tolerant trees grow up through the canopy the pioneers built, eventually overtopping and outliving them, until the forest reaches something close to a stable long-term composition often called, with some controversy over how stable it really is, a climax community.

## Why the Sequence Isn't Purely Mechanical

Early theories of succession, developed in the first decades of the twentieth century, treated this progression as almost deterministic, as though a landscape were an organism developing predictably from youth to maturity. Later ecologists complicated that picture considerably. Joseph Connell and Ralph Slatyer, writing in the 1970s, proposed that different species interact with the successional process in genuinely different ways rather than all following one script. In some cases, early species facilitate the ones that follow, the way alder's nitrogen fixation prepares ground for less specialized trees. In other cases, early species tolerate later arrivals without helping or hindering them much, simply persisting until slower species catch up. And in still other cases, early species actively inhibit whoever tries to follow, holding a site in an arrested state until the inhibitors themselves die of old age, disease, or a second disturbance, finally opening space for the next wave.

This matters because it means the same disturbance, in the same forest type, can produce different recovery trajectories depending on which species happen to arrive first, what the weather does in the first few critical years, and what browsing animals are present to eat seedlings before they establish. Succession is a strong tendency, not a fixed timetable.

## What Old Growth Adds That Young Forest Lacks

Reaching a stable species composition is not the same as reaching full structural maturity, and the distinction matters more than the succession story usually lets on. A secondary forest can achieve something close to its eventual climax mix of tree species within a century or so in many temperate regions, look completely forested to a casual observer, and still lack most of the physical features that define genuine old growth. Standing dead trees, called snags, take decades to accumulate in the numbers a mature forest eventually holds, and they matter enormously to the many species of woodpeckers, owls, bats, and cavity-nesting songbirds that depend on the soft, decaying wood snags provide, wood too far gone for a healthy living tree to offer but not yet collapsed into the leaf litter. Fallen logs on the forest floor, similarly, need enough time not only to fall but to work through their own decades-long decay process, hosting one community of insects and fungi when freshly down and a completely different one once they have softened into a spongy, moisture-holding ridge on the forest floor. Canopy gaps created when old, large trees finally die and fall open up patches of light distinct from anything a uniformly aged young stand produces, allowing a new pulse of shade-intolerant regeneration within an otherwise mature forest and adding yet another layer of structural variety.

This is why a forest that regrew after clear-cutting a century ago, however impressive it looks from below, is not ecologically equivalent to old growth that was never cut, even once the tree species match. It is missing the accumulated structural debris and variation that only time, and repeated small-scale disturbance within an otherwise mature stand, can produce. Some of the species most closely associated with old growth are not old-growth specialists because they need large living trees particularly, but because they need the dead and decaying wood, the multi-layered canopy, and the light-gap patchiness that only a forest old enough to have already lost some of its original trees can supply. Compositional maturity can arrive in decades. Structural maturity, in most forest types, still takes centuries, and no amount of the right species arriving on schedule can shortcut that second clock.

## Disturbance as a Recurring Feature, Not an Interruption

Perhaps the most significant shift in how ecologists think about succession has been the move away from treating disturbance as an aberration that succession exists to repair, and toward treating disturbance itself as a normal, recurring part of how forests are structured over long timescales. Windthrow from storms, insect outbreaks, fire, and disease are not rare accidents interrupting an otherwise static forest; in many forest types they arrive often enough, at a scale small enough, that the forest is best understood as a shifting mosaic of patches at different successional stages simultaneously, rather than one uniform stand slowly aging toward a single climax.

This reframing has practical consequences for how land is managed. A policy of suppressing every disturbance, whether fire or insect outbreak, does not preserve a forest in its ideal state; it removes the very process that generates the patchiness many forest species depend on, some of which need young, open, sun-drenched patches just as much as others need old, dark, structurally complex ones. The forest that results from decades of successful fire suppression is not more natural than one that burns periodically. It is simply a forest stuck partway through a process it was never meant to complete.
