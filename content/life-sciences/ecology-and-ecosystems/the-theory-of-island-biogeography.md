# The Theory of Island Biogeography

Take any island you like — a coral cay a few acres across, a temperate island the size of a small country — and count its species of birds, or reptiles, or flowering plants, and a pattern shows up almost every time. Bigger islands have more species than small ones. Islands close to a mainland have more species than equally sized islands far out in open ocean. This much had been observed by naturalists for a very long time, ever since the age of voyaging biologists like Darwin and Wallace, and by the middle of the twentieth century it had accumulated into a solid empirical regularity without much of a mechanism behind it. Why, precisely, should size and distance matter so much, and could the relationship be described with anything more rigorous than "bigger and closer tends to mean more species"?

## Two Ecologists and a Blank Notebook

The answer came in 1967, when the ecologists Robert MacArthur and E. O. Wilson published a short, dense, mathematically unusual book called The Theory of Island Biogeography. MacArthur was already known for bringing quantitative rigor to questions that most naturalists had approached descriptively; Wilson, at that point deep in his career studying ants, brought an entomologist's patience for painstaking fieldwork on small, self-contained systems. Their central move was to stop treating the number of species on an island as a static property to be explained by history — how many species happened to have colonized over geological time — and start treating it as a dynamic equilibrium: a balance between two ongoing, opposing rates, immigration and extinction, that would settle at a predictable number regardless of exactly which species were involved.

Immigration, in their model, depends heavily on distance. A species reaching a remote island has to survive whatever the crossing requires, whether that means an accidental sea voyage on a raft of vegetation, a long flight against the odds, or windborne transport for a seed or spore, and the odds of a successful crossing fall off sharply with distance. An island a few miles offshore should accumulate new colonists steadily; one a thousand miles out should receive them only rarely. Extinction, meanwhile, depends heavily on island size, because a small island supports smaller populations of every species living on it, and small populations are much more vulnerable to random bad luck — a drought, a disease outbreak, an unlucky run of poor breeding seasons — than large ones are. As the number of species already present on an island climbs, competition for limited space and resources intensifies too, pushing the extinction rate up further still.

Put those two curves on the same graph, immigration falling as more species accumulate and available niches fill up, extinction rising for the same reason, and they cross at a particular point: an equilibrium number of species, where new arrivals are being balanced by an equal rate of local extinctions, so that the island's species list turns over constantly in its details even while its total size stays roughly stable. Species are not simply added over time; they are also constantly being lost, replaced by new colonists, in what MacArthur and Wilson called a dynamic rather than static equilibrium.

## Testing an Idea That Looked Untestable

A theory describing something as slow and diffuse as island colonization might have remained purely mathematical, appealing on paper but nearly impossible to check against reality within a human lifetime. Wilson, together with the ecologist Daniel Simberloff, found a way around that problem with an experiment audacious in its simplicity. They identified a handful of small mangrove islets off the coast of Florida, tiny enough to be manageable and isolated enough to behave like real islands for the insects and other arthropods living in their canopies, surveyed the species present on each in detail, and then had them fumigated, killing off every insect, spider, and other arthropod on each islet while leaving the mangrove trees themselves alive.

Then they simply watched, and kept counting, as each defaunated islet was recolonized from scratch. Species arrived, in some cases the wrong ones at first, went extinct locally, were replaced by others, and within roughly a year or so each islet had climbed back to a total species count strikingly close to what had been there before the fumigation, even though the specific mix of species differed considerably from the original community. The number stabilized. The identities did not. It was about as clean a confirmation of the equilibrium concept as anyone could have hoped for from a real, uncontrolled outdoor experiment.

## Beyond Real Islands

The theory's afterlife turned out to be at least as significant as its original application, because ecologists soon noticed that plenty of things besides literal islands behave like islands in the relevant sense: isolated patches of habitat surrounded by a different, inhospitable habitat instead of open water. A forest fragment surrounded by farmland, a mountaintop patch of alpine habitat surrounded by lowland forest, a nature reserve surrounded by development, all present the same basic structure of isolated area and distance to a source of colonists that MacArthur and Wilson's model was built to describe.

This application became directly relevant to conservation biology just as habitat fragmentation was accelerating across much of the world, and it fed into a genuinely contentious debate within the field, often abbreviated SLOSS, over whether conservation land was better allocated as a single large reserve or several small reserves of the same total area. Island biogeography theory suggested, through the extinction side of its equilibrium, that a single large reserve should generally support more species over the long run than several small fragments of equivalent combined area, because small fragments would lose species to extinction faster and recolonization across intervening human land use is far harder than recolonization across open water ever was for the fumigated mangrove islets. The debate was never fully resolved into a single rule, because real landscapes bring in complications the original model never had to handle — corridors between fragments, differences between species in how they respond to isolation, the particular threats facing a particular reserve — but it reshaped how reserve design was discussed from that point forward, giving conservation planners a theoretical vocabulary for a set of tradeoffs they had previously been navigating mostly by intuition.
