# How Medicine Changed Its Mind About Depression

Robert Burton published *The Anatomy of Melancholy* in 1621 under a pseudonym,
and kept enlarging it for the rest of his life. He was a scholar at Oxford, a
man who by his own account suffered badly from the condition he was
cataloguing, and he explained that he wrote about misery in order to keep
himself busy enough not to be miserable. The book is enormous and
disorganised and full of digressions about fish and comets, but the spine of
it is a survey: what causes low mood, what sustains it, what has been tried
against it. Bad air. Solitude. Idleness. Excessive study. Love. Religious
terror. Poverty. Grief. He ranks none of these, because he cannot.

Four centuries later the catalogue is better evidenced and still largely
unranked. What has changed repeatedly, and sometimes violently, is the story
medicine has told about what sits underneath the list.

## Black Bile, and the Long Reign of Melancholy

The Hippocratic writers explained temperament by the balance of four humours,
and named the gloomy one after the fluid they held responsible: *melas kholē*,
black bile. Nothing about the theory was correct, but two things about it were
consequential. It made low mood a condition of the body rather than a moral
failure or a visitation, and it implied that the condition had causes,
courses, and possible remedies. That framing outlasted the humours themselves
by a very long way.

By the nineteenth century melancholia had become a staple of asylum medicine,
and by its end the fashionable diagnosis for the milder and more prosperous
version was neurasthenia — nervous exhaustion, a depletion of some finite
nervous energy by the pace of modern life. Around the turn of the twentieth
century Emil Kraepelin reorganised severe mental illness by watching what
happened to patients over years rather than by cataloguing their symptoms on a
given afternoon. He separated manic-depressive insanity, which came in
episodes with genuine recovery between them, from dementia praecox, which
tended to grind downward. That division, adjusted and argued over, still
organises psychiatry.

Then Freud, in an essay written during the First World War, drew the line that
dominated the next fifty years of thinking: grief and melancholia look alike,
but the grieving person has lost something in the world, while the melancholic
has lost something in himself and turns his reproaches inward. The
psychoanalytic account made depression intelligible as meaning. It also made
it more or less untestable, and it coexisted uneasily with the hospital
ward's practical division between endogenous depression, which was supposed to
arise from within for no reason, and reactive depression, which followed
misfortune. That split was intuitive, widely used, and did not survive careful
study: severe depressions turn out to be preceded by difficult life events
about as often as mild ones.

## Two Accidents in the 1950s

Iproniazid was developed to treat tuberculosis. It was moderately useful
against the infection, and staff in the sanatoria noticed something else —
patients became energised, sociable, in some cases conspicuously cheerful
while still gravely ill. Around the same time the Swiss psychiatrist Roland
Kuhn tested a compound chemically related to the new antipsychotic
chlorpromazine, hoping for a second antipsychotic. It did nothing much for
psychosis. On depressed patients it had an effect he could not ignore. That
compound became imipramine.

Neither drug came from a theory. The theory came afterwards, and it came from
noticing what the two had in common: both increased the availability of
monoamine neurotransmitters — noradrenaline, serotonin, dopamine — at the
synapse, one by blocking the enzyme that breaks them down, the other by
blocking their reuptake. Running the other way, reserpine, a blood-pressure
drug that depletes monoamines, acquired a reputation for lowering mood in some
patients, though the strength of that association was later questioned.

In the mid-1960s Joseph Schildkraut assembled these threads into the
catecholamine hypothesis of affective disorders: depression might involve a
deficiency of noradrenaline at functionally important receptor sites, mania an
excess. He was careful about it, and explicitly called the idea a reductionist
oversimplification that would need testing. Later versions shifted the
emphasis toward serotonin. Neither version was ever confirmed in the way its
popular reputation implied.

## The Chemical Imbalance and Its Long Afterlife

The problems were visible almost immediately. The drugs change monoamine levels
within hours, but patients do not improve for two to four weeks — a gap that a
simple deficiency model cannot explain. Experimentally lowering the dietary
precursor of serotonin in healthy volunteers does not reliably make them
depressed. Direct measurements of monoamine metabolites in depressed patients
never produced the clean deficit the theory required.

None of that stopped the shorthand. By the late 1980s, when fluoxetine and the
other selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors arrived, "chemical imbalance"
had become the standard explanation offered in consulting rooms and, more
insistently, in advertising. It is worth being fair about why clinicians used
it. It was quick, it moved blame off the patient's character, and it made
taking a tablet for a mood problem seem no odder than taking one for blood
pressure. The newer drugs spread as fast as they did not because they worked
better than the older tricyclics, which on the whole they did not, but because
they were far less dangerous in overdose and easier to tolerate.

The unwinding has caused its own confusion. When reviews began stating plainly
that the evidence never supported a simple serotonin deficit, a good many
people concluded that antidepressants must therefore be useless. That does not
follow. A treatment can work while the story originally told about why it
works is wrong; aspirin was in use for the better part of a century before
anyone understood prostaglandins. The honest position is that these drugs have
real but modest average effects, that the average conceals wide variation
between individuals, and that nobody can yet say with confidence what they are
doing that matters.

## A Diagnosis Built by Committee

In 1980 the third edition of the American diagnostic manual, assembled under
Robert Spitzer, rebuilt psychiatric diagnosis around explicit symptom criteria.
The motivation was reliability: two psychiatrists examining the same patient
had been reaching alarmingly different conclusions, and research was
impossible without agreement about who was being studied. Major depressive
disorder became a count — a minimum number of specified symptoms, present most
of the day nearly every day over a minimum duration, causing distress or
impairment.

It worked. Reliability improved dramatically. But a criteria set built this way
is combinatorially generous, and the consequence is a category that contains
very different people. One patient sleeps fourteen hours, eats constantly, and
cannot move. Another has not slept properly in a month, has lost weight, and
paces. Both meet criteria; they may share only the mood item. Whatever their
conditions have in common, it is not obviously a mechanism.

That heterogeneity has quietly sabotaged decades of research. Biological
markers, genetic signals, and treatment effects have all been sought in
samples assembled by a net rather than by a mechanism, and mixed samples wash
out real effects that exist only in subgroups. The question the field has
slowly reorganised itself around is no longer what depression is, but which
depression, in whom.

## What Replaced the Simple Story

Nothing tidy. Several partial accounts now run in parallel, each with genuine
support and none sufficient.

One holds that the relevant changes are structural rather than chemical.
Chronic stress is associated with measurable changes in the hippocampus and
prefrontal cortex, and antidepressants, exercise, and electroconvulsive therapy
all promote neural plasticity. On this reading the weeks-long lag before
improvement is not an embarrassment but a clue: what is being rebuilt takes
weeks to rebuild. A second account looks at the immune system. Sickness
behaviour — the withdrawal, exhaustion, loss of appetite and loss of interest
that come with influenza — resembles some depressions closely, immune
signalling molecules can produce depression-like states, and a subset of
depressed patients show raised inflammatory markers. Whether that is cause,
consequence, or a shared upstream factor remains unsettled.

Genetics delivered a hard lesson. For years a story about a common variant in
the serotonin transporter gene interacting with life stress was among the most
cited findings in psychiatry, and it did not replicate when tested at scale.
What large genome-wide studies show instead is a very polygenic architecture:
enormous numbers of common variants, each contributing almost nothing, with
heavy overlap between depression, anxiety, and ordinary variation in
temperament.

Meanwhile the social evidence has held up better than any of the biology.
George Brown and Tirril Harris, working in London, found that events preceding
depression were not simply unpleasant but carried particular qualities — loss,
humiliation, entrapment — and that whether such an event was followed by
depression depended heavily on circumstances that had nothing to do with the
event itself. And behavioural activation, a treatment built on the plain
observation that depressed people withdraw from anything rewarding and that
reversing the withdrawal helps, performs about as well as approaches with far
more elaborate theories behind them.

Then ketamine complicated the timetable again. It acts on the glutamate system
rather than the monoamines, and in some patients its antidepressant effect
appears within hours rather than weeks — which means the slow onset everyone
had built theories around is not an inherent property of treating depression
at all.

Burton would have recognised the situation. He listed what he had observed,
refused to pretend he could rank it, and noted that the same complaint arrived
by many different roads. The roads are better mapped now. The suspicion that
they lead to more than one destination is newer, and is probably the most
useful thing the last forty years produced.
