# ADHD and the Problem of Drawing the Line

Take a classroom of children who all started school in the same September. The
ones born just after the cut-off date are nearly a year older than the ones
born just before it. That difference is invisible on paper and enormous in
practice at that age, and it shows up in an unsettling place: across many
countries, researchers have repeatedly found that the youngest children in a
school year are more likely to be diagnosed with attention deficit
hyperactivity disorder than the oldest. Move the cut-off date and you move
which children get diagnosed.

This finding is usually deployed as proof that the diagnosis is bogus. It is
better understood as a demonstration of what happens whenever you draw a
threshold across a continuously distributed trait and then ask adults to apply
it by comparing a child to the children sitting next to him.

## Early Sightings

Heinrich Hoffmann was a physician in Frankfurt who, in the 1840s, wrote an
illustrated book of cautionary verses for his young son because he could not
find one he liked in the shops. *Struwwelpeter* is mostly grotesque — children
who play with matches, a boy whose thumbs are cut off — but two of its figures
are oddly clinical. Fidgety Philip rocks his chair at dinner until he goes over
backwards and brings the tablecloth and everything on it down with him. Johnny
Head-in-Air walks along watching the swallows and falls in the river. Neither
is presented as ill. They are presented as recognisable.

The first properly clinical description is usually credited to George Still,
who delivered a series of lectures to the Royal College of Physicians in 1902.
Still described children of normal intelligence who nonetheless could not
sustain attention, could not defer gratification, and could not consistently
govern their own behaviour in line with what they themselves knew they should
do. He framed this as a defect of moral control, which reads badly now, but he
also noted that it tended to run in families, that it appeared more often in
boys, and that it frequently coexisted with minor physical anomalies — none of
which he could have known by moralising. His framing has aged badly; his
observations have not.

Then the encephalitis lethargica pandemic that followed the First World War
left a great many surviving children restless, impulsive, distractible, and
volatile in ways they had not been before the infection. That produced a
category — post-encephalitic behaviour disorder — and an inference. If brain
injury could produce this behaviour, perhaps children who showed the behaviour
without any known infection had sustained brain injury too. The label became
minimal brain damage, and then, once it became clear that no damage could
actually be found, minimal brain dysfunction. For several decades a widely used
diagnosis rested on an unobserved lesion.

## The Accident at the Bradley Home

Charles Bradley ran a residential centre for children with behavioural and
neurological problems in Rhode Island. In the 1930s the available way to image
a child's brain was to drain cerebrospinal fluid and replace it with air, a
procedure that left patients with severe headaches for days. Bradley reasoned
that a stimulant might push the choroid plexus to produce replacement fluid
faster, and in 1937 he tried Benzedrine on a group of children who had been
through it.

The headaches were largely unaffected. What the staff noticed instead was that
a substantial minority of the children became strikingly better at schoolwork —
calmer, more persistent, more accurate, and in some cases visibly relieved. The
children themselves called them the arithmetic pills. Bradley published the
observation, remarked that it was surprising for a stimulant to produce
subdued behaviour, and the finding then sat almost unused for two decades while
child psychiatry was occupied elsewhere.

That apparent paradox became folklore: stimulants calm hyperactive children and
excite everyone else, so responding to one must prove the underlying biology is
different. Judith Rapoport's group tested the claim directly in the late 1970s
and found it false. Children without the diagnosis also showed improved
attention and reduced motor activity. Response to a treatment is not a
diagnostic test — a principle that holds across medicine, and is repeatedly
forgotten.

## Renaming the Thing, Five Times

The second edition of the American diagnostic manual, in 1968, called it
hyperkinetic reaction of childhood, putting the emphasis squarely on motor
overactivity. The third edition, in 1980, inverted that and called it attention
deficit disorder, with or without hyperactivity — a change that followed
Virginia Douglas's research in Montreal arguing that the core problems were
sustained attention and impulse control, and that hyperactivity was a
consequence rather than the thing itself. A revision in 1987 merged the
subtypes back into a single attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder. The
fourth edition, in 1994, split them again into three subtypes. The fifth kept
the three but called them presentations, raised the age by which symptoms must
have appeared from seven to twelve, dropped the prohibition on diagnosing it
alongside autism, and reduced the number of symptoms required for older
adolescents and adults.

This looks like committee churn, and some of it was. But each renaming was
attached to a real argument about what the central problem is, and the
arguments have not finished.

## What the Current Models Say

Russell Barkley's account places behavioural inhibition at the centre. The
primary difficulty is not attending but pausing — interrupting the immediately
prepotent response to make room for anything else. A great many capacities
depend on that pause: holding information in mind while you work on it, talking
yourself through a sequence, anticipating consequences, damping an emotional
reaction before it is expressed. Weaken the inhibition and the rest degrades in
predictable ways, which is why clinicians routinely see intense emotional
reactivity in these patients even though the diagnostic criteria never mention
it.

Edmund Sonuga-Barke proposed a second route. Some children have an altered
response to delay itself: waiting is genuinely more aversive to them than it is
to others, and behaviour that shortens or fills a delay — fidgeting, switching
tasks, leaving the room, seeking something more stimulating — is a sensible
response to that valuation rather than a failure of control. Two different
underlying problems, one very similar appearance from the front of a classroom.

Both accounts make better sense of the phenomenology than the name does. The
striking feature people report is not an inability to concentrate but an
inability to direct concentration by anything other than immediate interest.
Hours of absorbed, uninterruptible focus on something compelling coexists with
total inability to start something dull, and this looks like a contradiction
only if you imagine attention as a quantity someone has more or less of rather
than as a resource that has to be allocated.

The neuroscience supports this at the level of group averages and no further.
There are average differences in the maturation of frontal networks, in
dopamine signalling around reward and the timing of reinforcement, and in how
reliably the brain switches out of its default resting mode into task-focused
processing. The distributions overlap heavily. No scan diagnoses anybody, and
none is close to it.

Heritability is high — among the highest of any psychiatric presentation, with
twin studies consistently placing most of the variation in these traits on the
genetic side, in the same broad range as physical characteristics like height.
That genetic contribution is spread across enormous numbers of common variants
of tiny individual effect, overlapping substantially with other conditions.
There is no ADHD gene, and there will not be one.

## The Line, and Who It Moves For

Attention, activity, and impulsivity are continuously distributed in the
population, like blood pressure or height. There is no natural gap in the
distribution where a category could be cut without a decision. The diagnosis
puts the cut where impairment appears — where the traits stop being a
description of someone and start interfering with their education, their work,
their safety, or their relationships.

Nothing about that is unscientific. Hypertension is defined exactly the same
way, by consequences rather than by a discontinuity in nature. But it does mean
that how many people have the condition depends partly on where the line is
drawn and who is drawing it, and that the same child can be over the line in a
rigid school and under it in a flexible one. The setting is part of the unit of
analysis. That is not an argument that the difficulty is imaginary — the
impairment is entirely real to the person living it — but it does explain why
prevalence moves around so much, and why the relative age effect exists.

It also explains who gets missed. The inattentive presentation without
conspicuous hyperactivity inconveniences nobody except the person who has it. A
child who is quietly elsewhere for most of the school day generates no
complaints from adults, and so for decades girls in particular were identified
far less often than boys with similar underlying difficulty. A substantial
share of adult diagnoses now come from people encountering a description of
childhood inattention and recognising something nobody ever asked them about.
Whether adults can develop the condition without having had it as children
remains genuinely contested — some long-running cohort studies have found such
cases, and the disagreement about what they represent is unresolved.

None of this settles into a comfortable position. The traits themselves are not
inherently pathological, and whether a low threshold for switching attention is
a liability depends on what an environment rewards; long stretches of
sedentary, self-directed, deadline-governed work are a recent and peculiar
demand to place on a nervous system. But the costs are real too — in education
cut short, in jobs lost, in accidents — and pretending otherwise abandons the
people who need something done. The useful argument is not whether the
condition is real. It is what a threshold is for. A threshold is a decision
about who receives help, and it ought to be defended as one rather than
presented as a discovery.
