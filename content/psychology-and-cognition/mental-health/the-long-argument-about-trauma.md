# The Long Argument About Trauma

The Victorian railways produced a new kind of casualty. People walked away from
collisions with nothing broken, went home, and then could not work. They
trembled. They slept badly or not at all. Some could not board a train again.
A London surgeon named John Erichsen described the pattern in the 1860s and
proposed that the shaking of a high-speed crash had concussed the spinal cord
itself — an invisible lesion producing visible ruin. The condition was called
railway spine.

Herbert Page, a surgeon working for one of the railway companies, disagreed. No
lesion had ever been found at autopsy in such cases, and the symptoms appeared
in people who had been badly frightened without being physically shaken at all.
The cause, he argued, was fright.

The dispute was scientific, and it was also about money, because the railways
were facing compensation claims and the answer determined who paid. That
entanglement has never since let go of the subject. Whether an injury you
cannot see is real, and who is obliged to do something about it, has been
argued in courtrooms, pension tribunals, and military disciplinary hearings for
a hundred and sixty years, and the science has never been allowed to proceed
independently of the argument.

## The Wars

By the end of the nineteenth century, Jean-Martin Charcot in Paris was
demonstrating hysteria to audiences at the Salpêtrière, and his student Pierre
Janet had proposed something that still sounds current: that overwhelming
experience can fail to be integrated with the rest of a person's memory, and
then persist as a split-off fragment that intrudes on its own terms. Josef
Breuer and the young Freud began from a similar place, holding that hysterics
suffered from reminiscences. Freud's subsequent retreat from his claim that
such reminiscences were commonly of childhood sexual abuse is among the most
argued-over episodes in the history of the field, and the arguments have not
converged.

Then the First World War produced casualties on a scale that made the question
unavoidable. Men came out of the line mute, blind, paralysed, shaking
uncontrollably, unable to stop weeping. Charles Myers used the term shell shock
in *The Lancet* in 1915, and the initial theory was mechanical: the blast wave
from high-explosive shells was damaging the brain directly. The theory ran into
the fact that identical cases appeared in men who had never been close to an
explosion.

Military authorities found the ambiguity intolerable, because the two available
interpretations had entirely different consequences. If it was an injury, the
man was entitled to treatment, a pension, and his honour. If it was not, he was
a coward, and the army had established procedures for cowardice. Men were shot.
British pardons for some of those executions came only in the twenty-first
century.

W. H. R. Rivers, an anthropologist and physician treating officers at
Craiglockhart near Edinburgh, went against the prevailing advice. The standard
instruction was that a patient should put the experience out of his mind and
think of other things. Rivers observed that his patients were doing exactly
that all day and dreaming it all night, and concluded that the memory had to be
faced, talked through, and reworked into something bearable rather than sealed
off. His patients included Siegfried Sassoon.

The Second World War repeated the lesson from the beginning. The new terms were
combat fatigue and battle exhaustion, and the new insight — which was really the
old one — was that breakdown was a function of cumulative exposure rather than
of constitutional weakness. Given enough time under fire, everyone had a limit.
Military psychiatry developed a doctrine of treating men near the front,
immediately, with the explicit expectation that they would return to duty. It
worked better than evacuation, and its humanitarian and military purposes were
never cleanly separable. Abram Kardiner, writing in 1941, gave a description of
the chronic syndrome — startle, irritability, nightmares, a life narrowed around
avoiding reminders — that a clinician today would recognise without
difficulty.

And then it was forgotten again. The first American diagnostic manual included
a category for gross stress reaction; the second edition, published in 1968,
dropped it. This is the recurring shape of the subject. The knowledge is
acquired during a war or a scandal, loses its constituency, and disappears,
because attention to trauma has never been self-sustaining. It requires a
movement behind it.

## How the Diagnosis Got Into the Book

Two movements supplied one in the 1970s. Vietnam veterans, organised and
angry, had been running informal rap groups with sympathetic psychiatrists,
and pressed for a diagnosis that would locate the injury in what had happened
rather than in the character of the man it happened to. At the same time the
women's movement was making rape, battering, and incest visible as common
events rather than rare aberrations. Ann Burgess and Lynda Holmstrom,
interviewing women who came through a Boston emergency room, described a
consistent pattern of aftermath they called rape trauma syndrome.

The two pictures matched. The nightmares, the numbness, the hypervigilance, the
sudden intrusions, the avoidance of anything resembling the original scene —
these were the same in a rifleman and in a woman who had never been near a
war. Post-traumatic stress disorder entered the third edition of the manual in
1980.

It was, and remains, an unusual diagnosis. Nearly every other category in the
manual describes a pattern of symptoms and says nothing about what caused it.
This one writes the cause into the definition: there must have been an event,
and the event must meet a standard. That makes it the place where clinical
judgment, legal judgment, and entitlement collide. Successive editions have
argued over exactly how wide the gate should be — whether learning of a
violent death counts, whether watching footage counts, whether repeated
occupational exposure to the aftermath of violence counts — and every
adjustment has consequences for compensation systems. Page and Erichsen would
recognise the stakes exactly.

## What the Diagnosis Says Memory Does

The most distinctive feature is the quality of the intrusions. An ordinary
memory is retrieved deliberately, arrives with a sense of pastness, and can be
put down again. Traumatic intrusions frequently do none of this. They are
sensory rather than narrative — a smell, a sound, a fragment of visual
detail — they arrive uninvited when something in the environment matches a cue,
and they carry a disconcerting quality of happening now. Meanwhile deliberate
recall of the same event is often patchy and disordered.

Anke Ehlers and David Clark built an influential account around this: the
memory was encoded with poor elaboration and poor contextual grounding, so it
sits outside the ordinary autobiographical record and is triggered by
perceptual resemblance rather than retrieved by intention. The therapeutic
implication follows directly, and it is Rivers's implication. Effective
approaches involve engaging with the memory in a controlled way and giving it a
context and an ending, rather than managing life around not touching it.

The 1990s brought the memory wars, when claims of recovered repressed memories
of abuse — some emerging under suggestive questioning — collided with
experimental work, much of it by Elizabeth Loftus, showing how readily
confident and detailed memories of events that never happened can be
manufactured. Families were destroyed in both directions. The settlement that
eventually emerged is uncomfortable and correct: most people who were abused
remember it, memory is genuinely malleable under suggestion, and the second
fact does not refute the first.

## The Edges of the Concept

The most consistently surprising finding in this literature is how many people
are fine. Most people exposed to a potentially traumatic event do not develop a
disorder. George Bonanno's work tracking people over time after bereavements
and disasters found that the most common trajectory by a wide margin is a
period of disturbance followed by recovery, and that this is ordinary rather
than exceptional. What best predicts a bad outcome is less the objective
severity of the event than what surrounded it — prior history, how threatened
the person felt at the time, and above all the quality of support afterwards.
Single-session debriefing delivered to everyone immediately after a disaster,
which seemed obviously helpful, turned out not to help, and there is reason to
think it can interfere with a recovery that would have happened anyway.

Judith Herman's *Trauma and Recovery*, published in 1992, made the argument
that the single-incident template fits prolonged, inescapable, repeated
trauma badly — captivity, chronic abuse in childhood, coercive control — and
that these produce broader damage to emotion regulation, self-concept, and the
capacity for relationship. The World Health Organization's eleventh revision
of its classification adopted a version of complex post-traumatic stress
disorder; the American manual has not.

Since then the word has travelled a long way from the railway carriage. Work
linking adverse experiences in childhood to health outcomes decades later moved
trauma into public health, and from there into ordinary speech, where it now
covers everything from torture to a difficult performance review. The
psychologist Nick Haslam calls this concept creep, and the accounting is
genuinely mixed: broader recognition has brought services and sympathy to
people who previously got neither, while stretching a clinical term until it
strains, and encouraging some people to interpret ordinary adversity as injury
in a way that may work against the recovery most of them would otherwise make.

Herman's three stages — establishing safety, then remembering and mourning,
then reconnecting with ordinary life — have held up better than the diagnostic
criteria surrounding them. The third stage is the one that keeps getting
overlooked, and it is not something a person does alone. Rivers worked that
out in a hospital in Scotland while the guns were still firing, and the field
has been mislaying and rediscovering it ever since.
