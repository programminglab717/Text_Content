# The Many Ways Sleep Goes Wrong

It is quarter past three and someone is doing arithmetic in the dark. If I fall
asleep in the next twenty minutes that is still four and a half hours, which is
survivable, but the alarm is at seven forty and the meeting is at nine. The
arithmetic is the problem. Nobody has ever calculated their way into
unconsciousness, and the mental effort of trying guarantees the outcome it is
meant to prevent.

That scene belongs to one specific disorder. Sleep medicine, which barely
existed as a discipline before the 1970s, now recognises dozens, and they fail
in genuinely different ways — some are problems of timing, some of breathing,
some of the boundary between sleep and waking failing to hold. Lumping them
together as trouble sleeping is roughly as useful as lumping chest pain
together as trouble with the chest.

## What a Normal Night Is Doing

Two systems decide when you sleep. The first is a pressure that builds the
longer you are awake, tracked loosely by the accumulation of adenosine and
other byproducts of neural metabolism, and discharged by sleep itself.
Caffeine works by blocking adenosine receptors: the pressure is still there, it
just stops being reported. The second is a circadian oscillator in the
suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, a clock of around twenty-four
hours that runs whether or not you sleep and that is reset daily by light
striking specialised retinal cells containing melanopsin. Good sleep happens
when the pressure is high and the clock is pointing at night. When those two
signals disagree — as they do in jet lag and on every night shift — sleep gets
short, broken, and unrefreshing regardless of how tired the person is.

The night itself is structured. Sleep descends through light stages into
slow-wave sleep, the deep phase dominated by large synchronised cortical
oscillations, then ascends into REM, where the cortex is nearly as active as in
waking, the eyes move, dreams become vivid and narrative, and the brainstem
paralyses the skeletal muscles so the body does not act any of it out. That
cycle repeats roughly every ninety minutes, with slow-wave sleep concentrated
in the first third of the night and REM in the last. Wake someone at five in
the morning and you take mostly REM from them; keep them up until two and you
take mostly slow-wave sleep. The deficits that follow are not the same.

## Insomnia: A Disorder of Trying

Chronic insomnia is usually described with a three-part model. Some people are
predisposed — light sleepers, anxious temperaments, a family history. Something
precipitates it: bereavement, a newborn, a diagnosis, a deadline season. And
then perpetuating factors take over, which is where the disorder actually
lives. The person starts going to bed earlier to catch up, spending nine hours
in bed to get six hours of fragmented sleep. The bed, previously a neutral
object, becomes reliably associated with lying awake and worrying about lying
awake. What began as a reaction to a life event becomes a conditioned response
that outlives it by years.

Physiological measures in chronic insomnia tend to show hyperarousal rather
than sleep deprivation: elevated cortisol, higher metabolic rate, faster
cortical rhythms even during sleep. That helps explain the odd phenomenon
sometimes called paradoxical insomnia, where a person sincerely reports having
been awake all night while the recording shows several hours of sleep. They are
not lying. Sleep that is shot through with brief cortical arousals appears to
be experienced, and remembered, as wakefulness.

This is why the best-evidenced treatment for chronic insomnia is behavioural
rather than pharmacological, and why sleeping tablets, which work well for a
few nights, are poor long-term answers — they add sleep without unwinding the
conditioning, and tolerance tends to erode the benefit.

## Breathing That Stops

Obstructive sleep apnoea is a plumbing problem. During sleep the muscles
holding the upper airway open relax, and in people whose airway is already
narrow — through weight, jaw shape, tonsil size, or simple anatomical luck —
the airway collapses. Breathing stops. Oxygen falls, carbon dioxide rises, and
the brain produces a brief arousal, enough to restore muscle tone and reopen
the airway, usually not enough for the sleeper to remember. Then it happens
again. In severe cases the cycle repeats hundreds of times a night.

The person often has no idea. What they report is that they sleep eight hours
and wake unrefreshed, fall asleep in meetings, and have a headache in the
morning; a partner reports snoring punctuated by alarming silences. Because
each arousal triggers a surge of sympathetic activity, the consequences reach
well beyond tiredness — the condition is strongly linked to hypertension,
atrial fibrillation, stroke, and impaired glucose regulation, and the daytime
sleepiness makes it a serious road-safety matter. It is common, and it is
badly under-diagnosed, partly because the cardinal symptom happens while the
patient is unconscious.

A separate and rarer form, central sleep apnoea, is not obstructive at all: the
brainstem simply stops sending the signal to breathe, often in a waxing and
waning pattern seen in heart failure. The airway is open the whole time.

## Clocks Pointing the Wrong Way

Delayed sleep phase is the commonest circadian disorder and is routinely
mistaken for insomnia or laziness. The internal clock runs late, so the
person cannot fall asleep until two or three in the morning and cannot wake at
seven. Given a free schedule they sleep a normal quantity at abnormal hours and
feel fine. Forced onto a school or office timetable they accumulate a chronic
deficit. It is most common in adolescence, when the clock biologically shifts
later, which is the argument behind later school start times.

The mirror image, advanced sleep phase, drags sleep into the early evening and
wakefulness into the small hours, and becomes commoner with age. Blind people
with no light perception at all often develop a non-24-hour rhythm, drifting
later each day as their clock free-runs without the light signal that would
otherwise correct it, cycling in and out of phase with the world over weeks.
Shift work imposes the same conflict artificially, and no amount of
determination makes the clock agree.

Melatonin is widely misunderstood here. It is not a sedative. The pineal gland
releases it as a darkness signal, and taken as a supplement its main useful
effect is to shift the clock, which depends far more on when it is taken than
on how much.

## When Sleep and Waking Blur

Several disorders come from the boundary between states failing to hold.

Narcolepsy is the clearest case. In its classic form it involves the loss of a
small population of hypothalamic neurons that produce orexin, a peptide that
stabilises wakefulness. Without them the states become unstable in both
directions: irresistible daytime sleep attacks, fragmented night sleep, and
intrusions of REM phenomena into waking life. Sleep paralysis is REM atonia
persisting after the person has woken. Hypnagogic hallucinations are dream
imagery arriving before sleep does. Cataplexy — sudden loss of muscle tone
triggered by emotion, most reliably laughter — is REM paralysis breaking into
full consciousness, the person collapsing while entirely aware. The evidence
points to an autoimmune attack on the orexin neurons, strengthened
considerably when a spike in childhood narcolepsy followed the use of one
particular adjuvanted influenza vaccine in Europe around the 2009 pandemic.

The NREM parasomnias work differently. Sleepwalking, night terrors, and
confusional arousals emerge from deep slow-wave sleep, typically in the first
couple of hours, when part of the brain arouses and part stays asleep. The
motor and emotional systems come online; the systems that would supply
judgement and lay down memory do not. Hence the blank face, the complex but
purposeless behaviour, and the total amnesia the next morning. They run in
families, are commonest in children, and are reliably primed by sleep
deprivation, alcohol, fever, and anything else that deepens slow-wave sleep or
increases arousals from it.

Restless legs syndrome sits at the edge of this territory: an unpleasant urge
to move the legs that appears at rest, worsens in the evening, and is relieved
briefly by movement. It is associated with low iron availability in the brain
and with dopaminergic signalling, and it is a sensory-motor disorder that
wrecks sleep rather than a disorder of sleep itself.

The most consequential of the group is REM sleep behaviour disorder, in which
the paralysis that should accompany REM fails, and the sleeper acts out the
dream — punching, shouting, leaping from the bed, sometimes injuring a partner.
For a long time this looked like a curiosity. Then long-term follow-up studies
found something that has reshaped an entire area of neurology: the large
majority of people with the idiopathic form eventually develop Parkinson's
disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, or a related condition, often more than a
decade later. The brainstem nuclei that maintain REM atonia are among the first
casualties of the underlying pathology. A man shouting in his sleep at
fifty-five may be showing the earliest visible sign of a disease that will not
announce itself properly until he is seventy — which is why those patients are
now the population in which treatments meant to slow such diseases are most
keenly studied.
