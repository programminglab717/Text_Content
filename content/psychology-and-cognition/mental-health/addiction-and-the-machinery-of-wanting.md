# Addiction and the Machinery of Wanting

Ask a long-term smoker whether they enjoy cigarettes and you will often get a
flat no. The first one of the day, maybe. The rest taste of nothing much, cost
money they resent spending, and are followed by a small self-recrimination.
They smoke twenty a day anyway. This is the central puzzle of addiction, and
any account that cannot explain it is not worth much: the compulsion routinely
outlives the pleasure. People continue pursuing something they no longer like,
at escalating cost, while knowing exactly what it is doing to them.

For most of recorded history the explanation was character. Drunkenness was a
vice, appetite indulged where it should have been governed, and the remedy was
shame, punishment, or religious conversion. The twentieth century mostly
replaced that with a disease model — addiction as a chronic, relapsing brain
condition, no more a moral failing than diabetes. That shift did real good. It
moved people from jails toward clinics and gave families a framework that did
not require them to conclude their son was simply bad. But it also flattened
something, and the current picture is less tidy than either story.

## What the Drug Does to a Reward Circuit

Almost every substance people become addicted to converges on one piece of
neural plumbing: the dopamine projection from the midbrain to the ventral
striatum. Stimulants act on it directly, blocking or reversing the transporters
that clear dopamine from the synapse. Opioids and alcohol act indirectly, by
suppressing inhibitory neurons that normally keep dopamine cells in check.
Nicotine acts on receptors sitting on those same cells. The routes differ; the
destination does not.

It is tempting to read that circuit as a pleasure centre, but the evidence
points somewhere more interesting. Recordings from dopamine neurons show they
fire not in proportion to how good something is, but in proportion to how much
better it is than expected. An unanticipated reward produces a burst. Once a
cue reliably predicts the reward, the burst migrates backward onto the cue, and
the reward itself — now fully expected — produces almost nothing. If the
predicted reward fails to arrive, the neurons go quiet below baseline. This is
a teaching signal, not a feeling. Its job is to tag whatever preceded a
better-than-expected outcome as worth pursuing again.

Drugs subvert this by producing a dopamine signal pharmacologically rather than
through any actual improvement in circumstances. The signal cannot be predicted
away, because it does not come from the world; it comes from the chemistry. So
the tagging never stops. Every repetition stamps in the cues — the lighter, the
particular street corner, the sound of a can opening, the friend you always
used with — a little deeper.

Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson built a theory around exactly the dissociation
the smoker describes. They proposed that the brain runs wanting and liking on
partly separate systems: liking, the hedonic impact of a reward, handled by a
small and fragile set of opioid-sensitive sites, and wanting, the motivational
pull toward it, handled by dopamine. Repeated drug use sensitises the wanting
system while the liking system, if anything, habituates. The result is craving
that grows as enjoyment shrinks — not a metaphor but a measurable divergence.
Cue-triggered craving can be intense in someone who would tell you honestly
that the drug stopped working years ago.

Alongside this, the prefrontal systems that ordinarily weigh a distant
consequence against an immediate one function less well, both as a consequence
of heavy use and, in many people, as a predisposing trait. Steeper discounting
of the future is one of the more reliable findings in the field. The drug
becomes more vivid; the argument against it becomes thinner and further away.

## Withdrawal Is Not the Engine

The folk model of addiction puts withdrawal at the centre: the body adapts, the
drug is removed, the person feels appalling, and takes more to stop feeling
appalling. Physical dependence is real, sometimes medically dangerous — alcohol
and benzodiazepine withdrawal can kill — and it certainly sustains use in the
short term.

But it cannot be the engine, because removing it does not fix anything. Medical
detoxification reliably clears a person of a drug within days. Relapse rates
after detox alone are dismal, and relapse frequently happens months later, long
after any physiological adaptation has reversed, often triggered by a cue or a
stressor rather than by discomfort. Meanwhile people become severely addicted
to substances with mild or negligible withdrawal syndromes. What persists is
not the body's protest. It is the learning.

This is why some researchers, Marc Lewis among them, have pushed back on the
brain disease framing — not by denying the neural changes, which are well
documented, but by pointing out that learning changes brains too. A London
taxi driver's hippocampus changes. Falling in love changes dopamine signalling.
Showing that addiction has a neural signature does not by itself establish that
it belongs in the same category as Parkinson's. The counterargument is that the
disease framing earns its keep clinically and politically, and that a condition
can be both a learning phenomenon and a legitimate target for medicine.

## Context Does More Work Than People Expect

Two findings from the 1970s continue to embarrass any purely pharmacological
account. The first came out of Vietnam, where a large fraction of American
servicemen used heroin and many met criteria for addiction. Lee Robins followed
them home, expecting the worst. The great majority simply stopped, and did not
resume. The second was Bruce Alexander's Rat Park, in which rats housed in a
large enriched enclosure with company and space consumed markedly less
morphine solution than rats in bare isolation cages. The study has methodological
critics and an uneven replication record, and it should not be leaned on too
heavily, but the direction of its finding has been corroborated many times: an
animal or a person with something else going on is a less willing customer.

What changed for the veterans was everything except the pharmacology — the
cues, the peer group, the availability, the boredom, the fear. Set and setting
are not soft factors layered on top of the chemistry. They are part of the
mechanism, because the mechanism is one of learned association with a context.

The same logic runs through the epidemiology. Addiction clusters where
opportunity has thinned out, where communities have lost the institutions that
structured people's time, and where physical pain and psychological pain go
untreated. The economists Anne Case and Angus Deaton grouped overdose,
alcoholic liver disease, and suicide together as deaths of despair precisely
because the three track each other across places and cohorts in a way that
drug supply alone does not explain. Supply matters enormously — the potency of
what is available determines who dies — but it does not by itself determine who
reaches for it.

There is also a fact that rarely makes the news: most people who develop a
substance use disorder eventually stop. Many do so without formal treatment,
often in their late twenties and thirties, as responsibilities and identities
shift. Clinicians used to call this maturing out. It is invisible to treatment
services, which see the people for whom it did not happen, and that sampling
bias has shaped professional intuitions about how intractable the condition is.

Timing is part of the explanation on both ends. Addiction is overwhelmingly a
disorder that starts young. The great majority of people who develop one began
using heavily in adolescence or early adulthood, during the long window in
which reward circuitry is already running hot and the prefrontal systems that
restrain it are still being built. A brain in that state learns fast, which is
the point of adolescence, and learns drug associations fast as well. Someone
who reaches thirty without a serious problem is statistically unlikely to
acquire one, which is why prevention efforts concentrate on delay rather than
on lifelong vigilance.

The other consistent risk signal is early adversity. Childhood abuse, neglect,
and household chaos show a graded relationship with later substance problems —
more exposure, higher risk — and the relationship holds after the obvious
confounders are accounted for. The mechanism is not mysterious. A drug that
reliably shuts off an intolerable internal state is doing something valuable
for the person taking it, and the more intolerable the baseline, the more
valuable it is. Clinicians who have absorbed this tend to stop asking why
someone is still using and start asking what the using is for.

## What Helps

The most striking evidence in the field concerns opioid agonist treatment —
methadone and buprenorphine. These are not, as the persistent criticism has it,
merely swapping one drug for another. A long-acting agonist taken on a stable
schedule occupies the receptor without the sharp peaks and troughs that drive
the cycle, which lets a person work, sleep, and hold a life together. Across
many studies and settings, retention in this kind of treatment is associated
with substantially lower mortality than detoxification-and-abstinence
approaches. Medications for alcohol exist too, with more modest effects.

On the behavioural side, the best-evidenced intervention is also the least
fashionable: contingency management, which provides small tangible rewards for
verified abstinence. It works, particularly for stimulants where no medication
has proved reliably useful, and it has been chronically underused because
paying people to stop offends moral intuitions that the evidence does not
share. Cognitive and motivational therapies help, mutual-aid fellowships help
many people and suit others poorly, and harm reduction — naloxone
distribution, clean supplies, supervised consumption — keeps people alive long
enough for any of the rest to apply.

The diagnostic manuals have quietly caught up with the shape of the thing.
Where earlier editions split abuse from dependence, the current one treats
substance use disorder as a single dimension running from mild to severe, and
has admitted gambling as an addiction in its own right — an acknowledgement
that the machinery does not require a molecule to capture.
