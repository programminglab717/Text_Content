# Autism as a Neurodevelopmental Difference

A fluorescent tube in a school corridor flickers at a frequency most people
never consciously register. It is there in the visual field as a faint,
restless instability, and it hums. For a great many autistic children that hum
is not background. It is foreground, competing for attention with the teacher's
voice, the scrape of chairs, the smell of the canteen two doors down, and the
fact that the seam of a sock has rotated half a centimetre around the foot. The
child who melts down in that corridor is often described as having behavioural
problems. It is at least as accurate to say the corridor is loud in ways nobody
else has noticed.

That reframing — from something wrong with the person to a genuinely different
way of taking in and processing the world — is the shift that has reshaped how
autism is understood over the past three or four decades. It is not a shift
that dissolves difficulty. Autistic people can need very substantial support,
and some need it every day of their lives. But the question has moved from what
is broken to what is different, and from how to make an autistic person look
less autistic to how to make the world more navigable for someone whose wiring
is not the common one.

## Eleven Children, and a Very Bad Theory

The word autism predates the concept. Eugen Bleuler used it early in the
twentieth century to describe a withdrawal into inner life he saw in
schizophrenia, borrowing the Greek for self. The modern usage begins in the
1940s, when Leo Kanner in Baltimore described a small group of children who,
from very early in life, appeared to have an unusual relationship to other
people and a powerful need for sameness in their surroundings. At almost the
same moment, and apparently independently, Hans Asperger in Vienna described
children with similar social differences and intense circumscribed interests
but fluent, often precocious speech.

Kanner's papers are careful and observant, but he made one remark that did
enormous damage. He noticed that the parents who brought these children to him
tended to be professionals — doctors, academics, engineers — and described some
of them as emotionally cool. From this, and from the psychoanalytic climate of
the period, grew the idea that autism was caused by inadequate maternal warmth.
Bruno Bettelheim popularised the notion of the refrigerator mother, and for
roughly two decades mothers were told, by clinicians, that their children's
condition was their own doing. It was wrong, it was cruel, and it took
sustained pushback — notably from Bernard Rimland, a psychologist and the
father of an autistic son, whose 1964 book argued for a biological basis — to
break its grip.

What Kanner had actually noticed, in retrospect, was heritability. Autistic
traits run in families. The parents who struck him as detached and technical
were, in many cases, autistic themselves.

## From a Narrow Diagnosis to a Spectrum

For a long time autism was understood as rare and severe: a childhood condition
involving little or no speech and marked intellectual disability. The person
most responsible for widening that picture was Lorna Wing, a British
psychiatrist whose own daughter was autistic. Working with Judith Gould on a
population survey in a London borough, Wing found children who did not fit the
narrow Kanner profile but who shared a recognisable cluster of differences in
social interaction, communication, and imagination — what she called a triad.
Crucially, these children varied enormously in language and intellectual
ability. Wing also brought Asperger's work to English-speaking attention in the
early 1980s, and it was her framing that introduced the idea of an autism
spectrum.

Diagnostic manuals followed, slowly. Infantile autism entered the American
diagnostic system in 1980 as a condition distinct from childhood
schizophrenia — itself an important correction. Subsequent revisions added
Asperger's disorder and other subtypes, then the 2013 revision collapsed them
back into a single category, autism spectrum disorder, on the grounds that
clinicians could not reliably distinguish the subtypes from one another and
that the boundaries between them did not track anything meaningful in the
underlying biology. The same revision reorganised the triad into two domains,
social communication and restricted or repetitive behaviours, and formally
recognised sensory differences as a core feature rather than an incidental one.

Diagnosis rates rose steeply across this period, and the reason is mostly the
definition. When the criteria widen, when clinicians are trained to recognise
presentations they previously missed, when services are attached to the label
so parents pursue it, and when children who would once have been diagnosed with
intellectual disability or a language disorder are now diagnosed with autism
instead — a phenomenon researchers call diagnostic substitution — the counted
numbers go up without any change in the underlying population. That is not the
whole explanation, and researchers continue to argue about the residual. But it
is most of it.

The one explanation that has been thoroughly excluded is vaccination. The 1998
paper that proposed a link between the MMR vaccine and autism was retracted by
the journal that published it; its lead author lost his medical licence over
the conduct of the research. Very large epidemiological studies across several
countries, comparing vaccinated and unvaccinated children, have found no
association. The idea nonetheless persists, partly because the age at which
autistic traits become obvious happens to fall near the age of routine
immunisation, and coincidence in time is the raw material of causal intuition.

## What the Biology Supports

Autism is among the most heritable of neurodevelopmental conditions. Twin and
family studies point consistently in that direction, though heritability is a
population statistic and says nothing about the origin of any individual
person's autism. The genetic architecture is not one gene but hundreds,
possibly thousands, of common variants each contributing a small amount, plus a
smaller set of rare and sometimes spontaneous mutations with much larger
individual effects, several of which also raise the likelihood of intellectual
disability or epilepsy.

There is no brain scan that diagnoses autism, and no blood test. Group-level
differences in brain development have been reported — in early growth
trajectories, in the balance of long-range and local connectivity, in how
sensory cortex responds to repeated stimuli — but the effects are averages with
wide overlap, and they have not translated into anything usable for an
individual. Diagnosis remains what it has always been: skilled observation,
developmental history, and structured interview.

A useful way to hold all this is that autism is developmental rather than
degenerative. It is not something that arrives and then progresses. It is a
difference in how a nervous system builds itself from very early on, which
means it shapes everything downstream — how language is acquired, how attention
is allocated, how social inference develops — and which is also why the way it
presents changes enormously across a lifetime even though the underlying
difference does not.

## The View from Inside

Autistic self-advocates and autistic researchers have contributed ideas that
clinical observation alone did not produce. One is monotropism: the proposal
that autistic attention tends to pool deeply into a small number of interests
at a time, rather than spreading thinly across many. That single idea does a
surprising amount of work. It explains the depth of special interests, the
distress of interruption and transition, the apparent inattention when the
topic is not the one currently occupying the channel, and the flow states that
autistic people often describe as the best part of being autistic.

Another is the double empathy problem, articulated by the autistic researcher
Damian Milton. The conventional account holds that autistic people have a
deficit in reading other minds. Milton pointed out that the failure is mutual:
non-autistic people are just as bad at reading autistic expressions,
intonation, and intent, and misjudge autistic people's sincerity and emotion
routinely. Studies of information passed along chains of people support this —
autistic-to-autistic communication transmits information about as reliably as
non-autistic chains, while mixed chains degrade. The difficulty is not located
in one party. It sits in the gap.

A third is masking: the effortful suppression of natural behaviour to pass as
non-autistic. Scripting conversations in advance, rehearsing facial
expressions, suppressing the self-soothing movements collectively called
stimming. Masking is a skill, and it is often what allows an autistic adult to
hold a job. It is also expensive. Many autistic adults describe exhaustion and
functional collapse after sustained masking, and the pattern is one reason
autistic girls and women, who on average mask more effectively and whose
interests less often look stereotypically autistic, have been missed by
diagnosis for decades and are now being identified in adulthood in large
numbers.

## What Follows for Support

Reframing autism as a difference changes the goal of intervention. For most of
the twentieth century the implicit aim was normalisation — teaching the child
to sit still, make eye contact, and stop flapping, on the assumption that
looking less autistic was the same as doing better. Intensive behavioural
programmes built on that assumption remain contested, defended by some parents
and clinicians for the communication skills they can establish and criticised
by many autistic adults who experienced them as compliance training.

The alternative is not doing nothing. Autistic people have high rates of
co-occurring conditions — anxiety, epilepsy, gastrointestinal problems, ADHD,
sleep difficulty — and those are treatable in their own right. Communication
support, including non-speech methods, is genuinely enabling. So is adjusting
the environment: predictable routines, advance notice of change, quieter rooms,
written instructions instead of verbal ones, permission to stim. None of that
makes anyone less autistic. It makes a corridor that hums somewhat easier to
walk down.
