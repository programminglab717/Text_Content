# How We Know Whether Psychotherapy Works

In 1952 Hans Eysenck published a short, hostile paper arguing that there was no
evidence psychotherapy did anything at all. He assembled figures on patients
treated with psychoanalysis and with eclectic therapy, compared them against
records of untreated people discharged from hospitals and handled by insurance
companies, and concluded that roughly two-thirds of neurotic patients improved
substantially within two years whether or not anyone treated them. Treatment,
on his reading, was keeping pace with spontaneous remission and no better.

The paper's own evidence was poor. His comparison groups were not comparable to
his treated groups in severity, setting, or how improvement was judged, and
almost every subsequent analyst has said so. But the provocation was legitimate
and it landed, because at the time the field had almost nothing better to offer.
Psychotherapy was a set of theories with case histories attached. Eysenck's
challenge is the reason the outcome-research literature exists, and the
seventy-odd years since have been an extended, contested, and genuinely
informative attempt to answer him.

## Counting Up

The first serious answer came in the late 1970s, when Mary Lee Smith and Gene
Glass pooled hundreds of controlled studies using a then-novel technique for
combining results across experiments — a technique that helped establish
meta-analysis as a method in its own right. Their headline finding was that the
average treated person ended up better off than roughly eighty per cent of
untreated controls. Later and more careful syntheses have shaved that down, but
the basic conclusion has proved durable across thousands of trials: for
depression, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress, obsessive-compulsive
disorder, bulimia, insomnia, and several other conditions, structured
psychological treatment beats no treatment by a margin that is not plausibly an
artifact. Compared against medication for depression, the acute effects are
broadly similar, combining the two does better than either, and psychotherapy
tends to hold its advantage better after treatment ends — a difference that
makes intuitive sense, since a skill practised does not stop existing when the
sessions do.

So the crude question has an answer. The interesting questions are all
downstream of it.

## The Dodo Bird

Smith and Glass also found something awkward. When they compared therapies
against each other rather than against nothing, the differences mostly
evaporated. Behavioural, psychodynamic, humanistic, and eclectic approaches
produced roughly comparable outcomes.

This pattern already had a name. In 1936 Saul Rosenzweig had borrowed a line
from Alice in Wonderland, in which the Dodo adjudicates a chaotic race by
declaring that everybody has won and all must have prizes, and applied it to
competing schools of therapy. The dodo bird verdict has been argued about ever
since, and it has an obvious explanation: if all bona fide therapies work
through the same underlying processes, the branded differences between them
are decoration.

Those shared processes go under the heading of common factors, and the list is
less banal than it sounds. Being listened to by someone who is neither shocked
nor personally implicated is an unusual experience, and it is not available in
most people's lives at any price. Having formless distress converted into
something with a name, a shape, and an account of how it arose restores a sense
that events are intelligible, which by itself changes how threatening they
feel. Believing that the thing will help is not a nuisance variable to be
subtracted out; expectation demonstrably alters symptoms. Committing to a
weekly hour imposes a rhythm on a life that has usually lost one. And every
therapy, whatever it calls the procedure, involves saying out loud and
repeatedly the material a person has been organising their week around
avoiding.

The strongest single piece of evidence here concerns the therapeutic alliance —
the quality of the bond and the agreement on goals and tasks. Its correlation
with outcome is modest in size but is among the most consistently replicated
findings in the literature, holding across therapy types, disorders, and
measures. The honest caveat is that causal direction is hard to establish: a
patient who is already improving tends to feel warmer toward the person they
credit for it, so some of the alliance-outcome relationship runs backward.

There is also a finding that should make everyone cautious about head-to-head
comparisons. The single best predictor of which therapy wins a trial is often
which therapy the researchers running it believe in. This allegiance effect is
large, well documented, and not obviously dishonest — committed researchers
recruit better-trained therapists for their preferred arm, write better
manuals, and supervise more attentively.

## Where Specific Ingredients Do Show Up

The dodo verdict is not the whole story, and taken too far it becomes silly.
Some treatments clearly outperform others for specific problems, and the
clearest case is exposure.

For phobias, panic, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and post-traumatic stress,
treatments built on systematic, prolonged contact with the feared situation,
thought, or memory — while blocking the escape and reassurance behaviours that
normally cut it short — outperform generic supportive counselling by margins
that survive most of the corrections statisticians can apply. The mechanism has
a plausible learning account behind it: avoidance prevents the nervous system
from ever updating a prediction, and the treatment is essentially an
engineered opportunity for that update.

Dismantling studies, which strip components out of a package to see what
carries the effect, have produced other useful corrections. One influential
study of cognitive therapy for depression found that the behavioural component
alone — scheduling activity, restoring contact with things that once mattered —
performed about as well as the full package including the cognitive work, which
is part of why behavioural activation is now a treatment in its own right. The
eye movements in EMDR have fared less well under the same treatment, with
several analyses suggesting the therapy works largely through its exposure
component rather than through the bilateral stimulation that gives it its name.

So the field is not quite a flat plain. It is more like a plain with a few
hills on it.

## What the Trials Cannot Do Well

Psychotherapy research has structural problems that drug research does not, and
they all push in the direction of overstating benefit.

Nobody can be blinded. The patient knows whether they are receiving therapy;
the therapist certainly does; the person rating the outcome is frequently the
patient themselves, filling in a self-report questionnaire after months of
effort they would prefer not to have wasted. Control conditions make this
worse. A waitlist is not a placebo — it is a condition in which people are told
help is coming and asked to wait, which can actively suppress improvement.
Trials using waitlists produce systematically larger effects than trials using
active or treatment-as-usual comparisons.

Small trials with positive results get published; small trials with null
results often do not. When researchers such as Pim Cuijpers have applied
statistical corrections for this bias to the depression literature, the
estimated effects shrink considerably, though in most analyses they do not
vanish. There is also a slow deflation over time. Meta-analyses that plot measured
effects against publication year, rather than simply averaging them, have found
the benefit of some well-studied treatments shrinking as the decades pass.
Methodological quality improving is the charitable reading, and it is probably
the largest part of it: better randomisation and concealment, fuller accounting
for people who drop out, less reliance on outcome raters who know what they are
hoping to see. Broader and sicker patient populations account for some more.
Either way, an evidence base that gets weaker as it gets better run is telling
you something about the earlier estimates.

Follow-up is usually short, dropout is substantial and often analysed in ways
that flatter the treatment, and harm is barely measured at all. A small but
consistent minority of people get worse during psychotherapy, and until fairly
recently most trials did not bother to report deterioration rates, an omission
no drug trial would be permitted.

The largest gap, though, is between the trial and the clinic. Trials recruit
patients with one clearly defined problem, treated by supervised therapists
following a manual for a fixed number of sessions. Real services see people
with several problems at once, unstable housing, and therapists carrying
caseloads that make close supervision impossible. Effects in routine practice
are generally smaller than in trials, which is what one would expect and which
is rarely stated plainly to the people being referred.

Set against all that, one finding keeps surfacing and deserves more attention
than the brand wars ever got: therapists differ from each other more than
therapies do. When you can look across enough cases, some clinicians reliably
get better outcomes than their colleagues, using the same model in the same
clinic with comparable patients, and the variation between them is generally
larger than the variation between named treatments. What distinguishes them is
not credentials or years of experience, both of which predict outcome poorly.
It seems to have more to do with how they handle difficulty in the
relationship — whether ruptures get noticed and repaired rather than
politely ignored.

Which points at the most practical conclusion available from seventy years of
argument. Averages across trials cannot tell any individual whether their own
therapy is working. Tracking that directly can: services that ask patients to
complete a brief measure at every session, and put the results in front of the
therapist, catch the cases that are drifting off course and reduce the number
who end up worse. It is an unglamorous fix, closer to bookkeeping than to
theory, and it does more to improve outcomes than any of the schools ever
managed by arguing.
