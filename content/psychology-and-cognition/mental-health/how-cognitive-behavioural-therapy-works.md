# How Cognitive Behavioural Therapy Works

Aaron Beck set out, around the end of the 1950s, to prove psychoanalysis right.
He was an analytically trained psychiatrist at the University of Pennsylvania,
and he thought the theory deserved better evidence than it had. The
proposition he chose to test was the standard analytic account of depression:
that it is anger turned against the self. Hostility that cannot be expressed
outward gets redirected inward, and the depressed patient punishes himself
with it. If that were true, Beck reasoned, the dreams of depressed patients
should be unusually full of hostility, since dreams were where disguised
material surfaced.

He collected the dreams. They were not full of hostility. They were full of
loss, defeat, rejection, exclusion, being deprived of something — the same
themes, it turned out, that ran through what the patients said about their
waking lives when anyone asked them directly.

Then he noticed something else. Patients free-associating on the couch
sometimes had a second stream of thought running alongside the one they were
reporting: brief, fast commentary about the session, about themselves, about
how badly they were doing. They had not mentioned it because nobody had ever
asked. Beck called these automatic thoughts, and the therapy he eventually
built was organised around the claim that they are reportable, that they
follow recognisable patterns, and that they can be examined.

## Two Genealogies That Converged

Beck was not first, and not alone. Albert Ellis had arrived at something
similar a few years earlier by a different route, out of frustration with how
slowly analysis moved. Ellis taught that events do not cause emotional
consequences directly — a belief sits between them, and the belief is where the
trouble lives. He was interested in absolutist demands, the *musts* and
*shoulds* people impose on themselves and the world, and his style was
combative in a way Beck's never was. Where Ellis argued with beliefs, Beck
asked questions about them and let the patient do the arguing.

The other lineage was behavioural, older, and initially hostile to any talk of
thoughts at all. In the early 1920s Mary Cover Jones worked with a small boy
afraid of a rabbit, bringing the animal gradually closer while he ate
something he liked and watched other children handle it without concern. The
fear went. Three decades later Joseph Wolpe, working in South Africa, built a
method out of the same principle: teach a patient to relax deeply, have them
imagine the feared situation in graded steps, and pair the relaxation with
each step. He called it systematic desensitisation, and it did in a handful of
sessions what analysis had been failing to do in years.

By the late 1970s the two traditions had fused. The cognitive people had
discovered that changing what a patient did was often the fastest way to
change what they believed; the behavioural people had conceded that what
patients thought about an exposure affected what they learned from it. Beck's
1979 treatment manual for depression sealed it by making the therapy
specifiable — written down clearly enough that other people could be trained
to deliver it, and therefore clearly enough that it could be put into
randomised trials against everything else.

## What Actually Happens

The work begins with a formulation: a shared map, drawn collaboratively, of
how this particular problem is being kept going right now. That emphasis on
maintenance rather than origin is the therapy's defining commitment. The
question is not primarily why a fear of speaking in meetings began fifteen
years ago; it is what happens in the forty minutes before a meeting now, and
what the person does about it, and what that doing teaches them.

Sessions are structured and usually numbered from the start, with an agenda
set at the beginning and a review at the end. The structure is not
fussiness — a fixed number of sessions changes what both people do with them.

Much of the early work is simply making thoughts visible. The thoughts Beck
identified are fast, plausible, and normally unexamined; people experience
their content as a straightforward perception of reality rather than as a
claim about it. So the first task is catching them at the moment the mood
shifts, which is harder than it sounds and is usually where the initial
between-session work goes. What emerges tends to be repetitive, and over a few
weeks patterns become obvious: a single poor result read as proof of a general
rule, another person's neutral expression read as disapproval, an imagined
outcome treated as a forecast.

The pivotal move is what happens next, and it is not positive thinking. A
thought is treated as a hypothesis — something that might be accurate, might be
partly accurate, might be a habit — and then it is tested. Sometimes the test
is a matter of examining evidence in conversation. More often, and more
usefully, it is a behavioural experiment: the person states in advance what
they expect will happen, does the thing, and compares the prediction against
the outcome. Someone convinced their hands will shake so visibly that
colleagues will comment agrees to find out. The value of the experiment lies
precisely in its being a genuine question, which means the therapist has to be
willing to be wrong about the answer.

For depression the emphasis shifts toward activity. Depression collapses
contact with anything rewarding — people stop doing what they enjoyed because
they no longer feel like it, and the reduction in reward deepens the mood that
removed the motivation. Behavioural activation inverts the usual order,
treating action as the thing that comes first and motivation as what follows
it, sometimes by several days. For anxiety the emphasis is on approaching the
avoided situation in graduated steps while letting go of the safety
behaviours — the seat near the exit, the rehearsed opening line — that let a
person survive an experience without ever learning that it was survivable.

A course of this therapy is designed to make itself unnecessary. Toward the
end, work turns to consolidating what the person has learned into something
they can apply without a therapist in the room, and to anticipating the
circumstances under which the old patterns would return.

## The Third Wave

From the 1990s a set of related approaches grew out of, and partly against,
the standard model. Acceptance and commitment therapy, developed by Steven
Hayes, argued that disputing the content of a distressing thought concedes too
much — that the more useful target is the relationship a person has to their
thoughts, learning to notice one as a passing mental event rather than an
instruction. Marsha Linehan built dialectical behaviour therapy for chronically
suicidal patients who had been repeatedly failed by change-focused treatment,
balancing demands for change against explicit validation of how much sense
their distress made given their histories. Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy
took aim at rumination, and at the finding that people who have been depressed
before relapse partly because low mood reactivates whole patterns of thinking
that never fully dismantled.

These are often described as a break. They are better read as a set of
corrections, made mostly by people trained in the original approach and
working on the cases where it fell short.

## The Criticisms Worth Taking Seriously

The therapy spread further and faster than any other psychological treatment,
and not solely on merit. It was brief, manualisable, measurable, and teachable
to people without a decade of training, which made it the treatment health
systems and insurers could actually buy. England built a national programme
around it, training therapists in large numbers and publishing outcome data at
a scale nobody had attempted before, on an argument that was explicitly
economic as well as clinical.

Scale brought problems. Effect sizes reported in trials of cognitive therapy
for depression appear to have declined since the earliest studies, and the
plausible explanations — small, enthusiastic early trials, publication bias,
and the well-documented tendency of a therapy to perform better in studies run
by its advocates — are uncomfortable rather than damning. Comparative research
keeps finding that differences between established therapies are smaller than
their proponents predict, which suggests a good deal of the benefit comes from
factors they share: a credible explanation, a structured plan, and someone
paying sustained attention.

Manualisation degrades, too. Delivered without formulation, the therapy becomes
a sequence of worksheets, which is not what any of the trials tested. And
there is a real awkwardness at the centre of asking whether a distressing
thought is accurate, because sometimes it is. The job is genuinely precarious,
the neighbourhood genuinely unsafe, the discrimination genuinely happening.
Competent practitioners treat those as problems to be solved rather than
beliefs to be examined; incompetent practice slides into informing people that
their reasonable distress is a thinking error, which is both wrong and
insulting.

What survives all of that is the observation Beck made when his experiment
failed. People can report what runs through their minds in the moments their
mood turns, those reports are patterned rather than random, and the patterns
can be checked against the world instead of interpreted. Treating what patients
say as data rather than as material requiring decoding was a genuinely new
move, and almost everything built since has been built on it.
