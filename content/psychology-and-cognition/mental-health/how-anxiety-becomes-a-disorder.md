# How Anxiety Becomes a Disorder

You brake for a cyclist who has come out of nowhere, and by the time the car
has stopped the work is already finished. Your heart is going hard. Your hands
are damp on the wheel. There is a metallic taste somewhere at the back of your
mouth and a loose, hollowed feeling under the ribs. None of that was decided.
The braking itself happened faster than deliberation, and the body was flooded
with the chemistry to support it before you had assembled a single thought
about cyclists. A minute later it is already fading. Two minutes later you are
mostly annoyed about being late.

That is the fear system doing exactly what it evolved to do: fast, expensive,
and brief. Anxiety disorders are not that machinery breaking in some exotic
way. They are usually the same machinery firing when there is no cyclist,
failing to stand down once the cyclist has gone, or turning its attention to
events that have not happened yet and in most cases never will.

## The Smoke Detector Problem

Clinicians find it useful to separate fear from anxiety. Fear is the response
to a threat that is present now — sharp, object-focused, and built for
immediate action. Anxiety is anticipatory. It has no particular object, or a
vague and future-tense one, and it does not surge so much as hum: muscle
tension across the shoulders and jaw, sleep that comes late and breaks early,
an attention that keeps sweeping the room for something it cannot name. The
two states feel different from the inside and involve partly different brain
circuitry, though they share a great deal of hardware.

Any alarm system faces an asymmetry. A false alarm costs a few minutes of
wasted adrenaline and some embarrassment. A missed alarm, once in the history
of a lineage, costs everything. Selection therefore tunes thresholds low, and
the evolutionary physician Randolph Nesse gave this the name it deserves: the
smoke detector principle. A detector sensitive enough to catch the fire that
matters will also go off while you are making toast. Most of its alarms will be
false, and that is not a malfunction — it is the price of the ones that are
not.

This reframes what an anxiety disorder is. Someone with panic disorder does not
have a broken alarm. They have an alarm whose threshold has drifted downward,
attached to cues that do not warrant it, embedded in a set of habits that keep
the threshold exactly where it is.

## What the Body Is Actually Doing

The amygdala sits near the front of the temporal lobe on each side and acts as
a hub for threat-relevant information, receiving input from the senses and
from memory and projecting outward to the hypothalamus and brainstem. Its
output recruits the sympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system and the
adrenal glands. Heart rate and force of contraction rise. Blood is
redistributed toward the large muscles and away from skin and gut. Pupils
widen, airways open, digestion is suspended, sweat glands begin working — which
cools a body about to exert itself and, incidentally, improves grip. A slower
hormonal cascade runs in parallel, from the hypothalamus through the pituitary
to the adrenal cortex, releasing cortisol, mobilising glucose, and sustaining
the state over minutes and hours rather than seconds.

Regions of the prefrontal cortex can dampen amygdala output, which is roughly
what people mean by talking themselves down. The important asymmetry is that
this regulatory route is slower than the route that triggered the response.
You will always be alarmed before you are reassured.

Joseph LeDoux mapped much of this circuitry in rats and then spent years
arguing that the field had drawn the wrong conclusion from his own work. The
circuits, he maintained, produce defensive behaviour and bodily arousal; the
conscious feeling of fear is assembled separately, in cortical systems that
build an experience out of those signals together with memory, context, and
whatever concepts a person has available. If that is right, you can have the
bodily state without the label, and the label without much of the state. Panic
attacks that arrive from nowhere become less mysterious, as does the
disconcerting sense of unreality that often accompanies them.

Most of the symptoms people find frightening are ordinary consequences of
mobilisation. Breathing faster than metabolic demand requires lowers carbon
dioxide in the blood, which produces tingling in the fingers and around the
mouth, lightheadedness, and a flat, dreamlike detachment. None of it is
dangerous. All of it is extremely convincing.

## Learning It, and Then Keeping It

The classical account is conditioning: a neutral cue paired with something
aversive acquires predictive power and starts producing the response on its
own. That explains less than it appears to. Most people afraid of dogs were
never bitten, and most people who were bitten are not afraid of dogs. Fears
are also unevenly easy to acquire — snakes, heights, enclosed spaces, and
social disapproval take hold far more readily than electrical sockets, which
are objectively more dangerous and evolutionarily brand new. And fear can be
acquired entirely secondhand, by watching someone else react or simply by
being told.

For understanding why anxiety persists, though, the origin matters far less
than the maintenance. Avoidance is the central mechanism, and it works by
negative reinforcement: the relief that arrives the moment you turn back is
immediate, strong, and teaches one lesson — turning back worked. What never
happens is the discovery that going forward would have been survivable.

Safety behaviours are the subtler version. Sitting near the exit. Carrying
water. Rehearsing sentences before speaking. Gripping the supermarket trolley.
Keeping a phone in one hand. Each is a small hedge, and each allows the person
to attribute their survival to the hedge rather than to the harmlessness of the
situation. In social anxiety these carry a second cost: monitoring your own
performance consumes exactly the attention you would need to read the room, and
the resulting stiffness reads to others as coldness, which supplies genuine
evidence for the belief that started it.

David Clark's cognitive model of panic describes the tightest of these loops. A
benign sensation — a skipped beat, a wave of heat, a moment of unsteadiness —
is read as evidence of catastrophe: a heart attack, fainting, losing control.
That reading increases arousal, which produces more sensation, which confirms
the reading. On this account panic disorder is not really a disorder of panic
attacks. It is a disorder of the fear of them.

Worry in generalised anxiety disorder works differently. It is verbal,
abstract, and future-directed, and it has the peculiar property of suppressing
vivid imagery and the autonomic response that would accompany it. Worry is
avoidance conducted in the medium of language. It also tends to be experienced
as useful — worrying feels like preparation, and people can often name
occasions when it seemed to pay off — which is why it defends itself so well.

## The Family of Diagnoses

The diagnostic categories mostly carve the same machinery according to what it
has attached itself to. A specific phobia is bound to a circumscribed object or
situation. Social anxiety disorder is bound to scrutiny and evaluation by
others. Panic disorder is bound to the sensations of arousal themselves.
Agoraphobia is bound to situations from which escape would be difficult or help
unavailable. Generalised anxiety disorder has no particular object at all; the
worry roams, settling wherever it finds material.

The boundaries leak badly. Comorbidity is closer to the rule than the
exception, and underneath the categories sits a broad temperamental dimension —
call it negative emotionality, or neuroticism — that is substantially
heritable and shared with depression. That shared substrate is why depression
and anxiety are grouped together as internalizing conditions, and why
treatments developed for one often work on the other. It is also why the field
has been moving toward transdiagnostic approaches that target the underlying
processes rather than the labels.

The manuals themselves keep rearranging the furniture. Obsessive-compulsive
disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder both sat in the anxiety chapter for
decades and were moved out in the fifth edition of the American diagnostic
manual — an acknowledgement that although both involve intense anxiety, their
mechanisms and their responses to treatment look different enough to warrant
separate homes.

## Where the Line Falls

There is no blood test and no scan. The threshold is functional, and it rests
on three considerations: how persistent the anxiety is, how disproportionate it
is to the actual circumstances, and how much it interferes with the life the
person is trying to lead. Dread before an interview is not a disorder.
Rearranging a career to avoid interviews might be.

Context does real work here. Vigilance in a genuinely dangerous neighbourhood
is calibration, not pathology, and the same internal state gets carved
differently across cultures — in many settings people present to doctors with
palpitations, dizziness, or headaches rather than with a complaint about
worry, and the underlying condition is the same one.

What consistently helps involves arranging for the disconfirming experience
that avoidance has been preventing, usually alongside work on the beliefs that
make avoidance seem necessary. Michelle Craske and others have argued that the
old association is never really erased; what treatment builds is a new,
competing one, which then has to be strong enough and attached to enough
different cues to win when they collide. That framing explains something
clinicians have always observed — that fear can return in a new setting, after
a long gap, or under stress — and treats it as a property of how learning
works rather than as failure.

None of this machinery was built for comfort. It was built for a world in which
the cost of one unnecessary sprint was an afternoon's calories. What it was
never built for is being asked to evaluate threats that cannot be outrun and
do not resolve — the biopsy result, the mortgage, the conversation you have to
have on Monday — which is precisely the sort of thing it is now asked to
evaluate most days, and which leaves the detector humming with nowhere to
send anybody.
