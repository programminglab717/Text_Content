# Psychiatric Medication and the Chemical Story

In 1949 an Australian psychiatrist named John Cade injected guinea pigs with
urine from patients with mania, trying to find a toxin. To make one of his
compounds soluble he paired it with lithium, and noticed that the lithium
itself made the animals placid and easy to handle. He then tried it on a small
number of patients on a chronic ward, one of whom had been continuously manic
for years and who recovered well enough to go home. Cade's reasoning about the
guinea pigs was wrong in almost every particular. The drug works.

Nearly every foundational psychiatric medication has an origin story of this
shape, and the most consequential of them concerns chlorpromazine. It was
synthesised in a French laboratory as an antihistamine, taken up by a naval
surgeon interested in calming patients before anaesthesia, and passed along to
psychiatrists at a Paris hospital who reported in 1952 that it did something no
previous compound had done. Sedatives had existed for a century; they made a
distressed person unconscious. This made a person who was hearing voices stop
being tormented by them while remaining awake and able to talk.

Within a decade the drug was in use across Europe and North America, and the
architecture of psychiatric care began to change around it. The vast custodial
asylums that had absorbed the mentally ill since the nineteenth century started
emptying. It would be too neat to credit the pills alone — the political appetite
for closing those institutions, and the money involved, mattered at least as
much, and the community services promised to replace them were frequently never
built. But the possibility of treating psychosis outside a locked building
began with a compound that nobody had designed for the purpose. Imipramine was
tested as a possible antipsychotic, failed at that, and unexpectedly lifted
mood. Iproniazid was a tuberculosis drug whose patients were noticed to be
conspicuously cheerful on the ward. The pharmacopoeia was not deduced from a
theory of mental illness. It was stumbled into, mostly within a single decade,
by clinicians paying attention to side effects.

## The Hypothesis That Ran Backwards

Theory followed. Once researchers worked out what these compounds did to
neurotransmission, they reasoned backward: if a drug that increases available
serotonin and noradrenaline relieves depression, then depression must involve a
shortage of serotonin and noradrenaline. If drugs that block dopamine receptors
relieve psychosis, then psychosis must involve excess dopamine. These became
the monoamine hypothesis of depression and the dopamine hypothesis of
schizophrenia, and in simplified form they escaped into public language as the
chemical imbalance.

The inference is not illegitimate, but it is weak, and its weakness was visible
early. What a drug does tells you where a system can be pushed, not where it
went wrong. A diuretic reduces the swollen ankles of heart failure without
anyone concluding that the kidneys caused the illness; the problem is the pump,
and the kidney is simply the most accessible lever. More pointedly, in
psychiatry the pharmacology and the clinical response run on different clocks. A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor blocks the
transporter within hours of the first dose, yet the antidepressant effect, when
it comes, typically takes weeks. Whatever is doing the therapeutic work is
downstream — changes in receptor sensitivity, in gene expression, in the
signalling that governs how readily synapses form and reorganise — rather than
the transporter blockade itself.

Attempts to find the predicted deficiencies have mostly disappointed, and the
serotonin case has been argued over publicly enough that the argument itself
has become a source of confusion. The useful thing to notice is how little
follows either way. Specialists had largely abandoned the simple deficiency
model decades before the public did, and the drugs' evidence never rested on
it. A deficiency model is a claim about a cause; a trial result is a claim
about an effect; establishing the second has never required the first.

The dopamine story in psychosis has aged somewhat better, partly because
imaging work has located an abnormality more precisely, in the capacity to
synthesise dopamine in a particular striatal region. Even there the framing has
moved. The current account is less about a surplus of a chemical than about a
signalling system that tags ordinary events — a stranger's glance, a parked
car, a phrase on the radio — with the kind of urgent significance normally
reserved for things that matter enormously. Delusions, on this reading, are
partly a reasonable attempt to explain why everything suddenly feels
meaningful. What the medication does is turn down the tagging, which is also
why it blunts the drive and vividness of ordinary experience along with the
pathological kind.

## What the Classes Do

Broadly, five families do most of the work.

Antipsychotics all block dopamine D2 receptors to some degree, and the newer
ones also act on serotonin receptors. They reduce hallucinations, delusions,
and disorganised thinking, and their strongest evidence is in preventing
relapse: across many trials, people maintained on them relapse substantially
less often than those switched to placebo. They do not touch the so-called
negative symptoms — the flattening, withdrawal, and loss of drive — nearly as
well, and they carry real costs, including movement disorders, metabolic
effects, and in the case of the one genuinely distinctive drug, clozapine, a
blood-count risk serious enough to require ongoing monitoring. Clozapine also
works for a proportion of people whom nothing else has helped, which remains an
unexplained and clinically important anomaly.

Antidepressants increase the availability of serotonin, noradrenaline, or
both, and despite the name they are prescribed at least as often for anxiety
disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and post-traumatic stress as for
depression — one of several places where drug categories and diagnostic
categories fail to line up. Some are used for chronic pain and migraine on
mechanisms that have little to do with mood at all. Their common side effects
are unglamorous and underdiscussed: sexual dysfunction, which is frequent and
often persists as long as the drug is taken, and a flattening of emotional
range that some patients value during a crisis and resent afterwards.

Mood stabilisers are a mixed category: lithium, an element with no obvious
mechanism even now, plus several anticonvulsants adopted for reasons that were
partly empirical. Benzodiazepines act on GABA receptors and reduce anxiety
within minutes, which is exactly their problem — a drug that works that fast
and feels that good invites tolerance and dependence, and the consensus has
shifted firmly toward short, bounded use. Stimulants for attention deficit
hyperactivity disorder increase synaptic dopamine and noradrenaline in
prefrontal circuits and produce, in the short term, some of the largest symptom
changes measured anywhere in psychiatry.

## What "Works" Means in a Trial

A randomised trial compares two group averages. That statement is doing more
work than it appears to. If a drug produces a dramatic response in a third of
people, nothing in a third, and harm in a few, the average will look like a
modest benefit for everybody — which is a description almost nobody in the
trial would recognise. Averages are how the evidence is generated; individuals
are how it is used, and the gap between them explains a great deal of the
public argument about whether these medications work.

Placebo response in psychiatric trials is substantial, and it has been growing
over the decades, for reasons that include better-treated and less severely ill
trial populations and the effect of intensive study contact. Blinding is also
imperfect, because drugs with noticeable physical effects tell participants
which arm they are in; a trial nominally double-blind may be partly unblinded
by dry mouth.

Publication practices compounded the problem. When researchers went back to the
trials that companies had registered with the American regulator before results
were known, and compared them with what reached journals, the pattern was
unambiguous: studies with positive results were published almost without
exception, while a large share of null studies either never appeared or were
written up in ways that emphasised a secondary positive finding. The published
literature therefore overstated the benefit. Trial pre-registration, now
standard, exists because of exactly this.

What survives the corrections is a real but moderate effect for most of these
drugs in most conditions — larger in more severe illness, larger for relapse
prevention than for acute treatment in several categories, and larger for some
individuals than any average can convey. The honest summary is that psychiatric
medications are useful tools with meaningful limits, which is also a fair
description of most of general medicine.

## Stopping, Which Turns Out to Be Harder Than Starting

For a long time the standard line was that antidepressants are not addictive
and that any difficulty on stopping was a brief discontinuation syndrome
lasting a week or two. Addiction in the technical sense — craving, escalating
use, compulsion — is genuinely not what happens. But the broader claim was
based on thin evidence, mostly short trials, and it collided with large numbers
of patients reporting dizziness, electric-shock sensations, insomnia, and
severe anxiety lasting far longer, sometimes for months.

The picture that has emerged is that the nervous system adapts to sustained
receptor occupancy and takes time to readapt, that the relationship between
dose and receptor occupancy is steeply non-linear so that the final reductions
are the largest pharmacological changes, and that people who have taken a drug
for years are far more affected than people who have taken it for weeks. This
has driven a shift toward much slower, proportionally tapering reductions, and
toward treating the question of how long to continue as a decision to revisit
rather than a default to drift with. Distinguishing a withdrawal effect from a
returning illness is genuinely difficult and is one of the harder judgements in
the work.

Two other tendencies deserve naming. Polypharmacy accumulates: a drug is added
for a symptom, another to counter a side effect, and nothing is ever
subtracted, so a person arrives at five medications none of which anyone would
have chosen as a starting regimen. And measurement is patchy — psychiatry has
been slower than other specialties to track outcomes with structured scales
over time, which means responses are often judged from memory and impression.

What none of these drugs do is address a bad marriage, a job that is grinding
someone down, poverty, isolation, or grief. They change the internal weather,
sometimes enough that a person can act on the rest, and that is a narrower
claim than either the chemical imbalance story or its angriest critics tend to
make.
