# Attention and Its Limits

During the Second World War the British had a problem with the men watching
radar screens for submarines. They were alert, motivated, and well trained, and
they missed things. Not at the start of a watch — at the start they were
excellent — but after twenty or thirty minutes their detection of faint,
infrequent blips fell off noticeably and kept falling. A psychologist named
Norman Mackworth built a deliberately tedious apparatus to study it: a pointer
sweeping around a blank clock face, jumping one step at a time, occasionally
jumping two. Observers had to press a button on the double jumps. The decline
showed up within the first half hour, reliably, in people who wanted very much
to do well.

The vigilance decrement was one of the first hard demonstrations that attention
is not a matter of trying. It is a resource with a supply curve, and it behaves
in ways that have nothing to do with how much you care about the outcome. Most
of what has been learned since is about the shape of those constraints: what
gets through the gate, what happens to everything else, and where the real
bottleneck sits.

## The Filter and Its Leaks

The other wartime inheritance was the problem of the crowded radio channel —
several voices arriving at one pair of ears, one of which mattered. Colin Cherry
built the laboratory version in the early 1950s by feeding different speech to
each ear through headphones and asking listeners to shadow one stream, repeating
it aloud as it arrived. People could do it, with effort. Afterwards they could
say almost nothing about the ignored ear. Not the topic, not the words, often
not the language. They noticed if the voice changed from male to female, or if
it turned into a tone, but the content was simply unavailable.

Donald Broadbent proposed the natural architecture: a set of parallel sensory
channels feeding a filter that selects one on the basis of crude physical
properties — which ear, which pitch, which direction — and passes it along to a
limited-capacity system that extracts meaning. Everything else is discarded
before it is understood.

The model was clean and almost immediately leaky. Listeners who could not report
a word from the ignored ear would nonetheless notice their own name in it. If
the shadowed sentence ran on into the wrong ear, people would follow the meaning
across and shadow the unattended channel for a word or two before catching
themselves. Those leaks mean that unattended material is being processed for
meaning somewhere, at least well enough to be checked against a few very
important patterns. Anne Treisman's repair was to demote the filter from a gate
to an attenuator: unattended channels are turned down rather than off, and
whether a word registers depends on how strongly it is turned down against how
low its threshold happens to be. Your name has a threshold so low it gets
through a lot of attenuation. A stranger's name does not.

## Selection in Space, and the Binding Problem

Vision gave the same question a geometry. Michael Posner's cueing task is
almost embarrassingly simple: look at a fixation point, get a brief cue on one
side, then respond to a target that appears either there or elsewhere. Responses
to validly cued locations are faster by a few tens of milliseconds; invalidly
cued ones are slower. Crucially this works with the eyes held still, which means
attention can move independently of gaze. The metaphor that stuck was a
spotlight — a region of enhanced processing that can be shifted, and which
appears to be able to zoom, trading area for resolution.

The spotlight is a description rather than an explanation, and the deeper
account is about competition. Neurons in visual cortex have receptive fields
covering a patch of the world, and when two objects fall in the same field the
cell's response is roughly a weighted average of what each would have produced
alone. The objects are competing for the cell. Attention biases that competition
— signals from frontal and parietal regions boost the population representing
one object, so the cell responds as though the other were largely absent.
Selection is not a gate somewhere in the stream; it is a thumb on the scale,
applied everywhere at once.

This matters because of what vision does with features. Color, orientation,
motion, and depth are analyzed in largely separate populations, so a red
vertical bar next to a green horizontal one is, at some stage, just red, green,
vertical, horizontal. Something has to decide which goes with which. Treisman's
answer was that attention is that something: features register in parallel
across the whole field, but conjunctions require attention focused on a
location. The prediction is visible in search times. Finding the only red item
among green ones takes about as long regardless of how many green ones there
are — it pops out. Finding the red vertical among red horizontals and green
verticals takes longer with every distractor added, because you are checking
locations one region at a time. Push people to respond before attention arrives
and they report illusory conjunctions: a red X when the display contained a red
O and a green X.

## What You Do Not See

The consequence people find hardest to accept is that unattended things can be
large, central, and completely invisible. Ask viewers to count basketball passes
between players in white shirts and about half of them fail to notice a person
in a gorilla suit stroll through the middle of the scene, stop, and beat their
chest. They are not looking away; eye-tracking shows the gorilla lands on the
fovea. It simply never becomes an experience.

Change blindness makes the same point differently. Alternate two photographs
with a brief blank between them and observers can hunt for many seconds without
finding a large object that appears, vanishes, or changes color — a building, an
engine on an airliner. Without the blank the change produces a motion transient
and is instantly obvious; the blank swamps the transient, and without it the
visual system has no automatic alarm. It is possible to do this to someone in
person. Experimenters stopping pedestrians to ask directions, interrupted by
workmen carrying a door, have swapped themselves for a different person mid
conversation without a substantial fraction of the pedestrians noticing.

Time behaves the same way as space. Present a stream of letters at a rapid rate
and ask for two targets. If the second follows the first by roughly a fifth to a
half of a second, it is frequently missed entirely, while targets arriving
immediately after the first, or a second later, are reported fine. Attention
appears to be occupied consolidating the first target and the second one passes
through unrecorded. The blink is not in the eye.

## The Bottleneck at the Far End

All of this is selection on the way in. There is a separate and stubborner limit
on the way out. Give someone two simple tasks — one tone, one light, one hand
each — and stagger the stimuli. When the second arrives soon after the first,
the response to it is delayed, and the delay shrinks roughly in step with the
gap. The perceptual analysis of the second stimulus proceeds fine. What waits is
the stage where a decision is turned into an action, and that stage appears to
handle one thing at a time.

This is why talking on a phone while driving degrades driving, and why
hands-free arrangements help much less than people expect. The cost is not
manual. It is in the shared machinery for selecting responses, and in the way a
conversation with someone who cannot see the road pulls attention away from
hazards. Drivers on the phone show classic inattentional blindness: they look
directly at signs and pedestrians and do not report them.

Practice changes the picture, but less than the word "multitasking" implies.
What improves is usually the component tasks, until one of them runs off with
little need for central involvement — the way a fluent driver holds a lane
without deliberation. Genuine simultaneous performance of two demanding tasks
remains rare, and the people most confident in their ability to do it tend, on
testing, to be the worst at it.

## Holding On

The last limit is the one Mackworth found. Sustained attention to something
sparse and unchanging degrades within the first half hour, and the degradation is
not simple fatigue: it correlates with the mind going somewhere else. People
watching a rare-signal display drift into thoughts about their weekend, and they
often do not notice they have drifted until something prompts them to check.
Mind-wandering is the default state of a brain with nothing pressing to do, and
a radar screen is very close to nothing pressing.

Working memory sets the upper bound on how much can be held in that privileged
state at once. The old figure of about seven items was really a statement about
chunks, and the number of genuinely independent items people can hold when
rehearsal and grouping are blocked looks closer to three or four. Expertise does
not raise it; expertise makes each chunk bigger. A chess master glancing at a
real position holds a handful of configurations rather than twenty-five pieces,
and shows no advantage at all on a board of randomly scattered pieces, where the
chunks do not exist.
