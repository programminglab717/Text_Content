# The Picture in the Head

In 1880 Francis Galton sent his acquaintances a peculiar request. Think of the
table at which you ate breakfast this morning, he asked, and then report on the
image: is it dim or clear, is the illumination as bright as the real scene, are
the colors distinct, can you see all of it at once or only part?

He expected minor variation and got something closer to a schism. Many of his
scientific colleagues — men of the Royal Society, people whose whole occupation
was thinking carefully — replied that they had no idea what he was talking
about. They knew what their breakfast table looked like. They could describe it
in detail. But there was no picture. Some found the question faintly absurd, as
though he were asking what color their thoughts smelled of. Meanwhile the
general public, and women and children especially, sent back descriptions of
vivid scenes: the grain of the wood, the light from the window, the position of
the marmalade.

Galton drew the wrong conclusion, as he often did, deciding that abstract
thinkers had let their imagery atrophy through disuse. But the observation
itself was sound and it was more than a century ahead of anyone's ability to do
much with it. People differ enormously in what happens when they close their
eyes, most of them do not know it, and nearly everybody assumes the inside of
their own head is standard issue.

## The Long Silence

Imagery then spent most of the twentieth century off the table. The trouble
began with the introspectionist laboratories themselves. Trained observers in
Würzburg reported that some thoughts arrived with no imagery attached at all,
while Wundt's school in Leipzig maintained otherwise, and the resulting
imageless-thought controversy could not be settled because each lab's
introspectors reliably produced whatever their lab expected. That was fatal. A
method whose results depend on who is doing the looking is not a method.

Behaviorism finished the job. John Watson dismissed imagery as the ghost of a
discredited psychology, a private event that could not be measured and therefore
did not belong in science, and for several decades experimental psychology
proceeded as though the mind's eye did not exist. What eventually brought it
back was not a better form of introspection but a trick: stop asking people what
their images look like and start timing what they can do with them.

## Rotating Blocks

Roger Shepard and Jacqueline Metzler gave people pairs of drawings of
three-dimensional block figures and asked a simple question — same object or
mirror image? The pairs differed by a rotation, and the size of that rotation
varied from trial to trial. The answer times fell on a remarkably clean straight
line. The larger the angular difference between the two figures, the longer the
decision took, at a steady rate of so many degrees per second, as though the
figure were being physically turned in the head at a constant speed rather than
looked up in a table.

Stephen Kosslyn produced the other classic result. He had people memorize a map
of a fictional island with landmarks at known positions, then asked them to hold
the image in mind, fix on one landmark, and press a button when they had found
another. Scanning times rose with the map distance between the two points —
including distances the participants had never actually traversed. Something in
the representation had the structure of space in it.

This is the case for what Kosslyn called depictive representation: images that
are not merely descriptions of a scene but something that preserves the
geometry, where nearby things in the world are nearby in the representation and
where the medium imposes costs that a list of facts would not. A sentence about
a rabbit does not get harder to read if the rabbit is small. An image of a
rabbit next to an elephant, people report, has to be zoomed before the rabbit's
ears can be checked, and the zooming takes time.

## The Argument That Would Not Die

Zenon Pylyshyn spent decades arguing that none of this shows what it appears to
show, and the argument is more serious than it is usually given credit for.

His central move was to point out that all these experiments ask people to
imagine something happening, and people know how the thing would happen in
reality. Asked to imagine rotating a block, you know that real blocks rotate
through intermediate positions at finite speed, and you may simply be simulating
that knowledge, producing the appropriate timing because you know what the
appropriate timing is. Tacit knowledge, not pictures. The evidence that this is
at least partly true is that the effects can be manipulated by instruction: tell
people to imagine the scanning differently and the times change, which is not
how a genuine medium with fixed physical properties should behave.

Underneath that lay a deeper complaint. A picture in the head requires something
to look at it, and that something needs eyes of its own, and so on down. If the
interpretation of the image is doing the real cognitive work, the image is
decorative — an epiphenomenon, like the noise of an engine rather than its
torque.

The response has come largely from neuroscience, and it is substantial without
being conclusive. Imagining a visual scene activates the visual cortex,
including primary visual cortex, the first cortical way station for signals from
the eye, which is organized retinotopically — adjacent patches of cortex handle
adjacent patches of the visual field. When people imagine larger objects, the
activation spreads across more cortex in a way that respects that map.
Disrupting occipital cortex with magnetic stimulation impairs performance on
imagery tasks much as it impairs performance on the corresponding perceptual
ones. Patients with damage to visual areas lose imagery in ways that mirror
their perceptual losses, right down to losing imagery for color while retaining
it for shape. Whether early visual cortex is doing the depicting or merely being
echoed by it remains genuinely open, but the brain does appear to be using a
spatially organized medium rather than a list of propositions.

The two systems overlap so much that they interfere. Cheves Perky demonstrated
this in 1910 with one of the more elegant experiments in the field's history.
She asked people to stare at a blank screen and imagine a banana; unknown to
them, a very faint image of a banana was being projected onto the screen from
behind. They saw it. They just attributed it to their own imagination, and
reported afterward on the vividness of the picture they had made — some of them
remarking on details of orientation that they had not chosen and could not have
chosen, since the projector had.

## Aphantasia

Galton's question waited a long time for a follow-up. In the 2000s the
neurologist Adam Zeman saw a patient who had lost the ability to visualize after
a cardiac procedure — a man who had previously had rich imagery and now had
none, though he could still describe everything he knew about the appearance of
things. The case was published, a magazine wrote about it, and Zeman's inbox
filled with letters from people saying they had been like that their whole lives
and had assumed everyone was. He named the condition aphantasia, and its vivid
opposite hyperphantasia, and the effect of giving it a name has been to reveal
that it is not rare. Estimates put congenital aphantasia at a few people in a
hundred.

The discovery is frequently accompanied by a small crisis. People report having
spent decades believing that "picture a beach" was a figure of speech, that
counting sheep was a metaphor, that when a novel described a face everyone else
was also just holding a set of facts. Some find out in their forties, in an
argument with a spouse.

What makes this more than self-report is that imagery leaves objective traces.
Holding an image of a pattern in mind biases which of two competing images a
person subsequently sees when different patterns are presented to each eye — a
priming effect that tracks imagery strength and that aphantasic participants
largely do not show. Imagining a bright scene produces a small constriction of
the pupil, as though the eye were preparing for light that is not there, and
that response too is reduced or absent in people who report no imagery. Reading
a frightening passage produces less physiological arousal in aphantasics than in
others, which fits the picture of imagery as a route by which thought reaches
the body.

And yet aphantasic people are not cognitively impaired. Many do well on mental
rotation, apparently solving it some other way. Plenty work as engineers,
architects, designers. What they more often report is a thinner autobiographical
memory — an ability to state what happened at a wedding without any sense of
re-seeing it — which lines up with the finding that remembering an episode and
imagining a scene recruit much the same machinery.

That overlap is where the practical stakes are. Imagery is the working medium of
a good deal of therapy: exposure treatments that rely on imagined confrontation,
rehearsal of feared situations, the deliberate rewriting of intrusive images in
post-traumatic stress, where the symptom itself is a picture that arrives
uninvited and carries its full original charge. Athletes and musicians who
mentally rehearse produce measurable gains, presumably because motor imagery
engages a fair amount of the motor system. For most people the inner picture is
a tool that does real work. For a few in every hundred it is simply not there,
and they manage — which is itself a clue about how much of thinking needs
pictures at all.
