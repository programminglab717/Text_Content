# Heuristics and Biases in Judgment

Steve is shy and withdrawn, invariably helpful but with little interest in
people or in the world of reality. He is meek and tidy, with a need for order
and structure and a passion for detail. Is Steve more likely to be a librarian
or a farmer?

Almost everyone says librarian, and almost everyone is wrong, because there are
vastly more farmers than librarians and the description is not diagnostic enough
to overcome that. The interesting thing is not the error. It is that the error
is predictable. People do not answer this question randomly, or with a spread of
answers around the right one. They lean the same way, and they lean the same way
after the arithmetic has been explained to them.

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman began collecting results like this at the
Hebrew University at the end of the 1960s, and their first target was their own
colleagues. Statisticians, asked informally about the sample sizes needed to
replicate an effect, gave answers far too small — as though a small sample were
a faithful miniature of the population rather than a noisy one. If the people
who taught sampling theory got it wrong when they were not concentrating, the
problem was not ignorance. It was that intuition runs on something other than
probability theory, and that the something has a describable shape.

## Three Shortcuts

Their account, laid out in a short paper in 1974 that has been cited more than
most books, was that people answering hard questions about likelihood
substitute easier questions, and that a handful of substitutions do most of the
work.

The first is representativeness. Asked how probable it is that A belongs to
category B, people assess how much A resembles a typical B. Steve resembles the
stereotype of a librarian, so librarian wins. Resemblance is a perfectly useful
signal and it is often right. But resemblance is blind to things probability is
not blind to: how common the category is to begin with, how reliable the
evidence is, and how much regression toward the mean to expect. A sequence of
coin flips that reads heads-tails-tails-heads-tails-heads looks more random than
six heads in a row and is exactly as likely.

The second is availability. Asked how frequent something is, people consult how
easily instances come to mind. Ease of retrieval correlates with frequency,
which is why the heuristic exists. It also correlates with vividness, recency,
and press coverage, which is why people overestimate deaths from shark attacks,
tornadoes and homicide and underestimate deaths from strokes, asthma and
diabetes. Anything that makes examples easier to summon — a recent local case, a
dramatic story, personal involvement — inflates the estimate.

The third is anchoring. Faced with a number they do not know, people start from
whatever number is in front of them and adjust, and the adjustment is
insufficient. In the original demonstration the experimenters spun a wheel
rigged to stop at a high or low value, asked whether a quantity was above or
below that value, then asked for an estimate. The wheel moved the estimates. A
number known to be arbitrary, produced by a device the participants could watch,
still dragged the answer toward itself. Anchoring is the most robust of the
three and the one with the most obvious commercial applications.

## The Conjunction and the Base Rate

Two demonstrations became famous because they are so hard to argue with.

Linda is thirty-one, single, outspoken, and very bright. She studied philosophy,
was deeply concerned with discrimination and social justice, and participated in
antinuclear demonstrations. Which is more probable: that Linda is a bank teller,
or that Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement? Most people
choose the second, including people with graduate training in statistics. A
conjunction cannot be more probable than either of its elements — every feminist
bank teller is a bank teller — so this is not a matter of interpretation or
sloppy priors. Representativeness has simply overridden the logic, because the
conjunction fits the description better than the bare category does.

The base-rate demonstration is a cab that sideswipes a car at night. Eighty-five
percent of the city's cabs are Green and fifteen percent Blue. A witness
identifies the cab as Blue, and testing shows this witness correctly identifies
each colour eighty percent of the time. Most people put the probability that the
cab was Blue at around eighty percent — they take the witness at face value and
drop the composition of the fleet. The correct answer is closer to forty
percent, because Green cabs are so much more numerous that the witness's
mistakes on Green cabs outnumber the correct calls on Blue ones. Tellingly, if
the same statistic is given a causal flavour — Green cabs are involved in
eighty-five percent of accidents — people use it much more readily. The number
that is hard to ignore is the one that sounds like an explanation.

## Losses Loom Larger

The second half of the program was about value rather than probability, and it
turned into the piece of work that carried a Nobel Prize in economics.

A disease is expected to kill six hundred people. Program A saves two hundred
for certain; Program B gives a one-third chance of saving all six hundred and a
two-thirds chance of saving none. Most people take the certain option. Restate
the identical outcomes as deaths — four hundred die for certain, versus a
one-third chance nobody dies and a two-thirds chance six hundred die — and most
people take the gamble. The arithmetic is unchanged; only the word has moved.
People are risk-averse when they think they are choosing among gains and
risk-seeking when they think they are choosing among losses, and which of those
they think depends on where the baseline is put.

Prospect theory formalized this. Outcomes are evaluated as changes from a
reference point rather than as final states of wealth; the value function is
steeper for losses than for gains, so that giving something up hurts roughly
twice as much as getting it pleases; and small probabilities are overweighted
while large ones are underweighted, which is how the same person buys lottery
tickets and insurance. A great deal follows. Owners demand more to sell a mug
than buyers will pay for it, minutes after being handed one at random. Investors
hold losing positions to avoid realizing a loss. Money already spent, which
cannot be recovered and should not matter, keeps projects alive for years.

## The Case for the Defense

The program had a serious critic in Gerd Gigerenzer, whose objections were
methodological and philosophical at once.

The methodological complaint was that the errors depend on how the question is
posed. Doctors asked about a screening test in percentages — prevalence one
percent, false-positive rate nine percent — do badly at estimating the chance a
positive result means disease. Give them the same information as natural
frequencies — ten women in a thousand have it, nine of those test positive,
about ninety of the healthy ones also test positive — and performance improves
dramatically. If a format change fixes the error, the error may be in the format
rather than the mind.

The deeper complaint was about the standard. Calling a strategy biased implies a
correct answer it deviates from, and in laboratory problems the correct answer
is supplied by the experimenter. In the world, information is costly and time is
short, and a simple rule that ignores most of the available evidence often
predicts better than an elaborate model, because the model fits noise. Asked
which of two cities is larger, people who recognize only one of the names should
pick the one they recognize, and they will usually be right — recognition
correlates with size through the same media that produced the recognition.
Gigerenzer's argument is that heuristics are adapted to structures in the
environment, and are rational relative to those structures.

Kahneman's side never claimed otherwise about the origin of the shortcuts. The
dispute was about how often the environment cooperates. What both positions
share is the rejection of the alternative that people quietly compute expected
utility.

## What Survived

The field's reckoning with replication in the 2010s was hard on parts of this
literature and easy on others. Anchoring replicates across large multi-laboratory
efforts. So does the framing effect, and loss aversion in most of its standard
forms, and the conjunction error. What did not survive as well were some of the
more spectacular downstream claims that grew up in the same intellectual
neighbourhood — priming manipulations with large behavioural effects from
trivial cues, and the idea that self-control depletes like a fuel.

The clearest practical finding predates all of it. Since the 1950s, comparisons
of expert clinical judgment against simple statistical rules using the same
inputs have gone the same way with monotonous regularity: the rule matches or
beats the expert, in diagnosis, in parole decisions, in academic admissions, in
hiring. Experts see the cues the rule uses, and add inconsistency.

What follows is less exciting than debiasing training, which does not work
particularly well. What works is structure. Estimating a project's duration from
the record of similar past projects, rather than from a mental walkthrough of
this one, corrects the planning fallacy well enough that some governments now
require it for infrastructure budgets. Writing down criteria and scoring
candidates separately on each beats a holistic impression. Asking a team to
imagine the project has already failed and write the history of the failure
surfaces objections that no amount of asking for concerns will produce. None of
these make anyone smarter. They change what the judgment is made of.
