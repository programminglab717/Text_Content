# How Children Acquire Language

In the late 1950s a graduate student named Jean Berko showed preschoolers a card
with a cheerful blue blob on it. "This is a wug," she said. Then a second card
with two of them. "Now there is another one. There are two ___." The children
said "wugs," pronouncing the plural with the buzzing z-sound that English
requires after a voiced consonant rather than the hissing s that follows a
voiceless one. They had never heard the word before. Nobody had taught them the
rule, and no adult in their lives could have stated it.

That is the whole problem in one card. Children are not memorizing what they
hear. They are extracting something general from it and applying it to cases
that have never occurred. They do this on a schedule, without instruction,
across every language yet examined, and they finish the bulk of it before they
can tie their shoes. Explaining how has occupied linguists and psychologists for
most of a century, and the argument has been unusually bitter, in part because
the answer bears on what kind of thing a mind is.

## Tuning the Ear

The work starts before birth. A fetus hears the low-frequency envelope of its
mother's speech through the abdominal wall — not the consonants, but the rhythm
and the melody. Newborns a few days old will suck harder to hear a recording of
their mother's voice than a stranger's, and they show a preference for the
prosody of the language they were surrounded by over one with a different
rhythmic structure. The first thing acquired is not a word but a tune.

Within the first months infants discriminate speech sounds with a sharpness that
looks like equipment built for the job. They treat the continuum between "ba"
and "pa" categorically, hearing an abrupt change at roughly the boundary adult
speakers use rather than a gradual shift. Crucially, they do this for contrasts
their own language does not employ. An infant in an English-speaking household
distinguishes Hindi dental and retroflex consonants that its parents cannot hear
apart at all.

And then, before the first birthday, that ability narrows. Somewhere in the
second half of the first year, discrimination of non-native contrasts drops away
while native contrasts sharpen. The infant is not losing a capacity so much as
committing to a partition of the sound space tailored to the language actually
in use — which is efficient, and which is also why adult second-language
learners find some distinctions almost impossible to hear. The narrowing is
driven by experience with live speech in a way that resists shortcuts. Infants
exposed to a foreign language by a person present in the room retain its
contrasts; infants given the same amount from a screen generally do not.

Alongside this runs a statistical problem that is easy to overlook. Running
speech has no reliable gaps between words. Listening to an unfamiliar language
is the experience of hearing an unbroken stream, and that is what the infant
faces. One of the tidiest results in the field showed eight-month-olds listening
for a couple of minutes to a continuous, monotone stream of invented syllables
with no pauses and no intonation, in which the only cue to word boundaries was
that some syllables reliably followed others while some pairings occurred only
across boundaries. Afterwards the infants treated the statistically coherent
units as familiar and the others as novel. Two minutes of exposure, and they had
tracked the transitions.

## Getting from Sounds to Meanings

Segmenting the stream still leaves the question of what any of it refers to. The
classic puzzle is that a word uttered while a rabbit runs past could mean rabbit,
or running, or ears, or fur, or white, or dinner. Children converge on the adult
meaning far faster than the logic allows, which means they are bringing
constraints to the task.

Some of those constraints are social. Long before they speak, infants follow
gaze and points, check an adult's face to see what they are attending to, and
establish shared focus on an object. Word learning tracks this closely: a
toddler who hears a new label while looking at one toy, when the speaker is
plainly looking at another, attaches the label to the speaker's target rather
than their own. They are not pairing sounds with whatever happens to be salient.
They are inferring what someone meant.

Other constraints look more like default assumptions about how labels work.
Presented with one familiar object and one strange one, a toddler who hears an
unfamiliar word reaches for the strange one — the working assumption being that
things do not have two names. New words are latched on with startling speed,
often from a single exposure, with a rough meaning that gets refined later. The
result is a vocabulary that accelerates through the second year, from a handful
of words to hundreds, and then to thousands.

How much a child hears matters, though the size of the effect has been
overstated in popular accounts. A widely quoted figure for the gap in words
heard between richer and poorer households has not held up well under attempted
replication, and the quality of interaction — turns taken, responses contingent
on what the child just did — looks more important than raw volume.

## The Argument from Poverty

Grammar is where the fight happened. Noam Chomsky's argument, made in various
forms from the late 1950s onward, was that the input children receive is too
thin to determine the grammar they end up with. His favorite illustration
concerns how English forms yes-no questions. From "the man is tall" you get "is
the man tall," which is consistent with a simple rule: move the first "is" to
the front. Now take "the man who is tall is in the room." The simple rule
produces "is the man who tall is in the room," which no child has ever been
recorded saying. Children instead front the verb of the main clause, respecting
a structural hierarchy nobody mentioned to them. The relevant sentences are rare
in speech to children, and the errors that the simple rule would predict do not
occur even once.

Behind this sat a broader observation. Children are not systematically corrected
for grammar. Parents respond to whether what was said is true or interesting,
not to whether it is well formed, and children who are corrected tend to ignore
it. So the learner has positive examples and essentially no negative ones, which
in the general case is not enough to pin down a grammar. Chomsky's conclusion
was that the search space must be constrained in advance by something innate.

## What the Corpora Did to the Debate

The poverty argument was powerful and, for a long time, hard to test, because
nobody knew precisely what children heard. Large transcribed corpora of
caregiver speech changed that, and they complicated the picture in both
directions.

They showed that some things attributed to innate structure could be learned
from distributional regularities richer than anyone had assumed, and that
children's early grammar is far less general than adult grammar. A two-year-old
who produces a transitive frame flawlessly with one verb frequently cannot
produce it with another verb learned the same week. Early syntax looks like a
collection of item-specific constructions — small schemas organized around
particular words — that only gradually abstract into general patterns. That
trajectory is what you would expect from a learner generalizing over usage, and
it is not what you would expect from a grammar switched on at birth.

Overregularization fits the same pattern. Children who said "went" correctly
start saying "goed," which looks like a rule being discovered and overapplied.
But corpus counts show the errors are much rarer than the anecdotes suggest — a
small percentage of opportunities, persisting for years, rather than a clean
phase.

The debate has not resolved so much as shifted. Almost nobody now claims that
language is learned by general-purpose association with no constraints, and
almost nobody defends a rich, detailed universal grammar specifying the
categories of every language. What remains contested is how much of the
constraint is specific to language and how much falls out of memory, attention,
sequence learning, and the pressure to work out what other people intend.

## Languages That Grew Up in One Generation

The most striking evidence comes from children who had no language to learn.
Deaf children born to hearing parents, with no exposure to a sign language,
invent gesture systems of their own, and those homesign systems show structure
their parents' gestures do not: consistent ordering, a distinction between
things and actions, ways of marking recurrence.

At the end of the 1970s a school for deaf children opened in Managua. The
teaching method, based on lipreading and speech, was largely unsuccessful. But
the children, brought together in numbers for the first time, pooled their
homesigns in the playground and on the buses and produced a common pidgin. What
happened next is the interesting part. Younger children entering the community
did not merely learn that pidgin; they regularized it, adding consistent
grammatical devices the older signers had not used, breaking gestures into
recombinable components. The language got systematically more complex in the
hands of its youngest users. The raw material was social; the systematizing was
done by children.

That capacity does not last. Age of first exposure predicts eventual command of
a language with unfortunate reliability, in signed languages as in spoken ones,
and the effect is not explained by intelligence or effort. Deaf adults who
acquired a sign language in childhood outperform those who acquired it in
adolescence on exactly the grammatical machinery the Managua children were busy
inventing. The rare and ethically appalling cases of children deprived of
language altogether into their teens point the same way: vocabulary can be built
afterwards, sometimes substantially, and the syntax never quite arrives.
