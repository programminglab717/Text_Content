# What Counts as a Vegetable

In 1893 the Supreme Court of the United States ruled on whether a tomato is a
fruit. The question was not idle. Imported vegetables were taxed under the
tariff act and imported fruits were not, so an importer named Nix sued the
customs collector for a refund, arguing the botanically obvious: a tomato is the
ripened ovary of a flowering plant, seeds and all, and botanists everywhere call
that a fruit.

The Court agreed on the botany and ruled against him anyway. Tomatoes, the
opinion observed, are served at dinner with the fish and the meat, not afterward
as dessert. They belong with cucumbers and squashes and peas in the ordinary
speech of ordinary people, and the tariff was written in ordinary speech. Duty
owed.

It is a small case that puts a finger on something large. Two systems of
categories, both coherent, both useful, disagreeing — and a third question
underneath them, which is what a category is in the first place and what work it
does in a head.

Whatever else categories do, their main job is inference. Recognizing that the
thing on the path is a snake is worth almost nothing by itself; what matters is
everything that comes free with the label — that it can bite, that it will not
chase you far, that it was probably warming itself. A category is a bet that the
unobserved properties of a new thing will resemble those of things it already
groups with. Everything difficult about categorization follows from the fact
that this bet has to be made fast, on partial information, in a world whose
joints are not always where the words are.

## The Definition That Never Arrives

The inherited answer runs from Aristotle: a category is defined by features that
every member has and no non-member has. Necessary and sufficient conditions.
Membership is all-or-nothing, and the boundary is sharp.

This works beautifully for triangles and rather badly for everything else.
Wittgenstein made the standard demonstration with the word "game." Board games,
card games, ball games, ring-a-ring-o'-roses, solitaire, a child throwing a ball
against a wall. Look for the common thread: amusement? Chess tournaments are not
amusing for everyone playing. Competition? Solitaire has none. Rules? A child
inventing a game as she goes has none worth stating. Winning and losing, skill,
luck — each feature covers a swathe and fails somewhere. What he saw instead was
a network of overlapping resemblances, like the resemblances among members of a
family where everyone shares the nose with someone and the chin with someone
else and nobody has all of it.

Try the exercise with "chair" and it goes the same way. Four legs — beanbags and
stools fail. Has a back — stools again. For sitting on — so is a rock, a step, a
horse. You can keep patching, and the patches never close.

## Robins and Penguins

Eleanor Rosch's contribution in the 1970s was to stop treating the failure of
definitions as a philosophical embarrassment and start measuring what people
actually do instead.

Ask people to rate how good an example of "bird" various birds are and they
comply without complaint, which they should not be able to do if membership is
binary — being a bird ought to be like being pregnant. They rate robins and
sparrows high, hawks and owls in the middle, penguins and ostriches low, and
they agree with each other closely. Those ratings then predict a great deal.
Typical members are verified faster in sentence-checking tasks: "a robin is a
bird" is confirmed more quickly than "a penguin is a bird." They are produced
first when people list members of a category. Children learn them earlier. They
substitute better in sentences — "the bird sang outside the window" conjures a
sparrow, and becomes odd if you insist on a vulture. Typical members serve as
the reference point for the atypical ones and not the other way round; people
find it natural to say a penguin is a sort of bird, and strange to say a robin
is a sort of penguin.

Rosch's proposal was that categories are organized around prototypes — a central
tendency, a best example real or abstracted — with membership shading off by
similarity toward a fuzzy edge. It accounted for the data and it has an obvious
appeal, which makes the most important objection to it worth taking seriously.
Ask people to rate how good an example of "even number" the number four is, and
then the number one hundred and six, and they will do it. Four is rated a better
even number. This cannot mean that even-numberhood is graded; it is the cleanest
definition in existence. So typicality ratings are measuring something —
accessibility, familiarity, how readily an item comes to mind — that does not
always indicate the structure of the category itself. Graded ratings, on their
own, are weaker evidence than they look.

## The Level You Name Things At

The other durable result from that period concerns not which members are central
but which categories get used.

A German shepherd can truthfully be called an animal, a dog, or a German
shepherd. In practice people say dog, overwhelmingly, unless there is a reason
not to. Rosch called this the basic level and identified what makes it
privileged. It is the most general level at which the members still share an
overall shape, so a single mental image can stand for the category. It is the
level at which a common set of actions applies — you interact with dogs in a way
you do not interact with animals in general. Basic-level names tend to be short,
are learned first by children, and are the ones adults reach for by default. Go
up a level and you lose all the concrete detail; go down and you gain very
little new.

What makes this more than a curiosity is that the basic level moves. Bird is
basic for most people and is not basic for a birdwatcher, who sees a dunlin
rather than a bird. A dog breeder sees breeds. A radiologist sees a specific
pattern where you would see a chest. Expertise does not just add knowledge to a
category system; it re-slices the system at a finer grain, and the finer
categories become the automatic ones.

## The Case of Color

Color has served as the field's test bed for the question of how much of this
structure is imposed by language, because the physical input is continuous and
the words are not. Brent Berlin and Paul Kay surveyed color vocabularies across
many languages and found that the number of basic terms varies widely — some
languages get by with two or three — but the terms are not distributed
arbitrarily. Languages with few terms carve the space in predictable places, and
when speakers of any language are asked to point at the best example of each of
their terms, the best examples cluster in similar regions regardless of where
their language draws the boundaries.

Rosch tested this among the Dani of highland New Guinea, whose language
partitions color into essentially two terms. If color categories were simply
inherited from vocabulary, Dani speakers should have found any arbitrary color
equally learnable. They did not. Taught new color words, they acquired ones
centered on the focal reds and blues more readily than ones centered elsewhere,
which suggested that the visual system offers up some regions as better anchors
than others and language mostly decides how many anchors to use.

The story has grown more complicated since. Where a language puts its boundary
does appear to have modest effects on discrimination near that boundary, and the
effects show up more strongly in the visual field connected to the
language-dominant hemisphere, which is about as strange and as suggestive a
finding as that literature contains. The settled position is a compromise nobody
loves: perception constrains the possible categories, language selects among
them, and the selection then leans a little on perception.

## Prototypes, Exemplars, and What Similarity Cannot Do

An alternative to storing an abstracted prototype is to store the instances
themselves. Exemplar models hold that when you decide whether the thing in front
of you is a dog, you compare it against your accumulated memory of individual
dogs, all of them, weighted by similarity, and the sum decides. These models are
more computationally demanding and they fit experimental data better in a range
of situations — particularly where people are sensitive to the variability
within a category, or to the specific odd instances they were trained on, which
an averaged prototype has thrown away.

Both accounts run on similarity, and that is where the deeper objection bites.
Gregory Murphy and Douglas Medin pointed out that similarity is close to
unconstrained: any two things share indefinitely many properties if you are
permitted to count properties freely, so similarity can only explain
categorization if something else has already decided which features matter.
Something has, and it looks like knowledge about how the world works. People
group a man jumping into a swimming pool fully clothed with other emergencies,
not with other swimmers, because they have a theory about why he did it.
Lawrence Barsalou showed that people can construct sensible graded categories on
the spot for things that have no similarity structure at all — things to take
out of a burning house, ways to avoid being conscripted — and the structure
comes from the goal, not from shared features.

The most striking evidence for theory-driven categories comes from children.
Frank Keil described transformations to young children: a raccoon that
scientists shave, dye, and fit with a scent sac so that it looks and smells
exactly like a skunk. Past a certain age children insist it is still a raccoon.
Do the same story with a coffeepot remade into a bird feeder and they say it is
now a bird feeder. Living kinds are treated as having a hidden inner nature that
determines what they are regardless of appearance; artifacts are treated as
being whatever they are for. Susan Gelman's work mapped how early and how
robustly this essentialism appears, and it arrives long before children have any
idea what a gene is.

The intuition earns its keep with natural kinds. Tigers really do have an
underlying nature that explains their stripes, and betting on the hidden essence
rather than the surface is good inductive policy. Applied to people it goes
badly. The same machinery that lets a child hold on to the raccoon under the dye
happily treats nationality, class, gender and race as though they too concealed
an essence that explained behavior, and the categories feel natural in exactly
the way tiger does. That is not a bug in a separate social module. It is the
ordinary concept system, running on inputs it was never calibrated for.
