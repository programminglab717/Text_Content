# Perception as Inference

Hold a coin at arm's length and tilt it. The patch of light it throws onto your
retina is an ellipse, and the ellipse gets thinner the further you tilt. You do
not see an ellipse. You see a circular coin at a slant, and you go on seeing a
circular coin right up until the edge disappears. Nothing in the image told you
the coin was round. The image was equally consistent with an elliptical coin
held flat, or a coin-shaped hole, or a painted ellipse on a pane of glass, or an
enormous ellipse very far away. You picked one, instantly and without
deliberation, and the picking felt like nothing at all.

This is the fundamental awkwardness of vision, and of every other sense. The
data arriving at the receptors underdetermine the world that produced them. A
two-dimensional image is a projection, and projections throw information away.
Recovering the scene from the image is what engineers call an ill-posed problem:
infinitely many arrangements of surfaces, illuminants, and viewpoints produce
exactly the same pattern of light. The only way to get a single answer out of it
is to bring something extra to the table — assumptions about which
arrangements of the world are likely in the first place. Perception is not the
transmission of a scene to a viewer. It is a guess about causes, constrained by
prior expectation, that is usually right because the expectations are usually
good.

## Helmholtz's Wager

Hermann von Helmholtz put this in print in the middle of the nineteenth century
and called it unconscious inference. His argument was partly from illusions and
partly from the plain mechanics of the eye. He knew the retinal image was
inverted, blurred by the lens, riddled with chromatic aberration, interrupted by
a substantial hole where the optic nerve leaves, and smeared several times a
second by eye movements. None of that reaches experience. What reaches
experience is a stable, upright, continuous, sharply bounded world. Something in
between is doing a great deal of work, and doing it before the result is handed
over.

The word "inference" was chosen carefully and was controversial for exactly the
reason you would expect: inference sounds like reasoning, and nobody reasons
their way to a coin. Helmholtz's point was structural rather than
phenomenological. The operation has the logical form of an inference from
premises to a conclusion, drawn from the image plus a set of assumptions, and it
is not available to introspection or open to correction. You can know with
complete confidence that two lines are the same length and continue to see one
as longer. The inference does not care what you believe.

The modern version of the argument is stated in probability. Given an image, the
visual system is after the scene that best combines two quantities: how likely
that image would be if the scene were true, and how likely that kind of scene is
to occur at all. The second term is where the assumptions live. They are not
arbitrary. They are compressed statistics of the world the visual system evolved
and developed in.

## Assumptions You Never Agreed To

Some of those priors can be isolated. Light, on the surface of this planet, has
overwhelmingly come from above, and the visual system assumes it. A photograph
of a field of craters turned upside down becomes a field of domes, because the
shading pattern that means "concave, lit from above" is the same pattern that
means "convex, lit from below," and only one of those interpretations is
considered seriously. Faces get the same treatment more dramatically. Look at
the concave, hollow side of a face mask with reasonable lighting and it flips
inside out: you see a normal convex face, and it appears to rotate strangely and
follow you as you move. The prior for convex faces is strong enough to override
binocular depth information that is telling the truth. That override is weaker
in some people — the illusion is notably less compelling in schizophrenia — which
is one of the threads connecting ordinary perception to clinical symptoms.

Other priors are about geometry. Adelbert Ames built a room with a floor that
slopes and a far wall that recedes at an angle, cut so that from one particular
peephole it projects exactly the image a rectangular room would project. Viewers
hold on to the rectangular room and throw away the sizes of the people in it, so
a child at one corner towers over an adult at the other. Given the choice
between abandoning the assumption that rooms have square corners and abandoning
the assumption that people come in standard sizes, the visual system abandons
the people.

There are priors about motion, too. Slow motions are more common than fast ones,
and the system is biased accordingly. This is why a low-contrast object, whose
speed is measured noisily, appears to move more slowly than a high-contrast one
travelling at the same rate: with unreliable evidence, the estimate is pulled
toward the expectation. Drivers in fog systematically underestimate their speed
and the speed of everything around them, and they do so as a direct consequence
of a sensible statistical policy.

## Weighing the Witnesses

When two senses report on the same thing, the brain does not pick one. It
averages them, and it weights the average by reliability in something very close
to the optimal way. Have someone judge the height of a ridge both by looking at
it and by feeling it, then degrade the visual display with noise. As vision gets
worse, the combined judgment slides toward touch, smoothly and at about the rate
statistics says it should. Nobody decides to trust their fingers more. The
weighting is automatic and the combined estimate is more precise than either
sense alone.

The ordinary experience of this is ventriloquism, which requires no skill at
making your lips still. Vision localizes objects far more precisely than hearing
does, so when a visible mouth moves in time with a voice, the voice is assigned
to the mouth. Every cinema exploits it; the sound comes from speakers on the
walls and you hear it coming from a face. Push the conflict into the identity of
the sound rather than its location and you get something stranger. Dub the
syllable "ba" onto a face articulating "ga" and most people hear "da" — not one
input or the other, but a compromise consistent with both the acoustic evidence
and the visual evidence. Close your eyes and it reverts to "ba." Knowing all
this changes nothing.

## The Brain as a Predictor

The account that has done most to organize the last few decades takes the
inference one step further and makes it continuous. On this picture the cortex
is constantly generating predictions about its own incoming signals and sending
them downward, while what travels upward is mostly the residue — the part of the
input that the prediction failed to explain. Perception is what happens when the
prediction settles; the error signal is what forces it to change.

The appeal is partly in what it explains about cost. A system that transmits
only surprises is transmitting far less than one that ships every pixel upward,
and the brain is expensive enough that this matters. It also accounts for a pile
of otherwise odd findings: neurons that respond vigorously to an edge in
isolation and fall quiet when the same edge continues into a predictable
surrounding texture; the way an expected stimulus produces less activity and
faster recognition at once.

It makes sense of ambiguity, too. Show each eye a different image and experience
does not blend them or average them; it alternates, seconds at a time, one
interpretation then the other. Two hypotheses both explain the data, neither can
be reconciled with the other, and the system refuses to hold both. Ambiguous
figures behave the same way for the same reason.

The clinical extension is that a prediction weighted too heavily against the
evidence becomes a percept with no cause. People who hear voices are unusually
easy to condition into hearing a tone that is not played, after a few pairings
with a light. The expectation is doing what expectations always do; it is simply
doing too much of it.

## Where the Seams Show

If perception were a transcription, illusions would be malfunctions. If it is
inference, they are the places where a normally excellent assumption is being
applied to a scene engineered to violate it, and they become the best tool for
reading out what the assumptions are.

A grey patch on a checkerboard that looks pale in shadow and dark in light, when
both patches send the same grey to your eye, is not an error about the light. It
is a correct answer to a different and more useful question: what colour is that
surface, independently of what is falling on it. Surfaces are stable and
illumination is not, so the system reports the surface. The photograph of a dress
that split the internet a decade ago worked because its lighting cues were
genuinely uninformative, and different people's assumptions about the illuminant
diverged — bluish daylight for some, warm indoor light for others — with the
result that the same pixels were assigned to different fabric.

The uncomfortable part is not that these cases exist but that they do not feel
like cases. Everything you see arrives with the same air of directness. The
guess and the certainty are manufactured together, and the manufacturing is
invisible from inside.
