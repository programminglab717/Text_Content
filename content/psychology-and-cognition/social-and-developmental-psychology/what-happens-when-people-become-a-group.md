# What Happens When People Become a Group

Watch the first ten minutes of any committee that has never met before. Someone
sits at the head of the table without being asked to. Someone else makes a joke
about the coffee and, in doing so, quietly claims the job of keeping things
pleasant. Within a quarter of an hour the room has a speaking order, a rough
status ranking, a default level of formality, and an unspoken agreement about
how long a person may talk before being interrupted. Nobody voted on any of it.
By the third meeting it will be nearly impossible to change, and any new member
who violates it will be corrected within seconds, usually without a word being
said.

That rapid, invisible congealing is the phenomenon Kurt Lewin named group
dynamics in the 1940s, and it remains the most useful single observation in the
field: a collection of people is not the sum of the people. Lewin argued that
behavior is a function of the person and the environment together, and that the
environment worth studying is mostly social — a field of forces made of other
people's expectations, in which an individual moves the way a ball bearing moves
across a magnetic plate. He was also unusually interested in whether the
research was good for anything, and the centers he founded produced both the
laboratory tradition of small-group experiments and the applied tradition of
sensitivity training, the latter by accident, after facilitators at a workshop in
Maine discovered that participants who overheard the staff discussing them
learned more from that than from the sessions.

## Roles, Norms, and the Shape a Group Settles Into

The earliest laboratory result from Lewin's own group concerned climate. Boys'
hobby clubs were run by adults instructed to behave autocratically, democratically,
or with the near-total hands-off style called laissez-faire, and the differences
were not mainly in how much got made. Autocratic groups produced work while the
leader watched and stopped or turned destructive when he left. Democratic groups
kept going in his absence and were friendlier to each other. Laissez-faire
groups were unproductive and unhappy, which surprised people who had assumed
freedom was the operative variable. Leadership style, in other words, is not a
thermostat on effort; it changes what the group becomes.

Roles differentiate almost as fast as norms. Robert Bales, coding thousands of
utterances in problem-solving groups at Harvard, found a recurring split between
a task specialist — the person who pushes for decisions, supplies information,
and is often the most disliked by the end — and a socio-emotional specialist who
manages tension, jokes, and makes sure the quiet member gets a turn. Groups that
lose one of the two functions tend to deteriorate in predictable ways: all task
and they fracture, all warmth and they never decide anything.

Norms come in two kinds that are easy to confuse and behave very differently.
A descriptive norm is what people actually do; an injunctive norm is what people
approve of. Campaigns that announce how many people are doing the undesirable
thing — littering, binge drinking, skipping a vaccination — sometimes backfire
precisely because they broadcast a descriptive norm while intending an
injunctive one. The gap between the two also produces pluralistic ignorance, the
situation in which most members of a group privately reject a norm while
believing everyone else accepts it, and therefore publicly uphold it. It is a
stable arrangement, since the only evidence anyone has about everyone else's
beliefs is their public conduct.

Bruce Tuckman's sequence of forming, storming, norming, and performing has
become the standard vocabulary for the whole process, which is worth
remembering was a synthesis of existing literature rather than an experimental
finding. As a set of labels for things that often happen it is genuinely handy.
As a law it is oversold: plenty of groups skip the storm, some never leave it,
and many cycle back through earlier phases whenever membership or the task
changes.

## Process Losses

The unglamorous half of group dynamics concerns everything a group does worse
than its members would separately. Ivan Steiner formalized it: actual
productivity equals potential productivity minus process loss, and process loss
is where nearly all the interesting variance lives.

The oldest demonstration belongs to a French agricultural engineer, Maximilien
Ringelmann, who measured men pulling on a rope and found that average individual
effort declined as the group grew. That result splits into two mechanisms.
Coordination loss is mechanical — people pull at slightly different angles and
moments. Motivation loss is the part psychologists call social loafing, and it
was isolated by having blindfolded participants shout or clap while believing
others were doing so too. The single most effective correction is
identifiability. When individual contributions can be measured, or when a person
believes their effort is uniquely necessary, loafing largely disappears.

Brainstorming provides the neatest illustration of collective
underperformance. Groups instructed to generate ideas together reliably produce
fewer ideas, and often fewer good ones, than the same number of people working
alone and pooling their output afterward. The culprits are production blocking
— only one person can speak at a time, and while waiting you forget or discard
what you had — plus evaluation apprehension and a tendency for early
contributions to anchor everyone's search. Hybrid procedures that begin with
silent individual generation and only then move to discussion consistently beat
pure group brainstorming, which is why the better-run workshops now start with
sticky notes and silence.

Groups also fail at their supposed core advantage: pooling what members
individually know. In hidden-profile experiments, information is distributed so
that no single member has enough to identify the best option, but the group
collectively does. Groups in this setup mostly discuss what everyone already
knows, because shared information is more likely to be mentioned by someone, and
each mention prompts confirmation and repetition. The unique facts — precisely
the ones that would change the answer — often surface late or not at all.

The bystander effect is the same arithmetic applied to responsibility. After
John Darley and Bibb Latané ran their studies on intervention in staged
emergencies, the reliable finding was that the presence of others reduces the
probability that any given individual acts, through diffusion of responsibility
and through the ambiguity created by everyone else's apparent calm. The story
that launched the research — the widely reported claim that dozens of witnesses
watched a murder in Queens and did nothing — turned out to be substantially
wrong as journalism, and field studies of real public conflicts suggest
intervention by someone is more common than the laboratory version implies. The
mechanisms are real; the fable attached to them was not.

## Groups and Risk

A durable folk belief holds that committees moderate. They do the opposite. The
effect was discovered as the risky shift — groups making bolder choices than
their members' private averages — and was later generalized as group
polarization: discussion moves a group further in whatever direction it was
already leaning. Two mechanisms are usually credited. Discussion surfaces a
disproportionate number of arguments favoring the prevailing view, since that is
what most members can supply; and members who discover they are less committed
than the room adjust to protect their standing. Polarization explains why
deliberating juries, ideologically sorted neighborhoods, and self-selected
online communities all drift outward rather than toward the center.

Irving Janis's groupthink is the pathological version, assembled from
retrospective study of foreign-policy disasters — the Bay of Pigs being his
central exhibit, with the Cuban missile crisis deliberations offered as the same
people doing it better after they changed their procedures. The syndrome he
described includes an illusion of invulnerability, pressure on doubters,
self-censorship, mindguards who shield the leader from discordant information,
and a resulting illusion of unanimity. The caution is that the evidence base is
mostly case studies chosen because they ended badly, which is a design that
cannot show how often the same conditions produce fine decisions. Laboratory
tests have supported some ingredients, particularly the damage done by a leader
who states a preference early and by insulation from outside information, while
the full syndrome as a package has been harder to pin down.

## The Minority That Changes the Room

None of this would matter much if groups only ever absorbed their members.
Serge Moscovici's experiments pushed in the other direction: a consistent
minority, calling a blue slide green trial after trial without wavering,
produced few immediate public conversions and a measurable shift in private
judgment afterward, including on related tasks that the minority never
addressed. That pattern — majorities producing compliance that is broad and
shallow, minorities producing conversion that is narrow and deep — is the reason
dissent is worth protecting on grounds of decision quality alone, independent of
whether the dissenter is right. A group that contains one person willing to be
consistently unpopular searches harder, generates more alternatives, and notices
the unique information it would otherwise talk right past.
