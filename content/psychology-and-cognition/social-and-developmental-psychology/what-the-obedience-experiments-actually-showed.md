# What the Obedience Experiments Actually Showed

The machine is a grey box with a row of thirty toggle switches, each labeled
with a voltage and each group of four labeled again with a phrase: Slight Shock,
Moderate Shock, Intense Shock, Danger: Severe Shock. The last two switches carry
no words, only XXX. It was built to be convincing and it was convincing; it
survives today in a museum collection. Almost everything a person on the street can
tell you about social psychology traces back to this box and to two or three
other pieces of apparatus from the same two decades — a slide projector, a set
of printed cards, a basement corridor fitted out as a jail. Those studies are
also the ones that have been hardest on their own reputations in the years
since, and the shape of the reassessment is more interesting than the original
headlines.

## A Norm Out of Nothing

The sequence starts before the war, with Muzafer Sherif and a point of light in
a dark room. Stare at a stationary pinpoint in total darkness and it appears to
drift, because the eye has no frame of reference and its own micro-movements go
uncorrected. Ask someone how far it moved and they will invent an answer, and
after a few trials they settle into a personal range — some people say two
inches, some say eight. Put several such people in the room together and have
them call out their estimates, and within a session their ranges converge on a
common figure that belongs to none of them individually. The interesting part
came afterward. Tested alone again, participants kept using the group's figure,
not their original one. The norm had been internalized rather than merely
performed. Later researchers extended the design by replacing group members one
at a time, and found the arbitrary norm surviving across several such
generations of participants, drifting slowly, like a tradition whose origin
nobody present can remember.

## The Lines

Solomon Asch was uneasy with the implication. The autokinetic task is
ambiguous; of course people lean on each other when the stimulus tells them
nothing. He wanted to know what happened when the evidence was unmistakable, and
designed the plainest possible test: a card with a standard line, a card with
three comparison lines of obviously different lengths, and a group of seven or
eight people announcing aloud which comparison matched. All but one were
confederates, and on designated trials they gave the same wrong answer,
unanimously, before the real participant's turn.

Asch expected independence and got something messier. A substantial minority of
judgments went along with the wrong majority, and a clear majority of
participants conformed at least once. A sizeable group never conformed at all,
and the interviews afterward showed that the conformers were not homogeneous:
some believed they must have misperceived, some suspected the group was wrong
but would not be the lone voice, some simply did not want to make a scene. The
variations mattered more than the headline. Having a single ally who gave the
correct answer cut conformity dramatically — and it still fell when the ally
gave a different wrong answer, which shows that what breaks the effect is the
end of unanimity rather than social support for the truth. Letting participants
write answers privately collapsed the effect as well. Increasing the majority
beyond three or four added almost nothing.

Asch himself argued that the result was about the stubbornness of independence
as much as the pull of the group, a reading the textbooks quietly dropped.
Later work has qualified it further. A British replication carried out decades later found
very little conformity, prompting the suggestion that the original result owed
something to a particularly conformist American decade. Cross-national
comparisons suggest conformity in these tasks runs higher in societies with more
collectivist norms, and reviews assembling many replications have reported a
decline in conformity within the United States over the decades since. None of
this makes the effect an artifact. It makes it a variable rather than a
constant.

## The Shock Box

Stanley Milgram had been Asch's research assistant, and his design is Asch's
problem with the stakes raised. A participant recruited by newspaper
advertisement arrives at a laboratory at Yale, meets a mild middle-aged man
named Wallace who is in fact a confederate, and draws a rigged lot that makes
the participant the teacher. Wallace is strapped into a chair in an adjoining
room. The teacher reads word pairs and, on each error, moves one switch further
along the box. At 300 volts Wallace pounds on the wall. After that he stops
responding entirely, and the experimenter instructs that no response counts as a
wrong answer.

When the participant balks, the experimenter delivers a scripted prod: Please
continue. The experiment requires that you continue. It is absolutely essential
that you continue. You have no other choice, you must go on. The finding
everybody knows is that a majority of participants in the standard condition
went to the final switch.

The finding almost nobody knows is that Milgram ran a great many conditions, and
the rate moved all over the place depending on the arrangement. Obedience fell
when the learner was in the same room, fell further when the teacher had to
press the learner's hand onto a plate, fell when the experimenter gave his
instructions by telephone instead of from the next chair, and fell to almost
nothing when two confederate teachers rebelled and walked out first. It rose
when the participant only read the words while someone else threw the switches.
Moving the whole operation from Yale to a shabby office suite in Bridgeport
reduced it somewhat, though less than people expect. Read as a set, the
variations are not a demonstration that people obey. They are an inventory of
the conditions under which the obliging response is easy and the conditions
under which it becomes unbearable.

## What the Archive Added

Milgram's reputation has been through two overhauls. The first was ethical and
immediate: Diana Baumrind's critique argued that the procedure inflicted serious
distress on people who had not agreed to anything of the kind, and that the
scientific payoff did not obviously justify it. That argument is now settled
institutional practice; no review board would approve the original protocol.

The second came much later, when researchers went through the recordings and
correspondence in the Yale archive. Gina Perry's account documented that the
experimenter frequently departed from the four scripted prods, improvising,
cajoling, and pressing participants well beyond the published procedure; that
debriefing was in many cases delayed by a long period, contrary to the
impression Milgram gave; and that a meaningful number of participants had
doubted the shocks were real, which complicates the interpretation of what they
were doing when they pressed the switch.

The sharpest reinterpretation is Alexander Haslam and Stephen Reicher's. Going
through the audio, they observed that the prods did not work equally: appeals to
the requirements of the experiment and the value of the science tended to keep
people going, while the fourth prod — the one that removed choice altogether —
almost always preceded a refusal. If blind submission to authority were the
mechanism, the most authoritarian instruction should have been the most
effective. That it was the least effective points toward something they call
engaged followership: participants continued not because their agency had been
switched off, but because they had been recruited into a project they took to be
worthwhile, and they stopped when the appeal stopped flattering that
identification and became a naked command. Attempts to run the procedure under
modern ethical constraints, stopping at the point where the original results
show the outcome was largely determined, have produced compliance in the same
broad territory as Milgram's.

## The Prison That Was Not an Experiment

The Stanford prison study occupies a different position entirely, because the
reassessment has been closer to a demolition. Philip Zimbardo's 1971 simulation
in a basement at Stanford is usually narrated as evidence that ordinary students
assigned to be guards spontaneously became cruel. Archival work, notably by
Thibault Le Texier, together with participants' own later accounts, has
established that guards were briefed toward hostility rather than left to invent
it, that the study's warden was a research collaborator who intervened when
guards were insufficiently tough, and that at least one prisoner's celebrated
breakdown was, by his own later telling, performed in order to get out. There
was no control condition and no independent coding, and the study was never
subjected to ordinary peer review as an experiment. Separate work has shown that
volunteers responding to an advertisement mentioning a prison study differ in
measurable ways from those answering the same advertisement without that word,
so the sample was not neutral either.

When Reicher and Haslam ran a television-documented prison study of their own,
the dynamic was nearly the reverse: the guards failed to form a cohesive group
or agree on how to use their power, and the prisoners did. Roles did not simply
take people over. What mattered was which group managed to become a group, and
what its members thought they were for — which returns the whole line of
research to the question Sherif started with, about where a shared norm comes
from and how much authority it acquires once it exists.
