# How Infants Form Attachments

A carpeted room with two chairs and a heap of toys in the corner. A mother and
her one-year-old are shown in and left to settle. Over the next twenty minutes
or so a stranger comes in, the mother leaves, the mother returns, the mother
leaves again — a choreography of eight short episodes, most of them about three
minutes long, arranged so that the child's stress climbs in small, tolerable
steps. Behind the one-way glass, nobody is especially interested in whether the
baby cries. What matters is the reunion: what the child does in the first
seconds after the door opens again.

That procedure is the Strange Situation, and it is the reason developmental
psychology has a measurable object where it might otherwise have had only a
sentiment. Mary Ainsworth designed it in the 1960s at Johns Hopkins, after
years of watching mothers and infants in their own homes — first among Ganda
families in Uganda, later in Baltimore. The laboratory version compresses into
twenty minutes something she had spent hundreds of hours observing: the way an
infant uses a particular adult as a base from which to explore, and as a place
to go when exploring becomes frightening.

## The Problem Bowlby Was Trying to Solve

The theory behind the room came from John Bowlby, a British psychiatrist whose
early clinical work concerned delinquent adolescents and whose formative
professional years coincided with wartime evacuation, mass hospitalization of
children, and the postwar residue of orphanages. Institutions of the period fed
and washed children adequately and still produced listless, withdrawn, oddly
indiscriminate small people. The prevailing psychoanalytic explanation treated
the infant's tie to the mother as secondary, a habit built up through
association with feeding. Bowlby thought that got the causality backwards.

What gave him a different account was ethology. Konrad Lorenz's greylag
goslings followed the first moving object they saw, with no food involved.
Harry Harlow's rhesus infants, offered a wire surrogate that dispensed milk and
a cloth-covered surrogate that dispensed nothing, spent their time clinging to
the cloth and ran to it when frightened — a grim set of experiments whose
ethics have been argued over ever since, and whose central result nonetheless
demolished the cupboard-love theory. Bowlby concluded that proximity-seeking is
a behavioral system in its own right, selected for because infants who stayed
near a protective adult survived predators and accidents at higher rates. The
system is activated by fear, pain, illness, and separation, and deactivated by
contact. It is not a drive that gets used up; it is more like a thermostat.

This reframing carried a prediction that a feeding-based theory could not make.
If attachment is about protection rather than provision, then what the caregiver
does when the child is distressed should matter more than anything else — and
Ainsworth's home observations bore that out. Mothers who noticed their infants'
signals early, read them accurately, and responded promptly had infants who
behaved differently in the laboratory room from infants whose mothers were
inconsistent, intrusive, or reliably unavailable.

## Three Patterns, Then Four

The reunion behaviors sorted into a small number of recognizable configurations.
Most infants in Ainsworth's Baltimore sample — roughly two-thirds, a proportion
that has held up in many North American and Western European middle-class
samples since — protested the separation, greeted the mother on her return,
sought contact, settled, and went back to the toys. Ainsworth called this
pattern secure, labeled it B, and treated the return to exploration as the
diagnostic part. The attachment system had been switched on and then switched
off again by the caregiver's presence, which is precisely what it is for.

A second group barely protested and, on reunion, looked away, busied themselves
with a toy, or moved past the mother as if she were furniture. These were the
avoidant infants, group A. The obvious reading is that they were unbothered, and
that reading is wrong: physiological measures taken during the procedure show
these children are aroused, sometimes more so than the criers. What they have
learned is that displaying distress does not bring comfort, and may bring
rebuff, so the display is suppressed while the arousal is not.

A third group, resistant or ambivalent — group C — became extremely distressed
at separation and then, on reunion, could not use the reunion. They wanted
contact and fought it, arching away while clinging, crying after being picked
up, unable to return to play. Their caregivers, in home observation, tended to
be unpredictable rather than rejecting: sometimes warm and available, sometimes
preoccupied, with no pattern the infant could learn.

Two decades on, Mary Main and Judith Solomon noticed that the coding scheme was
forcing a residue of children into categories they did not fit. Watching those
tapes closely, they saw brief, strange interruptions: a child freezing
mid-approach, approaching with head averted, rocking on hands and knees, or
showing an expression of apprehension at the parent's entrance. They named this
disorganized attachment, group D, and it is now understood as the momentary
collapse of strategy that happens when the person who should resolve the fear is
also its source. Disorganized classifications are over-represented in samples
where maltreatment is known to have occurred, though they also appear where a
parent is frightened rather than frightening — grieving, dissociated, or
carrying an unresolved loss of their own.

Main also extended the work upward, into language. The Adult Attachment
Interview asks adults to describe their childhood relationships and to support
their descriptions with specific memories. What predicts an adult's infant's
classification, strikingly, is not whether the childhood described was happy but
whether the account of it is coherent — whether generalizations are backed by
episodes, whether the speaker can stay on the question, whether contradictions
are noticed. People who narrate a painful history clearly tend to have securely
attached infants. People whose accounts are thin and idealized, or flooded and
entangled, tend not to.

## Internal Working Models and the Question of Continuity

Bowlby's term for the residue attachment leaves behind was the internal working
model: a set of expectations about whether distress brings help and whether the
self is the sort of person who gets helped. The word "model" is doing real work
there. It is meant to be a representation that is updated by evidence, not a
stamp pressed into wax during a critical period.

Whether it actually gets updated is where the evidence is more interesting than
the popular version of the theory. Longitudinal projects that have followed
children from infancy into adulthood — the Minnesota study begun by Alan Sroufe
and Byron Egeland is the best known — find continuity that is real but
moderate. Infant classification predicts later peer competence, emotion
regulation, and romantic functioning at levels well above chance and well below
determinism. Classifications also change when circumstances change. A family
that stabilizes, a mother whose depression lifts, the arrival of a supportive
partner or grandparent: these shift children from insecure to secure
classifications, and losses and upheavals shift them the other way. The model
tracks the environment, which is what a model is supposed to do.

## The Qualifications That Matter

Three lines of criticism have genuinely reshaped the field, and none of them
amounts to a refutation.

The first is temperament. Jerome Kagan argued for years that the Strange
Situation may be measuring the infant's constitutional reactivity to novelty as
much as the history of a relationship — that an easily distressed baby will
look one way and a placid one another, regardless of who is holding the door.
The compromise position now generally held is that temperament shapes the form
distress takes while caregiving history shapes whether the distress gets
resolved, which is why an infant's classification can differ with mother and
with father. It is a property of a relationship, not of a child.

The second is culture. The three-category distribution is not universal.
Northern German samples have shown more avoidance, and Japanese samples more
resistance, and in both cases the plausible explanation is that the procedure
does not mean the same thing everywhere. A German infant raised toward early
self-reliance and a Japanese infant who has rarely been left alone with a
stranger are not experiencing the same twenty minutes. More fundamentally,
exclusive maternal care is an unusual arrangement in human history. In many
societies infants are carried, fed, and soothed by a shifting cast of siblings,
grandmothers, and unrelated adults, and they form multiple attachments
accordingly, ranked but not reducible to one.

The third concerns what the early theory claimed about damage. Bowlby's 1951
report for the World Health Organization was written in strong language and was
read, in the popular press of the time, as an argument that mothers should not
work. Michael Rutter's careful reanalysis separated deprivation — losing a bond
that existed — from privation, never having formed one, and showed that the
severe outcomes attributed to maternal absence tracked the latter, along with
the general poverty of institutional environments. His later work following
children adopted out of Romanian institutions found something more specific:
duration of deprivation before placement mattered, recovery was substantial for
many, and a minority carried persistent difficulties that ordinary family life
did not erase. That is a harder and more useful finding than either the original
alarm or the reflexive dismissal that followed it.

What survives all of this is modest and durable. Infants organize their behavior
around particular adults; the organization is visible, codable, and predictable
from how those adults respond to distress; and it shows up later in life as a
tilt rather than a verdict.
