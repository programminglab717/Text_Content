# Where Prejudice Comes From

In a laboratory in Bristol in the early 1970s, schoolboys were shown clusters of
dots on a screen and asked to guess how many there were. Afterward they were
told, on the basis of nothing, that they belonged to the group of
overestimators or the group of underestimators. They never met the other group.
They never met their own. They were then asked to allocate small sums of money
to two anonymous individuals identified only by code number and group
membership, using a set of printed matrices. They favored their own side. More
than that, given a choice between a matrix that maximized what their group
received and one that maximized the gap between the groups, a substantial number
chose the gap, taking less in absolute terms so long as the others took less
still.

Henri Tajfel had designed the study as a baseline. The idea was to strip a group
down past the point where any bias should be possible — no history, no contact,
no competition for resources, no stake at all — and then add ingredients until
discrimination appeared. It appeared at the baseline. Nothing needed adding.

That result reframed a field that had spent thirty years looking for prejudice
in the wrong place, and it is the natural starting point for any account of
where the phenomenon actually comes from.

## The Search for the Prejudiced Personality

The dominant explanation in the decade after the Second World War was that
bigotry is a symptom of a damaged individual. The Authoritarian Personality,
produced by Theodor Adorno and colleagues at Berkeley, argued that harsh,
status-anxious parenting produced adults who submitted to authority, punished
deviance, thought in rigid conventional categories, and displaced their
resentment onto minorities. The project built a questionnaire, the F-scale, and
found that scores on it correlated with ethnic hostility of several kinds at
once — which was itself a real and durable finding, since people who dislike one
out-group tend to dislike others.

The explanation was attacked almost immediately and has not fully recovered. The
F-scale was written so that agreement always indicated authoritarianism, which
made acquiescent respondents look authoritarian regardless of content. The
psychoanalytic machinery connecting toilet training to political attitudes was
not testable in the form given. The interviews were conducted by researchers who
already knew the respondents' scores. And the whole framework had a political
blind spot, locating rigidity on one side of the spectrum by construction. Bob
Altemeyer's later work salvaged the useful core by building a
right-wing-authoritarianism measure with balanced item wording and dropping the
developmental story, and something real survives there. But a personality theory
cannot explain the thing that most needs explaining: that prejudice rises and
falls in whole populations within a few years, faster than personalities form.

Gordon Allport's The Nature of Prejudice, published in the mid-1950s, made the
decisive move away from pathology. Allport's argument was that the cognitive
machinery underneath prejudice is entirely ordinary. Human beings categorize
because categorizing is how thought is economical; categories acquire emotional
tone; and a category with a tone attached, applied to people, is a stereotype.
The person holding it is not sick. That is the uncomfortable part.

## Categories Are Cheap

The cognitive research of the 1970s and after filled in Allport's sketch with
mechanisms, and most of them turn out to require no hostility at all.

Illusory correlation is the cleanest. Present people with descriptions of two
groups, one larger and one smaller, in which the ratio of desirable to
undesirable behaviors is identical for both. Because both minority membership
and negative behavior are relatively infrequent, their co-occurrences are
distinctive, and distinctive events are recalled disproportionately. People
finish the task believing the smaller group behaved worse. No motive, no
learning history, no conflict — just a quirk in how memory weights the unusual,
producing an outcome indistinguishable from prejudice.

Categorization also degrades memory in a specific way. In studies where
participants watch a discussion and later attribute statements to speakers,
errors cluster within categories: people confuse one member of a group with
another while rarely confusing across groups. The mind has encoded the category
and only loosely encoded the individual, which is the laboratory version of the
experience of being treated as a representative rather than a person.

Patricia Devine's contribution was to separate knowing a stereotype from
endorsing it. Members of a culture learn its stereotypes early and thoroughly,
the way they learn advertising jingles, and the associations activate
automatically on encountering a relevant cue regardless of what the person
believes. Low-prejudice people are not those in whom the association fails to
fire; they are those who notice it and override it, which takes attention,
time, and cognitive resources. That framing explains why fatigue, time pressure,
and distraction reliably make discriminatory behavior more likely in people who
sincerely disavow it.

## Camp, and the Problem With Superordinate Goals

The best-known field study of intergroup hostility ran at a summer camp in
Oklahoma. Muzafer Sherif divided boys into two groups, the Rattlers and the
Eagles, let each develop its own identity, and then arranged a tournament with
prizes. Hostility escalated from name-calling through flag-burning to raids on
each other's cabins. Attempts to fix it by simply bringing the groups together
for meals and films failed, becoming occasions for food fights. What worked, in
the account Sherif published, was a series of superordinate goals: a water
supply that had to be repaired by everyone, a truck that had to be hauled by
everyone, a film that could only be rented if both groups contributed.

The study is genuinely important and it needs handling with care. The samples
were small and consisted of boys selected for similarity. An earlier attempt at
the same design failed when the participants worked out what was happening and
refused to cooperate, and was abandoned rather than reported. Archival work has
since documented that the research staff, posing as camp counselors, did more
than observe — they helped provoke the conflict at several points — and that
the published account smoothed over episodes that did not fit. What remains
defensible is the general shape: competition over scarce goods sharpens group
boundaries, contact alone does nothing, and shared tasks that require each
other's effort can loosen them.

## Contact, and Its Conditions

Allport's contact hypothesis was never the claim that familiarity breeds
tolerance. He specified conditions: the parties must have roughly equal status
within the situation, pursue common goals, cooperate rather than compete, and
enjoy the support of authorities or custom. Contact without those conditions —
the contact of a servant with an employer, or of rival crowds at a border — has
no reason to help and frequently hurts.

Decades of accumulated studies, brought together in large reviews, support the
basic claim reasonably well, and also suggest the conditions operate more as
facilitating factors than as strict prerequisites. Two complications are worth
holding. First, causality runs both ways: people who are less prejudiced seek
out contact, so cross-sectional associations overstate the effect, and the
better evidence comes from situations where contact was assigned rather than
chosen, such as randomized roommate assignments. Second, the benefits are not
symmetric. Positive contact tends to reduce majority-group prejudice more than
it changes minority-group attitudes, and there is a body of work suggesting that
warm intergroup contact can reduce disadvantaged groups' perception of injustice
and their appetite for collective action — harmony purchased at the cost of the
very pressure that produces structural change.

## Measuring What People Will Not Say

Once open expression of prejudice became socially costly, self-report lost much
of its value, and the field turned to indirect measurement. Reaction-time
methods, the Implicit Association Test most prominently, ask people to sort
faces and words under different pairings and treat the difference in speed as an
index of association strength. The measures reliably show that most people in a
given culture carry the culture's associations, which is not in dispute.

What is in dispute is nearly everything downstream. Test–retest reliability for
individuals is modest enough that a single score is a poor description of a
person. Correlations with actual discriminatory behavior have been repeatedly
found to be small, and the debate over whether the test captures personal
attitudes or mere cultural knowledge has never been settled. Procedures that
shift implicit scores in the laboratory tend to produce effects that fade
quickly and do not clearly transfer to conduct, which is an awkward foundation
for the training industry built on top of the concept.

The strongest evidence that discrimination persists comes from a plainer method
entirely. Correspondence studies send matched applications differing only in a
name, a photograph, an address, or a signal of group membership, and count the
callbacks. They measure behavior with real consequences, they have been run
across many countries and many decades in housing, hiring, lending, and medical
referral, and they continue to find differential treatment where attitude
surveys report that prejudice has nearly disappeared.

That gap is the most useful thing the research has produced. What people say has
changed enormously. What categories do to perception, memory, and the first
half-second of a judgment has changed much less, and what institutions do with
the aggregate of those half-seconds is measurable whether or not anyone involved
can be called a bigot.
