# What Aging Does to Thinking

A retired classics teacher stands in her kitchen with her hand on a drawer,
unable to produce the word for the perforated bowl she wants. She knows it
starts with a hard sound. She knows it has three syllables. She can tell you,
without hesitation, that it comes from the Latin for strainer, and she can
conjugate the verb. The word arrives twenty minutes later, unbidden, while she
is watering the plants. Nothing about this is a sign of disease, and yet it is
unmistakably a change, and she has noticed it.

Cognitive aging is usually discussed as a single downward line, which is why so
much of the public conversation about it is unhelpful. The reality is a set of
curves moving in different directions at different rates, some of them rising
into the seventies and beyond. Knowing which is which turns out to matter a
great deal, both for deciding what is worth worrying about and for
distinguishing ordinary change from the beginning of something else.

## What Declines, and How Early

Processing speed is the first casualty and the most general one. The time
required to compare two symbols, decide which of two lights came on first, or
locate a target in a display begins lengthening in early adulthood and
continues more or less steadily thereafter. The change is small per year and
enormous across decades. Because so many cognitive operations depend on getting
intermediate results assembled before they decay, slowing propagates: a good
share of age differences on complex tasks disappears statistically once speed
is accounted for, which suggests that much of what looks like separate decline
in reasoning or memory is the same slowdown showing up in different clothing.

Working memory follows. Holding several items active while manipulating them —
keeping a phone number in mind while looking for a pen, following a sentence
with three embedded clauses, tracking who is who in a film's opening scenes —
becomes harder. Closely related is the weakening of inhibition: the ability to
keep irrelevant material out. Older adults are more disrupted by background
conversation, more likely to have an irrelevant association intrude, slower to
abandon a no-longer-useful rule. In a quiet room with one task, the deficit is
modest; in a noisy restaurant, it can be severe, and the person will report it
as a hearing problem or a memory problem when it is partly an attention
problem.

Episodic memory — the record of specific events with their context attached —
declines too, but unevenly. Recognition holds up far better than recall.
Offered a list of names, an older adult will pick out the one they met last
week; asked to produce it cold, they may not. The specific failure in the
kitchen is characteristic: word-finding trouble is overwhelmingly a retrieval
problem rather than a storage problem, which is why the target usually arrives
later, and why a first-letter cue so often unlocks it. Proper names go first
because they are arbitrary, have few semantic connections supporting them, and
are usually not rehearsed.

Source memory degrades faster than content memory. People remember the fact and
lose the provenance — where they read it, who said it, whether they had already
told this story to this person. Prospective memory, remembering to do something
at a future moment, is also vulnerable, although less so in daily life than in
the laboratory, because older adults are conspicuously better at using external
supports: lists, calendars, the pill organizer, the car keys hung in one place.

## What Holds, and What Improves

Vocabulary rises across most of adult life and often peaks in the sixties or
later. So does general knowledge, and so does the accumulated stock of
domain-specific expertise, which is why an experienced physician, editor, or
mechanic can outperform a faster-thinking novice by a wide margin on real
problems in their field. Psychologists have long divided the two, calling the
speed-and-manipulation side fluid ability and the knowledge side crystallized.
The fluid curve turns down early. The crystallized curve keeps climbing for
decades and only erodes late.

Procedural skill is stable. Playing an instrument, touch typing, driving a
familiar route, the hands' knowledge of a craft — none of this depends on the
systems that are slipping. Semantic memory, the broad web of facts and
meanings, stays largely intact. Recognition of faces and voices holds up
reasonably well. And there is good evidence that the emotional life improves:
across adulthood, negative affect tends to decline, emotional stability rises,
and older adults show a consistent bias toward attending to and remembering
positive material over negative. The most persuasive explanation is that as the
remaining time horizon shortens, goals shift from acquiring information toward
extracting meaning and regulating feeling, and attention follows the goals. The
same shift shows up in younger people facing a short horizon for other reasons,
which argues that it is about time perspective rather than age as such.

## What Is Happening Physically

The aging brain loses volume, and it does not lose it evenly. Prefrontal
regions shrink comparatively early, the hippocampus later but consequentially,
while primary sensory and motor cortex are relatively spared. The white matter
connecting regions deteriorates as well, with myelin degradation and small
vessel damage slowing and degrading long-range communication — a plausible
substrate for the general slowing described above. Dopamine receptor density
falls steadily through adult life, which matters for anything involving
updating, flexibility, and reward-guided learning.

Activation patterns change in ways that are not straightforwardly bad. Older
adults performing a task that engages one hemisphere in younger adults often
recruit both, and frontal regions are frequently more active rather than less.
The most common interpretation is compensation: additional circuitry is
enlisted to achieve a result that no longer comes cheaply, and among older
adults, those showing this pattern often perform better than those who do not.
The same finding can be read less optimistically as dedifferentiation — neural
representations becoming less distinct and less efficiently targeted. Both
things are probably happening.

Variability between individuals widens dramatically with age. Two seventy-five
year olds can differ more from each other cognitively than two twenty-five year
olds could, and average curves therefore describe almost nobody. A meaningful
part of that spread is vascular. What is bad for arteries is bad for cognition,
and hypertension, diabetes, and smoking in midlife predict cognitive outcomes
decades later with depressing reliability.

How the measurements are taken also shapes the picture, and the two standard
designs disagree. Comparing seventy-year-olds with thirty-year-olds today
confounds age with everything else that differs between the cohorts: years of
schooling, childhood nutrition, familiarity with the testing format, lifetime
exposure to lead and tobacco. Such comparisons make decline look earlier and
steeper than it is. Following the same people for decades removes that
confound but introduces others — the least healthy participants drop out, and
the survivors get repeated practice at the tests. Long-running longitudinal
studies have generally found the onset of meaningful decline later than the
snapshot comparisons suggest, with most abilities holding reasonably steady
through the fifties and into the sixties before the curves begin to bend.

## What Actually Helps

The honest summary of the intervention literature is short. Aerobic exercise
has the best evidence of anything tested: it is associated with better
executive function, and in trials with older sedentary adults, sustained
programs have produced measurable gains, with some evidence of effects on
hippocampal volume. Managing blood pressure matters. Sleep matters, and the
fragmentation of sleep that is common in later life is worth treating rather
than accepting.

Correcting hearing loss deserves more attention than it gets. Uncorrected
hearing loss is one of the strongest modifiable risk factors identified for
later cognitive decline, plausibly through several routes at once: the effort
of straining to hear consumes resources that would otherwise go to
comprehension and encoding, degraded input impoverishes what gets stored, and
the social withdrawal that follows removes the stimulation and connection that
protect.

Commercial brain training is the weakest link in the chain. People reliably
improve at the trained task, and the improvement reliably fails to transfer to
anything else. Getting faster at a computerized card-sorting game makes you
faster at that game. Engagement that involves genuine novelty, difficulty, and
social contact — learning an instrument, a language, a craft, in company —
looks more promising, though disentangling cause from selection is hard, since
the people who take up such things were different beforehand.

Education and occupational complexity correlate with maintained function, which
has been formalized as cognitive reserve: the idea that richer networks and
more flexible strategies let a person absorb a given amount of pathology with
fewer visible symptoms. The uncomfortable corollary is that reserve delays the
appearance of decline without preventing the underlying damage, so when
symptoms finally break through in a high-reserve person, the disease is further
along and the subsequent course can be steeper.

## Telling Normal From Not

The distinction families most want is also the one that cannot be made from a
single anecdote. Forgetting a name and retrieving it later is normal.
Forgetting the conversation in which the name was mentioned, repeatedly,
without recognition when told, is not. Misplacing keys is normal; putting them
somewhere with no plausible logic and no memory of having done it is a
different signal. Needing longer to learn a new phone is normal; being unable
to perform a procedure you have done weekly for years is not. Normal aging
slows and thins; it does not typically erase whole events, disrupt language
beyond word-finding, or derange judgment about money and safety.

The classics teacher, for what it is worth, will almost certainly be fine. Her
Latin is intact, her sentence is intact, her sense that something is missing is
itself evidence that the monitoring apparatus is working. It is the person who
has stopped noticing whose family should pay attention.
