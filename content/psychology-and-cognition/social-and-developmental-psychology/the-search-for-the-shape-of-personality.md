# The Search for the Shape of Personality

In the mid-1930s two psychologists at Harvard sat down with an unabridged
dictionary and read it from the beginning. Gordon Allport and Henry Odbert were
looking for every word in English that could be used to describe one person as
distinguishable from another. They found something close to eighteen thousand
of them — *punctual*, *sullen*, *gregarious*, *pompous*, *pious*,
*hot-headed*, along with thousands of near-synonyms and obsolete curiosities —
and then spent a great deal of labor sorting that pile into categories:
enduring dispositions, temporary states, social evaluations, and a residual
heap of everything else. The list of stable traits still ran to several
thousand entries.

The exercise sounds like lexicography, but the reasoning behind it was a real
hypothesis. If a difference between people matters enough, often enough, then
over the centuries a language will have coined a word for it. Whatever the true
structure of personality is, it should therefore be recoverable from the
vocabulary, encoded messily and redundantly in ordinary speech. That premise,
usually called the lexical hypothesis, set the agenda for the next sixty years.

## From Eighteen Thousand Words to Sixteen Factors

The obvious problem was that several thousand traits is not a theory. Raymond
Cattell took the Allport and Odbert list and began compressing it. He merged
synonyms, cut rare terms, and reduced the set to a few hundred, then to a few
dozen clusters, and finally applied factor analysis — a statistical method for
finding the smallest number of underlying dimensions that can account for the
pattern of correlations among many measured variables. If people who describe
themselves as talkative also tend to describe themselves as energetic and
sociable and bold, those four ratings are probably four windows onto one thing,
and the mathematics will say so.

Doing this in the 1940s meant doing it largely by hand, with rotations of the
factor matrix computed over weeks. Cattell arrived at sixteen factors and built
a questionnaire around them, and the sixteen-factor model had a long commercial
life. But almost nobody else could reproduce sixteen. Re-analyses of his own
correlation data by other investigators kept collapsing into a smaller number.
In 1949 Donald Fiske got five. In the early 1960s, Ernest Tupes and Raymond
Christal, working for the US Air Force on the problem of predicting which
officer candidates would succeed, ran factor analyses on eight different
samples of peer ratings and found the same five factors turning up in all of
them. Warren Norman replicated the result shortly afterward and attached names.

Then the finding sat almost unnoticed for fifteen years. The Air Force report
was a technical document with limited circulation, and the field had other
preoccupations, chiefly an argument about whether traits existed at all.

## The Attack on Traits

That argument arrived in 1968 with Walter Mischel's *Personality and
Assessment*, and it very nearly ended the enterprise. Mischel's case was
empirical and awkward. Trait scores, he pointed out, correlated only modestly
with actual behavior in any specific situation — a correlation ceiling low
enough that knowing someone's conscientiousness score told you remarkably
little about whether they would return a borrowed book. Worse, behavior showed
poor consistency across situations. A child who cheated on a test was not
notably more likely to cheat at a game. Honesty, the classic virtue-trait,
behaved less like a property of persons and more like a property of
circumstances.

The rebuttals took two decades and were ultimately convincing. The first was
statistical: a single behavior on a single occasion is a wildly unreliable
measurement, and traits were never supposed to predict single acts. Aggregate
across many occasions — count how often the book gets returned over two years,
not whether it came back on Tuesday — and the correlations climb sharply. The
second reframed what a trait is. Recorded moment to moment, a person's
extraverted behavior turns out to vary enormously across a week, covering
nearly the whole range of the scale. What distinguishes people is not a fixed
setting but the average of that distribution, and those averages are strikingly
stable. A trait is a central tendency in a cloud of variable behavior, not a
constant, and it was the wrong expectation of constancy that made the data look
damning.

## Five Again, and Then Six

By the early 1980s the five-factor result was being rediscovered from several
directions at once. Lewis Goldberg, running lexical studies with new samples
and better computing, kept recovering the same structure and gave it the name
that stuck: the Big Five. Paul Costa and Robert McCrae arrived at a
substantially identical model from questionnaire research rather than from
adjectives, and built the instrument that made it the field standard, along
with a scheme of six narrower facets beneath each broad domain.

The five are ordinarily labeled openness to experience, conscientiousness,
extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism. The labels are imperfect and the
boundaries are fuzzy — openness in particular has a long history of
disagreement over whether it is about intellect, imagination, or aesthetic
sensitivity — but the structure recurs. It recurs in self-reports, in ratings
by spouses and colleagues, in dozens of languages, and in samples from many
industrialized societies. Twin and adoption studies put the heritability of
each dimension at roughly half, with the remaining variance attributable
mostly to non-shared environment and measurement error rather than to the
family a person grew up in, a finding that has survived repeated attempts to
make it go away.

Hans Eysenck had meanwhile pursued a different strategy, insisting that a
personality dimension is only worth naming if it has a biological mechanism.
He settled on three: extraversion, neuroticism, and psychoticism, and
proposed that introverts differ from extraverts in baseline cortical arousal,
which is why one group seeks stimulation and the other avoids it. The specific
arousal theory did not survive intact, but the demand behind it — that
description should eventually connect to physiology — remains the most serious
complaint against a purely descriptive taxonomy.

Later lexical work in languages other than English produced a persistent
wrinkle. Michael Ashton and Kibeom Lee found that a sixth factor kept appearing
across studies in Korean, Italian, Hungarian, Polish, and other languages: a
dimension of sincerity, fairness, modesty, and the absence of greed and
entitlement, which they called honesty-humility. Their six-factor model
reorganizes agreeableness and neuroticism somewhat to accommodate it, and it
predicts certain outcomes — exploitation, cheating, sexual opportunism — better
than the five-factor arrangement, which tends to scatter that variance across
agreeableness and conscientiousness.

Neither five nor six is a magic number, and the better way to read the result
is as one level of a hierarchy. Below each broad factor sit narrower facets —
conscientiousness divides into orderliness, industriousness, dutifulness,
deliberation and more, and those facets sometimes predict specific outcomes
better than the domain that contains them. Above the broad factors, the five
are not quite independent of one another; conscientiousness, agreeableness and
emotional stability tend to correlate positively, as do extraversion and
openness, which has led some researchers to propose two higher-order metatraits
sitting over the whole set. Where you slice the hierarchy is a choice about
resolution rather than a discovery, and the right slice depends on what you are
trying to predict.

## What the Model Does and Does Not Do

The five dimensions predict real outcomes at meaningful but unspectacular
strengths. Conscientiousness predicts job performance across essentially every
occupation studied, along with academic attainment, and it predicts longevity
well enough that the association has been replicated in cohorts followed for
decades. Neuroticism predicts anxiety and mood disorders, relationship
dissolution, and a range of physical health complaints. Extraversion predicts
social network size and positive affect. Agreeableness predicts cooperation and
the absence of conflict. Openness predicts intellectual interests, political
liberalism in most Western samples, and artistic engagement.

They also change. The old claim that character is set like plaster by thirty
turned out to be wrong. Averaged across large samples, people become more
conscientious, more agreeable, and more emotionally stable from the late teens
through middle age — a drift regular enough to be called the maturity
principle. The changes are not large per decade, they can be accelerated by
life events such as a first serious job or a stable partnership, and the rank
ordering among people stays fairly consistent even as everyone shifts, but the
trajectory is real and it points in a socially convenient direction.

Two cautions belong beside all this. The first concerns the enormous popular
market in personality typing that has almost nothing to do with the research
above. The best-known instrument, developed by Katharine Briggs and her
daughter Isabel Briggs Myers from Carl Jung's speculative typology, sorts
people into sixteen types using four either-or dichotomies. The scores it is
built on are not actually bimodal — people pile up in the middle of each
dimension rather than at the poles — so a person a hair either side of a cutoff
gets a different four-letter label, which is why a substantial fraction of
takers are reclassified when they retest a few weeks later. It remains
immensely popular with employers, and its popularity is a fact about the appeal
of types rather than about personality.

The second caution is about generality. Nearly all the evidence comes from
literate populations filling out questionnaires, mostly in industrialized
societies. When researchers took the standard instruments to a forager-farmer
population in lowland Bolivia, the five-factor structure did not emerge
cleanly; what surfaced instead looked closer to two broad dimensions
concerning industriousness and prosociality. Similar difficulties have appeared
in other small-scale societies. That may reflect translation and testing
artifacts, or it may mean that the fivefold structure is partly a product of
the kind of differentiated social world in which people occupy many distinct
roles and need many distinct words to describe how others behave in them. The
dictionary Allport and Odbert read was, after all, a document of a particular
society, and the hypothesis that launched the whole project always implied that
the answer would be found in a language rather than in a species.
