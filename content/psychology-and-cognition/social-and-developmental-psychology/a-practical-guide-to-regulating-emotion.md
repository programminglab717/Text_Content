# A Practical Guide to Regulating Emotion

The email arrives at ten to five on a Friday. Two lines, no greeting, copying
your manager, and by the time you reach the second sentence your jaw has
tightened and your hands are already moving toward the reply box. You have
perhaps ninety seconds before you do something you will spend the weekend
thinking about.

Most advice about managing feelings arrives too late to be useful, aimed at
this exact moment, when the options have narrowed to a few bad ones. The
useful way to think about emotion regulation is as a timeline rather than a
single decision point. An emotion unfolds in a sequence — a situation occurs,
attention lands on part of it, that part gets appraised as meaning something
about your goals, and a coordinated response follows in physiology, expression
and behavior — and each stage in that sequence is a place where you can
intervene. The interventions available early are cheap and effective. The ones
available at the end are expensive and mostly cosmetic. Nearly all the
practical leverage comes from moving your intervention earlier.

## Choosing and Changing the Situation

The earliest move is not being there. This sounds like avoidance, and taken to
an extreme it becomes avoidance, which is the engine of most anxiety
disorders. But used deliberately it is the single most efficient tool
available, and the people who seem unusually even-tempered are frequently just
unusually good at arranging their lives. They do not check email after dinner.
They do not have the argument in the car. They schedule the difficult
conversation for a Tuesday morning rather than at the end of a long day. None
of this requires any self-control in the moment, which is precisely the point:
you are spending a small amount of foresight to avoid spending a large amount
of willpower later.

The test for whether situation selection has curdled into avoidance is whether
the choice shrinks your life. Declining a meeting you do not need to attend
costs nothing. Declining every meeting where you might be criticized costs your
career, and teaches your nervous system that the meeting was genuinely
dangerous, which makes the next one worse.

A step later comes changing the situation you are in rather than leaving it.
Asking a question that redirects a conversation. Saying plainly that you need
ten minutes. Turning the phone face down. Standing up. These are small
mechanical adjustments to the input, and they are underrated because they feel
too simple to count as coping.

## Where Attention Goes

Once you are in a situation you cannot leave or alter, the next control is
attention. Distraction is the crudest form and works better than its reputation
suggests, particularly when arousal is high. Under intense emotion there is not
enough cognitive room left over to do anything sophisticated, and getting
attention off the trigger long enough for arousal to come down is the sensible
first step. The caveat is that distraction does nothing to change the meaning
of the event, so it works as a holding action rather than a resolution. People
who rely on it exclusively find that the same situations keep producing the
same reactions indefinitely.

The failure mode of attentional control is rumination: attention locked on the
emotional material, circling it repeatedly, in an abstract and evaluative mode
— why did this happen to me, what is wrong with me, what does this say about my
life. It feels like problem-solving and it is reliably the opposite. Rumination
prolongs low mood, degrades actual problem-solving, and is among the better
predictors of who will develop and who will relapse into depression. Two things
interrupt it with decent evidence behind them: shifting from abstract framing
to concrete framing, asking *what exactly happened and what is the next
physical step*, rather than *why am I like this*; and behavioral interruption,
which mostly means getting up and doing something that requires attention.

## Changing What It Means

The most studied intervention sits at the appraisal stage, and it is worth
learning properly because it is the one that changes the emotion itself rather
than its expression. Reappraisal means altering your reading of the situation
before the emotional response has fully committed.

The crude version — telling yourself it is fine when it is not — does not work,
and the failure of that version is why people dismiss the whole technique. What
works is finding a reading that is genuinely plausible and that you had not
considered. The email might be a two-line email because the sender was writing
it from a train platform, having read the thread badly, ten minutes before
leaving for a flight. That may not be true. But it is no less likely than the
interpretation your body already committed to, and the fact that you generated
it at all breaks the automaticity of the first one.

Several variants are worth having available:

- **Distancing.** Describe the situation as an uninvolved observer would, in
  the third person, using your own name. This sounds ridiculous and works
  measurably; language about the self in the third person reduces emotional
  reactivity and improves the quality of reasoning about the problem.
- **Temporal distancing.** Ask how much of this will matter in a year. Not as a
  dismissal, but as a genuine estimate, which is usually lower than the
  present moment implies and occasionally higher, which is also worth knowing.
- **Reframing the goal.** Anxiety and excitement share most of their
  physiological signature. Interpreting a racing heart before a presentation as
  readiness rather than as impending failure is an easier sell to yourself than
  attempting to be calm, because it does not require the arousal to go away.

Reappraisal is most valuable exactly where action is unavailable: a diagnosis,
a rejection, weather, another person's behavior. Where the situation is
changeable, reappraising it is a way of talking yourself out of fixing it. The
skill is not reappraisal; the skill is knowing which kind of situation you are
in.

## The Last Resort, and Its Costs

If nothing has been done earlier, you are left with response modulation:
managing the emotion after it has arrived. This is where most people spend all
their effort, and it is the least efficient place to spend it.

Expressive suppression — keeping your face still while feeling everything —
has been compared directly with reappraisal in a large number of studies, and
it comes off badly. It reduces the outward display without reducing the
internal experience. It keeps physiological arousal elevated rather than
lowering it, and it seems to raise arousal in the person you are talking to as
well, who registers the flatness as something being withheld. It consumes
cognitive resources, so people who suppress during an event remember the event
less well afterward. Habitual suppressors report worse relationships and lower
well-being. It remains occasionally necessary — funerals, negotiations,
emergency rooms — but it is a tactic for the next twenty minutes, not a way of
living.

Two response-level interventions do earn their place. Slow breathing, with the
exhale distinctly longer than the inhale, lowers physiological arousal by a
route that does not require any cognitive work at all, which matters when there
is no cognitive capacity left. And naming the emotion, in plain words, to
yourself or aloud — *I am humiliated and I am angry about being humiliated* —
reduces its intensity reliably enough that the effect has been demonstrated
repeatedly in the laboratory. Putting feelings into words appears to engage the
same regions that dampen the response, which is one plausible reason talking
about a bad day helps even when nothing about the day changes.

## Flexibility Over Technique

The finding that has held up best across this whole literature is not about any
particular strategy. It is that the people who do well are the ones who use
different strategies in different circumstances and switch when something is
not working. Reappraisal is better than suppression on average and worse than
suppression when your position is weak and the other party is hostile.
Distraction is better than sitting with the feeling when the feeling is
overwhelming and worse when the feeling contains information you need.
Acceptance — allowing the emotion to be present without arguing with it — beats
nearly everything for physical pain, chronic conditions, and grief, where
struggle adds a second layer of suffering to the first.

Rigidity is the actual pathology. It shows up as one move applied to
everything: the person who reframes their way out of every conflict and
therefore never resolves one, the person who leaves every room, the person who
analyses each feeling into philosophical abstraction and never simply has it.

Two background conditions govern all of the above and neither is psychological.
Short sleep raises emotional reactivity and degrades the frontal control that
reappraisal depends on, so a tired person is attempting a harder task with
worse equipment. Alcohol narrows attention to the immediately salient, which is
why arguments that start after the second drink escalate in ways nobody
intended. Fixing either of those does more for most people's emotional life than
any technique in this article.

As for the email: the correct move was always to close the laptop and answer it
on Monday. You knew that already. The reason it is hard is that the body has
already prepared a response and disposing of prepared responses is the whole
problem — which is why the people who handle these moments well are usually not
handling them at all, but have arranged, quietly and some time ago, not to be
reading email at ten to five on a Friday.
