# The Long Argument Over Moral Development

Jean Piaget spent part of the 1920s crouching on Swiss pavements watching boys
play marbles. He was not interested in who won. He wanted to know what the
children thought the rules were, where they had come from, and whether they
could be changed — and he found that the answers shifted with age in a way that
had nothing to do with how well anyone actually played. Small children handled
the marbles in a private ritual of their own, loosely imitating older boys
without grasping that a game is a shared agreement. Somewhat older ones played
by rules they regarded as fixed, ancient, and handed down, possibly by adults,
possibly by God; asked whether the rules could be altered by agreement, they
said no, and were sometimes shocked at the question. Older boys still played
the most exactingly of all, negotiated variants before starting, and understood
perfectly well that the rules were a human invention that the players could
amend if everyone consented.

From that, and from a set of paired stories he told to children, Piaget drew a
distinction that has outlasted almost everything else in his moral psychology.
Young children treat rules as features of the world, like gravity; wrongdoing is
measured by the size of the damage, and punishment is understood to follow
offenses automatically, as though the universe were keeping accounts. A child
who breaks fifteen cups by accident is naughtier than one who breaks a single
cup while sneaking jam. Later, children shift to judging intention over outcome,
treat rules as agreements between equals, and start expecting punishment to fit
the offense rather than merely to hurt. Piaget thought the engine of the change
was not adult instruction but the company of other children — the experience of
negotiating with people who have roughly the same power you do.

## Kohlberg's Ladder

Lawrence Kohlberg took the developmental frame and built something far more
ambitious on it. Beginning in the late 1950s, he presented boys with dilemmas
in which two obligations collided, the most famous involving a man named Heinz
whose wife is dying and whose only recourse is to steal an overpriced drug. The
point was never whether the interviewee said steal or don't steal. Kohlberg
scored the justification, and argued that justifications fell into six stages
grouped in three levels.

At the preconventional level, the reasons are about consequences to the self:
he shouldn't steal because he'd be caught, or he should because he needs his
wife around. At the conventional level, the reasons are about roles and rules:
a good husband would do it, or the law exists and cannot be suspended for
individual cases. At the postconventional level, the reasons stand above the
rules and are used to evaluate them: laws are social contracts that serve
purposes, and where a law fails the purpose that justifies it, the purpose wins.
Kohlberg claimed the sequence was invariant, that nobody skipped stages or
regressed, and that the stages were universal because they were structures of
reasoning rather than content.

The framework dominated the field for two decades and then absorbed a series of
blows, some fatal to specific claims and none fatal to the whole.

The first came from inside. Stage six, the summit of universal ethical
principles, proved almost impossible to find in real interviews, and Kohlberg
eventually removed it from the working scoring manual, keeping it as a
theoretical construct rather than an empirical category. A developmental
endpoint that nobody reaches is an awkward thing to have at the top of a ladder.

The second came from Carol Gilligan, who had worked with Kohlberg and argued
that his instrument had a systematic tilt. The dilemmas pitted abstract claims
against each other and rewarded answers that ranked principles; responses framed
in terms of relationships, particular people, and the concrete consequences of
a choice for everyone entangled in it tended to score lower. She called the
second orientation an ethic of care, and attributed the tilt partly to
Kohlberg's original all-male longitudinal sample. Her claim that women reason in
the care voice and men in the justice voice has not held up especially well;
studies looking for a sex difference of that kind have produced an inconsistent
picture. What has held up is the weaker and more interesting version: care and
justice are two real orientations, most people use both, and which one a person
reaches for depends heavily on whether the dilemma is about strangers or
intimates.

The third came from anthropology and cross-cultural work. Richard Shweder's
fieldwork in India suggested that the moral domain in many societies is
organized around commitments Kohlberg's scheme could barely see — obligations
attached to one's position in a community, and a whole register of purity,
pollution, and sanctity that has nothing to do with harm or fairness. Scored on
Kohlberg's manual, a sophisticated adult reasoning within those commitments can
come out looking conventional or worse. That is a strong hint that the ladder
was measuring a particular kind of individualist articulacy along with, or
instead of, moral development.

## Domains, Not Stages

While the stage theory was being picked apart, Elliot Turiel was making a
different move: rather than one moral sense that matures, he proposed that
children sort social rules into distinct domains from very early on. Ask a
five-year-old whether it would be all right to talk during quiet time if the
teacher said so, and the child will generally say yes — rules of that kind are
made by authorities and can be unmade by them. Ask whether it would be all right
to hit another child if the teacher said so, and the answer is no, with the
justification usually being that it hurts. Young children distinguish moral
transgressions from conventional ones along several dimensions at once: whether
the rule holds in other schools and other countries, whether authority can waive
it, how serious the violation is, and what makes it wrong.

Domain theory reframed a lot of apparent stage differences as differences in how
a particular act is categorized. Much adult moral disagreement, on this view, is
not one party reasoning at a lower level than the other but the two parties
placing the same act in different domains — one treating a question as moral and
non-negotiable, the other as conventional or as a matter of personal
jurisdiction where nobody else gets a vote.

## Intuition Arrives First

The most disruptive challenge came from Jonathan Haidt, who attacked the
assumption underneath both Piaget and Kohlberg: that judgment follows reasoning.
His demonstration of choice involves stories built so that every harm-based
objection is blocked in advance — consensual, private, protected, with no victim
available. People confronted with these generally condemn the act immediately,
then produce a reason, watch the reason get dismantled, produce another, and
eventually arrive at a sputtering "I can't explain it, I just know it's wrong."
Haidt called this moral dumbfounding and read it as evidence that the judgment
arrives first, fast and affectively, with reasoning following as advocacy rather
than inquiry.

His subsequent moral foundations framework proposed that intuitions cluster
around a handful of concerns — care and harm, fairness, loyalty, authority,
sanctity, with liberty added later — and that political and cultural
disagreement often reflects different weightings across those clusters rather
than different quantities of moral development. The specific taxonomy remains
contested: the number of foundations, whether they factor cleanly, and whether
they are innate modules or learned emphases are all live questions. The broader
claim about the priority of intuition has fared better, though the strongest
version overshot. Deliberate reasoning demonstrably does change moral judgments,
particularly over longer timescales, in conversation with people whose
intuitions differ, and about cases where an initial reaction is clearly in
tension with a principle the person already holds.

## Judgment and Conduct

Running underneath all of this is a gap that the stage theorists never closed.
Knowing what is right, and being able to say why, is not the same as doing it.
The point was made devastatingly early, in a large study of schoolchildren's
honesty conducted in the late 1920s by Hugh Hartshorne and Mark May, which gave
children many opportunities to cheat, lie, and steal across different settings.
Cheating turned out to be far more situation-specific than anyone expected —
children who cheated on a test might not cheat at a game, and knowledge of moral
rules predicted conduct weakly. The results were taken as a blow against
character education, and later reanalyses softened the picture somewhat, but the
core finding that circumstances carry enormous weight has never gone away.

The other flank of the field now runs much younger than Piaget ever looked.
Toddlers barely able to walk will open a cupboard door for an adult who is
visibly struggling with an armload of books, without being asked and without
reward, which suggests that some dispositions toward cooperation are in place
long before any of the reasoning is. Infant work using looking-time and reaching
measures has gone further, reporting preferences for characters who help over
characters who hinder — a striking result that has proved harder to reproduce in
large, coordinated, multi-laboratory attempts than its fame in popular accounts
would suggest. That caution is worth holding onto, because the temptation to
read early sociability as proof of an innate moral faculty is exactly the kind of
overreach that has cost this field its previous consensus twice already.
