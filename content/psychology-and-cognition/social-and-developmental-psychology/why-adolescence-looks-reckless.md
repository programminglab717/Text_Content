# Why Adolescence Looks Reckless

Put a sixteen-year-old alone in a driving simulator and he drives about as
carefully as an adult. Put two of his friends in the back seat and his
willingness to run a yellow light goes up sharply. Run the same experiment on
adults and the friends make almost no difference. That single contrast does
more to explain adolescence than any amount of talk about hormones, and it
points at something that a century of hand-wringing about teenagers has mostly
missed: the problem is not that adolescents cannot assess risk. Ask them
directly and they estimate the odds of a bad outcome about as well as their
parents do, sometimes overestimating them. The problem is what a reward looks
like to them, and who is watching.

G. Stanley Hall gave the period its enduring label of storm and stress at the
beginning of the twentieth century, and the phrase has been an obstacle ever
since. It implies malfunction — a system running badly until it settles. The
better reading is that adolescence is a developmental stage with its own
design, one that appears in some form across mammals and does specific work.
It is less a breakdown than a rebuild carried out while the vehicle is still
being driven.

## Two Systems on Different Clocks

The most durable account of adolescent behavior involves two neural systems
that mature on different schedules. The first is the network that responds to
reward and social salience, running through the ventral striatum and its
dopaminergic inputs. Puberty remodels it. Sensitivity to reward — novelty,
money, status, the approval of peers — climbs steeply in the early teens and
peaks somewhere in the middle of the decade, higher than it was in childhood
and higher than it will ever be again.

The second system is the one that exercises control: the prefrontal regions
that hold a goal in mind, suppress a prepotent response, and project the
consequence of an action into next week. That system matures slowly and more
or less linearly, and it is not finished until the mid-twenties. Grey matter in
the cortex thins through adolescence as unused synapses are pruned and
surviving circuits are strengthened; white matter tracts continue myelinating,
speeding the traffic between distant regions, with the long-range connections
into the frontal lobe among the last to finish.

The gap between those two curves is where adolescence lives. For several years
a person is running maximum incentive salience on immature brakes. This is not
a metaphor for immaturity in general — it is specific. Adolescents are not
uniformly impulsive. In cool, unhurried, unemotional conditions, teenagers
reason nearly as well as adults, which is why they can write a convincing
essay about the dangers of drunk driving and then accept a ride. Under
emotional arousal, with peers present and a decision required now, the
performance gap opens wide. Legal systems that distinguish between what a
sixteen-year-old understands and what a sixteen-year-old can do in a parking
lot at midnight are tracking something real.

Peers are the amplifier. The presence of other adolescents increases activity
in reward-related regions during risky choices, which does not happen to the
same degree in adults. Social exclusion registers strongly too: being dropped
from a trivial online ball-tossing game with strangers produces distress in
adolescents out of any proportion to the stakes. From the inside, none of this
feels like a neural bias. It feels like the correct weighting of an obviously
important situation.

## The Widening Gap

Two social trends have pulled the ends of adolescence apart. Puberty now begins
earlier than it did a century ago, by a margin of a few years, driven largely
by improved nutrition and body composition and possibly by other factors that
remain argued over. Meanwhile the markers of adult standing — finished
education, stable income, independent household, children — have moved
steadily later. The result is a stretch of ten or fifteen years during which a
person is biologically adult, sexually mature, highly reward-sensitive, and
socially positioned as a dependent with little consequential responsibility.
No previous society arranged things this way. Whatever adolescence was designed
for, it was not designed to last that long.

Sleep makes the mismatch worse in a way that is almost comically avoidable. At
puberty, the circadian clock shifts later: melatonin release is delayed, and
falling asleep before eleven becomes genuinely difficult rather than merely
undesirable. Sleep need does not drop. A school day that starts at half past
seven therefore imposes chronic restriction on precisely the population whose
self-control is least robust, and the documented effects of later start times
on attendance, mood, and crash rates have been consistent enough that the
resistance to changing them is clearly about bus schedules rather than
evidence.

## What the Stage Is For

If adolescent risk-taking were simply a defect, natural selection would have
had a long time to remove it, and it has not. Consider what the period
accomplishes. In most social mammals, adolescence is when an animal leaves the
group it was born into, or renegotiates its standing within it. Doing that
requires exactly the traits that look pathological from a parent's chair: an
appetite for novelty, a tolerance for uncertainty, intense interest in
same-aged peers and declining interest in the adults who have been directing
you, willingness to try things whose outcome is unknown. An adolescent who
weighed every risk the way a cautious forty-year-old does would never leave
home, never approach a stranger, never attempt anything at which they might
visibly fail.

The same plasticity that makes the period dangerous makes it formative.
Adolescence appears to be a sensitive window for social learning in the way
early childhood is for language. Musical skill, athletic technique, a second
language's accent, political orientation, the shape of one's taste — all
consolidate during these years and become harder to reshape afterward. The
music a person loves at seventeen tends to be the music they love at sixty.
That is not sentimentality; it reflects a nervous system in a state of unusual
openness to whatever it encounters.

Criminologists have found a related pattern in offending. Rates of minor
lawbreaking rise through the teens, peak somewhere around the late teens or
very early twenties, and then fall away steeply — a curve so consistent across
societies and eras that it is treated as a basic fact. Most of the people on
the rising limb of that curve stop without intervention, aging out as their
circumstances and their control systems catch up. A much smaller group, whose
difficulties usually began long before adolescence, continues. Confusing the
two has produced a lot of bad policy, since sanctions calibrated to the second
group get applied to the first, at exactly the age when a criminal record does
the most durable damage to the trajectory that would otherwise have corrected
itself.

The window has a shadow side. Most psychiatric conditions that will appear in a
lifetime declare themselves by the mid-twenties, and many first appear in
adolescence: depression, anxiety disorders, eating disorders, the psychoses.
Substance use initiated during this period is more likely to become dependence
than use initiated later. A system remodeling itself under the influence of
experience is by definition a system that experience can damage.

## Storm and Stress, Reconsidered

The stereotype exaggerates the turbulence. Most adolescents do not have
disastrous relationships with their parents; conflict rises in early
adolescence, largely over mundane matters of autonomy and personal jurisdiction
rather than values, and then subsides. Most teenagers report reasonably good
relations with the adults in their lives and adopt broadly similar views on the
things those adults consider important. The proportion who pass through the
period in serious difficulty is a minority, although a substantial one, and
predicting membership in that minority depends far more on what is happening
around a young person — poverty, instability, violence, a parent's illness —
than on age itself.

What does change, almost universally, is the relationship between the self and
the audience. Early adolescence brings a new and painful capacity for thinking
about what other people are thinking about you, sometimes described as the
sense of an imaginary audience: the conviction that one's appearance and
missteps are being observed and evaluated by everyone. It is a byproduct of a
freshly available cognitive ability being applied without calibration. Younger
children cannot do it; adults can and mostly stop bothering. For a few years in
between, it runs constantly, and it explains a great deal of behavior that
looks like vanity and is closer to vertigo.

Identity work runs alongside all of it. The question of who one is becomes
answerable for the first time in adolescence, because answering it requires
holding several possible versions of a self in mind at once and comparing them
— a capacity that arrives with the rest of abstract reasoning. Hence the
trying-on of positions, affiliations, vocabularies, and wardrobes, often in
rapid succession and with total sincerity in each. Adults tend to read this as
insincerity. It is closer to drafting.

None of this argues for indulgence. The developmental account of adolescence
gets misread as an excuse, when what it actually recommends is structure:
environments where the cost of an ordinary mistake is survivable, where an
adolescent gets real responsibility rather than manufactured busywork, where
the reward system has something legitimate to chase. The interventions that
work tend to be environmental rather than educational. Graduated driver
licensing that limits teenage passengers reduces crashes; lectures about crash
statistics do not. Raising the price and reducing the availability of alcohol
changes drinking; assemblies about the dangers of drinking mostly do not. The
finding underneath all of it is that adolescents know the facts perfectly well,
and that knowing the facts was never what was going to govern the decision.
